What the mirror covers

No anchor-specific mirror survives; a mass-weighted version of the aggregation heuristics would be an extension, not a continuous analogue anchored to a named computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible positive scenario is a large panel of assessors comparing the same fixed alternatives using a finite judgment scale. A type would be an assessor’s complete pairwise-comparison matrix, together with any fixed role or credibility attributes; its mass would be that type’s share of the panel. The paper’s proposal to change experts’ influence based on their distance from the group ranking or their inconsistency has a natural type-level interpretation.

But there is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are Definitions 1–7; Sections 3.2–3.4 introduce heuristics, and Section 4 reports Monte Carlo experiments. For example, §4.2.2 reports winner-restoration rates on simulated panels, but that is not a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity result. I can’t honestly state an anchor-specific continuous problem or classify its complexity without inventing a result the paper does not claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has raised no anchor to defeat. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its contributions are three aggregation heuristics and Monte Carlo results for a particular attack model and simulated panels. The reported restoration rates are empirical claims, not complexity results. That is the strongest objection to treating this paper as a continuization target.

The large-panel scenario is still coherent: let a type be a complete pairwise-comparison matrix, and let its mass be the panel share reporting that matrix. For a fixed alternative set and finite judgment scale, the paper’s calculations carry over by replacing expert averages with mass-weighted sums. The GMM priorities, inconsistency scores, and APDD/AID weights can all be computed from the support types. That produces a continuous version of the aggregation procedure, but the paper supplies no algorithmic search problem whose complexity this would reveal.

The strongest candidate for a new question is to find the minimum share of a panel that must change or add reports to alter the winner. That connects naturally to the paper’s manipulation theme, but it adds an optimization objective, attack costs, and a threat model. The experiments fix the attack strategy and measure how the proposed heuristics respond; they do not formulate that minimum-mass problem. If the attack is kept as specified, the continuous version may reduce to evaluating weighted aggregates. If the attacker may choose reports and costs freely, those choices define a new problem whose relevance and difficulty the paper does not establish.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed *anchor* and weaker against the broader possibility: large-scale group decision making could make a population model sensible, and a mass-based manipulation problem could be worth studying. The paper alone, however, gives little reason to expect a worthwhile complexity landscape from continuizing its actual results.
