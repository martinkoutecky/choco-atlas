The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), type parameters \(\alpha_t\), per-capita stakes \(w_0,w_1\), and a rational polytope \(\mathcal Q\) of limiting obstruction profiles \((u,v)\), compute the minimum \(\kappa\) and an optimal offer-rate vector \(p\) satisfying \(\kappa=\min_{p\ge0}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}(u_t+v_t)p_t+\sum_{t\in T}v_t\alpha_t(w_1-w_0)\ge w_0\) for every \((u,v)\in\mathcal Q\); determine whether \(\kappa\le w_1\).

The model it lives in

A large legislature has a distribution \(\mu\) over complete delegate types, including preference intensity and voting role. Lobby 1 chooses an offer rate \(p_t\) for each type, paying \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\) per capita. The voting institution is represented by \(\mathcal Q\), the limiting set of type-mass profiles corresponding to the paper’s \(S_2\) and \(S_3\) portions of blocking coalitions; the offer must protect passage against every profile in that set.

The objection that survived

In an atomless legislature, an individual cannot be pivotal, so the normalized \(S_2/S_3\) profiles may import finite-player incentives that \(\mu\) and a mass quota do not determine; deriving canonical profiles and showing threshold convergence remain open.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3.2’s equilibrium threshold and optimal first-mover offer allocation. It leaves the paper’s covering and matching connections, least-core results including Proposition 4.1, and the applications to particular voting institutions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which succinct sequences of type-symmetric voting rules can the limiting obstruction polytope \(\mathcal Q\) be generated or separated over efficiently?
  • When does the normalized finite-game equilibrium threshold converge to the threshold defined by \(\mathcal Q\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a Continuum Victory-Threshold problem for the paper’s sequential lobbying game. The paper already allows real-valued offers, while its legislators remain a finite set. The mirror sends the legislature’s population to a distribution over types and asks for the same first mover’s minimum outlay.

The anchor is Proposition 3.2, proved in this paper. It is an equilibrium and LP characterization, rather than a P-versus-NP classification; the authors use the LP to compute examples. In their notation, \(\hat B_m\) is the family of minimal blocking coalitions under the paper’s partial order, and each such coalition \(S\) is split into \(S_2\) and \(S_3\). The proposition says: if \(W_1\) is at least \(\sum_{S\in\hat B_m}\delta(S)V(S)\) for every subbalancing vector \(\delta\), then there is a subgame-perfect equilibrium in which Lobby 1 makes an optimal offer \(t_1^*\) for the LP below, Lobby 0 offers zero, and the bill passes. If that inequality fails for some \(\delta\), then, for any \(S\in\hat B_m\) attaining the minimum of \(\sum_{i\in S_3}\alpha_i\Delta W\), provided this minimum is below \(W_0\), there is an equilibrium in which Lobby 1 offers zero, Lobby 0 pays \(\alpha_i\Delta W\) to members of \(S_3\) and zero to others, and the bill fails. The first mover’s offer LP is

\(\displaystyle \min_{t_1\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{n}}\sum_{i\in N}t_{1i} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{i\in S_2}t_{1i}+\sum_{i\in S_3}(t_{1i}+\alpha_i\Delta W)\ge W_0 \quad\forall S\in\hat B_m. \)

Here \(V(S)=W_0-\sum_{i\in S_3}\alpha_i\Delta W\) on \(\hat B_m\), and is zero elsewhere; LP duality gives the proposition’s subbalancing-vector threshold.

For the mirror, take a large federated legislature or membership assembly with many delegates. Delegates of the same type have the same policy-preference intensity \(\alpha_t\), voting role and weight under the institutional rule, and relevant payment conditions. If there are \(N\) delegates and \(\tau\) such types, the regime has \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\); a type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the legislature. This is a regime-specific proposal: a small committee of individually distinctive members would not be a persuasive limit object.

Define the institution’s obstruction-profile polytope \(\mathcal Q\). Its points \((u,v)\) record, for each type \(t\), the masses corresponding to the paper’s \(S_2\) and \(S_3\) portions of a blocking coalition. Thus \(u_t,v_t\ge0\) and \(u_t+v_t\le\mu_t\). The intended \(\mathcal Q\) is the closed convex hull of the normalized type-composition profiles of the rule’s \(\hat B_m\) coalitions along the large-legislature regime. It is part of the instance, with a rational polytope description; finding an efficient way to generate it from a succinct institutional rule is a further computational question.

The Continuum Victory-Threshold problem takes finite \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, type parameters \(\alpha_t\), per-capita lobby stakes \(w_0,w_1\), and \(\mathcal Q\). For each type, Lobby 1 chooses an offer rate \(p_t\) per member. The problem is to compute the least total per-capita offer cost and an optimal offer vector that protect passage against every obstruction profile:

\(\displaystyle \kappa=\min_{p\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\tau}}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t\in T}(u_t+v_t)p_t+ \sum_{t\in T}v_t\alpha_t(w_1-w_0)\ge w_0 \quad\forall (u,v)\in\mathcal Q. \)

Here \(w_j=W_j/N\) is the lobby’s stake per delegate, so the preference-compensation term is the normalized counterpart of \(\alpha_i\Delta W\). A solution is the value \(\kappa\) and an optimal \(p\). Under the same continuation convention, Lobby 1 has an equilibrium offer securing passage when its available per-capita stake \(w_1\) meets this computed cost; otherwise the no-offer, blocking outcome applies. The budget comparison is evaluated at the supplied \(w_1\), since the compensation term also depends on \(w_1-w_0\).

This remains recognizably their problem: two opposing lobbies move in sequence, legislators care about policy consequences, the voting institution supplies blocking coalitions, and the decision is the first mover’s cheapest robust offer. Offers can be symmetrized within a type when the rule and obstruction profiles are invariant under permutations of its members. The mirror covers Proposition 3.2’s victory-threshold and first-mover offer-allocation result, not every result or setting in the paper.

I expect Class A when \(\mathcal Q\) has an explicit rational polyhedral description or efficient linear optimization: checking a proposed offer against all profiles is linear optimization over \(\mathcal Q\), so the threshold problem is solvable as a robust LP. The central algorithmic question is whether natural, succinct voting-rule descriptions yield efficient separation over their obstruction profiles. Complex rule encodings could retain hard coalition structure.

The soft spot is real: individual pivotality is delicate in an atomless population. A continuum member has zero mass, while \(S_2/S_3\) in the paper distinguish members by whether removing one changes the blocking status. The limiting obstruction polytope has to preserve the normalized pivotal and nonpivotal masses from the finite legislatures; the mass distribution \(\mu\) alone may not determine it. Establishing canonical limits, and whether the threshold converges from finite instances, is part of the research question. That caveat limits the mirror, but the proposed problem preserves the paper’s actual optimization target and its institution-specific obstruction structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is built around Proposition 3.2, which is the right anchor: it gives an equilibrium characterization and an LP for the first mover’s minimum offer. The objection is not that the paper lacks computation. It is that the LP’s key structure is the finite legislature’s coalition system. The constraints range over \(\hat B_m\), and their costs distinguish \(S_2\) from \(S_3\) according to what happens when one legislator is removed.

That distinction is troublesome in an atomless population. Removing one member has zero effect on a coalition’s mass, so the continuum voting rule cannot recover the finite model’s distinction between \(S_2\) and \(S_3\). Yet that distinction determines whether the LP includes the preference-compensation term \(\alpha_i\Delta W\). A literal mass model therefore loses part of the incentive structure behind the threshold. Keeping a positive mass of \(S_3\)-type legislators in \(\mathcal Q\) preserves the calculation, but it imports finite-member pivotality as extra data: \(\mu\) and a limiting mass quota do not themselves specify it. Treat those members as a bloc with positive influence, and the bloc, rather than an atomless population member, becomes the player.

There is also a computational concern. The paper already casts offers as a continuous LP and relates the \(\alpha=0\) threshold to fractional covering. If the proposed obstruction polytope \(\mathcal Q\) is explicitly supplied, optimizing over it is a robust LP; the difficult work has been moved into constructing \(\mathcal Q\). If the input is instead a succinct voting rule, the separation problem is still about that rule’s blocking coalitions. The mass variables do not by themselves yield a new algorithmic structure. That makes this look more like a normalization of the paper’s institutional LP than a new continuous-population complexity question.

The best repair would be to specify a sequence of large legislatures with a fixed number of genuinely interchangeable types, scale the lobby stakes per member, and prove that the normalized \(S_2/S_3\) obstruction profiles and equilibrium threshold converge. That is a coherent asymptotic question, and it addresses the missing pivotality data by making the sequence part of the model. But it also exposes the limit of the negative case: an increasingly large, equal-weight membership assembly is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Its limiting obstruction profiles may be canonical, and the authors could recognize the resulting threshold as their problem. A symmetric-majority example may be mathematically simple, but simplicity of the answer does not disqualify the question under this programme.

So the pivotality problem is a substantial objection to the proposed \(\mathcal Q\)-based mirror as stated: it needs more than a distribution over types to preserve Proposition 3.2’s economics. I cannot honestly turn that into the required universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The large, repeated-role legislature remains a credible counterexample to that claim.
