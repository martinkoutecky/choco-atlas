What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Lemma 7's maximum-cost approximation result; the mechanism characterizations and other structural results are left aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Lemma 7, proved in this paper. It says that for every fixed \(n\ge2\), dimension \(m\ge1\), and \(L_p\) norm with \(1\le p\le\infty\), the optimal worst-case approximation ratio for maximum-cost facility location among deterministic strategyproof mechanisms is exactly \(2\). The paper proves the lower bound for every such mechanism and the matching upper bound using dictatorship; it notes that the line case was already known. This is an approximation guarantee, not a hardness or running-time result. The other numbered results mainly characterize mechanisms or their outputs.

A continuous version I would put forward is Continuous Cohort Maximum-Cost Facility Location. Fix \(m,p\), and a support size \(\tau\ge2\). An instance is a finite-support society \((\mu_t,x_t)_{t=1}^{\tau}\), where \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^m\) is cohort \(t\)’s common ideal location, \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) is its population share, and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A deterministic mechanism \(F\) chooses a facility location. Define
\[ \operatorname{MC}_{\mu}(w)=\max_{t:\mu_t>0}\|x_t-w\|_p, \qquad \operatorname{OPT}_{\mu}=\inf_{w\in\mathbb{R}^m}\operatorname{MC}_{\mu}(w). \]
Ask for the smallest worst-case ratio \(\rho\) achievable by a mechanism that is truthful for each cohort: if cohort \(t\) reports \(y\), its entire mass moves from \(x_t\) to \(y\), and its members’ cost cannot decrease. A solution is a mechanism defined on all such societies, a proof of its ratio, and a matching lower bound.

The regime would be a large city choosing a site for a shared service, with many residents grouped into a modest number of neighborhoods whose members share an ideal site. The masses represent neighborhood population shares; the strategic units are the neighborhood cohorts. That is the key modeling choice in this mirror.

I expect the answer to remain \(2\), by essentially the same argument as Lemma 7. If the outcome is \(B\) for two cohorts at \(A\) and \(B\), the ratio is \(2\). Otherwise, let the \(A\)-cohort report the current outcome \(W\). Cohort truthfulness forces the outcome to remain \(W\) when that cohort’s true location is \(W\), leaving locations \(W\) and \(B\), whose maximum-cost ratio is \(2\). For the upper bound, a designated cohort’s location as the facility gives ratio at most \(2\) by the triangle inequality. This is a clean structural transfer, though it does not produce a new complexity classification. Further questions include whether the same bound holds for anonymous mechanisms, and whether similar results hold for objectives that depend on the amounts of mass rather than only on which types have positive mass.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper studies individual strategyproofness, while this mirror gives each cohort a joint deviation over all its mass. If each resident instead has an individual report, one resident changes only \(1/N\) of the population; in the nonatomic limit, that change has zero mass and truthfulness can become vacuous. Also, maximum cost depends on support, not on the values of the positive masses. So this is a plausible cohort-level extension of the paper’s facility-location problem, but I would not present it as an unqualified mirror of the authors’ individual-agent model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 7 is a distortion theorem, not a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror. It gives no complexity bound or algorithm for bribery, control, campaigning, or another computational task: dictatorship supplies the upper bound, and the lower bound applies to every strategyproof mechanism. The proposed continuous question—minimize the worst-case ratio over truthful mechanisms—asks for another mechanism-design characterization. One could instead ask how to compute an optimal center of a finite set of locations, but that drops the strategyproofness question that makes Lemma 7 interesting. Asking how to compute a mechanism’s output requires first specifying a mechanism; the lemma gives no nontrivial algorithmic problem there.

The cohort version also changes the strategic unit. In the paper, one agent deviates. In a high-multiplicity society, a resident’s unilateral report moves only that resident’s share, not the entire mass of a type. If residents are nonatomic, an individual deviation leaves the distribution unchanged and truthfulness becomes vacuous. If an entire neighborhood moves its mass together, truthfulness becomes a coalition or bloc condition. That can be a sensible model for coordinated neighborhood representatives, but it is not the paper’s individual-strategyproofness question or its ordinary high-multiplicity version. The maximum-cost objective compounds the mismatch: it depends on which locations have positive mass, not how much mass they carry, so the proposed distribution adds no mass-sensitive computational task.

That defeats Lemma 7 as a ChoCo anchor, but it does not establish that no continuous facility-location question is worthwhile. A bloc model could be worth studying on its own. The honest negative case is strongest on scope: that would be a new coalition-mechanism problem, and this paper’s named result supplies no computational question for the programme to continuize.
