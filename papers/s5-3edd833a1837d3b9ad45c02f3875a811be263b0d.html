The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^d\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), determine the worst-case egalitarian approximation ratio of the weighted SD-lift rule and whether it is strategyproof under unilateral deviations \(\mu_{\epsilon,z}=(1-\epsilon)\nu+\epsilon\delta_z\), for every \(\epsilon>0\), other-agent distribution \(\nu\), true location \(p\), and report \(z\).

The model it lives in

Types are exact locations \(t\in T\) with population mass \(\mu_t\). The rule computes \(\bar x=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t t\) and \(\sigma^2=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\|t-\bar x\|^2\), then places the facility at \((\bar x,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\). Each individual deviation shifts only that individual’s mass; the objective is the maximum distance from the facility to the support, compared with the smallest-enclosing-ball radius in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). The moment identity gives strategyproofness and ratio at most \(2\), with supremum \(2\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 15’s SD-lift truthfulness and approximation guarantee. It leaves the paper’s other mechanisms and lower bounds unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is \(2\) the optimal worst-case approximation ratio among deterministic mass-strategyproof facility rules?
  • Which restrictions on support size or mass distribution permit a strictly smaller ratio?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Theorem 15, proved in this paper. It says that the SD-lift mechanism—placing the facility at the reported locations’ centroid, lifted by their standard deviation—is truthful and has approximation factor \(2\sqrt{(n-1)/n}\). The bound is tight for that mechanism: with one agent at distance \(2\) from the other \(n-1\), the isolated agent’s cost gives exactly that ratio. This is a constructive mechanism result, not a theorem that a problem is in P or NP-hard; the paper gives no named complexity classification.

The regime I would use is a large population concentrated at a moderate number \(\tau\) of service hubs. A type is an exact location in \(\mathbb R^d\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents there, and residents of the same type have identical distance costs and reporting options. Here \(n\gg\tau\). A facility may use the extra placement dimension studied in the paper—for example, an elevated service point for residents whose locations lie in a plane. The mass records the population; an individual deviation still changes the distribution by that individual’s mass, rather than moving the whole type.

My lead mirror is Measure SD-Lift. Its input is \(d\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\mathbb Q^d\), and positive rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to \(1\). The question is whether one can give a deterministic facility rule on such societies that is strategyproof for individual mass changes and has a bounded worst-case egalitarian approximation ratio. A solution is a rule \(M\), together with proofs of its incentive and approximation guarantees. For a society \(\mu\), the objective is the maximum distance from the facility to a location in \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\); the benchmark is the radius of the smallest enclosing ball in \(\mathbb R^d\).

The SD-lift rule is the direct measure version:
\[ \bar x=\int x\,d\mu(x),\qquad \sigma^2=\int\|x-\bar x\|^2\,d\mu(x),\qquad M(\mu)=(\bar x,\sigma)\in\mathbb R^{d+1}. \]
To state truthfulness without making an atomless individual’s deviation vacuous, let \(\nu\) describe the other agents and let an individual have mass \(\epsilon>0\). Reporting \(z\) produces \(\mu_{\epsilon,z}=(1-\epsilon)\nu+\epsilon\delta_z\). Require that an agent whose true location is \(p\) weakly prefers reporting \(p\) to any \(z\), for every such \(\nu,p,z,\epsilon\). This includes the ordinary one-person deviation in an \(n\)-agent profile when \(\epsilon=1/n\).

The key identity is
\[ \|(p,0)-M(\mu)\|^2=\int\|p-x\|^2\,d\mu(x). \]
Under \(\mu_{\epsilon,z}\), the right side is a constant independent of \(z\), plus \(\epsilon\|p-z\|^2\). It is minimized at \(z=p\), so the rule is mass-strategyproof. Also, every pair of support points is at distance at most \(2\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\). The identity therefore bounds every agent’s cost by \(2\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\). This factor is tight as a supremum for the rule: with masses \(1-\epsilon\) and \(\epsilon\) at two points, its ratio is \(2\sqrt{1-\epsilon}\), which tends to \(2\) as \(\epsilon\) tends to zero.

I expect this mirror result to be constructively tractable: the rule uses the first two moments and can be computed from the \(\tau\) support points. It is a direct population extension of Theorem 15, not an outcome-space or probability continuization. It covers that theorem alone; I would not stretch it to cover the paper’s other bounds. The natural next questions are whether \(2\) is optimal among all mass-strategyproof rules, and which features of the mass distribution permit a better ratio.

The weak point is that the paper’s finite-\(n\) guarantee is sharper than the distribution-only limit: \(2\sqrt{(n-1)/n}\) approaches \(2\). Also, “mass-strategyproofness” needs its individual-mass interpretation made explicit; a naïve unilateral deviation in an atomless population changes nothing. The weighted deviation definition above preserves the finite-profile incentive constraint and avoids that vacuity. The extension is therefore a credible analogue of their mechanism-design question, though it does not turn this paper into a complexity-theory result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 15 is the proponent’s only anchor. Its mass version is coherent: for a large population at a moderate number of service hubs, take exact locations as types and let an individual’s report move only their own mass. The moment identity still gives truthfulness, and the support points still define the egalitarian objective. I would not reject this mirror on multiplicity or identity grounds.

That concession exposes the strongest case against it for ChoCo: Theorem 15 is a mechanism-design guarantee, not a computational result of the kind this programme is set up to continuize. It proves truthfulness and an approximation ratio for a rule; it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic question about a society as input. The proposed question—whether a rule with those properties exists—repeats that design question for weighted populations.

A stronger proposed mirror would ask for the optimal approximation ratio over all deterministic mass-strategyproof rules. That remains a global mechanism-synthesis problem, quantified over profiles and reports, with no representation of mechanisms or computational input-output task specified. Imposing a restricted family of rules could make an algorithmic problem, but would introduce a new modelling choice rather than mirror Theorem 15. Computing this fixed SD-lift rule from finite-support moments is a separate, straightforward evaluation task.

So the negative case is strongest at the programme boundary: no plausible population scenario changes the kind of result this paper proves. The honest limitation is that the weighted theorem could be a clean standalone mechanism-design result. I cannot claim it has no value in that broader area; I can argue that it does not offer ChoCo the continuous computational-complexity question it is seeking.
