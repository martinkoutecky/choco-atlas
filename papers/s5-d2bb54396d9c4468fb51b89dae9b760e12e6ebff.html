The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of buyer types, per-capita supplies \(q_j>0\), and each type’s budget \(M_t\), global cap \(c_t\), and values \(u_{tj}\), compute a Fisher equilibrium \((p,(x_t)_{t\in T})\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\min(c_t,\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj})\). Require \(p_j\ge 0\), type-level demand \(x_t\in\arg\max_{z\ge 0}\{\min(c_t,\sum_j u_{tj}z_j):\sum_jp_jz_j\le M_t\}\), aggregate feasibility \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), and Walras’ law \(p_j(q_j-\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj})=0\) for every good \(j\).

The model it lives in

A society has type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with each type fully specified by its budget \(M_t\), cap \(c_t\), and values \(u_{tj}\). Goods remain divisible, with supply \(q_j\) per unit of population; each type receives a representative bundle \(x_t\), and aggregate use is \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\). Prices and bundles must form a Fisher equilibrium, and the objective is maximum welfare per unit of population.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorems 17 and 19. They leave aside Theorem 15’s algorithm for a modest MBB equilibrium, Theorem 16’s minimum-revenue procedure, and the structural results on modest MBB equilibria.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does welfare-maximizing equilibrium remain NP-hard when the support size \(\tau\) is bounded or restricted relative to the number of goods?
  • Does the polynomial-time result of Theorem 15 extend to rational type masses and per-capita supplies with a running time polynomial in the support size and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible direct mirror here. My lead is Theorem 17, which is proved in the paper by reduction from 3-Dimensional Matching. It states that computing a market equilibrium that maximizes social welfare is NP-hard. The paper’s second useful anchor is Theorem 19, also proved here: computing any equilibrium is PPAD-hard for Fisher markets with good-based budget-additive utilities. Its proof adapts Chen and Teng’s construction.

For both, a buyer type records the complete economic profile used by the problem: budget and utility parameters. In the Theorem 17 mirror that means \(M_t\), \(c_t\), and \(u_{tj}\); in Theorem 19 it means \(M_t\), \(c_{tj}\), and \(u_{tj}\). A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Goods remain divisible as in the paper. Let \(q_j\) be the supply of good \(j\) per unit of population, \(p_j\ge 0\) its price, and \(x_{tj}\) the amount received per buyer of type \(t\). Aggregate use is \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\).

The lead question is Continuous Welfare-Maximizing Fisher Equilibrium. Its input is the type distribution, rational per-type budgets and global caps, rational utility values, and rational per-capita supplies. A solution is prices \(p\) and bundles \(x_t\) such that every type receives a demand bundle,
\[ x_t\in\arg\max_{z\ge 0}\left\{\min\!\left(c_t,\sum_j u_{tj}z_j\right):\sum_jp_jz_j\le M_t\right\}, \]
goods are not overallocated, \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), and Walras’ law holds, \(p_j(q_j-\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj})=0\). Among all such equilibria, the output must maximize
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\min\!\left(c_t,\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\right). \]
This is the population version of the paper’s social-welfare objective over equilibria, with welfare measured per unit of population.

Theorem 17 transfers directly to this question. Given a finite market with \(N\) buyers and unit supply of each good, group buyers with identical economic profiles into types, set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and set \(q_j=1/N\). The continuous constraints are the finite market’s constraints divided by \(N\); mean welfare is the finite market’s welfare divided by \(N\). Averaging the bundles of identical buyers preserves demand, and assigning a type’s bundle to each buyer recovers a finite equilibrium. The theorem’s NP-hardness therefore carries over. This supply normalization is a natural co-modification: in a population of size \(N\), per-capita supply \(q_j\) corresponds to total supply \(Nq_j\).

The scenario I find plausible is a large advertising market with many campaigns drawn from a catalogue of repeated templates. A type specifies the campaign’s budget, value for each keyword or ad slot, and saturation cap. The population mass is the share of campaigns using each template; inventory is expressed per campaign. Millions of campaigns can then share a much smaller set of profiles. The model keeps the paper’s market and equilibrium questions while making the buyer population the continuous object.

The second mirror is Continuous Good-Capped Fisher Equilibrium Search. It has the same population, price, budget, demand, clearing, and Walras conditions, with utility
\[ \tilde u_t(x_t)=\sum_j\min(c_{tj},u_{tj}x_{tj}). \]
Its solution is any equilibrium \((p,x)\). This mirrors Theorem 19’s good-based budget-additive case, whose utility has a separate cap for each buyer and good. The same normalization maps its finite markets into the population formulation, so the PPAD-hardness transfers as well.

These are Class B outcomes: the reductions’ combinatorics remain in the goods and the growing set of economic profiles. The continuum does not make these two questions tractable. It does give the programme a clear boundary case: population multiplicity alone is not what makes these anchors hard. For the lead problem, a next question is whether maximizing welfare becomes tractable or fixed-parameter tractable when \(\tau\) is bounded or otherwise restricted. For the second, it is whether equilibrium search remains PPAD-hard under a small type menu or stronger restrictions on the caps.

The weakest point is that the hardness reductions need a growing number of types. Cloning each profile \(K\) times, and scaling total goods supply by \(K\), makes the population arbitrarily large relative to support without changing the normalized continuous instance. That gives a genuine high-multiplicity regime, but it does not establish hardness for a fixed catalogue of types. The mirror is strongest as a faithful population formulation and a complexity-boundary question; its hard instances do not show that a small-type continuum is itself hard. I cover Theorems 17 and 19, not the paper’s Theorem 15 algorithm for computing a modest MBB equilibrium.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is about the regime their hardness reductions actually cover. In Theorem 17, the 3-Dimensional Matching construction encodes the combinatorial instance in buyers’ economic profiles: there are buyers for the elements and for the triples. In Theorem 19, buyers encode the price-regulating markets and the game gadget. In both proposed mirrors, the support size \(\tau\) therefore grows with the hard instance. The population becomes a distribution, but the reduction’s distinct roles remain distinct types.

That limits what the reductions say about the regime most often motivating a continuous population: many agents drawn from a small menu of profiles. Cloning every buyer \(K\) times makes the population arbitrarily large while preserving the same type distribution and normalized supply. It shows that the mirrors admit large populations; it does not show that the hard instances have small support or that a continuum helps compress their structure. A stronger formulation would ask what happens when \(\tau\) is small, or when it is restricted relative to the number of goods. Neither theorem settles that question. This is a limitation of the anchors, though, not a reason the stronger question is unworthy.

Theorem 17 is otherwise a particularly clean mirror. Grouping identical buyers, dividing supplies by the original population size, and measuring welfare per capita preserves the equilibrium and the welfare-maximizing choice. Welfare per capita differs from total welfare by a constant factor. The objection that this is merely a finite market in disguise has little force here: weighted markets are exactly the high-multiplicity model the programme wants to study. Theorem 19 has the same basic answer. Its good-based caps are a recognized utility variant, and its equilibrium-search question remains computational after population mass is introduced. Neither mirror tracks buyer identity, and the paper itself does not already study a continuous population.

The result the proponent leaves out makes a universal negative harder still. Theorem 15 gives a polynomial-time algorithm for a modest MBB equilibrium. In a population model, identical agents can be aggregated into a representative type with budget and cap scaled by its mass; supplies and utilities can be normalized accordingly. That points to a tractable continuous mirror of a named computational result, though adapting and analyzing the algorithm for rational masses and supplies would still be work. The paper’s divisible goods do not count as population continuization, but they make the allocation side of this adaptation natural.

So the negative case I can defend is narrower: the two hardness anchors do not demonstrate an advantage for small-support societies, and their reductions preserve the hard structure in the type menu. That is useful caution about what their results establish. It does not defeat the mirrors, and it does not rule out a worthwhile small-support question. Under the programme’s stated standard, I cannot make a convincing case that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror.
