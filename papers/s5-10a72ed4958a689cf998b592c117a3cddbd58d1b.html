The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives and a finitely supported distribution \(\mu\) with positive binary-rational masses over pair-approval types \(\binom{[m]}{2}\), decide whether some candidate \(c\in[m]\) is fixed by every permutation \(\sigma\) satisfying \(\sigma\mu=\mu\). Return such a candidate when one exists.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over two-member approval types; its symmetries are alternative permutations preserving every type mass. The decision is whether a candidate is fixed by the society's entire symmetry group. This is a feasibility question with no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8's pair-approval, single-winner subcase; it leaves the theorem's other preference and decision settings, and the canonical-labeling algorithm results, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the exact complexity of binary-encoded rational-mass ANR-possibility, including a polynomial-size graph-isomorphism encoding and hardness on uniform-mass instances.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s ANR-possibility problem for pair-approval societies. It replaces integer voter counts with population shares while keeping the candidate set and the decision discrete.

For \(m\) alternatives, let each voter type be a two-member committee, so \(T_m=\binom{[m]}{2}\). An instance is a finite support list of types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Interpret \(\mu_t\) as the fraction of a large public-consultation population that approves pair \(t\). The number of people can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct types. A rule selects one alternative; anonymity is built into representing the society by its distribution, and neutrality requires \(r(\sigma\mu)=\sigma r(\mu)\) for every alternative permutation \(\sigma\).

Call a permutation a symmetry of the society if it preserves its masses: \(\sigma\mu=\mu\). The named problem is Continuous ANR Possibility for Pair-Approval Societies:

Given \(m\) and \(\mu\), is there an alternative \(c\in[m]\) fixed by every permutation preserving \(\mu\)? A YES solution is such a candidate; a NO instance has no such candidate. There is no optimization objective: this is a feasibility question about whether neutrality and a resolute choice can coexist at this society.

This is the local ANR question in the continuous model. A neutral resolute rule at \(\mu\) must choose a candidate fixed by every symmetry of \(\mu\); conversely, such a candidate permits an equivariant choice throughout \(\mu\)’s orbit. Thus the question is about the underlying voting problem, not a graph encoding.

My anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It states that for any functions \(\iota,\kappa\) with \(\iota(m)\ge 2\) and \(\kappa(m)\le m-1\) for all \(m\ge3\), deciding ANR-possibility for \((\committee{\iota},\committee{\kappa})\) is GI-complete. I rely on its \(\iota(m)=2,\ \kappa(m)=1\) case: with two-member approval types and a one-alternative decision, ANR-possibility is GI-complete. The paper proves this using its GI upper bound and a reduction from graph isomorphism.

The connection to the mirror is exact on finite profiles. If a profile has \(h_t\) voters of type \(t\) among \(n\) voters, set \(\mu_t=h_t/n\). Then \(\sigma\mu=\mu\) exactly when \(\sigma\) preserves the profile’s histogram, so the fixed-candidate condition is unchanged. In the paper’s graph construction, each graph edge becomes a two-member approval vote; the continuous counterpart assigns equal mass to each supported edge. The graph’s automorphisms are precisely the symmetries relevant to the candidate choices. The reduction therefore carries over, giving GI-hardness. For the upper direction, the support and rational masses form a binary-labeled graph; its mass-preserving automorphisms, and hence the candidate orbits, can be computed through a polynomial-size graph-isomorphism encoding. I expect this mirror to be GI-complete as well.

The regime is a large consultation over a fixed menu of alternatives, where people choose two proposals and many share the same approved pair. There may be many participants per type; the input records their shares rather than listing every person. The pair-approval restriction is a deliberate, studied subcase of the paper’s committee-preference settings. The authors should recognize the mirror: it preserves their preference types, their discrete decision, and the symmetry obstruction that defines ANR-possibility.

This case covers Theorem 8’s ANR-possibility result in the pair-approval, single-winner subcase. It does not claim a mirror for the paper’s canonical-labeling algorithms or its full range of preference and decision settings. I expect hardness to transfer: the decisive combinatorics are candidate symmetries, not the number of individually listed voters. That makes this a clear Class B mirror rather than an example where continuization dissolves hardness.

The weakest point is that local ANR for a measure-valued society is an extension the paper does not state explicitly; anonymity becomes natural once the society is represented directly by its distribution. Also, pair-approval consultations are a narrower scenario than large ranked elections, and this mirror offers no tractability gain. Those are real limits, but the paper already reduces its finite ANR question to histograms and candidate symmetries. Normalizing those histograms into population shares is a direct high-multiplicity version of the same problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 8 may offer little *new* computational substance after normalization. For pair approvals, the symmetry relevant to ANR-possibility is determined by whether a permutation preserves every type’s mass. If \(\mu\) has rational coordinates, choose a common denominator \(n\) and set \(h_t=n\mu_t\). Then \(\sigma\mu=\mu\) exactly when \(\sigma\) preserves the integer histogram \(h\). Scaling every mass by the same factor changes neither the stabilizer nor whether it fixes a candidate. The paper already formulates its question through histograms and their automorphisms, so its continuous counterpart may amount to quotienting out population size rather than introducing a new computational object.

That makes this a thin mirror. There is no intervention on mass, optimization objective, or threshold whose behavior changes when counts become shares; the relevant computation is exact symmetry. The proposed rational-mass model is also the right version to analyze: allowing arbitrary real masses would leave the programme’s finite-input setting, while adding prices or other voter attributes would change the problem being mirrored. The consultation scenario is plausible, but plausibility does not make the normalized version algorithmically distinct from the histogram question.

This objection is about marginal value, though, and it does not defeat the anchor under ChoCo’s stated standard. Theorem 8 names a computational problem, and the consultation has a credible high-multiplicity regime. An exact correspondence with the histogram problem can itself be a meaningful result; the programme explicitly treats discrete-to-continuous identities as results, and does not count high-multiplicity work as a novelty collision. Nor does the fact that the mirror may inherit the same complexity count against it.

I therefore cannot honestly make the universal negative case here. The best objection is that the mirror may be nearly an exact reformulation, with limited algorithmic novelty. But Continuous ANR Possibility for Pair-Approval Societies survives as a well-posed population-continuous mirror of Theorem 8, so this anchor is not defeated.
