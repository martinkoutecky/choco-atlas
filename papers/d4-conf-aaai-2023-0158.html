The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a named computational result in this paper that can serve as an anchor under the requested standard. Its only numbered result, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that after \(N\to\infty\) random queries to Boltzmann-rational teachers with positive, finite \(\beta\) values, the posterior over \(\mathbf w\) converges to the true value. That is a statistical convergence claim, not a result about a problem being tractable, hard, or in a complexity class. The algorithm and experiments do not supply another numbered computational result. So there are no eligible anchors, and I can’t honestly build the required anchored case.

The best plausible population scenario is a large panel of evaluators teaching one shared reward model. A complete type is the evaluator’s known rationality parameter \(\beta_j\); \(\mu_j\) is the fraction of the panel with that parameter. There may be millions of evaluators but only \(\tau\) distinct types. For a query with feature difference \(\varphi\), the decision is to choose a type \(j\) minimizing
\[ \mathbb E_{\mathbf w\sim P,\ I\sim P(I\mid\mathbf w;\varphi,\beta_j)} \left[\mathcal M\bigl(P(\cdot\mid I,\varphi,\beta_j),\mathbf w\bigr)\right], \]
where \(\mathcal M\) is the paper’s MSE or log-loss measure. The solution is the selected type and the posterior update after its response. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s teacher-selection problem.

Its weakness is substantial: the paper’s selection rule depends on the available \(\beta\) values, not on how many teachers have each value. The masses \(\mu_j\) establish that each type is available but otherwise do no work. Adding mass-dependent query limits or batch feedback could make population size matter, but would change the problem. I therefore regard this as a plausible reformulation of the setting, not an anchored computational mirror of a result proved in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous mirror. Theorem 1 is an asymptotic statistical claim about posterior convergence after infinitely many queries. The teacher-selection algorithm and experiments report no complexity result. The paper’s other continuous objects—the reward-weight vector, its posterior distribution, and probabilistic responses—are uncertainty and noise in the learning model, not a continuous population.

The best population scenario is a large panel of evaluators teaching one shared reward model. Teachers with the same known rationality parameter \(\beta\) are interchangeable, so a type can be specified by \(\beta\), with \(\mu_\beta\) the fraction of the panel of that type. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But the paper’s rule selects the \(\beta\) with the lowest expected post-query error. The mass \(\mu_\beta\) does not affect that calculation: with one query at a time, only which \(\beta\) values are available matters. Replacing the finite list by a distribution over \(\beta\) therefore leaves the rule dependent on the support, not on the society’s proportions.

A stronger version could impose limits on how often each type may be queried, or query a batch of teachers and update from aggregate responses. Then the masses would matter. But the paper’s unit of feedback is one selected teacher’s response, and its posterior update conditions on that teacher’s \(\beta\). A budgeted schedule or batch-feedback model changes that setup; it would be a new active-learning problem, not a continuous mirror of a result the paper establishes. It might be worth studying independently, but that possibility also makes the universal claim that no scenario could be worthwhile hard to defend.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: this paper supplies no computational theorem to mirror, and its most natural high-multiplicity reformulation leaves population mass computationally inert. That rules out the proposed anchor under the requested standard. It does not establish that every population-based extension is uninteresting.
