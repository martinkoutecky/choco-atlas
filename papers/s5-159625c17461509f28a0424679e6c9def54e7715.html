What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered. The theorems on communication, experience, and transparency and the experimental appeal rates are left without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is large public university admissions: many students, with relatively few distinct combinations of preference rankings and priority scores. A type would include both the complete ranking and the scores or priorities used by the mechanism; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s share of applicants. Capacities could scale with the population, and a continuum DA model could allocate mass across schools, including splitting a type at a cutoff. That is a credible population regime, though splitting an otherwise identical type makes the paper’s individual-level question—whether a student can verify *her* match—harder to carry over directly.

The decisive limitation is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. The nearest is Theorem 3, proved here: the promised environment is transparent if the communication protocol gives full disclosure, or if the mechanism is non-wasteful and structured-offers and the environment is verifiable through both experience and communication. Theorem 1 characterizes communication verifiability through informational equivalence to cutoffs; Theorem 2 characterizes experience verifiability through predictability. These are formal results about information and mechanisms, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The experimental percentages are empirical findings, not named complexity results.

So the positive case is only that a sensible continuum admissions regime is imaginable. There are zero qualifying anchors, and therefore no honest per-anchor continuous problem or expected complexity class to state. Supplying one would be proposing a new research question, not making a mirror of this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is the missing computational problem. Theorem 1 says that cutoffs characterize communication sufficient for each student to verify a match; Theorem 2 characterizes when each student’s experience suffices; Theorem 3 combines those conditions to establish transparency. These are results about information and possible matches, not algorithmic claims about runtime, hardness, or approximation. The experiment’s appeal rates are empirical findings. The paper gives no named computational result to carry over.

A large admissions market is a credible high-multiplicity regime, so I would not reject it on population grounds. The best candidate mirror would represent applicants by preference-and-score types, scale school capacities with population, and ask how to compute mass assignments and cutoffs—or how to test verifiability and transparency from aggregate data. But computing an aggregate DA outcome is not the object of these theorems. Their claims concern what *each student* can infer from their own scores, match, and sequence of offers, and whether an individual could detect a plausible deviation by the designer.

Aggregate masses do not in general retain that pointwise information. In an atomless model, an exceptional student’s assignment or history has zero mass; omitting that student loses the very evidence the definitions ask them to assess. Restoring the pointwise claim requires tracking individual histories or distinguishing features. And when a capacity cutoff divides otherwise identical applicants, the mass model must either split a type across schools or add a tie-breaking coordinate. One can study those enriched models, but changing the claim to “almost every student” or “no positive-mass group detects a deviation” changes the paper’s result.

The strongest rescue is to study the minimum feedback needed for transparency, which the paper itself leaves open. That is a plausible new project, but the paper supplies neither a computational formulation nor an objective for the cost of feedback, privacy, or message complexity. Making it computational and continuous would require adding both. So the proponent identifies a sensible admissions setting, but no continuous mirror of a named computational result: the proposed work would be a new information-design question adjacent to this paper, rather than the computational continuation ChoCo is meant to chart.
