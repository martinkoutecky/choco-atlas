The continuous mirror question

Input \(m\) item classes with rational per-person supplies \(q_j\), common prices \(p_j\), a finite set \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and additive valuations \(v_{tj}\). For each type, let \(\theta_t\) be the largest \(z\ge 0\) satisfying \(z=\sum_j q_j\max\{p_j,\min\{v_{tj},z\}\}\). Choose sold quantities \(s_j\ge 0\) and a finite-support mass assignment \(x_t(a,P)\ge 0\) to packages with integral goods vectors \(a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\) and cash \(P\ge 0\), such that \(\sum_{a,P}x_t(a,P)=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,a,P}x_t(a,P)a_j+s_j=q_j\), \(\sum_{t,a,P}x_t(a,P)P=\sum_jp_js_j\), and every positive-mass package for type \(t\) gives \(v_t\cdot a+P\ge\theta_t/2\). Is there an algorithm to find such an allocation in time polynomial in \(m\), \(|T|\), and the binary input length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with finitely many complete valuation types, each receiving mass across package outcomes. Each household’s goods bundle remains integral; goods and sale proceeds balance per person. The objective is feasibility: every recipient of type \(t\) gets utility at least \(\theta_t/2\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s polynomial-time share guarantee in the limit; it leaves the joint ε-SEFX FPTAS, the MMS results, and the chores and equal-proceeds extensions unexamined.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the feasible package assignments be represented and solved in polynomial time, for example through a configuration LP with an efficient pricing procedure?
  • Can continuous solutions be rounded to finite cohorts while preserving the TPS guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n\) agents with additive valuations and optional selling, an allocation giving each agent at least \(\frac{n}{2n-1}\)-TPS can be computed in polynomial time; it also gives an FPTAS for an allocation that is both \(\frac{n}{2n-1}\)-TPS and \(\epsilon\)-SEFX. [Proposition 3 in Feige and Gafni’s paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24600).

The paper has no named hardness result for this problem to anchor. Its cited weak NP-hardness for computing MMS concerns earlier work on the no-selling setting, while Proposition 1’s \(11/12\) bound is an allocation guarantee impossibility, not a computational complexity result. Proposition 3 is the better anchor here: TPS has a meaningful per-capita limit, whereas MMS is more sensitive to the indivisibility of individual bundles.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Consider a large cohort of residents receiving goods from a liquidation or resettlement programme: for example, many households with a small number of shared preference profiles, choosing among standardized classes of appliances, vehicles, or furnishings. Let \(T\) be the finite set of preference types, with population masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) specifies the household’s complete additive valuation vector \(v_t\); all households face the same market prices \(p_j\). There are \(m\) goods classes, with \(q_j\) indivisible copies of class \(j\) per person in the population. The regime is \(N\) households and \(Nq_j\) copies of each class, with \(N\) large and \(\tau=|T|\) relatively small. Rational \(\mu_t\) and \(q_j\) ensure finite populations with integer counts along suitable values of \(N\).

Call the problem Continuous TPS–SEFX Allocation with Indivisible Packages. Its input is \(m,T,\mu,q,p,(v_t)_{t\in T}\), all rational. For each type, define its per-capita TPS value \(\theta_t\) as the largest \(z\geq 0\) satisfying
\[ z=\sum_{j=1}^{m}q_j\max\{p_j,\min\{v_{tj},z\}\}. \]
This is the limit of the paper’s TPS equation when both population and the supply of each goods class scale with \(N\); likewise, \(\frac{N}{2N-1}\) tends to \(\frac12\).

A solution assigns mass \(x_t(a,P)\) of type \(t\) to each package \((a,P)\), where \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^m\) is an integral bundle of goods and \(P\geq0\) is money per recipient. It also chooses sold quantities \(s_j\geq0\), measured per person. Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_{a,P}x_t(a,P)=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,a,P}x_t(a,P)a_j+s_j=q_j,\qquad \sum_{t,a,P}x_t(a,P)P=\sum_jp_js_j. \]
Thus every unit of goods is either allocated whole or sold, and all proceeds are distributed. Each positive-mass package received by type \(t\) must give utility \(v_t\cdot a+P\geq \frac12\theta_t\). For the envy condition, put \(\bar v_{tj}=\max\{v_{tj},p_j\}\). Every type \(t\) package in the support must satisfy SEFX against every other positive-mass package: if the other package has positive money, compare the full \(\bar v_t\)-value plus money; if it has none, compare against that package after removing any one good copy and substituting its market price. The algorithmic question is whether such a finite-support mass allocation can be found in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\) for the \(\epsilon\)-SEFX version, and whether the share-only version has a polynomial-time algorithm.

The expected direction is Class A, provisionally. Money and sale proceeds are already divisible in the paper’s model; the continuization is of the population. The cohort’s mass can be divided among different packages, while every recipient still gets an integral bundle. That preserves the core decisions in the paper—what to sell, who gets each unsold good, and how to distribute proceeds. The paper’s Theorem 1.2, which guarantees an allocation that is both \(\frac{n}{2n-1}\)-TPS and SEFX for every finite instance, is additional support for the existence side of the limit question. [Theorem 1.2 and Proposition 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24600).

The main algorithmic unknown is whether the package choices can be represented by a configuration LP or similar optimization problem with efficient pricing, especially once SEFX couples packages in the output’s support. Further questions include whether the exact \(1/2\)-TPS and SEFX guarantee survives the limit, how to round a continuous solution back to finite cohorts, and whether the integral-package and envy constraints produce a continuum-specific hardness result.

My weakest point is the need to scale goods supply with population. With a fixed finite catalogue, per-agent TPS tends to zero as \(N\) grows, so the limit would say little. Repeated standardized goods are a natural co-modification for a large cohort, but they are an added regime assumption. I think the authors would still recognize the mirror: it preserves their optional-selling allocation problem and keeps each individual’s bundle indivisible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it needs a substantial change to the goods side of the model. If the paper’s fixed catalogue of goods stays fixed while the population grows, per-person TPS tends to zero. The proposed mirror avoids that degeneration by supplying \(Nq_j\) copies of each good class to \(N\) residents. That gives a sensible per-capita economy, but shifts the question toward allocating replicated commodities among a large cohort.

That is a real qualification, but it does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s scope: per-capita supplies that scale with population are an expressly allowed co-modification. Nor does the requirement that each resident receive an integral bundle defeat it. A continuum of residents can be divided among package types while every recipient keeps an indivisible bundle. In a large resettlement or liquidation programme with repeated goods and shared valuation profiles, mass is meaningful and identity need not matter.

A stronger version would drop SEFX and ask only for a mass allocation giving each type its limiting TPS guarantee. That removes the support-wide envy constraints, which are the most awkward part of the proposed formulation, while retaining the paper’s computational anchor. The resulting problem still has a clear algorithmic shape: allocate per-capita supplies across integral bundle configurations, with each type meeting a utility threshold. It may be hard to find or optimize over those configurations, but that is a pricing or configuration question—not a reason the mirror is ill-posed. Keeping SEFX makes the question more demanding, but a weak share-only version already suffices to sustain the anchor.

So the negative case is mainly about priority: the continuum may expose an integer-configuration bottleneck without making the allocation problem easier. That does not establish that the question is unworthy; whether continuous optimization can handle that bottleneck is precisely something to investigate. The paper has a genuine computational result to mirror, and I cannot honestly rule out a plausible high-multiplicity scenario for it. The case against a continuous mirror is therefore weak.
