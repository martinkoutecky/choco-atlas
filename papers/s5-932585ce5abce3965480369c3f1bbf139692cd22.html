The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for the paper’s GRUM parameter estimation problem in a large consumer panel. Suppose millions of customers fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of observed attribute profiles \(x_t\), and alternatives have common attributes \(z_j\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the population fraction with profile \(x_t\). Customers with the same profile have the same conditional ranking model, even though random utility may give them different realized rankings.

The nearest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for a location family, if the joint error density for each alternative and the prior over \(\Theta\) are log-concave, then the posterior \(Pr(\Theta\mid D)\) is concave up to a known transformation. This is a useful global-optimization result, but it does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm or a complexity classification. Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes when the likelihood’s global-maximizer set is bounded; it likewise does not classify computational complexity. So neither is a qualifying named complexity anchor under your stated rule. The paper has no numbered theorem asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar.

A concrete candidate mirror of Theorem 1 would be Continuous GRUM MAP Estimation. An instance gives alternatives and their features \(z_j\); a finite set \(T\) of customer attribute profiles \(x_t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\); and rational masses \(\rho_{t,\pi}\) of customers of each profile observed with ranking \(\pi\), where \(\sum_\pi \rho_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). It also gives a population scale \(N\), a location-family GRUM, and a log-concave prior. The question is to compute a parameter vector \(\Theta^*=(\vec\delta,B)\) maximizing
\[ \log Pr(\Theta)+N\sum_{t,\pi}\rho_{t,\pi}\log Pr_\Theta(\pi\mid x_t,z). \]
A solution is such a maximizing parameter vector. This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s inference objective: it replaces repeated customer records with their type and ranking masses.

I would expect concavity to make this a tractability candidate, but not claim that Theorem 1 settles it. The paper does not establish that the posterior can be evaluated or optimized exactly in polynomial time; its inference method uses Monte Carlo EM. The further question is whether an exact or approximation algorithm can exploit the concavity when the input is given by rational masses and support size \(\tau\). This proposed mirror covers parameter inference, not the adaptive choice of which customer to query next or the reported experimental gains. Its weakest point is that the attractive continuous question is supported by a structural theorem, not by a named complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continue. Theorem 1 establishes concavity of the posterior under distributional assumptions; Theorem 2 characterizes when its maximizers are bounded. Neither gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic guarantee. The MC-EM method and elicitation experiments are computational contributions, but the paper gives no worst-case bounds for them. So the proposed continuous MAP problem does not mirror a computational result in the sense ChoCo is charting.

The proposed mass-weighted likelihood is a sensible high-multiplicity model: customers can share an attribute profile, and observations can be grouped by profile and ranking. That is evidence for the regime, not an objection to it. But if the masses encode integer counts, the objective is the paper’s finite-sample likelihood written in compressed form. If arbitrary fractional masses are allowed, it becomes a population log-likelihood or weighted objective; that may be useful, but it is a new estimation problem, not a consequence of Theorem 1. In either version, concavity alone does not establish tractability. The paper’s ranking probabilities involve integration over utility regions, and its inference method uses Monte Carlo; it supplies neither an exact evaluation model nor a complexity analysis in \(m,\tau,L\).

A stronger candidate would give a population distribution over customer attributes and ranking outcomes, then ask for the parameter maximizing expected log-likelihood. But that is a statistical estimation question newly posed over a supplied distribution. The paper’s personalized-choice criterion already integrates over an attribute distribution \(\Delta\), while its elicitation algorithm targets individual agents from a finite pool. Turning that into fractional query allocations would change the elicitation problem: a query still obtains one person’s response, and the paper does not study mass-valued query budgets or their complexity.

I can’t honestly make the universal negative airtight. Repeated customer profiles are plausible, and this objective does not depend on named individuals, so there is no clear multiplicity or identity obstruction. The defensible against case is that the proposed mirror is not anchored by a computational theorem in this paper; at best, it motivates a separate computational-statistics project. If a new complexity landscape built from the paper’s inference objective counts as a worthwhile mirror, the proposed case may survive.
