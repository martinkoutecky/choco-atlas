The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\), finitely many types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict separable rankings over the \(2^K\) outcomes, and initial integer ballot bundles \(\omega_t\), determine whether there is a finite sequence of mass trades from the initial state to a state with no feasible blocking trade. In each state, \(\lambda_{t,v}\) is the mass of type \(t\) holding bundle \(v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^K\); a trade reassigns mass among bundles, conserves the total supply of votes on each proposal, and requires every participating agent to strictly prefer the resulting outcome. A proposal passes when its supporting vote mass exceeds its opposing vote mass, and a tie fails. Return a finite path to stability or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A continuous population with rational type masses, where types encode strict separable preferences and initial ballot bundles. Agents retain integer bundles; states track the mass of each type holding each bundle, and trades move mass among bundles while conserving proposal-specific vote supplies.

The objection that survived

The termination proof orders finitely many named voters and bounds each voter’s score; for divisible type mass, those steps establish no finite stabilizing path.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s finite-path existence question. It leaves Corollary 1’s stochastic convergence, Theorem 2’s pairwise minimal convergence, the welfare and Condorcet results, and the farsighted results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every such rational mass society admit a finite sequence of strictly improving trades ending at a stable state?
  • What are the decision and path-finding complexities in \(K\), the number of types \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. The numbered results concern existence, stability, and welfare; none classifies an algorithmic problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. The closest positive-side anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for every initial vote allocation \(v_0\), number of proposals \(K\), number of voters \(N\), and separable preference profile \(z\), there is a finite sequence of blocking trades ending at a stable vote allocation. That is a mathematical existence theorem, not a complexity result. Corollary 1, also proved here, adds that if every available blocking trade has positive selection probability, a stable allocation is reached with probability \(1\) in finite time. Neither supplies an eligible computational anchor.

A plausible continuous follow-on would preserve the paper’s vote-trading problem while making the population the continuous object. Consider a large member assembly in which many people share the same strict, separable rankings over the \(2^K\) outcomes and the same initial ballot bundle. Represent type \(t\) by that ranking and bundle \(\omega_t\), and represent its share of the population by rational mass \(\mu_t\). In an assembly with one ballot per proposal per member, every \(\omega_t=(1,\ldots,1)\). This is plausible for a large cooperative, union, or party organization with transferable vote rights and a relatively small number \(\tau\) of preference and endowment types compared with its membership.

Here is a prover-ready candidate, Continuous Pivot Stabilization. Its input is \(K\), a finite list of \(\tau\) types, their rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), their separable preference rankings, and their initial bundles \(\omega_t\). A state \(\lambda_{t,v}\) gives the mass of type \(t\) currently holding ballot bundle \(v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^K\); initially \(\lambda_{t,\omega_t}=\mu_t\). Proposal \(k\) passes when the mass of votes held by members who favor it exceeds the mass held by members who oppose it; a tie fails. The decision variable for a trade is a rational flow \(x_{t,v,w}\) of agents from bundle \(v\) to bundle \(w\). It must respect the available mass and conserve the total supply of votes on every proposal. Every positive-mass participant must strictly prefer the resulting outcome to the current one. The task is to find a finite sequence of such trades ending at a state with no feasible blocking trade, or report that no such sequence exists. A solution is the sequence of states and flows, with a stable terminal state. This is a reachability question; as in the paper, there is no cardinal welfare objective.

The connection to Theorem 1 is close in its underlying object: transferable votes, blocking trades, strict gains for every participant, and a stable endpoint. Only the population is continuized; proposals and individual ballot bundles remain discrete. For a fixed state and target outcome, checking whether a blocking mass transfer exists has an LP formulation. That makes tractability of the one-step question a credible Class A lead, especially for fixed \(K\). I would leave the full path problem open: the state can change after every trade, and the paper’s finite-state existence proof gives no bound on path length or computational method. I also would not claim Class B hardness transfers automatically, since a continuous population lets mass of a type split across trades.

The weak point is the regime. The paper studies committee members, often a small group, and assumes vote rights can be traded and enforced. Large assemblies with repeated preference and endowment types are conceivable, but their institutional fit needs to be argued for a particular application. More fundamentally, the proposed reachability problem is a motivated computational question suggested by Theorem 1, not a mirror of a computational result that the authors actually prove. Under the requested anchor rule, that distinction prevents this from being a full positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed Continuous Pivot Stabilization is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. Theorem 1 proves that a finite path to stability exists for finite electorates; Corollary 1 gives an almost-sure convergence statement under a specified selection rule. Neither classifies an algorithm, gives a running-time bound, or asks for a computational output. Turning either into “find a path” adds a computational task that the paper did not study.

There is a useful test for the proposed model. If trades move whole voters, clearing the rational masses’ denominators gives a finite electorate, and Theorem 1 already guarantees a finite path. That settles the candidate’s existence question, though not how efficiently to find the path. If instead the model allows arbitrary fractions of a type to split among ballot bundles, it becomes genuinely continuous—but the finite-state argument no longer applies. The state now ranges over distributions of integer bundles, and the paper’s proof uses a finite electorate and an ordering of named voters to establish termination. The suggested LP checks a possible blocking trade at one state; it does not supply an algorithm or a termination bound for finding a stable endpoint. Those are new questions about a new continuous dynamics.

A stronger computational version could ask for the shortest mass-trade path, or for an efficient algorithm that constructs one. But those add objectives and complexity claims absent from the paper. The authors might find such an extension interesting; that alone does not make it a computational mirror of Theorem 1. The same applies to continuizing Corollary 1: a stochastic process over mass-valued states would need its own specification and computational question.

I would not base the negative case on the absence of a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A large organization with repeated preference types and transferable vote rights is conceivable, and the mass model is coherent enough to investigate. So the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is too strong to establish honestly. The case against is narrower: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and the proponent’s best candidate is a potentially worthwhile follow-on rather than that missing mirror.
