The continuous mirror question

Given public buyer classes \(a\), a finite support of private types \(t=(a,v,b)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), class caps \(\kappa_a\), and supply \(q\) per buyer, optimize expected revenue per buyer over direct menus with deterministic allocations \(x_{a,r}\in[0,\kappa_a]\) and payment lotteries \(P_{a,r}\), where reports \(r\) are valuation-budget pairs. Require voluntary participation and dominant-strategy truthfulness for each type, treating utility as \(-\infty\) whenever a payment exceeds its true budget with positive probability. Require supply feasibility for every report distribution, equivalently \(\sum_a \rho_a\max_{r}x_{a,r}\le q\), where \(\rho_a=\sum_{t:a(t)=a}\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of buyers with finite private valuation-budget types carrying mass \(\mu\), public classes and caps, and supply scaled to \(q\) per buyer. A direct menu assigns each report an allocation and payment lottery; it maximizes revenue under truthfulness, voluntary participation, hard budgets, and capacity feasibility for arbitrary reports.

The objection that survived

The finite mechanism can make allocations depend on the full report profile, while a menu for an atomless population removes that interaction; the proponent flags this concern but does not show that the continuous optimum approximates the finite-\(N\) benchmark.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s Bayesian revenue result, not the fact that it already auctions a divisible good. The new continuous object would be the population of buyers.

Consider a large capacity market: many small firms bid for compute, electricity, or another divisible resource. Buyers fall into a modest number of public classes, each with the same purchase cap and type distribution. Within a class, buyers’ private types are valuation and budget pairs. Let \(t=(a,v_t,b_t)\), where \(a\) identifies the public class and its cap \(\kappa_a\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with support size \(\tau\). In the intended regime, the number of buyers \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\).

The supply should scale with the population: take total supply \(Nq\), so \(q\) is supply per buyer. That co-modification keeps the market meaningful as \(N\) grows. A direct mechanism offers a report-contingent allocation \(x_r\in[0,\kappa_a]\) and payment lottery \(P_r\) for each report \(r=(a,\hat v,\hat b)\). Truthful allocations must fit the supply, \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t\le q\). A type \(t\)’s utility from a report is \(v_t x_r-\mathbb E[P_r]\) when the payment never exceeds \(b_t\), and \(-\infty\) if there is positive probability of exceeding that hard budget. The menu must be voluntarily acceptable and truthful for each individual type; a buyer’s report chooses a contract even though that buyer has zero mass. The objective is to maximize expected revenue per buyer, \(\sum_t\mu_t\mathbb E[P_t]\). The solution is a feasible menu and its allocation and payment rules.

Lead anchor: Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. In the finite Bayesian problem, there are \(n\) bidders, each with an independent discrete distribution over valuation and budget, a public size cap, and one unit of divisible supply. The theorem analyzes the LP-based auction: after scaling by \(\alpha\) and applying the paper’s rounding rule, its realized allocation and payment for bidder \(i\) do not exceed that bidder’s LP allocation, size cap, LP payment, or reported budget, and allocations sum to at most one. For each possible report, its expected allocation and payment are at least \(\frac{\alpha-2}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\) times the corresponding LP values. The auction satisfies voluntary participation, incentive compatibility, and no positive transfers ex post when bidders cannot over-report their budgets. Applying the paper’s THREAT randomization prevents over-reporting; the resulting dominant-strategy mechanism satisfies those properties in expectation and is a \(5.83\)-approximation to optimal expected revenue. Choosing \(\alpha=2+\sqrt{2}\) gives the stated factor \(3+2\sqrt{2}\). The surrounding algorithm runs in time polynomial in the discrete type-space input.

I expect the mean-field problem to be tractable, plausibly even exactly solvable by a finite-type LP with the same budget-threat treatment. The reason is specific: with a fixed finite type support, an allocation and payment entry per report suffices, and the supply constraint is directly on mass. The finite auction’s profile-level allocation overload—and the rounding loss used to address it—may disappear when population proportions are the input. The paper’s theorem establishes a finite-market approximation, not this exact continuous result; determining whether the limit yields an exact LP is part of the proposed work.

This mirror covers the Bayesian revenue approximation, including private budgets, public size caps, dominant-strategy incentives, and resource feasibility. It does not claim to mirror the adaptive-clinching results for adversarial bidder profiles or the indivisible-unit counterexample. In particular, the good’s divisibility is retained, but it is not what makes this a continuous-population model.

The weak point is the mean-field step. In a continuum, a single buyer cannot change aggregate type masses, so finite-market profile interactions vanish. Individual truthfulness remains meaningful as a choice among report-contingent contracts, but an author or referee may question whether that menu captures the strategic role of other bidders’ reports in the finite auction. The case is strongest in markets where many buyers truly share a small number of public classes and the independent type mix is stable. Further questions are whether finite-market optimal revenue converges to the mean-field optimum, how quickly, and whether removing profile-level overload improves the \(5.83\) factor or makes the continuous problem exactly solvable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.2 is a genuine computational result, so there is no “nothing to mirror” objection. The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed mean-field LP has not preserved the theorem’s feasibility requirement.

The proposed constraint \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t\le q\) checks capacity only when everyone reports truthfully. A direct mechanism must also be feasible after arbitrary reports. With a fixed menu, every member of a class could choose its highest-allocation contract, so feasibility would require at least \(\sum_a \mu_a\max_r x_{a,r}\le q\). That may still yield a finite LP, but it optimizes a restricted class of menus, not the profile-dependent mechanisms benchmarked by Theorem 5.2. The theorem’s rounding loss addresses precisely the gap between interim allocations and feasibility at each report profile.

The stronger repair is to let outcomes depend on the aggregate report distribution. But in an atomless population, one buyer’s deviation does not change that distribution. Incentive compatibility then becomes a menu condition at the current aggregate, while feasibility must hold for every aggregate. Rules can behave one way at the anticipated distribution \(\mu\) and use a feasible fallback elsewhere. That is a coherent price-taking model, but its optimization problem is not automatically the limit of the finite auction: finite bidders change the profile when they deviate, and the theorem must handle every profile. Requiring robustness to finite-market fluctuations would need extra assumptions or a finite-\(N\) analysis.

This makes the opponent’s specific claim—“the overload disappears, so the exact optimum is a simple LP”—too quick. It has not shown that the LP covers the intended mechanism class or that its mechanisms correspond to the paper’s large-market regime. The best case against is that the proposed mirror either studies a restricted menu class or changes the strategic and feasibility model enough that it no longer explains the paper’s \(5.83\)-approximation result.

That is a real limitation, but it does not defeat every plausible mirror. Many small buyers with shared public caps and a finite set of valuation-budget types are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and a carefully specified continuum revenue problem could be a worthwhile Class A result. I cannot honestly claim that this anchor has been defeated universally; the negative case is strongest against the stated formulation and its asserted LP, and weaker against a separately developed mean-field mechanism-design model.
