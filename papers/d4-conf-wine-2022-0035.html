The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite-support society \(\mu\) over bidder classes \(k\) and finite signals \(s\), with nonnegative monotone anonymous values \(V_k(z;\nu)\) satisfying measure-SOS, can one compute in time polynomial in the support size, type descriptions, and input bit length a direct rate mechanism \(a_k(r;\nu),p_k(r;\nu)\) such that, for every background society \(\nu\), class \(k\), true signal \(z\), and report \(r\), truthful utility satisfies \(a_k(z;\nu)V_k(z;\nu)-p_k(z;\nu)\ge a_k(r;\nu)V_k(z;\nu)-p_k(r;\nu)\) and is nonnegative, feasibility satisfies \(\sum_{k,r}\nu_{k,r}a_k(r;\nu)\le 1\), and welfare satisfies \(\sum_{k,s}\mu_{k,s}a_k(s;\mu)V_k(s;\mu)\ge \max_{(k,s):\mu_{k,s}>0}V_k(s;\mu)/3.31543\)? The allocation and payment rates are the high-multiplicity normalization of per-agent probabilities and payments, with utilities scaled by the same factor.

The model it lives in

Use a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over bidder classes and signals, anonymous SOS values \(V_k(s;\mu)\), and direct allocation and payment rates \(a_k(s;\mu),p_k(s;\mu)\). Require \(\sum_{k,s}\mu_{k,s}a_k(s;\mu)\le 1\), rate-scaled ex-post IC and IR, and maximize welfare \(\sum_{k,s}\mu_{k,s}a_k(s;\mu)V_k(s;\mu)\) against the best supported value.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's \(3.31543\)-approximation and its combined contribution-based and random-sampling mechanisms in the stated anonymous finite-support regime. It leaves the standalone guarantees of Theorems 1 and 2 and the strong-SOS reduction in Theorem 5 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(3.31543\)-approximation remain achievable by a rate-scaled ex-post IC and IR mechanism for anonymous finite-support SOS populations?
  • Which value representations make constructing and evaluating such a mechanism polynomial in the support size and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Atomless SOS Single-Item Welfare Approximation, anchored in Theorem 4, proved in this paper. The theorem says that for agents with submodular-over-signals (SOS) valuations, there is a polynomial-time, ex-post IC and IR mechanism that achieves a \(3.31543\)-approximation to optimal welfare. This is the paper’s main algorithmic result; the paper offers no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer question.

Here is a continuous version that keeps the auction’s central question. Let \(K\) be a finite set of bidder classes, where a class specifies the agent’s public valuation function and all other relevant public characteristics, and let \(S\) be a finite ordered signal set. A population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(K\times S\), with support size \(\tau\). The intended regime is a large pool of bidders—say, advertisers competing for an impression—with many agents sharing a small number of public valuation classes and signal levels. Thus the number of agents \(N\) is large while \(\tau\ll N\).

For class \(k\), write \(V_k(z;\mu)\) for the value of an agent whose true signal is \(z\), given the distribution of other agents’ signals. Require nonnegative, monotone valuations and the measure version of SOS: raising the background signal distribution reduces the marginal effect of raising the agent’s own signal. A direct mechanism takes the reported population distribution and returns an allocation rate \(x_k(r;\mu)\) and payment rate \(p_k(r;\mu)\) for each class and report. Feasibility is \(\sum_{(k,r)}\mu_{k,r}x_k(r;\mu)\le 1\). These are rates per unit of population mass: they are the high-multiplicity normalization of individual allocation probabilities and payments, scaled by \(N\); scaling both preserves IC and IR. The welfare is \(\sum_{(k,r)}\mu_{k,r}x_k(r;\mu)V_k(r;\mu)\), and the one-item optimum is \(\max_{(k,r)\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}V_k(r;\mu)\).

The precise question is: given such a rational finite-support society and polynomial-time value access to its SOS valuation functions, construct and evaluate, in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the signal and class descriptions, and the input bit length, a direct mechanism that is feasible, ex-post IC and IR for every infinitesimal agent, and guarantees welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)/3.31543\) for every reported society \(\mu\). Ex-post IC compares truthful reporting with any other signal report while holding the other agents’ environment fixed; IR requires nonnegative truthful utility. This is a question about the underlying welfare-maximizing auction, not about an encoding of it.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The paper’s mechanism is explicit, and the finite-population feasibility and SOS arguments have natural measure-level counterparts; random sampling becomes a split of population mass. That makes extending the analysis plausible, though the theorem does not establish the extension. The resulting questions include whether the same factor survives exactly, whether a better factor is possible for atomless SOS societies, and what valuation representations permit polynomial time in \(\tau\).

The weak point is that this mirror assumes a mean-field structure: within a public class, values depend on the other agents through their signal distribution. The paper permits coordinate-specific interdependencies in a finite signal vector, and SOS alone does not imply that those can be summarized by \(\mu\). So the mirror is persuasive for large, relatively homogeneous bidder cohorts, but it is not a continuization of every instance covered by Theorem 4. Another limitation is that the paper’s theorem is a polynomial-time mechanism result, not a complexity classification for an optimization problem of the exponential-variable LP kind. The case is strongest as a concrete high-multiplicity mechanism-design mirror, rather than as evidence that the paper’s full complexity landscape has already been charted.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the only strong anchor here, and the case against it is weak. It is a named polynomial-time mechanism result, and a symmetric high-multiplicity auction is a plausible setting for a continuous mirror. The best objection is that the proposed mirror changes what the mechanism allocates.

The paper allocates one indivisible item to a particular bidder. In a population of size \(N\), let \(q_{k,s}\) be a symmetric bidder’s allocation probability. Feasibility is \(\sum_{k,s} h_{k,s}q_{k,s}\leq 1\), where \(h_{k,s}\) is the number of bidders of that class and signal. With normalized masses \(\mu_{k,s}=h_{k,s}/N\), the proposed rate is \(a_{k,s}=Nq_{k,s}\), giving \(\sum_{k,s}\mu_{k,s}a_{k,s}\leq 1\). This rate preserves the finite mechanism’s scaled welfare and incentive inequalities, but it is not an individual’s allocation probability in the atomless limit. The mirror therefore needs to define its object as a rate mechanism, with utility scaled by \(N\); it cannot simply claim ex-post IC and IR for ordinary infinitesimal bidders in a single-item auction.

There is a separate modeling restriction. The paper’s SOS condition constrains how a bidder’s value changes with each coordinate of the signal vector. It does not imply that values depend only on a histogram or population distribution. The proposed \(V_k(z;\mu)\) formulation assumes anonymous, mean-field interdependence. That may fit some bidder pools, but it is a narrower valuation model than the paper’s. A continuous result for it would be a new mechanism-design problem, not an automatic continuization of Theorem 4.

The stronger version of the mirror would keep finite \(N\), group exchangeable bidders by public valuation class, and encode their signal counts compactly. That preserves the single item and individual deviations; with anonymous valuations it is well-posed. But it is essentially a high-multiplicity mechanism-design problem, whose continuous limit would still need the rate interpretation above. I can’t rule out its value to the programme: a compact algorithm for it could be worthwhile, even though the paper already gives a polynomial-time mechanism in the explicitly listed bidders.

So the defensible negative case is that the proposed atomless mirror has not yet pinned down how its rates represent a single-item auction, and its mean-field assumption is substantive. That weakens this particular formulation; it does not defeat every plausible mirror. I would not honestly argue that no worthwhile scenario exists.
