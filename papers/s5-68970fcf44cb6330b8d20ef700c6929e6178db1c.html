What the mirror covers

The proposed evaluation covers only the ex-ante and ex-post proportional allocation formulas; it gives no computational analogue for their axiomatic characterizations or the paper's comparison results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a natural population model, but this paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it. Its central results are normative characterizations. Theorem 3, proved here, says that for \(|N|\ge3\) and \(|S|\ge3\), the ex-ante proportional rule is the unique rule satisfying NARAI, CONT, NAN, and NARAS. Theorem 4, also proved here, says that for \(|N|\ge3\), the ex-post proportional rule is the unique rule satisfying NARAI, CONT, NAN, SYM, and IND. Both give explicit formulas, but neither asserts a complexity result or presents a computational problem the paper solves. I would not count either as a qualifying computational anchor.

A plausible population regime is a large network of clinics receiving preparedness funds. A clinic’s type is its complete state-contingent claim vector: the amount it requests in each possible hazard state. Clinics in the same standardized profile class have identical claims; there may be many clinics but relatively few distinct types. Let \(\mu_a\) be the fraction of clinics of type \(a\), \(x_{as}\) that type’s claim in state \(s\), \(p_s\) the common state probability, and \(b>0\) the available resource per clinic. Require \(\sum_a\mu_a x_{as}\ge b\) in every state. Scaling the total resource with the population makes a type’s allocation meaningful as the network grows.

A precise, though unanchored, computational question would be Continuous Proportional-Rule Evaluation: given the finite support \((\mu_a,x_a)\), rational probabilities \(p\), and per-clinic resource \(b\), output the exact allocation to each type under the ex-ante and ex-post proportional rules. Writing \(Q=\sum_a\mu_a\sum_s p_s x_{as}\) and \(X_s=\sum_a\mu_a x_{as}\), the answers are

\[ y_a^{\mathrm{EA}}=b\frac{\sum_s p_s x_{as}}{Q}, \qquad y_a^{\mathrm{EP}}=b\sum_s p_s\frac{x_{as}}{X_s}. \]

The solution is the rational vector of per-clinic type allocations; \(\sum_a\mu_a y_a=b\). Theorems 3 and 4 motivate which rules to evaluate, but this is a straightforward extension of their formulas, not a computational result they establish. With explicitly listed states and types, I expect this problem to be tractable by direct arithmetic, polynomial in the input size.

That makes the weakest point decisive: the paper’s contribution is axiomatic, and its rules already have closed-form formulas. The mirror changes the population to mass, but it does not uncover a meaningful complexity landscape or test whether discrete hardness dissolves. It covers only evaluation of the two proportional rules, not their axiomatic characterizations. If the required anchor is a named computational result, there is no defensible anchor-based positive case here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against rests on a problem shared by both named anchors: Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 characterize rules; they do not solve a computational problem or establish a complexity result. Theorem 3 characterizes the ex-ante proportional rule by axioms, and Theorem 4 does the same for the ex-post rule. The paper’s “Continuity” axiom concerns how a rule changes with its finite-dimensional inputs; it does not make the population continuous. Neither theorem gives ChoCo a computational result to mirror.

The clinic example does support a sensible high-multiplicity model. Let type \(a\) be a clinic’s complete vector of claims \((x_{as})_{s\in S}\), let \(\mu_a\) be its population fraction, and let \(b\) be resources per clinic. With \(X_s=\sum_a\mu_a x_{as}\), the rules allocate each clinic of type \(a\)

\[ y_a^{\mathrm{EA}}=b\frac{\sum_s p_s x_{as}}{\sum_s p_s X_s}, \qquad y_a^{\mathrm{EP}}=b\sum_s p_s\frac{x_{as}}{X_s}. \]

So I would not argue that repeated types are implausible, that mass is meaningless, or that these formulas fail to extend. Those are weak objections. The extension is coherent, but it is a population-level restatement of rules the paper already defines, not a computational mirror of either characterization.

The proposed “Continuous Proportional-Rule Evaluation” is the best computational version offered, but it is unanchored: it asks for the formulas’ outputs, while the paper’s results establish no discrete computational task whose continuous version could be compared. With explicitly listed types and states, evaluation consists of the displayed sums and ratios. A continuous axiom characterization over a distribution of types would be a better mathematical extension of Theorems 3 and 4, but it would still be axiomatic rather than computational. Adding an optimization objective or a succinct encoding might create a computational problem, but its central difficulty would come from that new addition, not from continuizing a problem this paper studied.

The negative case is therefore strong on ChoCo’s scope and weak as a claim that no related question could ever be worthwhile. Clinics can plausibly form a high-multiplicity society, and a new computational problem could be built around these rules. But the paper itself supplies no computational anchor for such a mirror; its characterizations and closed-form allocation rules do not fill that gap.
