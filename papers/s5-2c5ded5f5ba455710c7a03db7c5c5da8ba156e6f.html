The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a continuous mirror is the population regime the paper discusses: a large election in which many voters share one of a modest number of complete types—preferences, utility vectors, beliefs, and behavioral dispositions. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\); voters’ strategic ballot choices determine the election outcome, and utilities evaluate that outcome. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, though the paper does not specify the behavioral model \(\omega\) needed to determine those choices.

But there is no qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness, tractability, or parameterized complexity. The claim that strategic voting can improve the frequency of selecting the utilitarian winner is attributed to Lehtinen’s earlier papers, not established here as a numbered result. And Section 5 explicitly says the broad claim about violating difference-making conditions has no general proof.

So I cannot honestly state a continuous problem *per anchor*. One could formulate a new computational study of how often strategic voting selects the utilitarian winner in a distribution over continuous societies, but that would extend the paper’s discussion rather than mirror a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the main obstacle: this paper gives no numbered computational result to mirror. Its sweeping claim that strategic voting can defeat every difference-making condition is expressly unsupported by a general proof, and the welfare-frequency findings it cites come from Lehtinen’s earlier work. Section 5’s examples are illustrations in a philosophical argument, not complexity results.

The strongest candidate for a mirror is the claim that strategic voting improves the chance of electing the utilitarian winner. But that mechanism is built around individual pivotality. The paper models a voter’s expected gain using probabilities such as \(p_{jk}\) of being pivotal between candidates \(j\) and \(k\). In an atomless continuous society, changing one voter’s ballot changes zero mass, so that voter cannot change the outcome. Strategic incentives disappear; individual voters are indifferent among ballots, and the paper’s central behavioral comparison loses its content.

One could make each type’s entire mass act as a coordinated bloc, but then the strategic actor is a bloc, not an individual voter with one vote. Or one could retain finite stochastic vote counts so individuals remain pivotal. That requires adding a noise law and behavioral model the paper does not specify; the resulting question is an analysis of a finite stochastic election, not a computational problem on a continuous society. Likewise, “how often” strategic voting selects the utilitarian winner is undefined for a fixed society \(\mu\), whose outcome is deterministic. It needs an additional distribution over societies, which is a new modeling choice rather than something supplied by this paper.

A large election with many voters sharing complete types is certainly a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The difficulty is that it preserves the paper’s voter population while removing the individual strategic mechanism its argument depends on. A stochastic or bloc-based extension might still interest the authors, so the universal claim that *no* scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is stronger than the evidence allows. The negative case is strongest as a verdict on mirroring this paper’s results; it is weaker as a claim that no adjacent computational project could be worthwhile.
