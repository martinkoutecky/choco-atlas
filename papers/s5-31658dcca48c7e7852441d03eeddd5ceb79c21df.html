The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k<|C|\), fault fraction \(\beta=t/n<1/3\), a public tie order, and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings and Byzantine status for the submitted quorum, compute the exact committee and round-by-round transfers using quota \(1/(k+1)\) in quorum-normalized units. Determine whether, for every \(0<j\le s\le k\), a group of quorum mass greater than \(j/(k+1)\) that ranks the same \(s\) candidates in its top \(s\) positions has at least \(j\) of those candidates elected. If \(\mu\) is normalized over the \(n-t\) quorum ballots, allow Byzantine mass up to \(\beta/(1-\beta)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ballot rankings and Byzantine status, normalized to the submitted quorum’s mass of one. The mass-valued BFT-STV rule applies quota \(1/(k+1)\), transfers surplus and eliminated-candidate mass across ranking types, and outputs a size-\(k\) committee; there is no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers BFT-STV committee selection and Theorem 1’s proportionality guarantee. It leaves Theorem 2’s non-dictatorship result, the blockchain theorems, consensus, performance claims, and the unsubstantiated NP-hardness claim in §6.6.3 untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Formalize the proportionality condition over quorum mass and verify that its normalization matches the paper’s condition.
  • Specify tie handling and exact rational surplus and elimination transfers, then establish termination and bit complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length.
  • Prove whether the mass-valued rule preserves Theorem 1’s proportionality guarantee under the stated Byzantine-mass bound.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: a continuous version of the BFT-STV election, not a continuization of the whole SocChain system.

There is an anchor problem, though. The paper gives no numbered theorem asserting a complexity classification such as NP-hardness or polynomial-time solvability. Section 6.6.3 says in passing that the STV computation is NP-hard, but that claim is not a numbered result and does not specify the precise problem. The closest named computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper, whose proof draws on Janson (2018): *BFT-STV satisfies Proportionality*. In full, for integers \(0<j\leq s\leq k\), if more than \(j(n-t)/(k+1)\) voters rank the same set of \(s\) candidates in their top \(s\) positions, then the elected committee includes at least \(j\) of those candidates. The authors say the argument holds even when Byzantine voters cast their ballots in the worst possible way. This is a result about the correctness of an election algorithm, not a complexity-class result, so it is only a provisional anchor under your stricter requirement.

The mirror I would investigate is Continuous Quorum BFT-STV. Its instance is a fixed candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(k<|C|\), a fault bound \(\beta<1/3\), a public tie-breaking order, and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ballot types. A type records a full ranking of \(C\) and whether the voter is Byzantine; \(\mu\) describes the ballots in the quorum submitted to the election. Run BFT-STV with each type’s mass in place of its ballot count, transferring mass according to the paper’s surplus and elimination rules. Since the quorum represents mass \(1-\beta\) of the full electorate, the continuous quota is \((1-\beta)/(k+1)\) in full-population units, or \(1/(k+1)\) when \(\mu\) is normalized over the quorum. The requested output is the \(k\)-candidate committee and its exact sequence of mass transfers and eliminations. There is no optimization objective: this is a rule-based winner-determination problem.

The intended regime is a large permissioned federation with many authenticated member nodes voting on a fixed slate of candidate governors. Common caucus or institutional mandate ballots could give many voters exactly the same ranking, so the number of voters \(N\) could greatly exceed the number of distinct types \(\tau\). That is a constructed but plausible scenario: the paper already has a bounded candidate slate and uses KYC and jurisdictional diversity to constrain candidate selection. The type must include the full ranking and Byzantine status, since both affect the problem’s inputs.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable, with computation over \(m\) candidates and \(\tau\) ballot types rather than over all \(N\) voters. Each round can aggregate the mass of each ranking type. Establishing a polynomial bound in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length would still require a careful analysis of exact fractional transfers. The paper’s Theorem 1 does not establish that bound, nor does it prove that a discrete hardness result disappears.

The mirror is plausible because it preserves the election the authors actually define: rankings over candidates, quota-based transfers, and a proportionality guarantee. It also makes explicit the type-level fractional transfers that an aggregate model requires. Its scope is only the BFT-STV committee-selection stage. The asynchronous consensus protocol, transaction lifecycle, and blockchain safety, liveness, and validity claims remain outside this mirror.

The weakest point is the anchor: no named complexity result supports a continuization question about hardness dissolving. Even the transfer to rational masses needs a proof; Theorem 1 covers the paper’s ballot model, not this continuous extension. So there is a concrete and, I think, author-recognizable mirror here, but the paper does not supply the kind of named complexity anchor your standard requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves that BFT-STV satisfies a proportionality condition; Theorem 2 proves non-dictatorship; the blockchain theorems establish safety, liveness, and validity. None classifies the complexity of a computational social choice problem. The stray claim in §6.6.3 that STV is NP-hard supplies no defined decision problem, reduction, or input encoding. The proponent’s proposed runtime question therefore starts a new investigation; it does not continuize a complexity result established by this paper.

The strongest version of their mirror is coherent: take a finite-support rational distribution over complete rankings, allow an adversarial submass up to a fraction \(\beta<1/3\), model the quorum and quota in mass units, and compute the committee and transfer sequence. A large federation with recurring institutional ballots could plausibly have many voters per type. The paper’s election outcome depends substantially on ballot counts, so aggregation is natural for this subproblem. That makes “no plausible continuous model” a weak argument.

The sharper objection is fit. Theorem 1’s conclusion is a representation guarantee: if a sufficiently large mass ranks a set of candidates at the top, enough of those candidates are elected. Its continuous counterpart asks whether that guarantee holds for fractional mass transfers. That is a theorem about a mass-valued rule’s correctness, rather than a complexity classification or an algorithmic result about bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness. Asking for runtime in \(m,\tau,L\) could be worthwhile, but the paper gives no named complexity result that makes that question its mirror. Adding worst-case quorum selection and Byzantine mass may make the computational question richer; it also makes the model a new robust-election analysis.

So the negative case is strongest as a programme-priority argument: the paper’s formal results do not anchor the kind of continuous computational question ChoCo is charting. It is weak as a universal claim. Continuous BFT-STV is a sensible model in at least some large-federation regimes, and this paper alone cannot establish that studying its computation would be unworthy.
