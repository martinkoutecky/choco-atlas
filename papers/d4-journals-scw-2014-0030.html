The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a credible high-multiplicity model for computing the paper’s folk cost shares. But the paper gives no named computational result to anchor it. Its nearest result, Theorem 3, proved here, says that a cost-sharing rule is the folk solution if and only if it satisfies Piecewise Linearity, Core Selection, Branch Cutting, and Equal Share of Cost Reduction. That is an axiomatic characterization; it does not classify the complexity of computing any problem. The other numbered theorems are characterizations too. The polynomial-time statement about finding a minimum spanning tree in Section 2.1 is unnumbered, cites Prim and Kruskal, and concerns building the network rather than computing cost shares. So Theorem 3 is the closest conceptual anchor, but it does not meet your computational-anchor requirement.

A plausible regime is millions of residents served through a much smaller number \(\tau\) of neighborhoods or service hubs. A type specifies a hub and the complete cost-relevant information shared by its residents; residents of the same type have identical costs, and links within a hub have zero cost. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of residents at hub \(t\). Edge costs between hubs and to the source are fixed, regardless of how many residents use a link. Here the population is large relative to the number of types, and mass is a natural description of the customer base at each hub.

The strongest candidate question is Folk-Share Evaluation\(_\infty\). Its input is a finite support \(T\), positive binary-rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to one, and a nonnegative rational edge-cost matrix \(c\) on \(T\cup\{0\}\). Form the irreducible matrix \(\bar c\) as in the paper and let \(v(S)=C(S;\bar c)\) be the folk cost game on a coalition of types. Draw an ordering of the types by repeatedly selecting a remaining type \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t/\sum_{u\text{ remaining}}\mu_u\). If \(S\) is the set before \(t\), the requested output is the exact aggregate payment assigned to each type:
\[ A_t=\mathbb{E}\big[v(S\cup\{t\})-v(S)\big]. \]
The corresponding per-unit-of-mass share is \(A_t/\mu_t\). An equivalent explicit definition uses \(q_\mu(S)\), the probability that \(S\) is exactly the set of types before \(t\):
\[ A_t=\sum_{S\subseteq T\setminus\{t\}}q_\mu(S)\frac{\mu_t}{1-\mu(S)} \bigl(v(S\cup\{t\})-v(S)\bigr), \qquad \mu(S)=\sum_{u\in S}\mu_u. \]
Thus the problem is to compute the folk allocation, not to optimize a new network or solve an axiomatic characterization.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity bridge under the stated hub model. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), take \(n_t\) identical residents of type \(t\). A coalition’s network cost depends only on which hubs it contains. In a uniformly random ordering of the residents, the order in which hubs first appear is exactly the mass-weighted ordering above. The aggregate Shapley payment to each hub’s residents therefore equals \(A_t\). The type masses are doing real work: they determine the order in which locations enter the coalition.

For exact evaluation, my provisional expectation is that polynomial time in \(\tau\) may be difficult: the output averages marginal costs over type orderings, so support-size combinatorics remain. The finite-replication identity makes hardness of the corresponding high-multiplicity computation the first place I would look. Additive approximation, by contrast, should be accessible by sampling these orderings. The paper proves neither claim. Natural follow-up questions are whether exact evaluation is polynomial in \(\tau\), whether it is hard even with restricted cost matrices, and whether useful deterministic approximation or parameterized algorithms exist.

The mirror’s scope is only the folk allocation characterized by Theorem 3. I would not claim that it mirrors the paper’s full characterization programme or that it captures the folk/cycle-complete distinction. Its weakest point is also decisive for grading: Theorem 3 tells us which rule the authors endorse under their axioms, but says nothing about the complexity of evaluating it. The model is a plausible computational extension of their problem, not a continuous mirror anchored by a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the paper’s actual results. Theorem 3 characterizes the folk solution by axioms; Theorems 1 and 2 characterize related cost-sharing rules. None states the complexity of computing a solution. The polynomial-time claim in Section 2.1 concerns finding a minimum spanning tree, not computing cost shares. So there is no named computational result here to mirror. The proposed evaluator is an added computational question, not a continuous version of a computational claim made in this paper.

Folk-Share Evaluation\(_\infty\) is the strongest proposed extension, and its hub scenario is plausible. I would not dismiss it on high-multiplicity grounds: with rational masses realized as proportions of cloned residents, the mass-weighted order does reproduce their aggregate Shapley payments. But that construction also exposes what has been added. The paper defines the folk solution as a Shapley value on a finite set of agents; its characterization does not introduce masses or specify that type masses should determine a random arrival order. The proposed question therefore studies a new, weighted Shapley extension. For a continuum population, the paper itself supplies no random-permutation rule or other axiom selecting that extension.

There is a direct continuum formulation, but under the paper’s fixed-edge-cost model, network cost depends on which types are present, not how much mass they have. Mass becomes relevant to the proposed allocation only through the added weighted ordering. That makes Folk-Share Evaluation\(_\infty\) a coherent follow-up, but a weak mirror of this paper’s results: its main computational question comes from a new modeling choice that the paper neither makes nor analyzes.

I would argue against treating this paper as a strong ChoCo mirror because it has no computational result to anchor one. I cannot honestly make the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile continuous scenario exists: the hub model survives as a plausible new problem. The negative case is strongest on the paper’s lack of computational content, and weak where it must rule out that extension as worthwhile.
