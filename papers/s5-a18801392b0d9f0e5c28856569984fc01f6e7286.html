The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: Continuous BP-FASP for a population of faculty-placement records.

My named anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for every BP-FASP instance, the LP-relaxed feasible region \(P_1\) of BLP #1 is contained in the LP-relaxed feasible region \(P_2\) of BLP #2, and that this inclusion can be strict. Thus BLP #1 always gives a bound at least as strong as BLP #2, and sometimes a stronger one; the paper also reports that BLP #2 can give competitive lower bounds much faster in preliminary tests. This is a formulation result the paper uses to compute BP-FASP instances. The manuscript has no numbered theorem asserting a complexity classification, so I would not present Theorem 2 as a hardness theorem.

Here is the underlying continuous problem. Let \(N\) be a fixed set of departments, and let \(E\subseteq\{(i,j):i,j\in N,\ i\ne j\}\) be the observed directed hiring relationships. Each population type is an ordered pair \((i,j)\): a faculty-placement record for a graduate of department \(i\) hired by department \(j\). Let \(\mu_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) be that type’s fraction of all non-self-hire records, with \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\mu_{ij}=1\). The support size is \(\tau=|E|\).

A solution is a weak ordering \(\sigma\) of the departments. A tied block is allowed only if every pair in it has positive mass in both directions: \(\mu_{ij}>0\) and \(\mu_{ji}>0\). The solution removes the mass of every type whose placement contradicts the ordering: \((i,j)\) is removed when \(j\) is ranked strictly above \(i\). Its cost is
\[ V_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\substack{(i,j)\in E\\j\succ_\sigma i}}\mu_{ij}. \]
Find an ordering of minimum cost and report that ordering and its removed mass. The retained graph is unicycle-free: arcs between distinct blocks all point consistently down the ordering, and tied blocks have the complete bidirectional relationships required by BP-FASP. This asks for the hierarchy that minimizes the share of hiring evidence it must discard; it does not make the LP encoding the problem.

The natural regime is a large, sparse, discipline-wide hiring census, possibly pooled across cohorts, with many placement records but a fixed department taxonomy. The number of records can grow while the possible type set remains bounded by \(m(m-1)\), and the support is often much smaller. Each record is indistinguishable from others with the same origin and destination for the objective this paper studies: those are precisely the details its graph and weights retain. The paper’s IEOR example has 1,179 faculty records, 83 departments, and roughly 5% of possible directed arcs. That is a plausible starting point, though its record-to-supported-type ratio is only moderate; a larger census makes the high-multiplicity regime clearer.

This is a credible mirror because the paper itself defines arc weights as counts of faculty placements and optimizes the total weight of removed arcs. Normalizing those counts to proportions preserves the weak order, feasible ties, and optimum: if the total count is \(W\), then \(\mu_{ij}=w_{ij}/W\), and the continuous objective is the paper’s objective divided by \(W\). Conversely, rational masses can be scaled by a common denominator to obtain discrete multiplicities. That close dictionary makes the model recognizable to these authors, even though it also makes the mirror’s novelty modest.

I expect Class B: hardness transfers. In Section 3.3, the paper says BP-FASP is NP-hard and APX-hard because FASP is a special case; this is stated in the prose, not as a numbered theorem. On the no-bidirectional-arc instances used for that special case, tied blocks are unavailable. Assigning equal rational mass to every arc makes the continuous objective exactly the number of removed arcs divided by \(|E|\), so solving the mirror solves the corresponding FASP instance. The combinatorics remain in the department set and its relationships, not in the number of placement records. Theorem 2 adds a separate question about the continuous instance’s LP bounds: how much stronger is BLP #1 in practice, and when is the faster BLP #2 preferable? Other questions include whether sparsity or the number of bidirectional pairs yields useful parameterized algorithms, and which graph restrictions might permit approximation schemes.

The scope is BP-FASP’s minimum-mass hierarchy and the formulations used to compute it. I would not claim this mirrors the weak-tournament extension as a population-of-voters problem, or the paper’s bootstrap, ERGM, or cut-imbalance analyses.

The weakest point is that this population interpretation is already very close to the paper’s weighted graph: the authors aggregate placement records into arc counts, and their action removes a whole relationship type. Because any positive residual mass still leaves that arc in the graph, allowing fractional deletion within a type does not improve the feasible hierarchy. So the mirror chiefly makes the placement population and its proportions explicit; it may not create a new optimization problem. I still consider it a sensible mirror: the authors’ objective is already about aggregate placement evidence, and this formulation makes the high-multiplicity input regime and the dependence on \(m\), \(\tau\), and rational bit length explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The missing numbered complexity theorem is not the problem: Theorem 2 is a named computational result, so it deserves a direct answer. The difficulty is that the proposed continuous input is already covered by the paper’s weighted-graph model. Its arc weights may be any nonnegative real values, and the IEOR weights are counts of placement records. For a society with masses \(\mu_{ij}\) on placement types \((i,j)\), the paper’s graph is obtained by setting \(w_{ij}=\mu_{ij}\). More generally, normalizing weights by their total changes the objective only by a positive scale factor:
\[ \sum_{j\succ_\sigma i}\mu_{ij} =\frac{1}{\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}}\sum_{j\succ_\sigma i}w_{ij}. \]
The optimal hierarchies and feasible ties are unchanged. Rational masses can also be scaled back to integer record counts.

The same equivalence applies to the named anchor. The BLP objectives merely receive the normalized weights as coefficients; the feasible regions \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) do not change. Thus Theorem 2’s containment and strictness hold for the proposed population input exactly as they do for the paper’s weighted graphs. The continuous input does not create a new LP question or a new optimization problem for that theorem. Population size and multiplicities disappear after aggregation into arc weights.

A more detailed type space—adding cohort, field, or faculty attributes—does not rescue this anchor while keeping its objective: the objective sees only the total mass on each department pair. Summing the detailed types by their endpoints returns the same weighted graph. If those extra attributes change the objective, that may define a worthwhile time-aware or attribute-aware ranking problem, but it is a different problem from the result being mirrored. Nor does fractional deletion supply a new version of BP-FASP: a positive residual mass still leaves the arc present, so the hierarchy constraints do not permit a partial arc to resolve a cycle.

So the proposed faculty-placement mirror is sensible as a population interpretation, but weak as a *new continuous mirror*: the paper already admits the relevant mass vectors as weights and already studies their formulations. Questions about sparsity or parameterized algorithms may still be worth asking, but they are questions about the weighted graph problem, not a payoff from continuizing its population.

That is the strongest negative case I can make against this anchor. I would not claim that the paper rules out every worthwhile continuous project nearby; extensions with genuinely new population-dependent objectives could be interesting. They would not, however, preserve Theorem 2 as the continuous result this case relies on.
