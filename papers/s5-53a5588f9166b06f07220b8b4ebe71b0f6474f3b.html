The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its theorems characterize self-stability and existence; they do not classify the complexity of computing a stable rule or constitution. Theorem 5 is the closest anchor: it gives a condition for when a majority-rule constitution is self-stable. Turning that characterization into a computational problem would be a new question built on the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result it established.

The most faithful population limit also removes the phenomenon behind Theorem 5. Let voter types have probabilities \(p\), distributed with masses \(\mu\), and let \(\bar p=\int p\,d\mu\). Under the paper’s independence assumption, a continuum society’s share supporting change is \(\bar p\) almost surely. A quota \(q<\bar p\) therefore selects change with certainty; \(q>\bar p\) selects the status quo with certainty. Type \(p\)’s expected utility is then \(p\) in the first case and \(1-p\) in the second. Their strict preferences over quotas depend only on whether \(p\) is above or below \(1/2\) and which side of \(\bar p\) the quota lies on. The conditional pivotal probabilities and subset comparisons in Theorem 5 disappear. This is not a complaint that the continuous answer might be simple: the finite model’s uncertainty-driven preferences over rules have vanished, leaving a deterministic threshold model.

A common shock is the best repair: let a latent state affect the population’s support for change, so aggregate uncertainty persists after the population grows. But that changes the source of uncertainty that drives the paper’s results. The authors explicitly rely on independence for single-peakedness and single crossing, and note that correlation can disrupt those properties. A computational study of a particular shock model could be worthwhile, but it would need new primitives and new results; with finitely many shock states, much of the added uncertainty may simply reduce to comparing those states, while richer representations put the computational burden in the shock model itself.

Repeated \(p\)-types are plausible in a large electorate, so lack of multiplicity is not the objection. The negative case is narrower: under the paper’s stated model, the natural continuum erases the pivotal uncertainty; preserving it requires an extension; and the paper supplies no computational result to anchor either route. That is a strong case against a ChoCo mirror of this paper, though not proof that a related project could never be valuable.
