The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results establish existence and properties of equilibria and bargaining outcomes; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The nearest hook is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: under its standing assumptions, every world has a unique bargaining outcome, and that outcome is the unique generalized zero of the characteristic function \(\xi\). That is a structural characterization, not a computational result, so I would not present it as an eligible anchor.

The strongest positive case is a computational follow-on to Theorem 4. Call it Finite-Type Bargaining Outcome Approximation. An instance has alternatives \(x\in[0,1]\), a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and for each type a rational, explicitly listed, concave piecewise-linear utility \(u_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) with a unique ideal point \(\bar x_t\). A proposer is drawn with probability \(\mu_t\), and agreement still requires unanimity. Given rational \(\epsilon>0\), compute \(z\in[0,1]\) within \(\epsilon\) of the unique bargaining outcome \(x^*\). Here mass is the fraction of agents of each complete utility type, and also that type’s recognition probability. The decision variable is the social alternative \(x\); there is no planner’s objective—the bargaining equilibrium limit defines the solution.

For this finite-support case, the characteristic function is
\[ \xi(x)=\left(\sum_{\bar x_t<x}\mu_t\right)\phi^-(x) +\left(\sum_{x<\bar x_t}\mu_t\right)\phi^+(x), \]
where
\[ \phi^-(x)=\min_{t\in T}\frac{u'_{t,-}(x)}{u_t(x)} \quad\text{and}\quad \phi^+(x)=\max_{t\in T}\frac{u'_{t,+}(x)}{u_t(x)}. \]
The outcome is its unique generalized zero, as in Theorem 4. I would expect the approximation problem to be tractable: for the stated encoding, \(\xi\) can be evaluated in polynomial time, and the paper proves it is decreasing (Proposition 6), giving a bisection approach with polynomially many evaluations in the input length and desired precision. Further questions include exact bit complexity and computation with more succinct utility descriptions.

A plausible regime is a large cooperative making a one-dimensional policy decision under a unanimity agreement rule, with the proposer selected uniformly from its members. Members with identical full utility functions—same ideal point, slopes, and other preference features—form a type; then \(n\gg\tau\) is credible and \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). The paper’s authors should recognize this as their bargaining problem: the alternative, recognition lottery, unanimity rule, and \(\delta\to1\) solution are retained. This proposal covers only computation of the asymptotic bargaining outcome, not finite-\(\delta\) equilibria or the paper’s other equilibrium results.

The weak point is substantial: the paper already makes the population continuous, so this is a computational study of an existing continuous model, not a continuization of a discrete problem with a named hardness result. The large-unanimity scenario may also be less natural than the paper’s finite bargaining examples. So it is a credible computational extension, but not a strong anchor-based continuous mirror under the requested criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject this paper as a continuous mirror, but not because a large unanimity bargaining group is implausible. The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its theorems concern equilibrium existence, uniqueness, limiting outcomes, and subgame perfection; none gives an algorithm or a complexity classification. Theorem 4 characterizes the bargaining outcome as a generalized zero of \(\xi\). That may invite a computational question, but it is not itself a result about computation.

The proponent’s Finite-Type Bargaining Outcome Approximation is the strongest candidate. Grouping agents with identical full utility functions into types is sensible, and a large group making a unanimous policy decision is a plausible setting. With rational masses and explicitly represented utilities, approximating the outcome is a well-posed task. But it computationalizes a model the paper already states over a probability space: \(N\) may be finite or infinite, and \(\mu\) already describes the population. Theorem 4 already uses the distribution of ideal points. Restricting that model to finite support and adding an encoding does not turn one of the paper’s discrete computational results into its continuous counterpart; it supplies a new computational question.

A stronger attempt might start with a finite bargaining group containing many repeated utility types and ask how computing its outcome relates to the measure-based outcome, perhaps with bounds as the group grows. That could be worthwhile work. But it would add a finite-to-continuous bridge that this paper does not establish, and it still would not mirror a computational result in the paper. Likewise, choosing richer types or asking about finite-\(\delta\) equilibria creates other computational questions without fixing the missing source problem.

So the negative case is strongest on the category boundary: the paper is already a continuous-population equilibrium analysis, and it contains no discrete complexity result for continuization to change. I cannot honestly claim that computing its outcome could never be useful; the proposed algorithmic follow-up is plausible. The case against is that such work would be a computational extension of this paper, not a continuous mirror of it.
