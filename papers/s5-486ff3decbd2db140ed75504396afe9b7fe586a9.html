The anchor — hardness

Theorem B.3

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem B.3 — survived: Its group quotas have a well-defined mass version, and the opponent accepts that this is a coherent problem for exchangeable metric cohorts.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), an explicitly listed family \(\mathcal G=\{G_1,\ldots,G_\gamma\}\) with \(G_j\subseteq T\), and an integer \(g\ge 1\), compute an optimal rational vector \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in G_j}x_t\ge \mu(G_j)/g\) for each \(j\), where \(\mu(G_j)=\sum_{t\in G_j}\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

The population consists of metric instances; a type records the instance’s membership in every listed group, the attributes used by this anchored problem. The distribution \(\mu\) gives each type’s share of the inventory, and \(x_t\) is the selected mass from that type. The objective is to minimize total selected mass subject to each group’s quota.

The objection that survived

The paper’s metrics are often atomic, and the low-support setting relies on many instances sharing group memberships; selecting cohort mass may therefore amount to a weighted benchmark rather than a deployable subset.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem B.3’s generalized representation quotas. It leaves positional representation, positional proportionality, Theorem B.4, and the empirical case studies without a mirror in this proposal.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an optimal mass solution be rounded to a finite subset while preserving the group quotas with a bounded additive loss?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is for the paper’s generalized representation problem. The population would be the inventory of evaluation metrics themselves. The alternatives they rank stay finite; I am not continuizing the models, hospitals, or rankings.

Consider a large benchmark bank generated from a modest collection of task templates, languages, and evaluation protocols. Many metric instances may then be interchangeable for the selection question. A type \(t\) records everything that matters: its ranking of the fixed alternatives and its membership in each group the practitioner wants represented, such as a language or task family. If the bank has \(n\) metrics but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(n\gg\tau\), let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the inventory of type \(t\). Selecting mass \(x_t\) means retaining that share of the metric population; in this regime, it represents selecting a fraction of a large cohort of exchangeable metric instances.

My anchor is Theorem B.3, proved in this paper in Appendix K.1. It says that, given a finite set \(N\), a family of groups \(\mathcal G\subseteq 2^N\), and a group size \(g\), finding the smallest \(K\subseteq N\) satisfying \(|K\cap G|\ge \lfloor |G|/g\rfloor\) for every \(G\in\mathcal G\) is NP-hard. The paper proves this by reduction from Set Cover. This is the paper’s generalized representation result, not a claim that its positional representation problem is NP-hard.

I would call the continuous question Mass Generalized Representation\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite type support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), an explicitly listed family of groups \(\mathcal G=\{G_1,\ldots,G_\gamma\}\), where each \(G_j\subseteq T\), and an integer \(g\). Write \(\mu(G)=\sum_{t\in G}\mu_t\). Choose masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in G_j}x_t\ge \mu(G_j)/g\) for every group. A solution is an optimal rational vector \(x\): the selected mass from each type.

The quota is the normalized limit of the paper’s quota. If a discrete inventory has \(n\) metrics and \(\mu(G)=|G|/n\), then \(\lfloor |G|/g\rfloor/n\) tends to \(\mu(G)/g\). The objective \(\sum_t x_t\) is the selected fraction of the full inventory, the normalization of minimizing \(|K|\). This formulation keeps the population continuous and makes selection a choice of subpopulation mass; it does not make the ranked alternatives fractional.

The continuous problem is a linear program, solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the listed group-incidence data, and the input bit length. I expect Class A: the integer selection problem in Theorem B.3 is hard, while the high-multiplicity mass version has a direct continuous-optimization formulation. The discrete hardness does not by itself transfer, because the mirror permits fractional selection from a type.

The weak point is that metric tasks are often atomic, and a fractional amount of one task may not be a meaningful practical choice. The mirror is most plausible when the bank contains large cohorts of exchangeable instances and selecting a fraction of a cohort is operationally meaningful. It covers Theorem B.3’s generalized group quotas, not the paper’s positional representation and proportionality results as a whole. A useful next question is when an optimal mass solution can be rounded to an actual finite subset while preserving the group quotas, with a bounded additive loss.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem B.3 is a real computational result, so the case cannot rest on a lack of an anchor or on prior continuous work. The strongest objection is that its “population” would be the inventory of metrics being selected, rather than agents whose preferences the paper aggregates. In the actual examples, metrics are atomic tasks or hospital measures: evaluating half a metric is not a smaller Lite suite.

The proposed cohort story helps, but it is narrow. To keep the type support small, many metric instances must share both the same rankings of alternatives and the same membership in every relevant group. In a large real benchmark bank, growth more often comes from distinct tasks, so the support grows with the inventory. If the instances really are interchangeable clones, selecting a fraction of a type is effectively assigning that clone type a fractional weight. That may be useful, but it changes the paper’s subset-selection object into a weighted benchmark.

There is also a sharp degeneration for the paper’s positional representation version of the mirror. Taking \(x_t=\mu_t/g\) gives \(x(G)=\mu(G)/g\) for every group, while \(\sum_t x_t=1/g\). Since the positional groups include the whole population, every feasible solution has mass at least \(1/g\). Thus the continuous optimum is exactly \(1/g\), independent of the rankings; the optimization no longer selects a representative subset. For generalized representation, where the groups need not include the whole population, the mass problem can remain nontrivial—but it is the standard fractional covering LP. That weakens its claim to be a distinctive computational social choice question.

These points make the practical and intellectual case for the mirror less compelling, but they do not defeat the strongest version of the anchor. For generated cohorts with many exchangeable metric instances, fractional selection is a coherent high-multiplicity limit, and the resulting LP directly answers a computational question corresponding to Theorem B.3. The honest negative case is therefore weak: I would not support a red verdict on this anchor.
