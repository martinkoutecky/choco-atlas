What the mirror covers

Neither named anchor has a qualifying computational mirror; the equilibrium existence and asymptotic welfare results remain outside the proposed complexity programme.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible high-multiplicity setting is the one the authors themselves mention: a large population of risk-neutral firms or shareholders. Suppose agents have one of a small number \(\tau\) of valuation vectors \(u_t\in[0,1]^m\), with type \(t\) held by fraction \(\mu_t\) of the population and \(\tau\ll n\). Those vectors, together with the common quadratic cost parameter \(c\), fully describe the preferences relevant to QTM. This is a credible setting for a continuous society: masses of agents share the same type, and the outcome depends on aggregate support for each alternative.

A natural first attempt would give each type a vote vector \(a(t)\), set aggregate support to \(V_j=N\int a_j(t)\,d\mu(t)\), and use the paper’s softmax rule \(Q_j=e^{V_j}/\sum_k e^{V_k}\). But that does not preserve the strategic problem the authors study. In an atomless population, one agent’s action has no effect on \(Q\); with positive quadratic cost, each agent’s best response is zero. The finite population’s pivotality, though vanishing, is essential to the paper’s result. Treating a whole type mass as a coordinated actor could restore influence, but would change the strategic unit and the underlying problem.

There is a strong population-asymptotic result in the paper, but it is not a qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that if \(F\) is supported on \([0,1]^m\), its coordinate means are all distinct, and it assigns positive probability to every neighborhood of a suitable point on each coordinate axis, then—after relabelling so alternative \(1\) has the highest mean—there is a sequence \(\beta_n\downarrow0\) such that for every \(n\) and every symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium, including mixed equilibria,
\[ \Pr\{Q_1\le 1-\beta_n\}\le\beta_n. \]
Proposition 1, also proved here, establishes that a symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium exists for every sample size \(n\) and cost parameter \(c>0\). Both are equilibrium and asymptotic-welfare results. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or proves hardness.

So the honest positive case is limited: the paper suggests a plausible high-multiplicity regime and a promising direction for future computational work on equilibria of QTM. But it supplies no named computational result to anchor that work. I would not present a continuous problem as mirroring one of its anchors; doing so would either mislabel Theorem 1 or replace its finite-agent strategic incentives with a different continuum game.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest possible anchor is Theorem 1, since it studies what happens as the group grows. But it proves an asymptotic equilibrium and welfare result, not a computational result: it gives no algorithm or complexity classification for a problem. Proposition 1 is an equilibrium existence result. The paper names no computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart.

A natural extension would ask: given a high-multiplicity distribution of valuation types, can we compute a symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium of QTM, or its outcome probabilities? That could become a worthwhile computational project, but it adds the computational question the paper does not ask. It also runs into a real modeling divide. In an atomless population, one agent’s action has no effect on the outcome, so with positive quadratic cost the best response is zero. The resulting uniform outcome probabilities do not capture Theorem 1’s vanishing but still consequential individual pivotality. Retaining finite population size preserves that pivotality, but then the problem still depends on \(n\), and computing equilibria is a new task.

Treating each type mass as a strategic actor, or rescaling costs to preserve individual incentives, might produce a nontrivial continuum game. But either choice changes the strategic unit or the mechanism’s incentives; neither is a continuous version of a computational result in this paper. Likewise, adding bribery or winner-robustness questions would define new problems without anchors in the paper.

The negative case is strongest on eligibility as a mirror, not on whether QTM could inspire future computational work. It could. But that work would be an adjacent research programme, not a continuization of a named result here.
