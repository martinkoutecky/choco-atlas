The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many cohorts \(i\) with rational masses \(\alpha_i\), weights \(w_i\), bounded value supports, and rational piecewise-constant value densities \(F_i\), together with per-capita supply \(\rho\), compute welfare-maximizing interim allocation and waiting-cost schedules \(q_i(v)\) and \(c_i(v)\) for the population measure \(\mu(i,dv)=\alpha_i\,dF_i(v)\). Require \(0\le q_i(v)\le1\), \(c_i(v)\ge0\), truthful reporting \(v q_i(v)-c_i(v)\ge v q_i(r)-c_i(r)\) for all \(v,r\), voluntary participation \(v q_i(v)-c_i(v)\ge0\), and supply \(\sum_i\alpha_i\int q_i(v)\,dF_i(v)\le\rho\); maximize \(\sum_i\alpha_i w_i\int (v q_i(v)-c_i(v))\,dF_i(v)\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of applicants with cohort and value types \((i,v)\), measure \(\mu(i,dv)=\alpha_i\,dF_i(v)\), and supply \(\rho\) goods per applicant. Each applicant receives at most one indivisible good; \(q_i(v)\) is the share or probability allocated to that type, and \(c_i(v)\) is expected waiting cost. The objective is weighted expected surplus subject to truthful reporting, participation, and aggregate supply.

The case FOR (proponent)

I see a credible positive case, with Proposition 1 as the lead. One qualification up front: the paper proves no named result saying “in P” or “NP-hard.” Its computationally relevant anchors are constructive optimization results: they characterize the optimum and give a way to implement it. The paper studies a finite set of agents with private values, not a continuous population, so the mirrors below are new limit problems rather than claims about what the paper already solved.

Lead — Continuous Ironed-Priority Rationing, mirroring Proposition 1, proved in this paper. The proposition says that, for given value distributions \(F_i\) and Pareto weights \(w_i\), the optimal direct mechanism assigns the \(m\) goods to agents with the highest priority levels, breaking ties by an equal-chance lottery. Those levels are \(\lambda_i(v_i)=w_i g_i(F_i(v_i))\), where \(g_i\) is the derivative of the convex hull \(G_i\) of the function \(H_i(z)=\int_0^z (1-u)/f_i(F_i^{-1}(u))\,du\). The canonical cost rule is \(c_i(v)=p_i(v)v_i-\int_{\underline v_i}^{v_i}p_i(x_i,v_{-i})\,dx_i\). The proposition also identifies useful special cases: with non-decreasing hazard rates, allocation reduces to a priority list based on weighted expected values; with symmetric agents and decreasing hazard rates, it screens fully by realized values.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. There are finitely many observable applicant cohorts \(i\), with rational masses \(\alpha_i>0\) summing to \(1\). Members of cohort \(i\) share observable characteristics and an independent private-value law \(F_i\), with bounded support and a finitely described density—for example, a rational piecewise-constant density. Each applicant’s value \(v\) is their maximum willingness to wait for one identical indivisible good. The planner’s weight for cohort \(i\) is \(w_i\). There is a supply of \(\rho\) goods per applicant, for rational \(0<\rho<1\); this is the co-modification that keeps supply meaningful as the population grows.

The population law is \(\mu(i,dv)=\alpha_i\,dF_i(v)\). Find interim allocation probabilities \(q_i(v)\) and expected waiting costs \(c_i(v)\) that maximize
\[ \sum_i \alpha_i w_i\int \bigl(vq_i(v)-c_i(v)\bigr)\,dF_i(v) \]
subject to \(0\le q_i(v)\le1\), \(c_i(v)\ge0\), truthful reporting and voluntary participation,
\[ vq_i(v)-c_i(v)\ge vq_i(r)-c_i(r)\quad\text{for every }v,r, \qquad vq_i(v)-c_i(v)\ge0, \]
and the per-capita supply constraint
\[ \sum_i\alpha_i\int q_i(v)\,dF_i(v)\le\rho. \]
Each applicant receives either zero or one good; a fractional \(q_i(v)\) means that this share of a tied or randomized population wins. A solution is the allocation rule and cost schedule, with an implementation that respects supply.

The natural candidate solution is the continuum counterpart of Proposition 1: compute the ironed priorities, choose a cutoff \(\kappa\) that allocates mass \(\rho\) to the highest priorities, and fractionally allocate at a tie. The truthful costs follow the envelope rule \(c_i(v)=vq_i(v)-\int_{\underline v_i}^{v}q_i(x)\,dx\). This is a good high-multiplicity regime for a public resource allocation problem: for instance, a city distributing subsidized housing or medical tests to many applicants across a small number of observable cohorts. The number of applicants is large relative to the number of cohorts; individual values remain private and independent as in the paper. The model keeps the authors’ incentive and welfare problem intact while replacing counts by mass.

Second anchor — Continuum Queue Implementation, mirroring Proposition 2, also proved here. For symmetric agents with bounded common value support, the proposition starts from the optimal direct mechanism and constructs a queue that implements it. It forms a random winner vector with marginal win probabilities \(p_i(v)\) and exactly \(m\) winners. For each value \(v_i\), it sets the arrival time to the expected canonical waiting cost conditional on winning, namely
\[ t(v_i)=\mathbb{E}_{v_{-i}}\!\left[ v_i-\frac{\int_{\underline v}^{v_i}p_i(x_i,v_{-i})\,dx_i}{p_i(v_i,v_{-i})} \;\middle|\; \ell_i^p(v)=1 \right]. \]
The separating region \(T\) is the image of \(t\). Under the paper’s queue rules and behavioral conditions, having each agent with value \(v_i\) arrive at \(t(v_i)\) is a Bayesian equilibrium that implements the optimal direct mechanism.

The corresponding continuous problem has one population of mass \(1\), common bounded value law \(F\), and supply ratio \(\rho\). Given \(F\) and \(\rho\), find an allowed arrival-time set \(T\) and a symmetric strategy \(t(v)\) such that the queue allocates mass \(\rho\), agents’ waiting costs are their time in line when they win, and the resulting equilibrium reproduces the optimal direct mechanism’s interim allocations and costs. A tie at the marginal arrival time rations the remaining supply across that tied mass. This is a natural implementation question for the same housing or medical-resource regime: the output is a queue rule people can actually follow, not just a direct-mechanism description. I expect this mirror to be Class A if the continuum equilibrium and the limit of the finite-agent construction are established.

Both questions are about the population and computational design of allocation rules. They do not claim novelty for lotteries, fractional outcomes, or continuous time. A finitely described density is the input representation for private values; the continuous object being studied is the applicant population, with supply scaled per capita.

The likely direction for the lead problem is Class A: the paper’s structural result reduces the optimization to ironing priorities and selecting a cutoff. With piecewise rational densities, the open computational work is to bound the bit complexity of constructing the convex hull and cutoff. The queue problem is also plausibly Class A once its limiting equilibrium is proved. Further questions include whether finite instances with \(m/n\to\rho\) converge to this optimum with a useful welfare bound, and whether the queue construction extends from symmetric applicants to finitely many cohorts.

The soft spot is that the paper’s private values have a continuous range, whereas the programme’s standard high-multiplicity input is a finite list of types with rational masses. Here the natural representation is a finite number of cohorts with finitely described value distributions; the realized value remains an individual’s private information. That is a real modelling extension, and the continuum queue’s equilibrium is not proved by Proposition 2 itself. Still, this seems like a strong mirror: it preserves the paper’s private information, waiting costs, incentive constraints, scarce indivisible goods, and welfare objective. The main additional work is to formalize the population limit and its input encoding, rather than to invent a different economic question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch of subject. Proposition 1 characterizes an optimal Bayesian mechanism, and Proposition 2 gives a queue equilibrium that implements it. Neither states a computational problem with an input encoding, an output representation, or a complexity bound. The paper lets the value distributions be general functions and describes allocation and cost rules over continuous report spaces. Turning either proposition into a complexity question therefore requires choosing new representations for both the input distributions and the mechanisms.

That issue is sharpest for Proposition 1. The proposed cohort model reads each agent’s prior \(F_i\) as the realized population share of values, \(\mu(i,dv)=\alpha_i\,dF_i(v)\). In the paper, \(F_i\) instead describes uncertainty about the value of a particular agent \(i\); agents draw independent private values. The cohort model replaces that finite-agent uncertainty with a deterministic mass distribution of realized values and allocation functions \(q_i(v)\). This is a plausible high-multiplicity reinterpretation, especially for large applicant pools, but it is a change in the economic object.

Keeping continuous values makes each exact value type have zero population mass, so the mirror needs a continuum of types and functional inputs and outputs. Replacing those values with finitely many levels gives positive mass to each type and a finite-dimensional mechanism-design problem, but changes the paper’s continuous-value screening model. The proponent’s piecewise-rational density is a reasonable way to make the functional version computable; it also shows the limitation of the negative case. I can question whether the resulting computation belongs in ChoCo’s complexity programme, but I cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror on modelling grounds.

Proposition 2 is more dependent on that choice. Its result implements the direct mechanism through individually chosen arrival times in a finite-agent Bayesian game. A continuum queue must define what happens to positive mass arriving together: fractional rationing can reproduce the lottery, but then the mirror needs an aggregate tie-breaking and queue model. With those choices made, the proposed arrival rule is derived from Proposition 1’s allocation and cost rules. The remaining question is largely whether that construction is an equilibrium in the chosen nonatomic game, rather than a separate computational problem. That makes Proposition 2 a weaker anchor for a programme focused on complexity and algorithms.

So the negative case is real but limited: the paper offers no direct complexity result, and both proposed mirrors need a new computational formulation. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Large populations seeking scarce housing, tests, or other public resources are plausible high-multiplicity regimes, and a finite-support version of the private-value model is easy to state. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that every such version is unworthy; the strongest objection is that the paper’s computational contribution would have to be built around its economic model, rather than extracted from a complexity result it already proves.
