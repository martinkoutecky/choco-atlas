The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered formal statement is Definition 1. Section 4 does report computational results in prose, including that computing Chamberlin–Courant winning committees is NP-hard, citing Procaccia et al. (2008) and Lu and Boutilier (2011). That claim was proved elsewhere, and it is the strongest candidate for a mirror, but it does not meet your anchor rule. There is therefore no continuous problem I can honestly present as anchored to a named result of this paper.

If the prose-cited claim is admitted as a lead, the strongest mirror is continuous Chamberlin–Courant winner determination. An instance has \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Choose a size-\(k\) committee \(S\) maximizing

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr). \]

Here, mass is the fraction of society holding each ranking, the decision is the committee \(S\), and the objective is its average Borda score for each type’s best-represented member. A plausible regime is a large electorate voting from a bounded set of recurring ranking templates: millions of voters and a support of hundreds or thousands of types.

The authors would likely recognize this as the same Chamberlin–Courant winner problem with a high-multiplicity society. The expected classification is hardness transfers: a discrete electorate with type counts \(n_t\) maps to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), preserving the winning committees, so discrete NP-hardness carries over. This mirror covers winner computation for Chamberlin–Courant; it does not cover the paper’s other computational challenges. It raises follow-up questions about approximation and parameterization by \(k\), \(\tau\), or \(m\).

The weakest point is decisive under your rules: this is a precise and plausible mirror of a complexity claim the paper discusses, but it is not anchored to a numbered result of the paper.

But if the Chamberlin–Courant claim is admitted anyway, I cannot honestly defeat its mirror. With ranking types \(t\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\), maximizing
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr) \]
is a well-posed computational question about a continuous society. Large electorates with recurring preference patterns are a plausible regime; committee selection does not depend on voter identities; and the paper’s discrete model already aggregates voters by their scores. The discrete-to-continuous correspondence is not an objection under the programme’s rules, and the cited NP-hardness is neither a novelty collision nor evidence that the question is uninteresting.

The Chamberlin–Courant candidate survives that broader test, and the honest negative case is weak if prose-cited results are allowed.
