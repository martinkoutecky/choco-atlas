The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita voting weights \(w_t>0\), a common prior \(p=\Pr(X)<1/2\), and a quota \(Q\) with \(0<Q\le W=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\), choose a distribution over targeted-mass vectors \(a\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) for state \(Y\). In state \(X\), recommend \(x\) to everyone; in state \(Y\), recommend \(x\) to targeted mass \(a_t\) of each type and \(y\) to the rest. Require behavioral obedience, \(\mathbb{E}[a_t]\le\mu_t p/(1-p)\) for every type, and maximize the ex-ante probability that \(x\) passes, where a signal passes exactly when \(\sum_t w_ta_t\ge Q\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete voter types carrying mass, each with a per-capita weight \(w_t\) and the paper’s common prior and preferences. The sender chooses a lottery over type-level targeted masses \(a_t\); the objective is the probability of approval, subject to type-by-type obedience. The optimal state-\(Y\) approval probability is \(\min\{1,\frac{p}{1-p}\frac{W}{Q}\}\), attained by targeting \(a_t=(Q/W)\mu_t\) on each approving signal with that probability.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s optimal-experiment problem for behavioral voters with a common prior. It leaves aside the sophisticated-voter equilibrium results, the apex-game characterizations, heterogeneous-prior extensions, and abstention.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite populations with \(n_t/n\) approximating each \(\mu_t\), what rounding guarantee relates the discrete optimum to the type-mass optimum, especially when the number of types is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s behavioral-voter problem. Its anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. This is a formulation anchor, not a polynomial-time claim: there is no numbered result in the article that classifies a problem as being in P or NP-hard. Proposition 1 says that, for behavioral voters, an optimal experiment is found by choosing probabilities \(p_j\) for state-\(Y\) signals that target each minimal winning coalition, plus \(p_0\) for the all-\(y\) signal. The LP maximizes \(1-\lambda_0(Y)p_0\), subject to \(p_j\ge 0\), \(\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_j=1\), and, for every receiver \(i\), \(\sum_{j:\,i\text{ is targeted by }s_j}p_j\le \lambda_0(X)/\lambda_0(Y)\). The article uses this formulation in its analysis and computations. Its introduction also cites NP-hardness of finding minimal winning coalitions, but that is prior work, not a numbered complexity result of this paper, so I do not count it as an anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Continuum Weighted-Persuasion. A scenario is a widely held company with, say, \(100{,}000\) beneficial owners in a handful of standardized voting-weight classes. There is no controlling shareholder. The owner count \(n\) is large while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) is small. A type \(t\) specifies an owner’s voting weight \(w_t\) and, as in the paper, their common prior and preferences; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the population. The CEO wants proposal \(x\) approved. The common prior of state \(X\) is \(p<1/2\), and approval requires at least \(Q\) total voting weight in favor.

The instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational weights \(w_t\), quota \(Q\), and prior \(p\). Write \(W=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\) for total voting weight. In state \(X\), everyone is truthfully recommended \(x\). In state \(Y\), the sender chooses a lottery over measurable subsets of owners to receive private recommendation \(x\); everyone else receives \(y\). A realization targeting mass \(a_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of type \(t\) passes the proposal exactly when \(\sum_t w_ta_t\ge Q\). The question is to find the maximum probability that \(x\) is implemented and an experiment attaining it. Each person still receives one of the paper’s two ordinary recommendations; only the targeted fraction of a type is continuous.

For an anonymous experiment, an individual of type \(t\) receives \(x\) in state \(Y\) with probability \(\mathbb{E}[a_t]/\mu_t\). Behavioral obedience therefore requires \(\mathbb{E}[a_t]\le\mu_t p/(1-p)\). If \(\alpha\) is the probability of approval in state \(Y\), every approving signal targets weight at least \(Q\), while obedience bounds expected targeted weight by \(pW/(1-p)\). Thus \(\alpha\le\min\{1,\frac{p}{1-p}\frac{W}{Q}\}\).

That bound is attained: on a successful state-\(Y\) signal, target a fraction \(Q/W\) of every type, choosing the subsets symmetrically within each type; use that signal with probability \(\alpha=\min\{1,\frac{p}{1-p}\frac{W}{Q}\}\), and send \(y\) to everyone otherwise. Each owner then has the same chance of receiving \(x\), regardless of type, and the approval signal reaches quota exactly. The optimal value is therefore \(p+(1-p)\min\{1,\frac{p}{1-p}\frac{W}{Q}\}\). A compact exact solution is just the value of \(\alpha\) and this sampling rule, computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. I expect this mirror to be Class A.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ problem: it keeps their sender, two states, private correlated messages, behavioral voters, heterogeneous voting weights, and weighted approval threshold. It models a diffuse owner base grouped by repeated complete types, precisely the regime where population fractions are natural. It mirrors Proposition 1’s underlying optimization, not the LP encoding, and it covers that behavioral problem in a continuous-population regime. It does not attempt to mirror the paper’s sophisticated-voter equilibrium results, its apex-game characterization, or its heterogeneous-prior extensions.

The soft spot is that this relaxation removes much of what makes Proposition 1 difficult. Finite coalitions must be built out of whole receivers; a continuum can select exactly \(Q/W\) of every type. Under a common prior, that proportional targeting makes the distribution of weights matter only through \(W/Q\), erasing the finite model’s detailed minimal-winning-coalition structure. That is the real acceptance test: would the authors and a referee regard fractional type-level targeting as the natural high-multiplicity version of their signaling problem? I think so, but I would not present the resulting easy solution as a complexity result about their finite model.

The next questions are whether finite instances with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) admit useful rounding guarantees; how the answer changes when obedience thresholds vary by type; and what happens with positive-mass controlling blocks or sophisticated voting. Those are separate extensions, not covered by this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this is a weak computational-results mirror. Proposition 1 gives an exact LP for the sender’s problem, but it makes no claim about running time, approximation, or parameterized complexity. Its variables are indexed by minimal winning coalitions, and the paper notes that finding those coalitions is NP-hard. So the proponent’s formula does not continuize a complexity result established by the paper; it takes the paper’s behavioral-voter optimization problem and gives it a new, atomless population model.

That model is mathematically coherent, though. With many owners in a few voting-weight classes, the paper’s sender, messages, voting rule, and common prior all remain intact. The paper itself offers shareholder blocs as an interpretation. Its allowance for fractional type-level targeting also fits the programme’s stated scope. In that model, a successful signal can target the same fraction of every type, reaching quota exactly; obedience then gives the bound \(\alpha\le\min\{1,\frac{p}{1-p}\frac{W}{Q}\}\). I see no fundamental flaw in the proposed construction. Adding type-specific priors would change the obedience capacities, but would not restore the finite model’s indivisible coalition structure.

There is a sharper limitation for the paper’s sophisticated-voter results: in an atomless population, each individual has zero influence on the vote, so pivotality-based strategic incentives disappear. That makes a continuum equilibrium version a poor mirror of the swing-voter analysis. It does not defeat the behavioral-voter anchor, which the paper treats separately.

So the negative case is weak against the actual anchor. I can argue that Proposition 1 is not a complexity classification, and that its continuum counterpart may be too simple to advance ChoCo’s algorithmic agenda. But simplicity is not, by itself, a reason the question is unworthy, and the programme explicitly welcomes tractable mirrors. I cannot honestly rule out this scenario or its continuous question.
