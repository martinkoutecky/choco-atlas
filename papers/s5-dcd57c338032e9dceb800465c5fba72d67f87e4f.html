The closest computational hook is the authors’ use of linear programming to calculate minimum integer weights preserving types. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no surviving anchor. Its only possible one is computing minimum integer weights preserving types, which the paper uses as an input to its regressions and says is an application of linear programming. The paper does not state a computational result about that problem. Its central contribution is empirical: a zero-inflated beta model and evidence about which variables predict cabinet membership and portfolio shares. It gives no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about complexity or algorithms. Under the programme’s stated criterion, that is the strongest case against a mirror of this paper.

The best rescue would be a large population of similar parties, with types recording their voting weights and other relevant attributes, and a computational question about predicting joint cabinet membership and portfolio allocation. But that would require building a new coalition-formation problem. The paper’s unit is an individual party in a specific government-formation situation; its MIW is derived from the full, discrete coalition structure. Replacing the party list with type masses therefore changes the object being analyzed, and retaining the exact coalition structure preserves the finite problem rather than mirroring a computational result from the paper. Treating citizens as the continuous population and deriving party seat shares would extend the paper’s pipeline in a different direction.

This makes the negative case strong on scope, but weak as a universal claim. A continuum of parties or party-like bargaining units could motivate worthwhile new computational work. The point is narrower: the paper itself supplies no computational result for that work to mirror.
