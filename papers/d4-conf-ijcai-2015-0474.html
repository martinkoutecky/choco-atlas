The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is a genuinely plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large group of deliberators share a fixed argument vocabulary, and each person’s complete type is their argumentation framework \(F\). Mass \(\mu_F\) is the fraction holding that framework. The paper itself notes that weighted attacks can represent the number of agents supporting an attack, so a mass version would assign \(w_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{F:(a,b)\in Att(F)}\mu_F\), then apply a weighted aggregation method. That is a sensible continuous-population research direction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–7 establish which rationality properties aggregation operators satisfy; they do not classify an algorithmic problem by complexity or give an algorithm. Calling one of them a complexity anchor would misstate what it proves.

The sole candidate is a society with type \(F\) and mass \(\mu_F\), aggregated using attack weights \(w_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{F:(a,b)\in Att(F)}\mu_F\). That is a natural high-multiplicity regime. But the paper already builds weighted argumentation frameworks from agents’ attack counts and uses them to define its FUS operators. Those operators depend on the profile through attack weights, not through the full distribution over AF types. Studying the complexity of evaluating their mass-weighted versions could be worthwhile new work, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the paper-anchored claim: there is no qualifying result to continue. It is weak as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The group-deliberation setting makes a continuous population plausible, and the supplied material does not rule out a useful new computational study of weighted aggregation.
