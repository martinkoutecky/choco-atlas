The continuous mirror question

Given an explicit finite state set \(S\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), type payoffs \(w_t(s)\), state-specific voting weights \(a_{s,t}>0\), and rational quotas \(\theta_s\) satisfying \(\frac12\sum_t a_{s,t}\mu_t\le\theta_s<\sum_t a_{s,t}\mu_t\), compute the fixed-point set \(D\) of maps satisfying the paper’s three stability axioms and, for each initial state \(s_0\), all attainable terminal states \(\phi(s_0)\). In state \(s\), a coalition is a vector of measurable type masses \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) and wins when \(\sum_t a_{s,t}q_t>\theta_s\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types, each with payoff \(w_t(s)\) and voting weight \(a_{s,t}\) in state \(s\). A coalition may include any mass \(q_t\) of each type, and wins under the state’s weighted quota. The decision is which states are fixed points of the farsighted stability maps and which terminal states those maps permit from each initial state.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s recursive characterization of stable states and outcome maps under explicit weighted quotas; it leaves the finite-player equilibrium foundation in Theorem 2, arbitrary representations of \(W_s\), and the paper’s other results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what quota and tie conventions does this mass-coalition recursion correspond to a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s finite-player game?
  • Which broader explicit representations of state-dependent winning rules preserve polynomial-time evaluation in \(|S|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is the computation of farsightedly stable constitutional outcomes in a large society with only a few recurring political types. This paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result. The anchor I can defend is its constructive one: Theorem 1, “Axiomatic Characterization of Stable States,” proved here. Under Assumptions 1 and 2, it says that at least one outcome map \(\phi:S\to S\) satisfies the three stability axioms; every such map can be built recursively from an ordering of the states; all such maps have the same fixed points \(D\); and, with Assumption 3, any two maps select payoff-equivalent outcomes from each initial state. In the recursion, set \(\phi(\mu_1)=\mu_1\). At each later state \(\mu_k\), form \(M_k\) from earlier fixed points that are strictly preferred to \(\mu_k\) by a winning coalition in \(\mu_k\). If \(M_k\) is empty, leave \(\mu_k\) fixed; otherwise map it to an element of \(M_k\) that no other element of \(M_k\) is strictly preferred to in \(\mu_k\). The theorem’s assumptions are the paper’s upward-closed, pairwise-intersecting winning-coalition rules and its two acyclicity conditions on payoffs and those rules. This is a constructive characterization, not a complexity classification stated by the authors.

Continuous Dynamic Constitutional Stability would take as input a finite set of constitutional states \(S\), a support of \(\tau\) voter types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and each type’s payoff \(w_t(s)\) in every state. A type also includes everything else the model uses about an individual: here, their voting weight under each state’s rules. Write that weight as \(a_{s,t}>0\). In state \(s\), a coalition with type-mass vector \(q\), where \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\), wins if
\[ \sum_t a_{s,t}q_t>\theta_s, \qquad \frac12\sum_t a_{s,t}\mu_t\le\theta_s<\sum_t a_{s,t}\mu_t. \]
These strict weighted-majority rules are one concrete, plausible family of the paper’s winning-coalition systems; they ensure that winning coalitions overlap in positive mass. Thus \(y\succ_x x\) exactly when the total voting weight of types that strictly prefer \(y\) to \(x\) exceeds \(\theta_x\). The question is: for every initial constitution, compute the set \(D\) of stable constitutions and the set of terminal outcomes permitted by maps satisfying the paper’s three axioms. A solution is those sets, or a particular valid map if the instance has a unique outcome. There is no social-welfare maximization objective in this problem: it computes the paper’s farsighted stability outcome.

A convincing regime is constitutional choice in a large country or association whose members fall into a modest number of recurring types—say, income or occupational groups with the same payoff profile across constitutions and the same political weight. There may be millions of people but only tens of types, so \(N\gg\tau\). Mass is the fraction of society of each type; coalitions are measurable portions of those masses. The state space remains the paper’s finite, discrete set of institutional arrangements. Only the population is continuized. This keeps the central issue intact: a group may favor reform today, yet resist it because the reform leads to a different future distribution of power. The authors already model state-dependent payoffs and state-dependent winning rules, so this is a direct extension of their object, not a welfare-maximizing simplification.

For this explicit-state, threshold-rule encoding, I expect Class A: tractable. Each comparison \(y\succ_x x\) is a sum over the \(\tau\) types, followed by the theorem’s finite recursion over \(S\). The resulting algorithm is polynomial in the explicitly listed \(|S|\), \(\tau\), and rational input bit length \(L\). That polynomial-time claim is a consequence of applying Theorem 1 to this specified encoding; it is not a claim made in the paper. It covers the paper’s recursive characterization of stable states and outcome maps. It does not cover the full noncooperative voting game, arbitrary succinctly encoded state spaces, or every possible family of winning rules.

The weakest point is the bridge from the paper’s dynamic game to a continuum of agents. Theorem 1’s axiomatic characterization is the safest anchor because its input is already aggregate: payoffs and winning coalitions. The paper’s separate Theorem 2 connects those axioms to a pure-strategy Markov-perfect equilibrium for finite players and sufficiently patient agents. In a nonatomic population, however, an individual has zero mass and cannot be pivotal in the usual voting model. I would not claim that Theorem 2’s equilibrium foundation transfers unchanged. A continuous version needs either an explicit aggregate coalition-choice protocol or a finite, high-multiplicity limit argument. A referee could also regard the result as an efficient implementation of an existing recursive characterization rather than a new complexity landscape. The positive case survives in a narrower form: Theorem 1 yields a concrete continuous-population computation problem, and its tractability depends on keeping state comparisons easy to price from type masses.

The next questions are whether the same tractability persists under richer type-level winning rules; how the continuous outcomes correspond to finite elections at quota boundaries and ties; and what happens when the state space is succinctly encoded or grows with population size. Those are meaningful boundaries for this mirror. The paper’s analyses of equilibrium protocols and ordered-state conditions are related machinery, but I would not stretch this case to claim that they are already computational results or that the whole paper has been continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the fatal weakness in this case as a *mirror*: it is an axiomatic characterization with a recursive construction, not a named computational result. The paper defines no computational problem, input encoding, complexity bound, approximation task, or lower bound. Theorem 2 characterizes equilibrium outcomes; it also does not study their computational complexity. So the proposed computation would create a new problem inspired by the paper, rather than continuize one of its computational results.

The proponent’s strongest scenario is credible: citizens in a large society share a modest number of payoff and voting-weight types, and each constitution has a weighted quota. For that model, the comparison \(y\succ_x x\) can be determined from the mass of types that strictly prefer \(y\) to \(x\). That is a coherent continuous-population question. Atomlessness alone does not defeat its axiomatic version; it mainly complicates the proposed link to the paper’s finite-player equilibrium.

The gap is that the paper allows very general state-specific winning-coalition families \(W_s\). Its assumptions require monotonicity and intersection, not a quota or any representation in terms of a small number of types. The quota model supplies precisely that missing structure. For the general model, encoding enough information about who can form winning coalitions may require distinctions among individuals and erase the small-support regime. Thus the proposed algorithmic question depends on a new representation of political rules, rather than a computational bottleneck identified in the paper.

The stronger dynamic-game version needs still more new structure: in a diffuse population, an individual has no pivotal mass, while the paper’s equilibrium analysis is built around finite players voting over transitions. One can define a mass-coalition protocol, but that would be a new behavioral foundation for the aggregate model.

So the best negative case is about fit with ChoCo’s computational agenda, not the plausibility of the population scenario. The threshold model is a sensible research question; I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous model could be made from this paper. The case against is that its sole anchor does not mirror a computational result, and the most convincing repair adds a rule representation and computational object that the paper itself never studies.
