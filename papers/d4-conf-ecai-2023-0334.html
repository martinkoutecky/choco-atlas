The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete utility types \(T\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\), indivisible item types with rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\), and additive utilities \(u_t(i)\), decide whether there exists a finitely supported mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^m\) satisfying type-mass and supply constraints, envy-freeness across all positive-mass bundles, and Pareto efficiency under type-preserving mass couplings; construct such an allocation when one exists.

The model it lives in

A configuration-mass model with complete utility types \(t\), population masses \(\mu_t\), integral bundles \(b\), variables \(x_{t,b}\), per-capita supply constraints, and a feasibility objective requiring envy-freeness plus absence of any positive-mass Pareto improvement.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the core EEF–Allocation existence and constructive FPT result, but not the general PILP machinery or every fairness and efficiency variant listed in Section 5.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(\mathrm{EEF\text{-}Allocation}_\infty\) admit an FPT algorithm parameterized by \(\tau+m\) under the utility and encoding restrictions of Theorem 1?
  • Can every feasible envy-free Pareto-efficient mass allocation be represented with support bounded as a function of \(\tau+m\), or is the configuration-and-coupling problem continuum-specifically hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a genuine population-scale extension of the paper’s EEF–Allocation problem. The paper already establishes that high multiplicity is meaningful for fair allocation, but its agents are still a finite named set. The mirror continuizes the agent population while keeping the allocated objects indivisible.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states:

Let \(I\) be an instance of EEF–Allocation with \(n\) agents, \(m\) item types, and maximum utility value \(u_{\max}=2^{o(\log |I|)}\). Then there is an algorithm deciding whether \(I\) admits an envy-free Pareto-efficient allocation in time \(f(m+n)\cdot \operatorname{poly}(|I|)\), for some computable function \(f\).

The point is that item multiplicities and utility values may be binary encoded, subject to the theorem’s stated bound on \(u_{\max}\). The paper’s proof is constructive in the sense that the parametric-ILP procedure also produces an envy-free Pareto-efficient allocation on yes-instances.

The paper contains no comparable numbered hardness theorem of its own. Its \(\Sigma_2^P\)-completeness and NP-hardness statements are attributed to earlier papers, so I would not use them as anchors under the programme’s rules.

I would mirror Theorem 1 with the following problem.

Continuous EEF–Allocation, \(\mathrm{EEF\text{-}Allocation}_\infty\). An instance consists of:

  • finitely many complete agent types \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\);
  • a rational population distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\);
  • \(m\) indivisible item types;
  • a rational per-capita supply \(q_i\ge 0\) for each item type \(i\);
  • additive utilities \(u_t(i)\in\mathbb Z\), encoded as in the paper.

A type \(t\) is the complete utility vector \((u_t(1),\ldots,u_t(m))\). If the model used prices, eligibility, or budgets, those would also belong to the type; agents differing in any relevant feature would be different types.

A bundle is still integral: \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\). The decision variable is a finitely supported mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(b\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,b} b_i x_{t,b}\le q_i \]

for every item type \(i\). Thus only population mass is divisible; no individual receives a fractional item.

The allocation is envy-free if, whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(x_{t',b'}>0\),

\[ \sum_i u_t(i)b_i\ge \sum_i u_t(i)b'_i. \]

This is exactly the finite definition applied to every positive-mass agent and every bundle occurring in the population.

Pareto efficiency must also be defined at the mass level. An allocation \(x\) is Pareto-dominated if there is another feasible allocation \(y\) and a coupling \(z_{t,b,b'}\) transferring the mass originally receiving \(b\) to new bundles \(b'\), with

\[ \sum_{b'}z_{t,b,b'}=x_{t,b},\qquad \sum_b z_{t,b,b'}=y_{t,b'}, \]

such that every moved mass weakly improves,

\[ u_t\cdot b'\ge u_t\cdot b, \]

and a positive mass strictly improves:

\[ \sum_{t,b,b'}(u_t\cdot b'-u_t\cdot b)z_{t,b,b'}>0. \]

The problem asks whether an envy-free, Pareto-efficient mass allocation exists, and on a yes-instance asks for such an \(x\).

This is a plausible mirror in a large course-allocation system, food-bank network, or standardized aid-distribution programme. There may be millions of recipients but only \(\tau\) recurring complete valuation profiles—say, degree or need categories—and \(m\) standardized item types. The item supply naturally scales with the population: \(N\) recipients have \(Nq_i\) copies of item \(i\). The regime is \(N\gg \tau,m\), not the paper’s inheritance example with only a few heirs.

The finite bridge is direct. Given a population \(N\), set

\[ n_t=N\mu_t,\qquad M_i=Nq_i. \]

Any finite allocation induces \(x_{t,b}\) by counting the fraction of type-\(t\) agents receiving each integral bundle. Conversely, any rational finitely supported \(x\) can be replicated as a finite allocation after clearing denominators. Envy-freeness is preserved exactly, and the coupling above is the mass analogue of an agent-by-agent Pareto improvement.

I would provisionally expect \(\mathrm{EEF\text{-}Allocation}_\infty\) to be Class A, parameterized by \(\tau+m\), under the same sort of utility bound as Theorem 1. The reason is that the paper’s successful machinery is already designed to ignore large multiplicities: it compresses the allocation problem into bounded-dimensional parametric integer programming. Aggregating repeated utility rows into \(\tau\) types and treating \(\mu\) and \(q\) as rational right-hand sides is a natural next step. A configuration-LP or parametric-ILP formulation might then provide a finite-support bound and an FPT algorithm.

That conclusion is a conjectural target, not a theorem inherited automatically from Theorem 1. If the necessary configuration or pricing problem remains hard even for fixed \(\tau+m\), this would instead be a valuable Class C boundary. If hardness survives only because the number of distinct utility types or item types grows, that would be a Class B phenomenon.

The weakest point is exact Pareto efficiency. The paper’s proof uses an integral allocation vector with one coordinate for each named agent. In the mirror, one type may split across many integral bundles, and the resulting dominance relation is a coupling over bundle configurations. There is no guarantee in the paper that such a coupling has polynomial or FPT-size support. Prohibiting type-splitting would make the problem easier but less faithful; allowing it makes the mirror faithful but leaves the main algorithmic question open.

That weakness does not invalidate the mirror itself. The paper studies the same underlying fairness-and-efficiency question, and its existing high-multiplicity allocation model is supporting evidence rather than a collision. The continuous question is whether envy-free Pareto efficiency remains computationally tractable when the number of agents becomes a population distribution and individual bundles remain indivisible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only viable anchor is Theorem 1, and the strongest objection is that its algorithm does not actually continuize the agent population. The theorem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(n+m\), where \(n\) is the number of named agents. Its parametric-ILP construction has \(mn\) integer allocation variables, one block for each agent. High multiplicity concerns the item copies \(m_i\), not the agents. Nothing in the proof aggregates agents with identical utility vectors.

The proposed replacement of \(n\) by the number of types \(\tau\) is therefore a new problem, not a continuous reading of the theorem. In the proposed model, the natural formulation has variables \(x_{t,b}\) for every integral bundle \(b\):

\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b}b_i x_{t,b}\le q_i. \]

The resource constraints are linear, but the two properties that matter are not. Envy-freeness is a support condition:

\[ x_{t,b}>0,\ x_{t',b'}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_t\cdot b\ge u_t\cdot b'. \]

Pareto efficiency is the absence of a second configuration together with a type-preserving improvement coupling. Thus the population continuum does not produce the LP structure that makes ChoCo’s representative problems interesting. It produces an enormous configuration problem whose difficulty comes from integral bundles and support selection, not from population multiplicity.

The finite bridge does not repair this. For rational data, clearing denominators turns \(\mu_t\) and \(q_i\) into \(N\mu_t\) repeated agents and \(Nq_i\) item copies. This is a useful equivalence, but it shows that the proposed model is an exact compressed encoding of finite EEF–Allocation instances with potentially huge \(N\). Theorem 1 then gives a bound depending on \(f(N+m)\), which is useless in the intended regime. The desired result—dependence on \(\tau+m\) rather than \(N+m\)—would require a wholly new compression theorem, not a continuization of the paper’s argument.

The obvious repairs all change the problem. Forcing every type to receive one common bundle makes the model a weighted finite ILP and excludes legitimate finite allocations in which identical agents receive different bundles. Allowing fractional bundles turns it into divisible-goods or ex-ante fair allocation, which is outcome-space continuity rather than population continuization. Keeping integral bundles while allowing type splitting preserves the intended semantics, but leaves the unsupported configuration-and-coupling problem above. Adding prices, eligibility, or budgets merely enlarges the type description and does not remove this obstruction.

This is a serious reason to reject the proponent’s claim that Theorem 1 plausibly points directly toward Class A. It does not. The paper supplies neither a finite-support theorem nor an algorithm for the mass-level Pareto relation, and its parametric-ILP machinery does not survive the replacement of named agents by a distribution.

But the universal negative case is honestly weak. A population of millions of recipients with finitely many complete valuation types, standardized indivisible bundles, and per-capita supplies is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The finite bridge is evidence that the model is mathematically coherent, not a collision; existing high-multiplicity work supports rather than defeats it. The unresolved question of whether exact envy-free Pareto efficiency is FPT in \(\tau+m\), or instead yields a continuum-specific hardness boundary, could itself be worthwhile.

So the strongest defensible criticism is that the positive case overstates what Theorem 1 contributes and has not yet shown a tractable continuous problem. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. On the programme’s stated standard, this anchor probably survives.
