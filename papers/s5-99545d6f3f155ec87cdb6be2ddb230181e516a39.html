The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) and initial superior-opinion mass \(p\), let \(\mu^{(N)}=(1-k_N/N,k_N/N)\), where \(k_N=\lceil pN\rceil\). At state \(k\), an update transfers mass \(1/N\) upward with probability \(u_N(k)=(1-k/N)\left[\alpha+(1-\alpha)(k/(N-1))^2\right]\), downward with probability \(d_N(k)=(1-\alpha)(k/N)((N-k)/(N-1))^2\), and otherwise leaves the mass unchanged. Classify the expected time to reach superior-opinion mass \(1\) as \(N\to\infty\); for \(\alpha<1/9\), determine the critical initial mass separating \(\Theta(N\log N)\) time from exponential expected time.

The model it lives in

A stochastic population represented by the two opinion masses \(\mu=(1-x,x)\), with population scale \(N\), bias \(\alpha\), and \(1/N\)-mass updates. The quantity is the expected time to reach \(x=1\), retaining the finite-population noise whose effect Theorem 1 measures.

A strict nonatomic limit removes the finite-size fluctuations behind the exact-consensus-time result; the proponent acknowledges this and keeps \(N\)-scaled stochastic mass steps.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the consensus time of a large, well-mixed opinion population. I would use only one anchor: Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It is a named runtime result for a distributed update protocol, rather than a \(P\)-versus-\(NP\) result for a separate decision problem. If “computational result” is restricted to the latter, this paper has no qualifying anchor.

The regime is a large platform or population in which every agent has the same interaction role and bias parameter \(\alpha\), and each regular update samples two agents uniformly. There are two types, distinguished by opinion: \(0\), the inferior opinion, and \(1\), the superior opinion. Their masses are \(1-p\) and \(p\). The population size \(N\) can be much larger than the support size \(\tau=2\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime because agents with the same opinion are interchangeable on the complete graph, and the paper itself reduces the process to the count of agents holding opinion \(1\).

Theorem 1 says, for complete graphs:

  • For every \(\alpha\in(1/9,1)\), the mean time to reach consensus on opinion \(1\) is \(\Theta(N\log N)\) from every initial fraction \(p\in[0,1)\).
  • For every \(\alpha\in(0,1/9)\), there is a critical fraction \(p_c(\alpha)\in(0,1)\) such that consensus time is \(\Theta(N\log N)\) when \(p\ge p_c(\alpha)\). The threshold is the unique solution in \((\bar x_\alpha,1)\) of

\[ \int_{x_\alpha}^{p_c(\alpha)} \log\!\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)x(1-x)} {\alpha+(1-\alpha)x^2}\right)\,dx=0, \]
where \(x_\alpha=\frac14\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{8\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)\) and \(\bar x_\alpha=\frac14\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{8\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)\).

  • For every \(\alpha\in(0,1/9)\) and \(p<p_c(\alpha)\), the mean consensus time is \(\Omega(\exp(\Theta(N)))\).

The theorem is proved for a slightly modified count chain; the paper’s Remark 2 says the same asymptotic bounds hold for the original 2-choices process.

I would hand a prover Continuous-Mass Consensus-Time Classification. An instance gives rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), an initial fraction \(p\in[0,1)\), and the family of mass societies
\[ \mu^{(N)}=(1-k_N/N,\;k_N/N),\qquad k_N=\lceil pN\rceil. \]
One update transfers mass \(1/N\) between the two types. At state \(x=k/N\), the exact complete-graph transition probabilities are
\[ u_N(x)=(1-x)\!\left[\alpha+(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{k}{N-1}\right)^2\right] \]
for a transfer from \(0\) to \(1\), and
\[ d_N(x)=(1-\alpha)\frac{k}{N} \left(\frac{N-k-1}{N-1}\right)^2 \]
for a transfer from \(1\) to \(0\); otherwise the mass stays put. The question is to classify the asymptotic expected hitting time of \(x=1\) as \(N\to\infty\), and, in the low-bias regime, determine the critical initial mass. The state transition is the action variable; the quantity being evaluated is expected consensus time. This mirrors the protocol the authors study, rather than inventing an intervention they did not consider.

I expect this problem to be tractable in the paper’s sense: Theorem 1 gives the asymptotic classes and an integral characterization of the threshold. It also generates a natural follow-up: give bit-complexity bounds for deciding or approximating the threshold from rational \(\alpha\) and \(p\). Other questions include the omitted boundary \(\alpha=1/9\), and whether comparable characterizations hold for dense graphs beyond complete graphs. My proposed mirror covers the paper’s complete-graph theorem, not its simulation results or the broader graph cases.

The soft spot is that the mirror must retain \(N\), since individual updates move mass in increments of \(1/N\). In a literal nonatomic limit, the stochastic fluctuations disappear; the resulting mean-field flow does not capture the rare excursions behind the exponential consensus times or necessarily reproduce \(p_c(\alpha)\). So this is a high-multiplicity, mass-based mirror of a stochastic process, not a claim that the theorem survives unchanged in a deterministic continuum. That makes the case narrower, but the two-type population model and its consensus-time question remain ones these authors would recognize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one strong anchor: Theorem 1’s expected consensus-time bounds on complete graphs. I would concede that this is an excellent high-multiplicity regime. Agents with the same opinion are interchangeable, and the paper reduces the dynamics to the count of agents holding opinion \(1\). The objection is not that the population cannot sensibly be represented by masses.

The hinge is what kind of result Theorem 1 gives. It analyzes the expected absorption time of a fixed stochastic protocol. No algorithm takes a society as input and returns an intervention, ranking, or other computed choice; the theorem gives a scaling law for the protocol’s dynamics. That makes it a result in stochastic-process analysis, not the computational complexity of a problem over societies that ChoCo is meant to chart. Calling consensus time a runtime does not by itself supply the missing input-to-output problem.

The best continuous version of the model also loses the theorem’s central observable. If \(x\) is the mass holding the superior opinion, the complete-graph fluid limit, on time scaled by \(N\), has drift
\[ \dot{x}=(1-x)\bigl[\alpha+(1-\alpha)(2x^2-x)\bigr]. \]
For low bias, this flow has a stable interior equilibrium and an unstable threshold. It captures the typical trajectory: below the unstable point, the mass settles at the interior equilibrium; above it, the mass moves toward \(1\). But it never reaches exact consensus in finite fluid time. More seriously, its threshold is not the paper’s \(p_c(\alpha)\): the latter reflects rare excursions whose contribution makes the *mean* absorption time exponential even for some starts above the fluid threshold. The continuum flow erases precisely those finite-population fluctuations.

One could ask for time to \(\varepsilon\)-consensus instead. That is a sensible question about the fluid trajectory, but it discards the last-agent bottleneck and the rare excursions behind the paper’s mean-time result. Or one could retain noise of size \(1/\sqrt{N}\), mass steps of \(1/N\), and \(N\) as a parameter, then study a large-deviation action. That could be worthwhile stochastic-dynamics work, but the continuum would be a tool for analyzing a sequence of finite populations, not a continuous society problem of the kind ChoCo targets. A more heterogeneous type model might create a computational problem, but it would be a new extension rather than a mirror anchored in this paper’s theorem.

So the negative case rests on a mismatch, not on the two-type regime being artificial: the paper has a named and interesting runtime theorem, but its object is analytic dynamics, and the natural population continuum drops the finite-size effects that make the theorem interesting. The case is not airtight if ChoCo broadens to computational methods for large-deviation dynamics; on the paper’s stated computational-social-choice remit, that would be a substantial shift in scope.
