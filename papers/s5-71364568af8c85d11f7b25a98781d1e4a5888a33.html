The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a committee size \(k\), two candidate groups \(P_1,P_2\) with each candidate in at most one group, integer bounds \(\ell_i,u_i\), and a feasible committee, let \(T\) be a finite support of complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For a size-\(k\) committee \(S\), define \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr)\). Can a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) always find a feasible \(S\) with \(W_\mu(S)\ge(1-1/e+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}\), for fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), where \(\tau=|T|\) and \(L\) is the mass encoding length?

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) is a complete ranking and carries population mass \(\mu_t\). The decision is an integral committee, represented by \(x_c\in\{0,1\}\), with \(\sum_c x_c=k\) and \(\ell_i\le\sum_{c\in P_i}x_c\le u_i\); maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(m-\min_{c:x_c=1}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(\beta\)-CC part of Theorem 19 for two candidate groups and \(\Delta=1\). It leaves the theorem’s \(\alpha\)-CC part and the paper’s other approximation, hardness, and algorithmic results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • When \(\tau\) is fixed or bounded, can the \(\beta\)-CC fairness problem achieve an approximation ratio better than \(1-1/e\) in time polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is fair β-CC committee selection over a continuous voter population. Picture a large content platform choosing \(k\) items for a shared feature slate. Millions of users have preferences represented by a modest number \(\tau\) of exact ranking profiles, and each item belongs to at most one of two categories subject to representation bounds. A type is a complete ranking of the catalogue; its mass is the fraction of users with that ranking. The number of users \(N\) can be far larger than \(\tau\). The category constraints apply to the selected items, just as in the paper.

The anchor is Theorem 19, “APX-hardness for \(\alpha\)-CC and \(\beta\)-CC,” proved in Appendix A. It states that for constrained \(\alpha\)-CC or \(\beta\)-CC multiwinner voting with \(\Delta=1\) and \(p=2\), no polynomial-time \((1-1/e+\varepsilon)\)-approximation exists for any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), unless \(P=NP\). The paper notes that unconstrained \(\alpha\)-CC is maximum \(k\)-coverage, and proves the \(\beta\)-CC hardness by a reduction from maximum coverage. That reduction represents sets by candidates and elements by voters; its two candidate groups and exact quotas force the committee to choose \(k\) set-candidates. The ranking scores then encode how many elements those sets cover. The theorem relies on prior maximum-coverage hardness, but the constrained-voting result and reduction are proved in this paper. Its contrast with the known PTAS for unconstrained \(\beta\)-CC makes the fairness-constrained result particularly useful as an anchor.

Here is the corresponding prover-ready question. Continuous \(\beta\)-CC Fair Committee Approximation: an instance consists of \(m\) candidates, a committee size \(k\), two candidate groups \(P_1,P_2\) with each candidate in at most one group, integer bounds \(\ell_i,u_i\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings. Each ranking \(t\in T\) has a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Assume a feasible committee exists. For \(S\subseteq C\), let \(r_t(S)=\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\), and define

\[ W_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(m-r_t(S)\bigr). \]

The task is to find a size-\(k\) committee satisfying \(\ell_i\le |S\cap P_i|\le u_i\) that maximizes \(W_\mu(S)\). In particular, for fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), can a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) always return a feasible \(S\) with \(W_\mu(S)\ge (1-1/e+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}\), where \(L\) is the binary input length of the masses? This is the lead mirror.

I expect the answer to be hardness transfers (Class B). Given a discrete election with \(n\) voters, assign each ranking type the mass equal to its number of voters divided by \(n\). Then \(W_\mu(S)\) is exactly the discrete \(\beta\)-CC score divided by \(n\); the feasible committees and approximation ratios are unchanged. The reduction’s support has at most \(n\) types, and these rational masses have polynomial-size encodings. Thus an algorithm above the stated ratio for the continuous problem would give one for the paper’s discrete hard instances. The mass formulation preserves the underlying choice over candidates and the voter-weighted objective, so it remains recognizably the same problem to the authors.

The mirror covers the \(\beta\)-CC part of Theorem 19 in the two-group, \(\Delta=1\) regime. It leaves the committee integral and the quotas as input; only the voter population becomes a distribution. Further questions include whether a \((1-1/e)\)-approximation can be implemented directly in time polynomial in \((m,\tau,L)\), without expanding rational masses into individual voters, and whether bounded \(\tau\) permits stronger algorithms.

The weakest point is that Theorem 19’s reduction may use a support size that grows with the source instance. It shows hardness for the continuous input class, but does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) stays small as the population grows. In the proposed platform regime, \(N\gg\tau\); resolving that low-support case would be a separate result. The mirror is still a faithful computational question, while its known hardness boundary is driven by the committee-selection combinatorics.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against Theorem 19 as the flagship mirror is that its reduction makes the population a way to encode a set system. It creates one voter per element, and the two candidate quotas force the committee to choose the set-candidates. The hard combinatorics sit in selecting those candidates under fairness constraints; the population contributes the coverage weights. In the proposed continuous version, the central change is therefore from a voter list to a weighted sum over ranking types. The reduction gives no reason to expect the result to illuminate a new tractable regime when \(\tau\) is small: its support can grow with the source instance.

That makes this a weak example of continuization opening up new optimization machinery. It asks whether the same committee problem can be solved with binary-rational weights and running time polynomial in \((m,\tau,L)\). That is a reasonable high-multiplicity question, but the paper’s hardness theorem itself does not explore what happens when many users share relatively few rankings. And I cannot count the fact that its hardness transfers as an objection: under the programme’s rules, that is a result about the mirror’s answer, not a reason the question is unworthy.

I cannot make the stronger claim that no plausible scenario works. A platform with many users in recurring preference cohorts gives ranking mass a natural interpretation; the \(\beta\)-CC score remains an aggregate over those cohorts, while the committee and candidate-group constraints remain meaningful. Nor does the score vanish in the limit: normalizing by population size preserves its objective comparisons. A richer mirror could also derive proportional-representation bounds from voter-type masses, though it would need a careful integral-committee formulation.

So the negative case is weak where it matters most. Theorem 19 is not a collision, and its reduction leaves open a potentially useful low-support complexity question. The strongest honest objection is about priority: this anchor shows that the continuous encoding preserves a known hard choice problem, but it gives little evidence that continuization will reveal a new algorithmic boundary. That is not enough to rule out a worthwhile mirror, and a thoughtful reader should probably accept this anchor.
