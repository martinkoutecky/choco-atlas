The continuous mirror question

Given firms \(F\), a finite support \(T\) of complete productivity types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational values \(a_{it}\), and a fixed assignment \(x_{it}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_i x_{it}=\mu_t\), compute the largest core factor and a supporting wage schedule and firm-profit vector. With \(B_i=\sum_t x_{it}a_{it}\), cohort wages \(z_{ht}\), and \(s_{iht}\ge 0\), the equivalent question is to minimize \(\rho\) subject to \(\sum_t x_{it}z_{it}+\sum_{h,t}x_{ht}s_{iht}\le \rho B_i\) for each \(i\), and \(s_{iht}\ge a_{it}-z_{ht}\); the factor is \(\alpha=1/\rho\).

The model it lives in

The society is a nonatomic mass of workers with complete productivity types \(t\), each assigned integrally to one firm; \(x_{ht}\) records the mass of type \(t\) currently at firm \(h\). A wage \(z_{ht}\) is paid uniformly within that type–employer cohort, and coalition deviations may recruit measurable portions of cohorts. The decision is the largest supportable core factor after compensation is redesigned, computed by the cohort LP in the mirror question.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact core-factor computation and compensation redesign for a fixed assignment, including the capacity-scaled variant supported by Theorem 14. It leaves the EF1 allocation algorithms, the \(H_m(\delta)\) frontier, and the EFX+ guarantees outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove the exact dictionary between rational type-mass instances and finite replicated instances after clearing denominators, including equality of their optimal core factors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is for the paper’s fixed-allocation stability problem. A natural regime is a large internal labor market: many workers, a small support of productivity profiles, and a fixed personnel plan whose compensation is then redesigned to make coalition deviations as difficult as possible. A type \(t\) is a complete productivity profile \((a_{it})_{i\in F}\), with any other relevant worker attributes included if the model uses them. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the workforce with that profile. For example, a large employer might have millions of workers across a handful of business units but only a few dozen recurring role profiles.

The lead mirror is the Mass-Capacity Core-Factor Problem. Its instance is a finite list of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational firm values \(a_{it}\), a fixed assignment \(x_{it}\) of each type’s mass to firms, and rational per-capita capacities \(c_i\). Here \(\sum_i x_{it}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{it}\le c_i\). Each unit of mass is assigned integrally to one firm; \(x_{it}\) records how much of type \(t\) is assigned to \(i\). A deviation may recruit up to \(c_i\) mass for each participating firm from the whole population, including workers currently assigned to that firm. The question is to find the largest core factor \(\alpha\) supportable by nonnegative wages and firm profits. A solution consists of that factor and a wage schedule and profit vector certifying all coalition constraints.

The anchor is Theorem 14, proved in the paper. For a fixed capacity matching, it defines
\[ D_i^{(r)}(\mu,z) = \sum_{j\in\mu(i)} z_j + \operatorname{Top}_{r_i}\bigl(([a_{ij}-z_j]_+)_{j\in W}\bigr), \qquad \Phi_r(\mu)=\min_{z\ge0}\max_i \frac{D_i^{(r)}(\mu,z)}{B_i}, \]
and proves that the optimal core factor is \(\alpha_r(\mu)=1/\Phi_r(\mu)\). The top-\(r_i\) deviation term ranges over the entire worker set. The paper’s Appendix B, also proved there, gives the linearization
\[ \operatorname{Top}_{r}(u)=\min_{q\ge0}\left(rq+\sum_j[u_j-q]_+\right) \]
and an LP computing the factor exactly. Thus the anchor includes both the stability identity and a computation of it.

For the mass version, index a wage cohort by its type \(t\) and current firm \(h\); write its normalized wage as \(z_{ht}\). Let \(B_i=\sum_t x_{it}a_{it}\). Define the mass recruited by firm \(i\)’s best capacity-limited deviation as
\[ \operatorname{Top}_{c_i}(g) = \max_{\substack{0\le u_{ht}\le x_{ht}\\ \sum_{h,t}u_{ht}\le c_i}} \sum_{h,t}u_{ht}g_{ht}. \]
Then the proposed continuous pressure is
\[ D_i^c(x,z) = \sum_t x_{it}z_{it} + \operatorname{Top}_{c_i}\bigl(([a_{it}-z_{ht}]_+)_{h,t}\bigr), \qquad \Phi_c(x)=\min_{z\ge0}\max_i\frac{D_i^c(x,z)}{B_i}. \]
The prover’s question is whether the fixed assignment supports \(\alpha_c(x)=1/\Phi_c(x)\), with the corresponding wages and profits. The natural expectation is Class A: the exact capacity threshold formulation gives the finite LP
\[ \begin{aligned} \min\quad &\rho\\ \text{s.t.}\quad &\sum_t x_{it}z_{it}+c_iq_i+\sum_{h,t}x_{ht}s_{iht}\le \rho B_i &&\forall i,\\ &s_{iht}\ge a_{it}-z_{ht}-q_i &&\forall i,h,t,\\ &s_{iht},z_{ht},q_i,\rho\ge0. \end{aligned} \]
It has size polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). Given an optimum, \(\alpha=1/\rho\), actual wages \(y_{ht}=\alpha z_{ht}\), and profits \(B_i-\alpha\sum_t x_{it}z_{it}\) provide the requested solution. This is a proposed continuous theorem, not a result already claimed by the paper.

A simpler, uncapacitated mirror follows from Proposition 4, proved in the paper. The proposition says that for a fixed matching \(X\), \(\Phi(X)\) is the value of the LP
\[ \min \rho \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{j\in X_i}z_j+\sum_j s_{ij}\le\rho B_i,\quad s_{ij}\ge a_{ij}-z_j,\quad s_{ij},z_j,\rho\ge0, \]
and hence the largest core factor and supporting wages are computable exactly in polynomial time. The related Theorem 1, also proved here, identifies that factor as \(1/\min_{z\ge0}\max_i R_i(X,z)\).

Its mass mirror, the Type-Mass Fixed-Assignment Core-Factor Problem, has the same type masses and fixed assignment \(x_{it}\), but no recruitment capacities. Its answer is the largest core factor and a supporting wage schedule. Its pressure is
\[ D_i(x,z)=\sum_t x_{it}z_{it}+\sum_{h,t}x_{ht}[a_{it}-z_{ht}]_+, \qquad \Phi(x)=\min_{z\ge0}\max_i \frac{D_i(x,z)}{B_i}. \]
The corresponding LP replaces the paper’s individual-worker rows by type-employer cohorts:
\[ \min \rho \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t x_{it}z_{it}+\sum_{h,t}x_{ht}s_{iht}\le\rho B_i,\quad s_{iht}\ge a_{it}-z_{ht},\quad s_{iht},z_{ht},\rho\ge0. \]
This too is expected to be Class A, with polynomial size in \(m,\tau,L\). Wages may be made uniform within a type-employer cohort: averaging wages preserves total payments and, by convexity of positive-part deviation gains, does not increase the pressure terms. The capacity version uses the same aggregation, with the top-capacity threshold accounting for partial recruitment from a cohort.

Both mirrors have a direct high-multiplicity interpretation. Clearing denominators turns rational type and assignment masses into counts in a finite replicated population, and capacities into worker counts. Conversely, the continuous input describes those repeated types by their masses instead of listing every worker. The regime has many workers relative to \(m\) and \(\tau\), while the number of distinct complete types remains moderate. Scaling capacity with population size is a co-modification of the model that makes sense here: \(c_i\) is a firm’s share of workforce capacity, and deviations can recruit fractional mass.

This is deliberately a narrow scope claim. It mirrors the fixed-allocation compensation and stability calculation in Proposition 4 and Theorem 14; it does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s EF1 allocation algorithm, EFX results, or minimax frontier. That makes the question plausible to the authors: it keeps their coalition-stability objective and fixed personnel allocation, while changing the population representation and scaling recruitment capacity.

The weakest point is that these are algorithmic anchors, not hardness results, so they do not test whether discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum. The other limitation is scope: a fixed-allocation LP is only one part of the paper’s fairness–stability programme. A natural next question is whether the allocation can also be chosen under a suitable continuum limit of EF1; for an atomless population, removing one individual has zero mass, so that limit is envy-freeness. That joint allocation problem is distinct from the fixed-assignment mirrors above and should be posed separately.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the mirror leaves the paper’s allocation problem behind. Proposition 4 and Theorem 1 compute stability after the matching is fixed; they do not choose a fair matching. Theorem 14 does the same with capacity limits. Since these are already polynomial-size LPs, a continuum version would not expose a hard combinatorial question or require the column-generation machinery that motivates much of the programme. Its main gain would be compressing repeated worker rows into type masses.

That is a real limitation, but it does not defeat either anchor. A large organization with many workers in a modest number of standardized productivity profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. For the uncapacitated result, grouping workers by profile and current employer gives an LP whose size depends on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. When counts are encoded in binary, this can avoid expanding a huge replicated workforce. The paper already proves the individual-worker LP; the type-mass formulation is a direct route to a high-multiplicity algorithm, which is precisely the bridge the programme seeks.

The capacity mirror is more vulnerable: it scales recruitment limits with the population, whereas the paper states capacities as worker counts. But per-capita capacity is a recognizable model for firms whose workforce share grows with the market, and the programme explicitly permits that kind of co-modification. Theorem 14 also gives a clear computational anchor: its Top-\(r\) deviation term becomes a fractional-mass threshold LP. I cannot rule out that the authors would regard this as their problem.

Nor does the atomless limit of EF1 help the negative case. That could complicate a mirror of the allocation results, but the proposed anchors concern fixed-match stability and do not require continuizing EF1.

So the negative argument is mainly about priority: these mirrors may be less ambitious than studying how fairness-constrained allocation changes under continuization. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Both anchors are named computational results, a plausible repeated-type regime exists, and the proposed mass models appear computationally well posed. Any stronger rejection would rely on objections the programme rules out, such as treating an easy or unchanged answer as a reason the question is not worth asking.
