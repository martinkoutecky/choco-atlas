The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a scale \(H\), per-agent payoffs \(r_t\), and type data \(b_t\), \(p_{tj}\), \(u_{tj}\), and item access sets \(R_t\), does there exist a blocking subpopulation? Equivalently, is the maximum positive over \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), continuous aggregate uses \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), and integer totals \(y_j\in\mathbb Z_+\), subject to \(x_{tj}\le H u_{tj}q_t\), \(y_j=\sum_{t:j\in R_t}x_{tj}\), \(\sum_j a_jy_j\le H\sum_t b_tq_t\), and \(y_j\le\alpha H\sum_{t:j\in R_t}u_{tj}q_t\), of \(\sum_t\sum_{j\in R_t}p_{tj}x_{tj}-H\sum_t r_tq_t\)?

The model it lives in

An atomless population has type masses \(\mu_t\); a candidate blocking subpopulation chooses masses \(q_t\le\mu_t\). Its type-level average item uses \(x_{tj}\) may be fractional, while coalition-level project totals \(y_j\) remain integral. The objective is project value minus the total payoff assigned to the selected mass, \(H\sum_t r_tq_t\), under the paper’s pooled-budget and restricted-pooling constraints.

The objection that survived

The easiest route to preserve a discrete project decision may use a blocking mass of only \(1/H\), whose project value stays finite as \(H\) grows; the proponent flags possible convexification but does not address whether this small-mass behavior fits an extensive population model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 10’s within-coalition Core-blocking separation problem. It leaves the full optimal stable coalition-structure problem and payoff-refinement results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the complexity of this mixed-integer separation problem depend on the support size \(\tau\), input bit length, scale \(H\), and pooling parameter \(\alpha\)?
  • Can hardness be established for extensive coalitions, or does it rely on blockers whose mass vanishes as \(H\) grows?
  • What approximation relation connects finite high-multiplicity instances to this mass-based separation problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s Core-blocking separation problem, carried over to a population that can divide by type mass.

The anchor is Proposition 10 (Separation), proved in this paper. It says that, for a fixed coalition \(c\) and payoff vector \(\bar v\), deciding whether there is a subcoalition \(s\subseteq c\) with \(\sum_{i\in s}\bar v_i<V(s)\) is NP-complete. The paper proves membership by observing that a blocking subset together with a feasible plan of value above its allocated payoff is a certificate; hardness comes from Knapsack, already when \(|c|=1\).

My lead mirror is Continuum Core-Blocking Separation. Take a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types and an incumbent coalition with type masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type records every feature the model uses: budget, item access, capacities, and item profits. Let \(r_t\) be the payoff per agent of type \(t\), and let \(H\) scale the normalized masses to agent-equivalent quantities. In the paper’s cooperative knapsack setting, a candidate blocking subpopulation has masses \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Let \(x_{tj}\) be its aggregate use of item \(j\) by type \(t\), and \(y_j\) its integer total use of that item. The separation problem is whether the following maximum is positive:

\[ \max\ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in R_t}p_{tj}x_{tj} -H\sum_{t\in T}r_tq_t \]

subject to \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\), \(0\le x_{tj}\le H u_{tj}q_t\), \(y_j=\sum_{t:j\in R_t}x_{tj}\), \(\sum_j a_jy_j\le H\sum_t b_tq_t\), \(y_j\le\alpha H\sum_{t:j\in R_t}u_{tj}q_t\), and \(y_j\in\mathbb Z_+\). Here \(R_t\) is the set of items available to type \(t\). A positive solution \((q,x,y)\) identifies a subpopulation and a feasible coalition plan whose value exceeds its total payoff. A maximum of zero means no subpopulation blocks. Because each positive-mass type cohort is atomless, any allowed vector \(q\) can be realized as a measurable subpopulation.

This is the paper’s own stability question: test whether some part of a formed coalition would rather deviate. The mass vector \(q\) is an active choice in the separation problem, so society is the continuous object; the mirror does not rely on fractional outcomes alone. Type prices and capacities are legitimate inputs because a type is the complete description relevant to the game. The model also keeps the paper’s integer coalition-level item use, while type-level allocations can be fractional as mass averages.

A plausible regime is a large system of municipal service districts or public project authorities operating under standardized procurement rules. Suppose many thousands of authorities draw from a small menu of exact budget, eligibility, capacity, and valuation profiles. Then \(H\) is large while the support size \(\tau=|\{t:\mu_t>0\}|\) is modest. The scenario depends on genuine standardization: if each authority has distinct project values or eligibility, then \(\tau\) may grow with \(H\), and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears.

I would provisionally expect Class C, continuum-specific hardness, because integer project-use decisions remain coupled to the freely chosen masses. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Proposition 10: its Knapsack reduction does not automatically carry over when a type’s mass can be chosen fractionally. A prover should determine whether the resulting bounded mixed-integer separation problem has a new hardness reduction or admits a structural algorithm, and how either answer depends on \(\tau\), \(H\), and the pooling parameter \(\alpha\). A related question is how finely a finite high-multiplicity instance approximates the mass solution.

The mirror covers the paper’s internal Core-separation problem and its Proposition 10 hardness result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s full optimal stable coalition-structure problem or its payoff-refinement results.

My main reservation is that the anchor’s hardness proof is a one-player Knapsack instance, whereas the mirror allows a type cohort to split into arbitrary mass and averages individual choices across that cohort. That may convexify away the very combinatorics responsible for the anchor. Retaining integer \(y_j\) gives a concrete reason to investigate the mirror, but establishing that the hard part survives is its central proof obligation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 10 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper proves a named NP-completeness result, and “there is no result to mirror” does not apply. The strongest case against is about whether its hardness belongs to a meaningful continuous-population regime.

The proposed type must include the whole data profile used by the game: budget, item access, capacities, profits, and private constraints. In the paper’s benchmark, item access is randomized and profits vary by player, so exact types proliferate with the population. A small support requires a different regime with substantial standardization. That regime is plausible for some procurement systems, but it is not what the experiments model, and the mirror’s computational value depends on finding a concrete application where many genuinely interchangeable authorities can form coalitions.

There is also a modeling fork. If agents’ indivisible choices are aggregated over an atomless type, their average use can be fractional; the knapsack integrality behind Proposition 10 may then disappear, leaving an LP separation problem. To preserve the hardness, one can keep integer coalition-level project decisions, as the paper itself allows for binary activation. But then the most direct reduction can rely on a blocker whose mass is only \(1/H\): with \(H\) agent-equivalents, a binary project can be supported by that slice, while the payoff charged to it is \(H r(1/H)=r\). This can reproduce the one-player knapsack threshold. As \(H\) grows, however, the blocker’s mass tends to zero while its project value stays finite. That is a warning that the reduction preserves an atomic, fixed-project feature inside a continuum model; it does not establish hardness for a limit with extensive, mass-continuous worth. Scaling project opportunities and values with population avoids that pathology, but also makes aggregate integer quantities increasingly dense relative to population.

That fork does not defeat the best mirror outright. A setting with indivisible shared projects and a legitimate role for very small blocking groups could make the mixed-integer mass-separation question worth studying. Nor does the fact that the result may simply remain hard count against it. But the proponent’s proposed hardness class is a hypothesis, and the exact reduction most readily available exploits the very small-mass boundary that a continuous model must explain.

I would argue against prioritizing this as a ChoCo mirror until that boundary is resolved and the type regime is grounded in an application. The case is not airtight: a well-specified public-project model could survive both objections. So the honest negative finding is limited: the proposed mirror is not yet shown to be a worthwhile *continuous-population* complexity problem, but I cannot defend the universal claim that no such scenario exists.
