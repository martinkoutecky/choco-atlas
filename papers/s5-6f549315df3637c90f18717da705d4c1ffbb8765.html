The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric relation \(w:T\times T\to\{-\infty,1\}\) with \(w_{tt}=1\), choose a finite set of groups and nonnegative masses \(x_{t,g}\) such that \(\sum_g x_{t,g}=\mu_t\) and \(x_{t,g}x_{s,g}=0\) for every enemy pair \(w_{ts}=-\infty\); maximize \(\Phi(x)=\sum_g\sum_{t,s:w_{ts}=1}x_{t,g}x_{s,g}\).

The model it lives in

A population model on twin classes of the original conflict graph: each type is a complete interaction profile, \(x_{t,g}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to group \(g\), enemy types cannot share a group, and welfare is the continuum limit of ordered pair utility.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 5’s maximum-social-welfare optimization and its inapproximability; it leaves the convergence-time, stability, dynamics, and other extension results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity in support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\) for arbitrary rational masses?
  • How does the complexity change for nonuniform interaction weights, where splitting type mass may affect the optimum?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is for Lemma 5, proved in this paper. In the uniform coloring game with \(W=\{-\infty,1\}\), it says that for every \(0<\varepsilon<1\), unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm can approximate a maximum-social-welfare partition within ratio \(n^{\varepsilon-1}\). The proof here uses a cited inapproximability result for Maximum Independent Set; its key objective is \(\phi(P)=\sum_X |X|^2=f(P)+n\), where \(f\) is the paper’s total utility.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large information-sharing platform whose users fall into repeated privacy and benefit profiles. Members of a profile have the same relationship to every other profile, including their own. For example, a platform might have millions of users but only \(\tau\) distinct interaction profiles, with many users in each. This is a restricted, block-structured social network: profiles are twin classes in the paper’s graph. Each user still joins one discrete group; only the fraction of a profile assigned to each group is continuous.

I would hand a prover this problem:

Continuous Maximum-Social-Welfare Partition. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a symmetric relation \(w:T\times T\to\{-\infty,1\}\). Here \(w_{tt}=1\); \(w_{ts}=1\) means members of those profiles are friends, while \(w_{ts}=-\infty\) means they are enemies. A solution is a finite collection of groups represented by masses \(x_{t,g}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_g x_{t,g}=\mu_t\). No group may contain positive mass of both endpoints of an enemy pair. Find a feasible assignment maximizing
\[ \Phi(x)=\sum_g\sum_{t,s:\,w_{ts}=1}x_{t,g}x_{s,g}. \]
The output is the group assignment of profile mass, not an encoding or a relaxation of the underlying question.

This is a genuine population continuization: \(\mu_t\) is a fraction of society, and \(x_{t,g}\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\) in group \(g\). It allows a type’s mass to split across groups while every individual still receives exactly one group. In this uniform case, splitting does not improve the optimum: for a type already present in several groups, its contribution varies convexly with how its mass is distributed among them, so moving all of it to one of those groups can do at least as well. Thus an optimum exists in which each type goes wholly to one group.

For equal masses \(\mu_t=1/\tau\), such an optimum is exactly a partition of the \(\tau\) profile types. Its value is \(\sum_X |X|^2/\tau^2\), the paper’s \(\phi(P)\) scaled by \(\tau^2\). Equivalently, take the paper’s conflict graph on \(\tau\) vertices and make each vertex a large cohort of identical users: as cohort size grows, normalized discrete welfare converges to this mass objective. This makes the mirror particularly credible to the authors: it is their group-formation objective on a high-multiplicity network, with fractional type-level averages and unchanged individual group membership.

I expect Class B: hardness transfers. In this mirror, the continuum optimum on equal-mass instances recovers the same type-partition objective used in Lemma 5, so the approximation barrier should carry over with \(n\) replaced by support size \(\tau\). The hard combinatorics live in the profile interaction graph, not in the number of copies of each profile.

This case covers Lemma 5 and the paper’s maximum-welfare objective; it does not claim a continuous mirror for the convergence-time or \(k\)-stability results. The main weakness is that the support \(\tau\) must grow with the hard conflict graph. Replicating each profile makes the population much larger than the support, but the hardness is still carried by the number and arrangement of distinct profiles. That leaves useful follow-up questions: what is the exact complexity in \((\tau,L)\) for arbitrary rational masses, and what changes for nonuniform interaction weights, where splitting type mass might affect the optimum?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the mirror’s useful scope is narrower than the paper’s graph model. A type must encode a user’s entire interaction profile. In a network with individualized friendships or privacy risks, that profile may be unique to each user, leaving support size \(\tau\) close to population size. Repeated profile labels alone do not give the block structure this mirror needs. And the hardness behind Lemma 5 uses an unrestricted conflict graph on \(\tau\) types, so it says little about a platform regime with millions of users and few types.

That limits the motivating reach, but it does not defeat the mirror. Role based groups with profile determined interactions are a coherent high multiplicity setting. With \(K\) identical users per type, normalized discrete welfare converges to the proposed mass objective; equal masses recover the paper’s objective on the type graph up to scaling. The mass assignment is well defined, and the fractional type level output is a natural population quantity.

Lemma 5 is a named approximation hardness result about a computational problem. The paper has no continuous treatment of that problem, and the proposed mirror is a population continuization. Hardness in \(\tau\) is a meaningful result under ChoCo’s stated scope, even if it shows that replication alone does not make the problem tractable.

So the practical objection is real, while the universal negative case is weak: this anchor survives. I would expect the reader to green the paper on it.
