What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Theorems 3.1, 4.1, 5.1, and 6.1 state mathematical bounds, while the false-name conjecture and experiments provide no named computational guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible continuous-population regime is a weighted voting body with many interchangeable unit-weight members and a few weight classes of larger voters. With \(N\) large and the number of weight types \(\tau\) small, that is a natural high-multiplicity setting. The paper itself points to oceanic games with finitely many significant players and a continuum of small ones, though it distinguishes its own results as non-asymptotic.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor the required mirror. Theorem 3.1 proves \(\bar R_\phi=2\); Theorem 4.1 proves the absolute and normalized Banzhaf ratios are unbounded; Theorem 5.1 gives \(\bar R_\rho\leq 3\); and Theorem 6.1 bounds aggregate post-split power for the Shapley–Shubik and Deegan–Packel indices. These are mathematical bounds, not complexity classifications or algorithmic results. The false-name conjecture and its experiments do not supply one either.

So I can’t honestly give a prover-ready continuous problem per eligible anchor: there are none. A continuum version of the power-versus-proportion analysis is a plausible research direction, but presenting it as a computational mirror of this paper’s results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor. Theorem 3.1 bounds the Shapley–Shubik ratio by \(2\); Theorem 4.1 shows the Banzhaf ratio is unbounded; Theorem 5.1 bounds the Deegan–Packel ratio by \(3\); and Theorem 6.1 bounds aggregate power after splitting. Each is an inequality about power indices, with no algorithm or complexity claim. The false-name conjecture and exhaustive experiments add no such result. The NP-hardness and PP-completeness claims in the introduction are attributed to earlier papers.

A plausible regime does exist: finitely many large weighted players alongside a high-multiplicity population of unit-weight members. The paper itself cites oceanic games, so this regime is sensible. Its natural continuous questions would ask whether the power-to-proportion bounds persist for a continuum population. Those are analytic questions about limiting power indices. The indices here are defined through finite random permutations, subsets, or minimal winning coalitions; taking limits may be interesting, but it does not supply a computational result for this paper to mirror.

The strongest computational rescue would be to ask how to compute power indices or find an optimal false-name split in a continuous model. The paper establishes no computational result about either task. Theorem 6.1 gives a bound for every strategy profile without computing a best response or equilibrium. Replacing integer partitions with continuous split allocations would pose a new optimization problem, not extend a result the paper proves.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s anchored-mirror standard: there is no named computational result here to continuize. The universal claim is weaker. The high-multiplicity scenario is plausible, and a separate computational study of continuous power-index evaluation could be worthwhile; the paper simply does not provide the anchor for calling it a mirror.
