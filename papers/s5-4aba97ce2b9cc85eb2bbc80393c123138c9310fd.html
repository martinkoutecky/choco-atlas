The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t\), community label \(c(t)\), and original assignment marginals \(p_{ts}\), along with two school capacities \(q_s\) summing to \(1\), determine the complexity of finding a lottery over feasible mass assignments \(x_{ts}\). Each realization must satisfy \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}=q_s\), and the lottery must satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[x_{ts}]=\mu_t p_{ts}\). Its objective is to maximize \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{c,s}X_{cs}^{2}]\), where \(X_{cs}=\sum_{t:c(t)=c}x_{ts}\).

The model it lives in

Use a finite support of types that completely specify community, reported ranking, priority, and any other assignment-relevant attributes. Each type carries rational mass \(\mu_t\). In each realization, measurable subsets of a type’s mass may go to different schools, while each individual receives one school; the lottery preserves each type’s original assignment marginals and maximizes expected same-community pair mass, \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{c,s}X_{cs}^{2}]\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s two-school correlated-lottery optimization hardness. It leaves Theorem 1’s mechanism characterization, Theorem 2’s large-market formulas, the comparative statics, and the empirical heuristic results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an approximation-preserving reduction establish the paper’s no-FPTAS conclusion for rational-mass instances?
  • What are the complexity and approximation guarantees when the support size \(\tau\) is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the finite-support, high-multiplicity version of their two-school correlated-lottery problem. A type is a neighborhood, reported ranking, and priority class; its mass is the fraction of students of that type. In a realistic regime, a district might have hundreds or thousands of such types but many more students, with large cohorts sharing each type.

For two schools, let \(x_{ts}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\) in one realized assignment. Each realization must assign all mass and respect capacities: \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}=q_s\). Given original per-student assignment probabilities \(p_{ts}\), a lottery over these realizations must satisfy \(\mathbb E[x_{ts}]=\mu_t p_{ts}\). The question is to find a lottery maximizing

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{c}\sum_{s=1}^{2} X_{cs}^{2}\right], \qquad X_{cs}=\sum_{t:\,c(t)=c}x_{ts}. \]

This is the continuum measure of same-community pairs assigned together. A type’s mass may be split between schools within a realization, while each individual still receives one school; atomlessness lets us implement those masses by assigning measurable subsets of a cohort. The requested solution is a maximum-value lottery over feasible assignments, preserving each type’s original marginal probabilities.

My anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper: “Unless P=NP, even with 2 schools, there exists no polynomial time algorithm (or FPTAS) to compute the maximum achievable cohesion by correlated lottery implementation.” Its underlying problem is the finite-student optimization: choose a lottery over capacity-feasible assignments, preserve every student’s school-assignment probabilities, and maximize expected same-community peers.

For the continuous mirror, I expect exact optimization to remain NP-hard. A balanced PARTITION instance gives a direct reduction. For integers \(a_c\) summing to \(2B\), use one type per community, mass \(w_c=a_c/(2B)\), two school capacities \(1/2\), and marginal probability \(1/2\) for every student to attend either school. The objective is at most \(\sum_c w_c^2\), with equality only when each community’s mass goes entirely to one school in every realization. Capacity then requires a subset of communities of total mass \(1/2\), exactly a partition. Conversely, given such a partition, randomly swap its two sides to preserve every individual’s marginal probability and attain the bound. Thus an exact optimizer decides PARTITION, even with a continuum of students inside each type and only two schools.

This is closest to hardness transferring: the packing obstruction survives in community masses and capacity constraints. It does not show that hardness always survives high multiplicity; here the combinatorics remain in the finite support and its rational masses. My reduction proves exact NP-hardness only. I would not claim the Proposition 1 no-FPTAS result transfers without a stronger approximation-preserving reduction. Further questions include whether an FPTAS exists, whether fixed \(\tau\) is tractable, and what approximation guarantees are possible for larger numbers of schools.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves their assignment marginals, their correlated-lottery decision, and their cohesion objective. Their Theorem 2 already solves a related analytic problem for a continuum of communities under homogeneous cutoffs. The mirror here asks for computational complexity with finite-support rational masses, rather than repeating that formula. It covers Proposition 1 only; it does not mirror the mechanism characterization, comparative statics, or Boston experiments.

My weakest point is that the paper itself says its packing hardness persists with infinitely many students per community when the number of communities is finite. That makes this mirror especially plausible, but also means its central hardness claim is already foreshadowed—perhaps effectively asserted—in the paper. The continuous-population move is still real: it replaces individual counts with masses and permits fractional type-level assignments. In this instance, maximizing cohesion pushes those fractions back to whole-community allocations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat Proposition 1’s anchor. The proposed mirror is well posed: use types that include community, preferences, and priority, so mass represents students who are interchangeable for the assignment problem. The balanced-partition reduction appears sound. Cohesion reaches its upper bound only when each community goes wholly to one school in each realization; capacity then requires an exact half-mass partition. Randomly swapping the two sides preserves every type’s assignment marginals.

The paper’s Theorem 2 does not settle this version. It gives an analytic formula for a continuum of communities under homogeneous cutoffs; it does not give a complexity classification for finitely many rational-mass cohorts. The paper itself points to packing hardness when there are finitely many communities but infinitely many students within each. That makes the proposed mirror close to an obstruction the authors already recognize, but under the programme’s criteria, high multiplicity supports the model’s plausibility; it does not invalidate the question.

The strongest remaining criticism is narrower: the reduction establishes exact NP-hardness, not the paper’s stronger no-FPTAS claim, and it leaves approximation and parameterized complexity open. That limits the proponent’s evidence, but it does not defeat the exact-complexity mirror. I find no honest basis here for the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
