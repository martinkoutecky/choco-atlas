The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), buyer types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), integer values \(v_{ti}\), demand limits \(d_t\), and nonnegative rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\), compute a component-wise least Walrasian price vector \(p\) and a supporting allocation. Let \(Y_t=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m:\sum_i y_i\le d_t,\ y_i=0\text{ whenever }q_i=0\}\), and let \(\lambda_{t,y}\ge0\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(y\in Y_t\). Require \(\sum_{y\in Y_t}\lambda_{t,y}=\mu_t\), positive-mass bundles to maximize \(\sum_i(v_{ti}-p_i)y_i\) over \(Y_t\), aggregate use of each good to be at most \(q_i\), total sales to equal \(\min\{\sum_i q_i,\sum_t\mu_t d_t\}\), and each positive-priced good to be fully sold.

The model it lives in

Use the configuration welfare LP with variables \(\lambda_{t,y}\), type-mass constraints, and per-capita supply constraints. Its dual has one utility variable per type and one price per good; optimize over the optimal dual face to select the component-wise least Walrasian prices. Pricing reduces to finding each type’s best bundle under its demand cap.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s buyer-optimal price and supporting-allocation result. It does not claim to continuize Theorem 16’s comparative statics or the discussion of VCG prices.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove that the component-wise least price exists for this rational-mass model and that minimizing the sum of prices over the optimal dual face selects it.
  • Give bit-polynomial bounds in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length, including recovery of a sparse supporting allocation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that the Price-Raising Algorithm returns the component-wise minimum competitive price vector; the Allocation Algorithm returns a stable allocation that sells as much as possible and sells every item with positive price. Therefore those prices are the buyer-optimal Walrasian prices. The theorem is an algorithmic result, not a polynomial-time bound: the paper’s price-increment auction has a pseudo-polynomial iteration bound in the price values. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a hardness mirror on.

My lead is the Continuous Buyer-Optimal Walrasian Equilibrium problem. Let the finite support \(T\) contain \(\tau\) buyer types. Type \(t\) has per-unit values \(v_{ti}\) for the \(m\) goods and an integral demand limit \(d_t\); its mass is a binary rational \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Give good \(i\) a per-capita supply \(q_i\), also a nonnegative rational. The type description is complete for the model: buyers of type \(t\) share the same values and demand limit.

A solution is a nonnegative per-unit price vector \(p\) and an allocation of type mass to integral bundles. Write \(\lambda_{t,y}\) for the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(y\), where \(y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\) and \(\sum_i y_i\le d_t\). Require \(\sum_y\lambda_{t,y}=\mu_t\); any bundle receiving positive mass must maximize \(\sum_i(v_{ti}-p_i)y_i\) among that type’s feasible bundles; and aggregate use of each good must not exceed \(q_i\). Require sales to total \(\min\{\sum_i q_i,\sum_t\mu_t d_t\}\), with every positive-priced good fully sold. Among supporting prices, return the component-wise least price vector (equivalently, the price vector minimizing \(\sum_i p_i\) when the least vector exists). Thus each buyer still receives an integral bundle: only the *mass of buyers* assigned to different bundles is fractional.

A convincing regime is a very large wholesale market serving many small retailers or facilities buying from a modest catalogue of standardized goods. Buyers with the same procurement values and demand cap form a type, so the number of agents can be large while \(\tau\) remains small. If a finite market has \(N\mu_t\) buyers of each type and \(Nq_i\) units of each good, for a suitable common multiple \(N\), it is exactly a high-multiplicity instance whose normalized counts are these masses and per-capita supplies. Scaling supply with population is part of the mirror: otherwise the market’s resource availability changes as the population grows. The paper’s objects remain indivisible, and the allocation variable records how much mass receives each integral bundle; it is not a lottery over outcomes for a single buyer.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem: same truncated-additive preferences, demand caps, prices per good, stable individual choices, market clearing, and buyer-optimal prices. The paper already places price computation in an optimization setting: its conclusion notes that buyer-optimal prices can be computed directly by LP. For the continuous mirror, the configuration LP makes the population aggregation explicit. Its welfare form has variables \(\lambda_{t,y}\), type-mass constraints, and per-capita supply constraints. Its dual has one utility variable \(\alpha_t\) per type and one price \(p_i\) per good, with constraints
\[ \alpha_t+\sum_i p_i y_i\ \ge\ \sum_i v_{ti}y_i \]
for every feasible bundle \(y\). After finding the optimal dual face, minimize \(\sum_i p_i\) over that face to select the buyer-optimal price vector. Although there are exponentially many bundles, separation is simple for truncated-additive values: for each type, maximize net value subject to its demand cap by choosing the best positive net-value units. The GLS ellipsoid/separation machinery can therefore handle the price computation in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length; a sparse supporting allocation can be recovered from the primal. I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable. That argument uses the continuous optimization tools ChoCo is meant to bring into reach, rather than claiming that Theorem 4 itself proves a polynomial-time bound.

The scope here is Theorem 4’s buyer-optimal price and supporting-allocation result. I would not stretch the mirror to cover the paper’s comparative-statics theorem or its discussion of VCG prices. The chief weakness is that the paper proves its auction result in a finite-buyer representation and its iterative algorithm remains pseudo-polynomial; it does not prove the continuous LP-and-separation algorithm above. The continuous question is still faithful to the underlying market problem, but the polynomial-time conclusion is a reasoned extension, not a result established by these authors. Further questions are whether the aggregated flow auction can achieve a bit-polynomial or strongly polynomial bound, and how its continuous solution rounds to finite high-multiplicity markets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the only strong anchor, and the best case against it is that its continuous version may have very little new algorithmic content. The paper itself says buyer-optimal prices can be computed directly by LP. The proposed mirror’s configuration LP is the welfare LP with buyers grouped by type and quantities normalized; its separation problem is simply each type’s demand query. Standard LP and oracle methods already compute Walrasian prices for gross-substitutes markets.[The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14262), [computing Walrasian equilibria](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-018-1334-9) This makes the mirror look like a clean application of familiar machinery, rather than a new complexity landscape. The paper’s auction, meanwhile, has a pseudo-polynomial iteration bound.

That objection is limited. The proposed market is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many buyers can share complete preference and demand types, while per-capita supply scales with population. Splitting type mass among integral bundles is a coherent way to represent an atomless population; neither individual identity nor degeneracy rules it out. And the paper’s LP is for finite buyers, so it does not itself establish the claimed bit-polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\). Showing that the type-compressed continuous problem has that bound would be a valid computational result.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor under ChoCo’s stated standard. “The answer may follow from familiar LP machinery” is a weak worth-the-time objection, not a fundamental failure of the mirror; the programme explicitly allows a well-posed tractable mirror, even when its answer is unsurprising. The negative case against this paper is therefore weak.
