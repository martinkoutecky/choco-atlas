What the mirror covers

The candidate restates Theorem 1’s dual-balance sufficient condition; it does not cover the paper’s other sufficient condition or define a general no-whip decision problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

On a strict reading of the anchor requirement, this paper has no named computational result: it proves no complexity classification or algorithmic result. Lemma 1 is an identity, Lemma 2 is a sufficient condition, and Theorem 1 is another sufficient condition. None says that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or similar. So the paper cannot support a full ChoCo case in the requested sense.

The strongest positive opening is that the paper itself treats criteria as voters. A plausible regime would have many equally weighted assessment units—such as repeated competency checks or standardized evaluation panels—ranking the same fixed set of employees, with many units sharing one of a relatively small number of weak rankings. A type is one such weak ranking; its mass is the fraction of assessment units with that ranking. This is a population of criteria or evaluators, not of employees, and the scenario is somewhat constructed. Still, it follows the paper’s own formal mapping rather than changing the problem.

The nearest named result is Theorem 1 (A sufficient condition), proved in this paper. It says that if each alternative’s mean preference-possibility-map row is symmetric across ranking positions—\(p_{i,1}=p_{i,m}\), \(p_{i,2}=p_{i,m-1}\), and so on—then none of the alternatives will be whipped by Rank and Yank. Equivalently, the entries of the sum matrix satisfy the same symmetry. The theorem is explicitly sufficient, not necessary.

A narrow continuous analogue would be Dual-Balance No-Whip Certification. An instance gives \(m\) alternatives and a finite support of weak-ranking types \(t\), each with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to one. For each type, ties occupy their common consecutive positions uniformly, as in the paper’s PPM definition. Form \(P_{i,k}=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathrm{PPM}(t)_{i,k}\). Decide whether \(P_{i,k}=P_{i,m+1-k}\) for every alternative \(i\) and position \(k\). A “yes” certifies the no-whip guarantee asserted by Theorem 1; a “no” means only that this sufficient condition does not certify it. There is no action variable or optimization objective in the paper’s result. This check is tractable by direct computation in \(m,\tau,L\), but that is a derived algorithm for checking the theorem’s hypothesis, not a computational result proved by the authors.

The weakest point is also decisive: this mirrors the theorem’s balance condition, not a computational problem classified by the paper, and it does not decide the general no-whip question. Moreover, rational masses can be represented by a finite multiset of criteria, so continuization mostly compresses repeated criteria; it does not reveal a new complexity boundary. The most honest positive claim is therefore modest: Theorem 1 has a natural continuous-population restatement, but this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor for a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not count Theorem 1 as a computational anchor. It proves a sufficient mathematical condition; the paper gives no algorithmic or complexity result. The proposed “Dual-Balance No-Whip Certification” asks whether that condition holds, but that decision problem is supplied by the analyst, not studied by the authors.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many evaluators rank the same alternatives, with repeated weak rankings. That fits the paper’s own treatment of criteria as voters, so I would not reject the mirror on population grounds. For a type distribution \(\mu\), its mean PPM is simply \(P_{ik}=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathrm{PPM}(t)_{ik}\), and the theorem’s condition becomes \(P_{ik}=P_{i,m+1-k}\). This is a faithful continuous restatement, but it remains a test of the theorem’s hypothesis—not a computational result from the paper. The PPM’s use of probabilities for tied positions also does not, by itself, make the population continuous.

A stronger candidate would ask whether a weighted society produces a “no-whip” outcome generally, rather than whether it satisfies this one sufficient condition. But the paper never gives a precise decision predicate for that question: it lists several outcome patterns and says they lead to no whipping, without formally defining the full set of acceptable outcomes or how the forced ranking acts on them. One could formalize a particular graph-pattern test, or add an optimization objective such as the least mass change needed to produce a no-whip outcome. Those may be worthwhile new questions, but they are not mirrors of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s fit for ChoCo: even in a sensible evaluator-population regime, its named theorem offers only a direct continuous condition-check. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could ever be built from the topic. The paper’s lack of a computational result is decisive against treating its theorem as an anchor; the broader claim that no future formulation could be worthwhile is less secure.
