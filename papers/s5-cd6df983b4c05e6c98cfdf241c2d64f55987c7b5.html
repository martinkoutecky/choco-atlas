The continuous mirror question

Given school feature vectors \(f_j\), supply shares \(q_j\) summing to \(1\), and preference types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\) and vectors \(u_t\), consider feasible allocations \(p_{tj}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_j p_{tj}=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{tj}=q_j\). Define type utility \(U_t(p)=\sum_j p_{tj}(u_t\cdot f_j)\), random-assignment baseline \(o_t=\sum_j q_j(u_t\cdot f_j)\), and weighted geometric welfare \(G(p)=\prod_t (U_t(p)-o_t)^{\mu_t}\) when all gains are positive. Project types and schools onto the leading right singular vector of the school feature matrix and match their masses in sorted order, splitting mass at boundaries as needed. Under the paper’s low-dimensionality condition, does this continuous sorting rule achieve \(G(p)\ge (1-O(\sigma_2^2/\sigma_1^2))\max_{p'}G(p')\) over feasible allocations?

The model it lives in

A continuum of students represented by a finite support of complete preference types and masses, matched to fixed schools with supply shares; each type receives a distribution over schools, and the objective is weighted geometric welfare of gains over random assignment.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Theorem 1 (Efficiency), proved in this paper, is the one named algorithmic result worth anchoring on. It claims that, with additive utilities \(U_{ij}=u_i\cdot f_j\) and low effective dimensionality \(\sigma_1\gg\sigma_2\), Algorithm 1’s allocation \(P^*\) has
\[ \mathrm{NSW}(P^*\mid U)\ge \left(1-O\!\left(\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2}\right)\right) \max_{P\in\mathcal P}\mathrm{NSW}(P\mid U). \]
In other words, the paper claims that projecting preferences and objects onto the leading singular direction, then sorting and matching, approximately maximizes Nash social welfare. This is an algorithmic welfare guarantee, not a numbered complexity-class theorem. The paper has no numbered result establishing NP-hardness, membership in P, or a parameterized complexity classification; the HZ hardness results it discusses are cited background, not its own numbered anchors.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large school district where families report weights from a standardized menu over a small set of school features. For example, there might be tens of thousands of students, a few hundred schools, and hundreds or a few thousand distinct complete preference types. Students of a type have the same feature weights and any other parameters the market uses; schools have fixed feature vectors, and their capacity shares remain roughly stable as the cohort grows. The paper itself motivates school choice and already models capacities and feature-based cardinal utilities, so this population regime is close to its stated problem.

Here is the corresponding continuous problem. Let \(f_j\in\mathbb Q^X\) be the feature vector of school \(j\), with supply share \(q_j\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j q_j=1\). The population has support \(T\) of \(\tau\) preference types, with type \(t\)’s vector \(u_t\in\mathbb Q^X\) and mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A solution is a conditional allocation \(p_{tj}\ge0\): the share of type \(t\) assigned to school \(j\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_j p_{tj}=1\quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_t\mu_t p_{tj}=q_j\quad(j=1,\ldots,J). \]
Each type’s expected utility is \(U_t(p)=\sum_jp_{tj}(u_t\cdot f_j)\). Its random-assignment baseline is \(o_t=\sum_jq_j(u_t\cdot f_j)\), so its gain is \(g_t(p)=U_t(p)-o_t\).

Call the problem Continuous Feature-NSW Allocation: given these binary-rational data and \(\varepsilon>0\), return a feasible \(p\) maximizing
\[ \Phi(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log g_t(p), \]
to additive \(\varepsilon\), or report that no feasible allocation gives every type a strictly positive gain. This is the continuous analogue of the paper’s Nash social welfare objective: if a finite cohort has \(n_t\) agents of each type, then \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and \(\Phi\) is the log of the finite product of gains divided by \(n\). In the continuum, \(p\) is an allocation distribution for each type; an atomless mass of agents can be partitioned accordingly, so this does not mean giving a fractional school to an individual.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). The feasible set is a transportation polytope, each \(g_t\) is affine, and maximizing the weighted sum of log gains is a concave maximization problem over that polytope. Strictly positive gains can be checked by a linear program; when they are feasible, convex-optimization methods can seek an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal allocation. This is the underlying allocation problem Theorem 1 purports to approximate. A separate question is whether the paper’s specific leading-singular-vector sorting rule achieves its claimed ratio in the continuous model.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s welfare-allocation result only. Theorem 2’s distributional-truthfulness claim, Theorem 3’s symmetry claim, and Theorem 4’s robust-optimization connection are not needed to make this case.

The soft spot is significant: the theorem’s printed proof does not establish its claimed welfare bound as written. It moves from sorting to maximizing total projected utility, then treats the resulting allocation as though it maximized projected NSW; that implication is not shown. The bounds also do not adequately control heterogeneous agents’ gains over their baselines. And this fractional-allocation NSW problem appears tractable already, so the mirror does not demonstrate that a discrete hardness barrier disappears. The positive case is that the high-multiplicity population model is a natural object for the authors’ market, and it yields a precise allocation problem they should recognize; the paper’s theorem motivates asking about it, but does not settle the continuous guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only defensible attack is on the kind of result Theorem 1 offers. It gives a welfare guarantee for one algorithm; the paper’s explicit running-time claim is an unnumbered SVD-and-sorting bound, not a complexity classification of a computational problem. The proponent’s Continuous Feature-NSW problem—optimize over all type-level allocations—is therefore a plausible allocation problem, but it is not a direct mirror of Theorem 1’s guarantee for the paper’s particular rule.

That distinction does not supply a deeper objection. A natural high-multiplicity regime exists: a large school or course market where agents report from a standardized menu of feature-preference types and capacities scale with the cohort. Each type can be complete in the programme’s sense, and its mass and each school’s supply share give a coherent continuous instance. The objective \(\sum_t \mu_t\log g_t\) is the normalized log-NSW of finite replications of those types. Probabilistic assignments and fixed schools do not prevent the population from being the continuous object.

The closest mirror of the theorem would keep the SVD sorting rule, represent each preference type by its mass, and ask whether its welfare guarantee survives in that market. That is well posed and close to the authors’ stated problem. I can argue that it is a narrow extension of an already specified algorithm, rather than a new complexity landscape; I cannot show that the question is not worthwhile. HZ’s probabilistic allocations are not a continuous-population collision, and the paper’s other numbered claims do not turn this into one.

So the negative case against this anchor is weak. Theorem 1 is not a complexity-class result, which makes it a less direct ChoCo target, but the high-multiplicity mirror is sensible and recognizable. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
