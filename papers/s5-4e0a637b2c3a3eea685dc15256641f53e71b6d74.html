The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types, where each type \(t\) has a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(m\) house categories and a per-capita endowment vector \(e_t\) with \(\sum_j e_{tj}=1\), compute a common per-capita allocation \(p_t\) for each type. Require \(\sum_j p_{tj}\le 1\), aggregate use \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{tj}\le w_j=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tj}\), rank-prefix individual rationality for every type, and no justified envy between any pair of types. The allocation must be ordinally efficient: no feasible reassignment weakly improves every type by first-order stochastic dominance and strictly improves a positive-mass type.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with finitely many complete types, each defined by a shared strict ranking and per-capita endowment. Decision variables are the type-level allocations \(p_t\); category supplies are \(w_j=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tj}\), and a type-and-rank max-flow network is the candidate computational model.

The objection that survived

For exact duplicate types, aggregating the CC network may amount to a narrow high-multiplicity compression question rather than a broad new complexity landscape.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the CC allocation guarantees of individual rationality, ordinal efficiency, and no justified envy, with the paper’s max-flow computation as the algorithmic basis. It leaves the strategyproofness impossibilities and the conflict with equal-endowment no envy outside the proposed allocation question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does scaling the finite CC network by type masses produce exactly the finite CC allocation whenever \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and under what assumptions?
  • Can the type-and-rank construction compute an allocation satisfying all three guarantees in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a type-aggregated version of the paper’s Controlled-Consuming allocation problem. One qualification about the anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable. The named results are guarantees about the CC mechanism; the polynomial-time claim appears in the contributions and in §3.5. I would not present the propositions themselves as a numbered “in P” theorem.

The named anchor is the CC result package proved in §3.4: Proposition 1 says the CC mechanism’s allocation is individually rational; Proposition 3 says it is ordinally efficient; and Proposition 4 says it satisfies no justified envy. Together, for the paper’s strict-preference, doubly stochastic-endowment setting, they establish that CC computes an allocation with all three properties. The paper’s §3.5 analysis says the algorithm can be implemented using \(O(n^3)\) maximum-flow computations. My mirror is of that allocation problem, not of the fractional-allocation representation, which the paper already uses.

Here is the continuous problem. There are \(m\) house categories and a unit-mass, atomless population. The input lists \(\tau\) positive-mass types. Each type \(t\) consists of a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of the \(m\) categories and a complete per-capita endowment vector \(e_t\), with \(e_{tj}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j e_{tj}=1\). Its population share is a binary rational \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Category \(j\)’s per-capita supply is \(w_j=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tj}\).

Find a vector \(p_t\) for each type: every member of type \(t\) receives that allocation, \(\sum_j p_{tj}\le 1\), and total use of each category satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{tj}\le w_j\). Require individual rationality in the paper’s sense: for every rank \(k\), the amount type \(t\) receives among its top \(k\) categories is at least its endowment in those categories. Require ordinal efficiency: there is no feasible reassignment \(q_t\) that weakly improves every type by first-order stochastic dominance and strictly improves a positive-mass set. Require no justified envy: for no pair \(t,u\) does \(p_t\) fail to dominate \(p_u\) under \(t\)’s ranking while also being individually rational for \(u\) under \(u\)’s ranking. A solution is the list of allocations \(p_t\); there is no objective to minimize.

This models a large housing program with a fixed menu of room or time-share categories. Many applicants have the same complete ranking and the same initial rights, so the number of applicants can grow while \(\tau\) remains modest. The paper itself motivates fractional rights through time-sharing and lottery assignments, and its model permits arbitrary quantities of houses. The mirror keeps those features and makes the population the continuous object: each type’s mass is allocated, and its per-capita supply is scaled with population size.

I expect this problem to be tractable, in polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s CC method already works with max-flow networks, and repeated agents of one complete type can be represented by aggregate capacities; a type-and-rank network has size governed by \(\tau\) and \(m\), rather than the head count. That is a reasoned prediction for the mirror, not a theorem proved in this paper. The main follow-up is to establish the finite-to-continuous dictionary: when \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), does the aggregated CC computation exactly match the relevant finite allocation, and under what assumptions? One could then ask whether the paper’s strategyproofness impossibilities, such as Theorem 3, persist for an atomless population.

The weakest point is the regime. Applicants with preferences over individually distinct addresses and individualized ownership claims may have \(\tau\) close to the population size, leaving little high-multiplicity gain. This mirror is strongest where the categories are genuinely interchangeable or time-shared and many people really do have the same rankings and rights. The paper’s own fractional-rights interpretation makes that a credible instance regime, but it does not justify treating every housing allocation market as a continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is about the anchor, not the population model. Propositions 1, 3, and 4 establish that CC’s output is individually rational, ordinally efficient, and free of justified envy. They do not state a complexity result. The polynomial running-time claim appears in the surrounding discussion and §3.5, where the algorithm is bounded by \(O(n^3)\) maximum-flow computations. So this is a real algorithmic contribution, but the proposed mirror is not a continuization of a named complexity theorem.

That point does not defeat the best version of the mirror. In a large time-sharing or housing program, many participants could share the same complete ranking and fractional rights. Their type would include both, and supply would scale with type mass. The paper explicitly allows time-sharing and arbitrary quantities of houses, so this is a plausible co-modification. Nor does type pooling discard information the problem needs: with identical preferences and endowments, individual rationality and no justified envy together force identical allocations. A type-level allocation is therefore a natural object.

The best case against is that this mirror may add little new algorithmic structure. For exact duplicate types, the finite CC network appears amenable to aggregation: identical rank nodes can be represented by weighted capacities. The additional question is then whether this compression runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), rather than in the population size. That may be worthwhile to formalize, but it is a narrow high-multiplicity encoding question, not an obvious new complexity landscape. Calling it immediate would assume the answer; calling it a major new frontier would overstate what the paper suggests.

The strategyproofness impossibilities do not strengthen the case against the CC mirror. An individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so a deviation that leaves the aggregate distribution unchanged needs a different interpretation. A positive-mass group deviation could be studied, but that changes the incentive question.

So I would argue for lower priority, not for ruling out every worthwhile mirror. The proponent’s time-sharing scenario survives the main objections, and I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no good continuous mirror exists.
