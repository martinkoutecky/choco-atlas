The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^2\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_p>0\) summing to \(1\), and either the \(\ell_1\) or \(\ell_\infty\) norm, decide whether there is a set \(S\subseteq P\) with \(|S|\leq 3\) such that \(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^2}\sum_{p:\,\|p-x\|<\|p-s\|\ \forall s\in S}\mu_p\leq 1/2\), and return such an \(S\) when one exists.

The model it lives in

Each type is a planar location \(p\in P\) with mass \(\mu_p\), and is also eligible as a peer candidate. The defender selects at most three support locations; a challenger chooses any point \(x\in\mathbb{R}^2\); the captured population is the total mass of types that prefer \(x\) to every selected location.

The objection that survived

The paper proves the guarantee for unit-mass point sets and gives no running-time bound, so the arbitrary rational-mass version still needs a proof and complexity analysis.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the strong peer-selection guarantee under \(\ell_1\) and \(\ell_\infty\), and its strong rectangular-net basis. It leaves aside the \(\ell_2\) metric-election bound, the single-candidate factors, the norm-independent Voronoi bound, and the equidistant-voter proposition.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the three-point half-mass guarantee extend from unweighted point sets to arbitrary binary-rational masses?
  • If it does, can a qualifying coalition be found in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. Its named results give geometric bounds on committee size and challenger share, equalities between game variants, and \(\varepsilon\)-net guarantees. None asserts a complexity status such as polynomial-time solvability, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. The paper therefore has no named computational result in the sense required here.

The strongest positive candidate is a weighted strong peer-selection problem. An instance is a finite set of planar locations \(P\subset\mathbb{Q}^2\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_p\) summing to \(1\), and a norm \(\ell_1\) or \(\ell_\infty\). Choose a defending set \(S\subseteq P\), \(|S|\le k\), to minimize
\[ \Phi_\mu(S)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^2} \sum_{\substack{p\in P:\\ \|p-x\|<\|p-s\|\ \text{for every }s\in S}}\mu_p. \]
Here \(x\) is a freely placed challenger, and \(\Phi_\mu(S)\) is the fraction of the population that prefers it to every defender. A concrete regime is a large electorate whose members share locations, and therefore preferences, across a relatively small number \(\tau=|P|\) of spatial types.

The closest result is the paper’s theorem that three voters suffice for strong peer selection on the plane under \(\ell_1\) and \(\ell_\infty\): in each norm, a set of at most three voters prevents every free challenger from attracting more than half the voters. The authors prove this here, using their strong rectangular net bound \(\varsigma_3^{\mathcal R}\le 1/2\). It is a good anchor for the *mathematical problem* above, since the defender’s feasible choices and the challenger’s objective are preserved while voter counts become type masses. It is not an eligible anchor under your rule: the result is an existence guarantee, not a stated computational-complexity result. The supplied text also does not display its theorem number, so I cannot quote a number faithfully.

I would expect the weighted \(k=3\), half-share version to be tractable: the proof is constructive, and its point-selection arguments appear amenable to weighted masses. That expectation is an inference; extending the proof and establishing a running time would be new work. The weakest point is that this mirror may yield a geometric guarantee more than a complexity landscape, and the paper itself supplies neither a weighted theorem nor a complexity analysis. It covers one result on strong peer selection, not the paper’s other election models.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its three-peer and four-candidate theorems, the strong rectangular-net bound, and the exact challenger-share factors are geometric guarantees. They establish that suitable coalitions exist; they do not analyze the complexity of finding one or classify an algorithmic problem.

The proposed \(\Phi_\mu(S)\) is a coherent weighted version of strong peer selection, and spatial types with population masses are a plausible regime. But optimizing \(\Phi_\mu\), or deciding whether some coalition meets a threshold, adds a computational task the paper does not study. Moving from the fixed three-peer guarantee to a broader optimization problem does not fix that gap: it makes the project more ambitious, but less directly a mirror of a result in this paper. The strong rectangular-net theorem has the same limitation.

So the proponent’s candidate fails the stated anchor test, even though it may be a worthwhile new problem suggested by the paper. That distinction is also where this negative case is weakest: if ChoCo accepts computational problems suggested by a paper’s central model, rather than requiring a computational result to mirror, weighted strong peer selection is a credible candidate. I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario; I can argue that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for one.
