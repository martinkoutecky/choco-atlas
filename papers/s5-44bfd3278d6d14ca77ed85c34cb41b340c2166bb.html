What the mirror covers

No continuous computational mirror is anchored by Theorems 1–3; they establish equilibrium and information-aggregation results, not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a coherent continuous-population model from the paper, but I can’t make the requested positive case rest on a qualifying computational result. The paper’s voters have signal types \(t\), with state-dependent proportions \(r_i(t)\); in a unit-mass limit, those proportions are the masses of the voter types. Their decisions are voting probabilities \(\sigma(t)\), and the outcomes are aggregate vote shares. But the paper studies equilibrium existence and information aggregation in that model, not the computational complexity of an underlying problem.

The closest anchors are Theorem 1 (proved here), which says an informationally efficient limit equilibrium exists for every number of alternatives under condition (2); Theorem 2 (proved here), which characterizes when an unresponsive equilibrium exists; and Theorem 3 (proved here), which says all limit equilibria are efficient with two alternatives and informative types. These are substantial equilibrium results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in \(P\), hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Treating them as such would overstate the paper.

A computational question about finding or manipulating equilibria in a continuous type population could be posed, but it would be a new question, not a mirror anchored by one of this paper’s named computational results. And equilibrium existence in a limit Poisson electorate is already the paper’s subject, so merely restating it with type masses would not supply the computational mirror the programme seeks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that none of the three anchors is a computational result. The paper already has a natural high-multiplicity description: in state \(\omega_i\), signal types have proportions \(r_i(t)\), and strategies produce aggregate vote shares. That makes the population interpretation sensible. It does not turn equilibrium existence or efficiency into an algorithmic classification.

Theorem 1 is existential. Its proof explicitly constructs type strategies whose vote shares select the correct alternative, then uses an ex ante utility maximizer to establish an efficient equilibrium. Asking whether an efficient equilibrium exists under condition (2) is already answered; checking that condition and producing the constructed vote-share witness are straightforward. A stronger question—compute a particular limit equilibrium from rational priors and signal distributions—would need its own input, output, and complexity definition. The theorem does not supply that problem.

Theorem 2’s unresponsive-equilibrium condition is a finite set of inequalities among the priors and type likelihoods. Testing it, or checking the candidate alternatives, is direct arithmetic. Theorem 3 likewise settles the proposed yes/no question for two alternatives under informative types. One could ask for a full equilibrium correspondence, or for responsive inefficient equilibria with three or more alternatives. That may be a worthwhile new equilibrium-computation project, but it is not a computational question this paper’s named results establish.

There is also a problem with the strongest continuous version of these proposals. The strategic incentives in the paper come from rare but positive-probability pivotal events in finite Poisson elections, compared by their asymptotic rates. In an atomless mass electorate, an individual deviation changes no vote share, so those incentives disappear and equilibrium can become vacuous. Preserving the pivotality calculation means retaining the Poisson finite-population game and its limit—the paper’s existing analytic model—rather than getting a new computational object from type masses.

So I would reject a continuous mirror of this paper under ChoCo’s computational-result criterion. I would not claim that no future scenario could yield a valuable algorithmic equilibrium problem; the paper itself does not rule that out. But such a project would need independent motivation and a new computational formulation, not a mirror anchored by Theorems 1–3.
