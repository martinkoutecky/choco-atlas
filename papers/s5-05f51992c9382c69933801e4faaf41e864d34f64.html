Why no mirror

Proposition 3 is a constructive strategic implementation theorem, but its guarantee is about what players receive at equilibrium, not about computing an outcome or the difficulty of computation. The cohort-bloc version is a precise and plausible game-theoretic extension, and its recursive payoffs may be easy to evaluate, but that does not make the paper’s anchored result computational under the stated criterion.

What the mirror covers

The proposed extension lifts Proposition 3’s SPE implementation claim to cohort blocs; it leaves the other propositions and the paper’s recursive allocation-rule definition unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that for every superadditive rooted-tree TU-game, Mechanism (B) implements the sequential equal surplus division payoff vector \(\Phi^e(N,v,D)\) in subgame-perfect equilibria: an equilibrium exists, and every SPE gives the prescribed payoffs. But there is a qualification the positive case cannot get around: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result—no result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Proposition 3 is an implementation theorem, not a complexity theorem.

A plausible, narrow extension would be Continuous Cohort Implementation. Picture a branching river system with many residents but a small number of recurring, payoff-relevant roles. A type records a complete role: river position and branch, water access, production technology, outside option, and bargaining authority. A society is a rational mass vector \(\mu\) over the finite type set \(T\), arranged as a rooted tree \(D\). Let \(V(q)\) give the transferable worth generated by a coalition with mass vector \(q\), where \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), \(V(0)=0\), and \(V\) is superadditive for disjoint mass vectors. For a computational version, take \(V\) to have a rational piecewise-linear representation.

The key modeling choice is that each type cohort acts as one bargaining bloc. At node \(i\), the bloc and its immediate child blocs use the paper’s bidding and take-it-or-leave-it procedure; transfers are aggregate amounts. Write \(q^i\) for the full mass vector in \(i\)’s subtree, and \(Ch(i)\) for its children. Given the worth function \(V_i\) at that recursive stage, define

\[ \sigma_i = \frac{V_i(q^i)-V_i(\mu_i e_i)-\sum_{j\in Ch(i)}V_i(q^j)} {1+|Ch(i)|}. \]

The aggregate payoff to bloc \(i\) is \(z_i=V_i(\mu_i e_i)+\sigma_i\). For each child \(j\), recurse on its subtree after adding \(\sigma_i\) to the worth of that branch’s full mass vector. The per-capita outcome for type \(i\) is \(z_i/\mu_i\).

The question to hand a prover is: given \(D,\mu,V\), does this cohort bargaining game have an SPE, and does every SPE produce the recursively defined aggregate payoffs \(z_i\)? The expected answer is tractable for the stated representation: the recursion uses a number of evaluations of \(V\) linear in \(|T|\), and the paper’s finite-player implementation theorem applies to the induced type-bloc game. The direct computational follow-up is to compute the per-capita vector \(z_i/\mu_i\); further questions include what happens when cohorts cannot bind their members to a common strategy, or when their bargaining power scales with mass.

This is a positive case only for Proposition 3, and it is conditional on the bloc interpretation. It is plausible for river districts or cooperatives bargaining on behalf of large, homogeneous constituencies. The weakness is that the paper’s players are strategic individuals, whereas this mirror gives each type cohort one strategic seat. That co-modification may be natural for some institutions, but it is not merely replacing a finite population by a distribution. Also, since the paper proves no computational result, this is an adjacent high-multiplicity application of its implementation theorem—not a qualifying computational anchor under the stated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is the only anchor, and it establishes a game theoretic implementation result: for each finite superadditive rooted tree game, the mechanism has an SPE and every SPE produces the prescribed payoffs. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The proposed question—whether a cohort bargaining game has an SPE with those payoffs—repeats that kind of result, rather than giving the continuous mirror a computational question of its own.

The strongest version of the proposed model is plausible: many farmers or residents could be represented by a few homogeneous river district blocs, each with a mass and a representative who can bargain for it. But then the strategic players are the blocs, and the mass changes their worths rather than the number of strategic players. Once \(V\) can be evaluated efficiently, the payoff rule is computed by its tree recursion, with a number of steps linear in the number of blocs. Any harder computation would come from the chosen representation of \(V\), not from continuizing the population or from Proposition 3. Calling this “continuous cohort implementation” does not turn it into a complexity result.

There is a harder modeling choice if the mass is meant to consist of atomless individuals who each bargain. The mechanism selects a proposer by net bids and requires unanimous acceptance; in an atomless population, there is no natural individual proposer, and unanimity may give veto power to an agent with zero mass. A delegate protocol could address that, but it returns to the finite bloc model. Likewise, replacing equal division among bargaining entities with mass-weighted division might make outcomes independent of how cohorts are split, but it changes the allocation rule. Those may be worthwhile institutional models, yet neither supplies a computational mirror of this paper’s result.

I would not claim that high-multiplicity river districts are implausible, or that no continuous extension could be interesting. The negative case is narrower: even the best such scenario yields a continuous equilibrium or implementation question, not the computational complexity question ChoCo is organized to study. On the stated programme criteria, Proposition 3 does not provide a qualifying anchor.
