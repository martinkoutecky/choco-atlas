What the mirror covers

The proposed analogy tracks Theorem 2’s quota structure and Theorem 3’s strong-anonymity boundary cases; it leaves Proposition 1 and Theorem 1’s general implementation implications untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a high-multiplicity binary referendum with a fixed, small number \(K\) of constituencies: many voters, but only \(K\) blocks and two preference types, \(x\succ y\) and \(y\succ x\). Type would be “constituency \(k\), preference \(x\succ y\)” or “constituency \(k\), preference \(y\succ x\),” and mass would be the fraction of the electorate of that type. Thus the support has size at most \(2K\), even as the electorate grows. The paper’s partition and anonymity assumptions make this regime plausible: identities within a block matter less than the block’s support.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It characterizes anonymous committees relative to a partition whose extended majority voting rules are OSP with respect to that partition. Such a rule has the required property exactly when the blocks can be ordered and assigned compatible integer quotas \(Q=(q_1,\ldots,q_K)\), so that its minimal winning coalitions are
\[ \mathcal{C}_{m}^{x} = \bigcup_{k=1}^{K} \left\{ \bigcup_{t=1}^{k}T_t\cup\{i_k\} : T_t\subseteq S_t,\ |T_t|=q_t,\ i_k\in S_k\setminus T_k \right\}. \]
This is a striking structural classification, but it is not a computational-complexity result. Theorem 3, also proved here, similarly characterizes strong anonymity: for quota \(q\), OSP with respect to the partition holds exactly when \(q\in\{1,n\}\), or \(K=1\), or \(K=2\) with \(|S_1|\in\{1,n-1\}\). That too is an implementation characterization, not a claim that a computational problem is in P or hard. I find no numbered result in the paper asserting a complexity status of the kind required for an anchor; Theorems 2 and 3 are only the nearest candidates.

A possible follow-on question would replace supporter counts by block-level supporter masses. Given block masses \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K\) and a monotone outcome rule on the vector of \(x\)-support masses, characterize the rules implemented by an ordered sequence of quota tests: in block \(k\), choose \(x\) if its support exceeds \(q_k\), choose \(y\) if it falls below \(q_k\), and continue on equality, with equality at the final block yielding \(y\). The solution would be an ordering, real quotas, and the corresponding sequential mechanism. This is the most concrete continuous analogue of Theorem 2’s quota structure.

The weak point is central. With individually negligible voters, a unilateral report changes no mass, so obvious strategy-proofness can become vacuous. To preserve strategic content, the follow-on would need coordinated deviations by positive-mass cohorts, changing the strategic unit from an individual voter to a cohort. That is a substantive change to the paper’s problem, and the resulting question is an implementation classification rather than the computational landscape ChoCo targets. So the constituency setting offers a plausible *structural* mirror, but I cannot honestly supply a qualifying named computational anchor or claim that this paper establishes a computational problem for continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational problem to continuize. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 give implementation implications; Theorems 2 and 3 classify which finite-agent voting rules are obviously strategy-proof under specified anonymity conditions. None states an algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or hardness result. That leaves no named computational anchor for ChoCo’s target.

The best mass-based version of Theorem 2 is coherent: fix \(K\) blocks, let \(\beta_k\) be the mass supporting \(x\) in block \(k\), and replace each integer quota by a mass threshold \(\lambda_k\). Then characterize the rules implemented by an ordered sequence of threshold tests, with equality passing the decision to the next block. But the resulting contribution is still a characterization of implementability. Evaluating a given sequence of thresholds is direct; asking which monotone mass rules admit such a sequence requires an input representation whose choice would largely determine the computational difficulty. That does not uncover an algorithmic question already present in the paper.

The more fundamental issue is that the finite proofs depend on individual voters being pivotal. In an atomless population, changing one person’s report changes no \(\beta_k\). The minimal winning coalitions used in Theorem 2 also have no natural counterpart for positive mass thresholds: under a nonatomic measure, a winning set can generally be reduced to a smaller winning subset. Theorem 3’s boundary cases show the same strain. A quota of one voter and a quota of two voters both normalize to zero as the electorate grows; a one-voter block becomes a block of zero mass. The paper’s pivotality distinctions disappear at the limit. OSP’s worst-truthful versus best-deviation comparison may still constrain a chosen game tree, but it no longer tests how an individual report affects the population outcome.

A repair could make positive-mass cohorts the strategic actors, or allow coordinated mass deviations. That would preserve threshold effects, but it changes the unit of agency: in this paper, partition members’ strategies are held fixed while one member deviates. A cohort-level model could be worthwhile on its own; it does not provide a computational mirror of these results.

So the negative case is strong for this paper under ChoCo’s computational scope. It cannot honestly establish the literal claim that no plausible continuous model exists: cohort implementation is a credible adjacent question. Its weakness is that such a model might interest the authors; its difficulty is that the paper itself gives no computational problem for it to inherit.
