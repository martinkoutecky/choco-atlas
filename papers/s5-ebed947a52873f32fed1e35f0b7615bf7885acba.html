The case FOR (proponent)

I can identify a plausible continuous direction, but I can’t make it a compliant positive case under your anchor rule: this paper has no named result establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Its numbered theorems are game-theoretic characterizations, not computational results.

The strongest bridge is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: for the preference revelation game induced by the Boston mechanism, with true preferences \(P_I\), the set of Nash equilibrium outcomes is exactly the set of stable matchings under \(P_I\). Theorem 3, also proved here, gives the corresponding result for the Boston mechanism with type-specific quotas: its Nash equilibrium outcomes are exactly the weakly stable matchings under the true preferences. These are useful structural results, but neither states a complexity bound.

If that kind of result were accepted as an anchor, a plausible candidate would be Continuous Weak-Stability Computation. An instance would give schools with rational capacity shares and quota shares, plus a finite support of student types with rational masses. Each type would specify a strict school preference, a controlled-choice category, and a priority class at each school. A solution would be a fractional assignment \(x_{t,s}\) of each type’s mass to schools that respects capacities and quotas and is weakly stable. The natural regime is a large school-district cohort with many students but relatively few recurring preference, eligibility, and priority-class combinations. Type-level deferred acceptance suggests tractability in \(m\), support size, and input bit length. Its further questions would include how this fractional solution relates to finite high-multiplicity instances and whether it characterizes limits of Boston-game equilibrium outcomes.

The weaknesses are substantial. The paper assumes strict priorities over individual students; encoding those priorities as part of a complete type can make nearly every student a different type. Grouping students into priority classes and using fractional tie handling changes that assumption. More seriously, an atomless student’s unilateral report change has zero effect on the aggregate Boston outcome, so the paper’s Nash-equilibrium characterization does not carry over automatically. The stable-flow problem may be a sensible continuous matching problem, but it could lose the strategic Boston question that Theorem 1 answers.

So Theorem 1 offers a genuine conceptual bridge, and Theorem 3 makes a type-based continuum especially tempting. But under the requested standard for named computational anchors, the honest conclusion is that this paper supplies none.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s two named anchors do not meet the programme’s computational standard. Theorem 1 characterizes the Nash equilibrium outcomes of a finite Boston assignment game; Theorem 3 gives the corresponding characterization with type-specific quotas. Neither poses or resolves a complexity question. “Compute a fractional weakly stable assignment” is a new problem proposed from their structure, not a computational result of this paper.

The strongest repaired version is sensible: represent a large district by masses of students sharing preferences, priority tiers, and controlled-choice categories; scale capacities and quotas with the cohort; and ask for a stable assignment of those masses. That is a plausible continuous matching problem, and Theorem 3 makes the grouping especially natural. The priority details are a real modelling choice—strict rankings of named students cannot simply be copied into a finite type space—but priority tiers with fractional tie handling could be a recognizable co-modification. I can’t honestly dismiss this mirror as meaningless or rule out that the authors would see it as their problem.

The closer attempt to preserve the paper’s distinctive Boston-game result has a sharper obstacle. Its proofs use a student’s unilateral report change to secure a seat. In a model that tracks only type masses, one student has zero mass, so that deviation does not change the aggregate assignment in the way the finite proof requires. Replacing individual Nash deviations with coordinated changes by a positive mass, or with a nonatomic equilibrium concept, changes the question the theorem answers. A model that tracks continuous priority positions might preserve more of the strategic detail, but it would no longer be the proposed finite-support, type-level mirror.

So the negative case is strongest on the stipulated anchor test: the paper offers no named computational result to continuize, and the proponent’s computation problem is an adjacent proposal. It is weaker against the broader claim that some continuous matching project would be worthwhile. The population regime is credible, and a continuous stable-assignment question survives as a plausible follow-on; I would not claim the supplied material rules it out.
