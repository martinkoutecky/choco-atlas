The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the setting: a large resource economy could have many consumers but relatively few complete preference types. Let type \(t\) denote a classical preference, let \(\mu_t\) be its population share, and let \(x_t\) be the per-capita bundle assigned to that type. Feasibility is \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t \leq \omega\). A direct mechanism would map reported preferences to allocations, with Pareto efficiency and individual strategy-proofness as its requirements. That is a plausible high-multiplicity economic model.

But the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it. Its main result is printed simply as “Theorem,” without a number: when \(L\geq N\), every Pareto-efficient and strategy-proof social choice function is alternately dictatorial. The unnumbered corollary extends that statement to a larger preference domain. The numbered propositions and lemmas establish structural and topological facts about option sets; none classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The paper gives no algorithmic input, running-time question, or complexity result.

The candidate mirror also strains the theorem’s content. Its conclusion singles out one agent who receives the whole endowment. A type-level allocation records average bundles across a group and can erase that individual. Treating an entire type as the recipient changes the conclusion. And with finitely many goods, the theorem’s condition \(L\geq N\) cannot carry over to an atomless population without substantially changing the model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Its main theorem and corollary characterize Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof mechanisms axiomatically; the numbered propositions and lemmas establish structural properties used in that proof. None poses an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. So the proponent’s large-economy example motivates a continuous economic model, but it supplies no ChoCo anchor.

The strongest nearby proposal would use finitely many preference types, rational population masses, and per-capita bundles, then ask whether a Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof mechanism exists or can be found. That is a plausible new research question. The paper’s theorem, however, concerns a mechanism over individual reports and concludes that each allocation gives the whole endowment to one individual. Replacing individuals with type masses changes what strategy-proofness means: treating a type as one strategic player gives that player control over a group, while checking individual deviations in a mass model requires a new mechanism definition. Neither question is a computational result in this paper.

There is also a mismatch in the theorem’s regime: it assumes \(L\geq N\). With a fixed number of goods and a growing population, that condition eventually fails. Setting \(N\) equal to the number of types would restore the inequality only by changing the strategic players from individuals to types.

Thus none of the proposed anchors survives as a continuous computational mirror. The negative case is strong for this paper’s ChoCo fit, though it cannot rule out useful computational questions in a related high-multiplicity exchange model; those would need to be developed independently of the results here.
