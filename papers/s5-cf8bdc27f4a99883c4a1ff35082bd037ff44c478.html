The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a valid computational anchor in this paper. Theorems 1 and 2 characterize the one-to-one vNM farsightedly stable sets; Theorem 3 gives the many-to-one characterization under substitutable preferences; Corollary 1 treats the top-coalition case. These are structural game-theoretic results. None asserts a complexity classification or presents an algorithmic result of the kind your anchor rule requires.

A continuous matching market with types defined by complete preferences and mass defined by each type’s population share is a plausible research direction. One could ask which mass allocations form vNM farsightedly stable sets. But that would be a proposed new question, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Calling one of these theorems an in-P result would overstate what the authors prove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 says a core matching forms a singleton farsightedly stable set; Theorem 2 characterizes all such sets; Theorem 3 extends that characterization to many-to-one markets; Corollary 1 gives a special case under the top-coalition property. These are mathematical characterizations, not complexity results or algorithmic results. Treating any of them as a tractability anchor would misstate what the paper proves.

The best continuous version is easy to describe: let agents fall into finitely many complete preference types, give each type a rational mass, represent matchings by flows between types, and define farsighted deviations using measurable coalitions. One could ask whether the continuous farsightedly stable sets are exactly singleton core allocations, including in the many-to-one case. That is a coherent population-continuum question. But it remains a new equilibrium characterization, not a computational mirror of these theorems. The same is true of continuizing the top-coalition result. The proposed version therefore gives the paper a plausible theoretical sequel, while leaving the programme’s computational question unanchored.

That distinction is the negative case’s strength; its limit is also clear. A typed continuum matching model is not inherently nonsensical, and the paper’s results do not rule out a worthwhile computational project built on it. I would not claim that no such project could matter. I would claim that none of the named results supplies the computational anchor ChoCo asks for, so this paper is a poor fit for the continuization programme.
