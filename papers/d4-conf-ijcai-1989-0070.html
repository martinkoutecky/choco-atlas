There is a plausible population regime behind the model: a large public consultation or administrative evidence pool addressing one proposition. A type could comprise a source’s opinion certainty, topic-specific competence, power, goals, and relevant scripts; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of reports with that complete profile. The continuous task would process those source-type masses through credibility adjustment and opinion combination, then return a certainty for the proposition and the resulting judgment. That is recognizably related to the paper’s aim of forming a fact from credibility-weighted opinions.

The weakness is decisive for this assignment: the paper does not specify the combination and update rules precisely enough to make that task a prover-ready computational problem. It also does not establish a computational result to mirror. Turning the conceptual procedure into a formal continuous problem could be worthwhile, but presenting it as a mirror of a named result here would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no named results to anchor a continuous mirror to. The paper states no computational problem, complexity bound, or algorithmic guarantee. It describes a cognitive architecture—COMBINE, DOUBT, and REINTERPRET—with heuristic choices, configurable modes, and an illustrative example whose values are arbitrary. That makes the proponent’s admission decisive for this assignment: the paper offers no computational result for the programme to mirror.

The proposed large consultation is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. One could define types by opinion certainty, competence, power, goals, and scripts, then ask how a distribution of reports affects a judgment. But even this stronger version needs choices the paper does not settle: how opinions combine, how credibility changes, how doubt and reinterpretation are selected, and how the cycle stops. A continuous distribution can be passed through any specified procedure; the resulting complexity question would belong to the new procedure an analyst defines, not to a result in this paper.

One could also add an intervention question, such as how much evidence must change to flip the judgment. That would create a computational problem closer to bribery or control, but it would be a new research model. The paper does not define such an intervention or claim a result about it.

So the negative case is strong about *mirroring this paper*, but it cannot establish that no worthwhile continuous computational study of opinion aggregation is possible. The consultation scenario could motivate one. It simply would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result that the paper contains.
