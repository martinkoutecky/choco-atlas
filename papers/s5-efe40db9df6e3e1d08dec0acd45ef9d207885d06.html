What the mirror covers

The proposed limit concerns Theorem 5.2’s AOT ordering of \(R_1,R_2,R_3\); it leaves the finite-\(n\) probability formulas, weighted comparisons, admissible-rule enumeration, and model extensions outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is a population limit of the paper’s ROC comparison. Imagine a very large panel whose members share competence \(\theta\), with \(\tau=1\) ex ante type. Under each true state, the four possible judgment ballots acquire the mass fractions given by Proposition 3.1 as the panel grows. A candidate continuous problem is: given rational \(\theta\in(1/2,1)\) and weight \(w\in(0,1)\), choose \(R_1\), \(R_2\), or \(R_3\) to maximize the limit of its weighted ROC score. The solution is the selected rule. This captures the paper’s rule-comparison question in a plausible large-panel regime.

The closest anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved here: under assumptions (C1)–(C4), for every odd \(n\ge3\) and every \(\theta>1/2\), \(AOT(R_3)\le AOT(R_2)<AOT(R_1)\), with the first inequality strict when \(n\ge7\). It gives a clear result to compare against the population limit. In that limit, however, the vote masses become deterministic: \(R_1\) has \(TPR=1\) and \(FPR=0\); \(R_2\) has \(TPR=1\) only when \(\theta>(\sqrt5-1)/2\); and \(R_3\) only when \(\theta>1/\sqrt2\). The limit keeps the ordering but loses its strictness once all three rules classify correctly.

That is a coherent continuous analogue of the paper’s finite-panel performance analysis, covering its model and rule-comparison results in Sections 3–5. Its weakness is decisive for ChoCo’s requested test: Theorem 5.2 is an analytic comparison of error rates, not a computational result. The paper gives no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. So this candidate cannot serve as an eligible computational anchor, and I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem per anchor. The paper’s exact probability formulas and numerical calculations do not fill that gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem for ChoCo is that the paper states no named computational result. Theorem 5.2 compares ROC scores through finite-\(n\) probability formulas; it gives no complexity classification, algorithmic bound, or hardness result. The exact formulas and numerical computations do not supply one. So the proponent’s anchor is a real result, but not a computational anchor of the kind the programme is meant to mirror.

The proposed large-panel scenario is plausible, and I would not reject it on grounds of scale or type space. But under the paper’s i.i.d. model with fixed competence \(\theta\), the continuum removes the sampling variation that makes the ROC comparison interesting. For \(\theta>1/2\), the mass version of IbyI classifies all four true states correctly: \(TPR=1\), \(FPR=0\), and \(WAOT_w=1/2\). R2 and R3 also classify correctly above their stated thresholds, \((\sqrt{5}-1)/2\) and \(1/\sqrt{2}\), respectively. Away from those boundaries, choosing among the three rules reduces to threshold comparisons; the finite-panel differences in Theorem 5.2 come from error probabilities around the expected vote masses, which vanish in this limit.

A stronger candidate is the paper’s own open problem in Section 7: choose the best rule among all admissible rules. Yet in the same continuum model, the four truth states produce only four deterministic mass profiles. IbyI is an admissible rule that separates them perfectly, so rule optimization only needs to consider labels on those four profiles; the rule’s behavior elsewhere on the simplex has no effect on its score. That does not yield a substantial high-multiplicity complexity question.

A nondegenerate version could add common shocks or a distribution over possible competence profiles, then optimize a rule over the resulting random mass profiles. That is a plausible direction, and the authors discuss related model extensions. But its computational content would depend on this additional uncertainty model; it would not be a computational mirror of Theorem 5.2, which has none.

So I can make a strong case against the proposed anchor as a ChoCo contribution, but not an airtight universal case against every possible extension. The all-admissible-rules question leaves a genuine opening if someone specifies a nondegenerate, finitely represented population model.
