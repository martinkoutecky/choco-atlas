What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation covers the Kendall-depth evaluation and deepest-ranking results in Propositions 5 and 6; it leaves the learning bounds, trimming and robustness results, and statistical applications aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is the paper’s Kendall-\(\tau\) depth and its deepest ranking. The population scenario is a large preference panel, recommender service, or repeated ranking survey in which many people rank the same \(n\) alternatives and exact rankings recur. A type is a complete ranking \(\rho\in S_n\); its mass \(\mu_\rho\) is the fraction of people with that ranking. The regime is \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of people and \(\tau\) the number of distinct observed rankings. The decision is to choose a summary ranking, with quality measured by its average Kendall distance from the population.

The lead anchor is Proposition 6, proved in the paper’s supplement as a reformulation of Theorem 5 of [11]. For a stochastically transitive distribution, it gives the maximum Kendall depth,
\[ D_P^*=\sum_{i<j}\left(\frac12+\left|p_{ij}-\frac12\right|\right), \]
and says a ranking is deepest exactly when it orders every pair with \(p_{ij}\ne \tfrac12\) according to the pairwise majority. It also gives the minimum depth,
\[ D_P=\sum_{i<j}\left(\frac12-\left|p_{ij}-\frac12\right|\right), \]
with least-deep rankings ordering each non-tied pair against that majority. If \(P\) is strictly stochastically transitive, the deepest and least-deep rankings are unique; the deepest ranking’s position formula is \(\sigma^*(i)=1+\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf 1\{p_{ij}<1/2\}\). The proposition further expresses the depth loss of any ranking as
\[ D_P^*-D_P(\sigma) =2\sum_{i<j}\left|p_{ij}-\frac12\right| \mathbf 1\{\sigma\text{ disagrees with the majority on }(i,j)\}. \]

The corresponding continuous problem is Strictly Transitive Population Consensus. Its input is \(n\), a listed support of \(\tau\) rankings with positive binary-rational masses summing to one, and the promise that the induced pairwise probabilities make the society strictly stochastically transitive. It asks for the unique ranking maximizing
\[ D_\mu(\sigma)=\binom n2-\sum_{\rho\in S_n}\mu_\rho\,d_\tau(\rho,\sigma), \]
and its exact depth. A solution is that ranking and value. Because strict stochastic transitivity makes the pairwise majority order transitive, the result gives a direct polynomial-time method: compute the pairwise probabilities from the listed types, then output the ranking in majority order. This is Class A, though the tractability comes from the transitivity promise, not from a general relaxation.

A second, narrower anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper. For Kendall depth it gives the exact pairwise formula
\[ D_P(\sigma)=\binom n2 -\sum_{i<j}p_{ij}\mathbf 1\{\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\} -\sum_{i<j}(1-p_{ij})\mathbf 1\{\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)\}. \]
The paper notes that this makes depth computation \(O(n^2)\) when the pairwise probabilities are available. Its continuous problem is Kendall-Depth Evaluation: given the rational type masses, a candidate ranking \(\sigma\), and \(n\), return the exact value \(D_\mu(\sigma)\). With an explicit support, one can compute the \(p_{ij}\) in \(O(\tau n^2)\) arithmetic operations; exact bit complexity is polynomial in the listed input length. This is also Class A. Proposition 5 is the simpler algorithmic anchor; Proposition 6 is the stronger one because it solves a ranking-selection problem rather than only scoring a proposed ranking.

The authors should recognize both questions: their paper defines \(P\) as a distribution over rankings, defines depth through expected distance under \(P\), and identifies deepest rankings with metric medians. The population interpretation preserves complete preference types, uses mass as population share, and keeps the ranking outcome integral. It does not replace the society with fractional outcomes or introduce an unrelated noise process. The exact bridge to high multiplicity is immediate: a discrete profile with \(n_\rho\) voters of type \(\rho\) has \(\mu_\rho=n_\rho/N\), and its average Kendall distance is precisely the continuous objective above.

The further question is what happens without the transitivity promise. Then finding a deepest Kendall ranking is the weighted Kemeny problem. A discrete profile embeds by setting \(\mu_\rho=n_\rho/N\), so its optimum is preserved; the paper cites NP-hardness for median-order aggregation, though it does not prove a named hardness theorem of its own. The natural expectation is Class B: the alternative-ordering combinatorics remain, and rational masses do not remove them. That is a useful boundary around the tractable Proposition 6 case, but it is not a hardness anchor from this paper. The mirror here covers the Kendall-depth evaluation and center results; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s statistical learning bounds, trimming procedure, robustness result, visualizations, or homogeneity tests.

The weakest point is also decisive for novelty: \(P\) is already the distribution over ranking types, and the paper already computes expected distances and their deepest rankings. Thus these are exceptionally clear, author-recognizable population questions, but they are close to a novelty collision: the proposed mirror largely names the paper’s own distributional model as a continuous society. I would argue strongly that the paper contains good continuous-population computational questions, while conceding that this is weak evidence for a *new* continuization of its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the two anchors already use the continuous object ChoCo would introduce. The paper takes \(P\) to be a distribution on \(S_n\), defines depth as
\[ D_P(\sigma)=\binom n2-\mathbb E_P[d_\tau(\sigma,\Sigma)], \]
and defines a median by minimizing that same expected distance. A society with ranking types and masses \(\mu_\rho\) is the same probability vector, coordinate by coordinate. When a discrete profile has \(n_\rho\) people of type \(\rho\), the paper’s empirical distribution is already \(\widehat P_N(\rho)=n_\rho/N\). Calling those masses population shares instead of probabilities changes the interpretation, not the computational question.

That makes Proposition 6 a collision. Its strictly transitive case characterizes the deepest ranking by the pairwise majority order; a continuous mirror with rational type masses would restate that characterization on an explicit support. Dropping the transitivity promise gives the expected Kendall-loss minimization already defined in the paper’s consensus-ranking setup. Whether that problem is easy or hard is an answer to the question, not an objection to asking it. The problem is that neither version adds a distinct continuous-population model.

Proposition 5 has the same issue. The proposed evaluation task is exactly the paper’s depth functional, and the proposition already expresses it through pairwise probabilities. Computing those probabilities from listed masses gives an efficient support-based implementation, but it does not create a new functional or a new population question. The paper also already works with empirical distributions and discusses pairwise observations as an alternative statistical framework.

There is no numbered result here that classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 5 and 6 give identities and structural characterizations; Proposition 7 gives statistical deviation bounds, and Proposition 8 gives a robustness comparison. A new study of support-sensitive algorithms could be useful, but it would be an algorithmic follow-on about a model and objective the paper already formulates—not a continuization of a result whose society is still finite.

The negative case is therefore strongest on novelty, not on whether the population scenario is sensible. Large preference panels with repeated rankings are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and these authors would readily recognize the masses as their distribution \(P\). That credibility is exactly why the collision matters: for both anchors, the proposed mirror renames the paper’s existing distributional question rather than making the society newly continuous.
