The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror to. The strongest relevant source is Theorem 3.8, proved here: the authors construct a three-agent, eight-good instance with cyclically relabelled weighted-coverage valuations in which no EFX allocation exists. Theorem 1.1 states the same kind of nonexistence result; Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 3.4 give a subadditive instance with no \(\alpha\)-EFX allocation for any \(\alpha\in(2^{-1/6},1]\). These are existence and nonexistence theorems, not results about computational complexity. So none qualifies as an anchor under your rule.

The strongest *unanchored* positive case is that Theorem 3.8 gives an unusually clean starting point for a high-multiplicity EFX question. Take many agents in three complete valuation types, \(u_0,u_1,u_2\), using the paper’s cyclic weighted-coverage valuations. Give each type mass \(\lambda\), and provide a supply of \(\lambda\) copies of each of the eight good categories. A solution is a mass vector \(x_{i,S}\ge 0\), where \(x_{i,S}\) is the mass of type \(i\) receiving bundle \(S\subseteq G\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{i,S}=\lambda \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{i,S:\,g\in S}x_{i,S}=\lambda \quad\text{for every good }g. \]
Require that every pair of bundles used with positive mass satisfies EFX: whenever \(x_{i,S}>0\) and \(x_{j,T}>0\), \(u_i(S)\ge u_i(T\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in T\). The problem is to decide whether such a mass allocation exists and, if so, return \(x\).

A plausible regime is a large allocation market with many households but only three recurring, fully specified valuation profiles; inventories of each good category grow with the population. Then the number of agents can be large while the number of types stays at three. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s EFX allocation question. Its key new question is whether allowing each type’s mass to split across bundles can restore EFX. Theorem 3.8 does not answer that: it rules out assignments of three individual bundles, not these aggregate distributions.

That gap is also the weakest point. The paper studies a tiny instance with named, indivisible goods, while the proposed model replicates goods and permits type mass to split among bundles. Authors might recognize the underlying fairness question, but the continuous model changes the allocation regime. Moreover, with eight goods and three types fixed, this is not an asymptotic complexity result; a meaningful complexity classification would need a variable-parameter family. I would not assign it a tractability or hardness class from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchors: none is a computational result. Theorem 3.8, like Theorem 1.1, proves nonexistence for a particular three-agent, eight-good instance. Theorem 3.4 gives an approximation barrier for another constructed instance. They establish that EFX can fail, not the complexity of deciding or computing anything over a family of inputs. So the proponent’s claim that these theorems anchor a ChoCo complexity mirror does not hold.

The better candidate is a high-multiplicity version of EFX existence. For example, take three valuation types from the paper, with masses \(\mu_t=1/3\), and scale supplies of the eight good categories with the population. Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(S\). Require \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), impose the per-capita supply constraints, and require that every two bundles with positive assigned mass satisfy EFX under the first recipient’s valuation. This preserves indivisible bundles for each person; only the population is represented by mass. A large market of households with three recurring valuation profiles is plausible, and the paper’s cyclic weighted-coverage valuations supply concrete types.

That is a coherent computational question, and it survives the obvious objections about indivisible goods and named individuals. But it is not a continuous version of the paper’s result in the stronger sense the programme seeks: the paper gives no input family, algorithmic task, or parameterized structure to carry over. Turning its single witness into a scalable market problem requires specifying how valuation types, goods, supplies, and their encodings vary. That may be worthwhile research, but the motivating problem is then a new EFX market problem, not a complexity question exposed by this paper’s theorem. With the paper’s fixed three types and eight categories, there is no scalable instance dimension to study.

The subadditive approximation result has the same limitation. One could ask for the best \(\alpha\)-EFX guarantee in mass markets, but the paper supplies a finite obstruction, not a computational result or a natural family of such markets. The proposed mirror is therefore possible, but weakly anchored.

So the honest negative case is narrow: the paper is a poor fit for a programme specifically charting computational complexity mirrors. It does not support the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A plausible mass-market EFX problem remains; what is missing is evidence from this paper that it is the authors’ problem to pursue.
