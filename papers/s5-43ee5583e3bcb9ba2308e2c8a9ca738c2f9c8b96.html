The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor compliant case for a continuous mirror: the paper has no named result about computational complexity or algorithms. It studies when rankings and statistical test conclusions change under aggregation.

The numbered results are mathematical characterizations and constructions:

  • Theorem 1, attributed to Haunsperger (2003), says an \(n\times m\) rank matrix is consistent under replication if and only if it is row ordered.
  • Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that for every \(m\) and \(n\), there is a row ordered data set for which the Kruskal–Wallis, Bhapkar’s \(V\), and Mann–Whitney tests all find insufficient evidence that the alternatives come from different distributions.
  • Theorem 3, attributed here to Haunsperger (2003), says that for the standard \(n\times m\) rank matrix, swapping two adjacent entries in one row can make the aggregate ranking differ from the original ranking after just two copies.

None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper computes test statistics and examines structural conditions; it does not formulate a computational problem of that kind.

A plausible population setting would be large repeated surveys, with mass over respondent score or ranking profiles and a question about whether pooling survey waves changes the test conclusion. But that would be a newly formulated statistical question, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Under the requested standard, there are no anchors and therefore no anchor backed continuous problems to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that none of the three anchors is a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes replication consistency; Theorem 2 constructs row-ordered data with nonsignificant tests; Theorem 3 gives a sensitivity construction, attributed to Haunsperger. None states a complexity bound or classifies an algorithmic problem. Theorem 1’s criterion may even make finite-instance recognition straightforward, but the paper does not present that as a complexity question. So these results do not supply the kind of computational claim ChoCo is set up to continuize.

The best version of Theorem 1’s mirror would represent survey waves as distributions over complete score vectors, then ask whether pooling them changes a rank ordering. That is a coherent continuous statistical question: the score vectors retain the cross-wave information needed to recompute ranks. But it is a new distributional question, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper. The same distinction applies to Theorem 3. Its construction turns on one adjacent swap; as population mass, that individual has weight \(1/n\), which vanishes in a limit. Giving the perturbation fixed positive mass could yield an interesting robustness question, but it changes the perturbation being studied.

Theorem 2 has a different obstacle. A continuous distribution alone does not determine a finite-sample significance level: the sample size and sampling model matter. Add those back and use asymptotic tests, and the question becomes one of evaluating statistical functionals of the score distributions. That may be useful, but it is an inferential extension, not a complexity claim from this paper. Exact finite-sample significance, meanwhile, brings the discrete sample back into the model.

These points defeat the named anchors as computational mirrors, but they do not prove the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A distributional robustness project about pooling survey populations could be sensible, and the authors might recognize it as related to their subject. The honest negative case is therefore strong within ChoCo’s stated complexity remit, but weak as a claim about every possible continuous research question suggested by the paper.
