What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the Borda cost of transforming between two matchings; it leaves the axiomatic characterization and the paper's applications to stability, fairness, and other market properties without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is a continuous version of the paper’s *object*: measuring how much agents’ partners change when a mechanism changes. Imagine a large roommate market with finitely many preference types, each ranking partner types, and masses \(\mu_t\) with \(n\gg |T|\). If \(M\) and \(M'\) are two matching outcomes on that population, let \(b_t(u)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}\mu_v\) be the mass-weighted Borda score of partner type \(u\). Given the mass \(z_{tuv}\) of type-\(t\) agents whose partner changes from type \(u\) under \(M\) to type \(v\) under \(M'\), the continuous transformation cost is
\[ D(M,M')=\sum_{t,u,v} z_{tuv}\lvert b_t(u)-b_t(v)\rvert. \]
This is plausible for large cohorts whose members share preferences over a small set of roommate types. Computing \(D\) from the type-transition masses is tractable. But it is only a candidate analogue of the paper’s measure, not a qualifying anchor: type-level rankings and tracking changes by mass coarsen the paper’s rankings over named individuals.

The paper has no named computational result of the required kind. Theorem 1, proved here, says that under the paper’s six conditions the distance from any matching \(\mu\) to the identity matching equals the Borda measure, \(\sum_{i\in N}|\mu,\mu^I|_{R_i}\). Theorem 2, also proved here, extends that identity to any two matchings \(\mu,\tilde\mu\): \(\delta_P(\mu,\tilde\mu)=\sum_{i\in N}|\mu,\tilde\mu|_{R_i}\). Both are axiomatic characterization results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The discussion of mechanisms’ computational complexity and the cited algorithmic work do not supply a numbered computational anchor for this paper’s Borda measure.

So there is no honest per-anchor complexity question to hand to a prover. One could study computing the continuous cost above, but that is not a named result of this paper, and evaluating the displayed sum is not the complexity landscape the paper investigates. The main weakness is therefore decisive for ChoCo: the paper’s contribution is normative characterization, while axiomatic continuization is outside the programme’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered computational result in this paper to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize a distance from six axioms; they do not classify an algorithmic problem. For the paper’s stated object, computing the distance is already a direct sum over agents. A continuous type version would replace that sum with an aggregate formula, which is easy to evaluate. A continuous uniqueness theorem for the measure would continue the paper’s axiomatic contribution, a topic ChoCo explicitly sets aside.

The proposed mass-weighted Borda distance has a plausible large-market interpretation. Type-level roommate markets are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, so lack of repeated types is not a convincing objection. But the proposed transition mass \(z_{tuv}\) records how the *same* agents’ partners change between outcomes. Two type-level matching flows do not determine that cross-outcome coupling. Supplying it as input adds information; choosing a canonical minimum-cost coupling turns the task into a transport problem with rank-ordered costs and a straightforward finite optimization formulation. Either way, it supplies no difficult pricing question or complexity boundary for the paper’s result.

The paper’s applications suggest stronger computational questions: find the stable matching nearest to a given one, or choose a median stable matching. Those may be worthwhile projects for large markets. They require adding an optimization problem and stability constraints that the paper never analyzes; any complexity result would belong to those additions, not to a continuization of its characterization. The paper’s Borda score also compares partners by their ranks among named individuals. Compressing agents into types preserves it only under a new type-level preference model; preserving the named-agent ranks may require keeping enough distinctions that the support grows with the market.

So the negative case is strong on the paper-specific question: none of its named results is a computational result, and the direct continuous version of its measure appears algorithmically routine. It is weaker as the universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous question can grow from this paper. Its discussion of stability and large matching markets gives a credible starting point for adjacent algorithmic work, even though that work would not mirror a result proved here.
