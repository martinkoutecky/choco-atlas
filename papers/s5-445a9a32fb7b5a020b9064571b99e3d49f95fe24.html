The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no eligible complexity anchor. Its numbered results establish incentive and core properties in a Cournot game; none classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. So I can’t give the requested anchor-based case for a continuous *computational* mirror without inventing a result the paper does not contain.

The closest result is Proposition 4.1, proved in this paper. For \(n\ge3\), let \(\eta^{-1}(n)=(n^2-2n-1)/(3n^2-1)\) and \(h(n)=(4n-9)/(4n+3)\). When \(\eta^{-1}(n)<\epsilon<1\), every non-grand coalition structure has an empty core; the grand coalition has an empty core when \(\eta^{-1}(n)<\epsilon<h(n)\), and a nonempty core when \(h(n)\le\epsilon<1\). That is a named, useful game-theoretic characterization, but it is not a complexity result.

A many-firm market with a few cost or technology types could motivate a continuous population model of cartel formation and core stability. But this paper’s advantage belongs to a single firm: its population share is \(1/n\), while the stable interval’s width, \(1-h(n)=12/(4n+3)\), tends to zero. Giving the advantaged type positive mass would change the regime, and Proposition 4.1 does not establish what happens there. That may be a worthwhile new game-theoretic question; it is not a defensible continuous computational mirror anchored in this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Its propositions characterize incentives and core existence in a Cournot game; they give no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or approximation question. Proposition 4.1 is a precise and useful characterization, but mirroring it as a question about continuum-agent core stability would be an analytic game-theory project, outside ChoCo’s computational remit. The same applies to the cartel-formation results and Proposition 5.1 on technology copying.

Proposition 4.1 also resists a direct population limit. Its proof relies on a finite set of individually blocking firms, including the single advantaged firm. In an atomless population, each individual has zero mass, so those singleton deviations no longer give the constraints that produce \(h(n)=(4n-9)/(4n+3)\). Meanwhile \(h(n)\to1\), and the stable interval \([h(n),1)\) shrinks to zero width. That is a real degeneration of this result’s finite-player structure.

A positive-mass type of advantaged firms is the best repair, and it could be a sensible economic scenario. But it requires new choices about how mixed-type cartels produce, how technology changes their costs, how profits are divided across mass, and which mass deviations count. Those choices define a new continuum partition-function game. The paper supplies no computational task or result for that game. The integer number of cartels \(\rho\) and the named firm whose technology drives the incentives also prevent a direct transfer of the paper’s formulas to a distribution over types.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an existing computational mirror: its named results are of the wrong kind, and the direct continuum version loses key finite-player structure. The universal claim is weaker. A positive-mass technology type could motivate a worthwhile new model, perhaps with computational questions about stable coalition structures. I cannot honestly rule that out; it would be a new research direction, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in this paper’s results.
