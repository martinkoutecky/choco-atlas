The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow, high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s Kemeny problem with ties.

Imagine a very large ranking panel on a fixed set \(V\) of \(m\) candidates, where many respondents submit the same complete ranking with ties. Let each distinct weak ranking \(\rho\) be a type, with rational mass \(\mu_\rho\), and let the explicit support have size \(\tau\). The continuous problem is: given \((V,T,\mu)\), output a weak ranking \(\pi\) minimizing
\[ D_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{\rho\in T}\mu_\rho d_{\mathrm{KS}}(\pi,\rho). \]
Mass means share of the panel; the solution is an aggregate ranking. A plausible regime has many respondents but relatively few distinct ballot types. This is the continuous KPT: its objective is the per-capita Kemeny–Snell distance, and its types and output are exactly those of the paper’s rank-aggregation problem. The authors should recognize it as their problem expressed for a population rather than a voter list.

My lead anchor is Lemma 1, proved here. It gives an optimization-preserving graph formulation for LOPT: every ranking with ties \(\pi\) induces a feasible arc set \(F_\pi\) for \(WUCF(H_W,W)\), with \(f_W^{LOPT}(\pi)=g_W(F_\pi)\). Conversely, every feasible \(F\) has a parsimonious extension \(\pi_F\) with \(g_W(F)=f_W^{LOPT}(\pi_F)\). Thus \(\pi\) is optimal exactly when \(F_\pi\) is optimal, and every parsimonious extension of an optimal \(F\) is optimal. The continuous KPT objective induces pairwise LOPT weights by replacing the paper’s ranking counts with masses; the same correspondence then applies. The question for a prover remains the underlying one—compute an optimal aggregate ranking from \((V,T,\mu)\)—not the graph encoding.

I expect hardness to transfer. A discrete KPT profile with counts \(n_\rho\) maps to \(\mu_\rho=n_\rho/n\); this scales the objective without changing its minimizers. For strict input rankings, the paper says KPT and KP have the same optimal solutions, and it cites KP as NP-hard even with four input rankings. That cited hardness is useful context, but it is not a numbered computational result in this paper, so I do not treat it as an anchor. The likely hardness is driven by \(m\), not by needing a large support. The resulting questions include the complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether the graph structure yields useful algorithms on restricted instances or for approximation.

The paper’s Theorems 1 and 2 provide relevant structure—their NSCC ordering guarantee and tie-dominant-component guarantees apply to the LOPT weights induced by the population—but they are structural theorems, not complexity results. I do not count them as additional anchors. Lemma 1 is the sole anchor here because it is the paper’s named formulation result, which the paper uses to prove its main properties.

The weakest point is that the paper gives no named complexity classification or algorithm for LOPT. This mirror therefore opens a computational question rather than mirroring a complexity theorem, and continuous KPT is an exact normalization of discrete KPT, so it may bring no tractability gain. The positive case is that it is still a natural, precise high-multiplicity problem, and the formulation result supplies a concrete bridge to the paper’s optimization model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent’s sole anchor, Lemma 1, is an optimization-preserving graph formulation for arbitrary pairwise weights. Its proof does not use voter counts. Replacing counts by rational masses in KPT leaves the graph construction and objective identity unchanged, so the continuous version adds no content to the lemma itself. Theorems 1 and 2 are structural statements about optimal rankings, not computational results; applying them to weights induced by a distribution simply restricts their existing claims to a special case.

That is a case against calling this a continuization of the paper’s contribution. It does not defeat the stronger mirror, though: a large panel with repeated weak rankings, represented by support types \(\rho\) and rational masses \(\mu_\rho\), gives a sensible high-multiplicity problem of minimizing \(\sum_\rho \mu_\rho d_{\mathrm{KS}}(\pi,\rho)\). The authors define KPT as an optimization problem, and the support size and mass bit lengths make complexity questions about that compressed input meaningful. A rational distribution can be realized by a finite electorate after scaling by a common denominator; that connection is exactly the high-multiplicity bridge, not a reason to dismiss the question.

So the honest negative case is weak. Lemma 1 is not itself a complexity result to mirror, but continuous KPT remains a credible computational question the authors would recognize. I cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
