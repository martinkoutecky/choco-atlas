What the mirror covers

The proposed replication direction concerns Theorems 5, 10, and 11; the paper's value and core characterizations are also not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive lead is a possible high multiplicity limit of the bargaining model, but this paper has no named computational result to anchor it. It proves equilibrium and value characterizations; it does not establish that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or any other complexity class.

The closest result is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: for strictly zero-monotonic TU games, the bargaining mechanism has a unique expected subgame perfect equilibrium payoff, and that payoff is the selective value. Theorem 10, also proved here, gives the same uniqueness and payoff under a tie-breaking rule for zero-monotonic games. Theorem 11 proves it for the \(\varepsilon\)-penalty mechanism on zero-monotonic games. These are substantial results, but none analyzes the computational cost of finding the payoff. Proposition 2 and Corollary 3 likewise characterize the value or its relationship to the core; they are not complexity results.

A plausible setting for a *new* continuous extension would be a large consortium with finitely many contributor types: each type specifies the resources, outside option, and bargaining role that make its members interchangeable. Let \(\mu_k\) be the population share of type \(k\), and let a coalition’s surplus depend on its vector of type masses. One could ask for the limiting expected equilibrium payoff per member of each type in replicated finite games, with payoffs scaled so they remain finite as the population grows. The action would still be each member’s commitment; the output would be a type-level payoff vector. That is a concrete high multiplicity question, and a replication limit could make type-level averages fractional.

But I would not present that as an established continuous mirror of Theorem 5. Its mechanism relies on individually strategic players and a final player who clears the market. In an atomless population, individuals have zero mass and there is no last player; preserving the result would require a new limit model and additional assumptions. So the candidate has no defensible expected complexity class yet, and the authors might regard it as a useful extension rather than their problem.

Thus the positive case is limited: the paper’s finite bargaining model has a natural population-replication direction, but it does not supply a named computational anchor for ChoCo’s complexity question. Also, the divisible commodity in the paper is outcome-space continuity; it does not itself continuize the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s candidate has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 5 proves a unique expected subgame-perfect equilibrium payoff under strict zero-monotonicity; Theorems 10 and 11 give related uniqueness results under different tie-breaking and penalty assumptions. These are equilibrium characterizations. The paper states no computational problem and proves no algorithmic upper bound, hardness result, or parameterized result. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, that is decisive: treating one of these theorems as an anchor requires adding a computational question the paper did not ask.

The consortium example is a plausible direction, but making it precise means adding several pieces the paper leaves open. One could choose a finite set of player types, define coalition worth as a function \(V\) of type masses, specify how finite games approximate \(V\), and ask for an algorithm to compute limiting per-type payoffs. That could be worthwhile research. But the representation of \(V\), the scaling of payoffs, and the limiting procedure would define the computational problem; Theorem 5 supplies none of them. Computing the selective value \(\sigma(v)\) is another possible algorithmic question, but its complexity depends on how \(v\) is represented. In either case, the new encoding and computation problem—not a complexity result from this paper—would be doing the work.

There is also a real modeling challenge in carrying the mechanism itself to a continuum: its last player chooses the excluded coalition. A population of atomless agents has no last member. One could specify a limiting terminal-clearing rule, or retain a distinguished market-clearing player, but either choice adds protocol structure that needs its own defense. This does not rule out a sensible continuous game; it underscores how much must be built before there is a computational mirror.

So the negative case is strongest against this paper as a ChoCo anchor: it has no named computational result, and the promising consortium formulation is a substantial new problem rather than a continuation of one. The case is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous research can grow from the paper. A well-specified continuum bargaining game, with a natural computational task, could be valuable; the paper itself does not establish that such a task is already there.
