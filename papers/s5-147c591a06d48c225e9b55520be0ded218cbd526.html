The continuous mirror question

Given a finite conflict graph \(G=(V,E)\) with a supplied \(\ell\)-expression, a finite support \(T\) of complete valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), integer profits \(p_t(v)\ge 0\), and rational per-capita stocks \(b_v\ge 0\), what is the largest integer \(q\) for which there exist nonnegative rational masses \(x_{t,S}\), over types \(t\in T\) and independent sets \(S\) of \(G\), such that \(\sum_{S:p_t(S)\ge q}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v,\ p_t(S)\ge q}x_{t,S}\le b_v\) for every \(v\in V\)?

The model it lives in

A recurring allocation market with a finite catalogue of item categories, a conflict graph on categories, and a large population represented by masses of complete valuation types. Each unit of mass receives an integral conflict-free bundle; rational per-capita stocks constrain how much mass can receive each category, and the objective maximizes the minimum satisfaction.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers max-min allocation under the bounded clique-width promise of Theorem 4, with Theorem 5 offering the same question under a bounded tree-independence decomposition. It leaves the convex bipartite algorithm, the paper’s FPTAS implications, and hardness results attributed to prior work outside its main focus.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\ell\), can the threshold LP be solved exactly in time polynomial in the graph size, type support \(\tau\), input bit length, and the relevant profit bound \(Q\), with an explicit dependence on \(\ell\)?
  • Can a pricing dynamic program for bounded clique-width be combined with LP separation to establish that complexity bound?
  • Does the corresponding pricing and LP approach yield an algorithm under the bounded tree-independence decomposition promise of Theorem 5?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a large, recurring allocation market. There are many recipients but relatively few complete utility types: two recipients have the same type exactly when they value every item category in the same way. The conflict graph is over the finite catalogue of item categories, and each category has a stock that scales with the population. For instance, a large annual cohort might allocate standardized benefits or access passes, with conflicts representing categories no one person may combine. The number of recipients \(N\) is large; the support size \(\tau\) of their type distribution is small by comparison.

Call the lead question Clique-Width Mass Fair Allocation Under Conflicts. Its input is a conflict graph \(G=(V,E)\) with a supplied \(\ell\)-expression, positive rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), integer additive profits \(p_t(v)\ge 0\), and rational per-capita stocks \(b_v\ge 0\). For an independent set \(S\), write \(p_t(S)=\sum_{v\in S}p_t(v)\). The question is to find the largest integer \(q\) for which there are nonnegative rational masses \(x_{t,S}\), indexed by types and independent sets, satisfying

\[ \sum_{S:\,p_t(S)\ge q}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_t\sum_{\substack{S\ni v\\p_t(S)\ge q}}x_{t,S}\le b_v \quad\text{for every }v. \]

The output is \(q\) and such an assignment. Each unit of mass receives an integral, conflict-free bundle worth at least \(q\); \(x_{t,S}\) says how much of type \(t\) receives bundle \(S\). Thus the objective remains max-min satisfaction, while the finite goods become standardized categories stocked in proportion to the cohort. Unused stock is allowed, as in the paper’s partial colorings.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for every pair of positive integers \(k,\ell\), Fair \(k\)-Division Under Conflicts is solvable in time \(\mathcal{O}(4^{k\ell}|\sigma|(Q+1)^{2k})\) when the conflict graph has clique-width at most \(\ell\) and is supplied with an \(\ell\)-expression \(\sigma\), where \(Q=\max_{1\le j\le k}p_j(V(G))\). This is a pseudo-polynomial algorithm for the paper’s finite-agent problem. It is my strongest anchor because it makes the relevant structural promise explicit and gives an algorithm built around the same independent-set bundles and additive profits.

I expect the continuous version under that promise to be tractable. At a fixed \(q\), the constraints above form an LP with exponentially many bundle variables. Its pricing step optimizes an additive reduced-cost weight over independent sets whose type-specific profit is at least \(q\). A dynamic program on the supplied expression can track selected labels, profit up to \(Q=\max_t p_t(V)\), and best reduced cost. That gives a plausible pseudo-polynomial pricing oracle for fixed \(\ell\), and hence a pseudo-polynomial LP algorithm; checking integer thresholds up to \(Q\) gives the optimum. This is a proposed route for the mirror, not a result proved by Theorem 4. The paper’s FPTAS observation also suggests asking whether this threshold formulation admits an FPTAS.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here: for every positive \(k,\ell\), Fair \(k\)-Division Under Conflicts is solvable in \(\mathcal{O}(b n^{k\ell+1}(Q+1)^{2k})\) when the \(n\)-vertex conflict graph is supplied with a tree decomposition having \(b\) bags, each with independence number at most \(\ell\), and \(Q=\max_{1\le j\le k}p_j(V(G))\). Its continuous counterpart is Tree-Independence Mass Fair Allocation Under Conflicts: the same instance and threshold-allocation question, with that supplied decomposition as the graph promise. I again expect tractability, using a pricing dynamic program that tracks profit and the independent set selected in each bag. Each bag has at most \(n^\ell\) independent subsets to consider, so for fixed \(\ell\) this points to pseudo-polynomial time in \(Q\). This promise is valuable in its own right: it includes graph families, such as chordal graphs, that need not be covered by the clique-width promise.

The paper’s named complexity results in the supplied text are algorithmic. Its hardness discussion is important context—the strong hardness on bipartite graphs is attributed to the preceding paper—but it is not a numbered hardness result here, so I do not use it as an anchor. It does indicate a likely boundary: with one type and \(b_v=1\), the unrestricted-graph version has optimum equal to maximum-weight independent set. A feasible mass assignment then has at least one bundle in its support, and every supported bundle must meet the threshold; conversely, a maximum-weight independent set can be given to the whole population. The paper itself notes that this one-agent problem is strongly NP-hard on general graphs. That is evidence for hardness on unrestricted graphs, while the two width promises give plausible tractable regions.

These mirrors stay close to the paper’s underlying question: recipients, additive valuations, indivisible bundles, pairwise conflicts, and max-min utility all remain. The continuous object is the recipient population: each \(t\) describes recipients’ full valuation vectors and \(\mu_t\) is their population share. For a finite realization with \(N\) recipients, type \(t\) has \(N\mu_t\) recipients and category \(v\) has \(Nb_v\) copies. Large \(N\) with small \(\tau\) is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The fractions describe how many people receive each integral bundle; they do not make the bundles themselves fractional.

The weakest point is the stock co-modification. The paper studies a finite catalogue of one-off items and fixes \(k\); the mirror uses repeated copies of item categories so supply can scale with a large recipient population. That makes sense for recurring cohorts and standardized goods, and it is the kind of per-capita scaling the programme permits, but an author insisting on one-off items might see it as a different application. Also, neither anchor directly proves the continuous result: the paper’s running times are for fixed finite \(k\), whereas the proposed LP needs a new pricing argument whose dependence on \(\tau\), bit length, and \(Q\) should be established. That gap is a concrete research question, not a reason to treat the population mirror as a mere restatement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorems 4 and 5 solve a problem whose tractable regime fixes the number \(k\) of agents. Their dynamic programs track \(k\) named agents’ profit profiles; the parameters \(n\), \(Q\), and the graph decompositions describe the item side. So their results do not establish tractability as the recipient population grows. A literal limit with fixed one-copy inventory would also lose its substance: as the population grows, per-capita supply tends to zero.

That objection reaches both anchors, but it does not defeat their best continuous versions. Theorem 4’s supplied clique-width expression and Theorem 5’s supplied tree decomposition can remain promises on a finite catalogue of item categories. In a recurring allocation setting, let many recipients share one of a small number of complete valuation vectors, and scale category stocks with population. The threshold question then has a coherent configuration-LP formulation: every unit of recipient mass gets an integral, conflict-free bundle, while category capacities limit how much mass can receive each category. The same model accommodates both graph promises. This is a real co-modification, but the programme explicitly allows per-capita supplies when population continuization requires them.

Nor does the fixed-\(k\) running time settle whether that model is worthwhile. It means the paper’s algorithms do not directly answer the new question; it does not make the question ill-posed. A cohort with few valuation types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the pricing problems suggested by the graph structure are concrete computational questions. The paper gives no continuous solution to them, so “already done” is not an objection either.

I can therefore make a credible case that these theorems are weak *evidence* for a continuous mirror: their stated tractable regime is bounded-agent, and the scaled-inventory version needs a model the paper does not analyze. I cannot honestly turn that into the requested universal negative. The per-capita-stock version is a sensible population mirror of both anchors, and the mismatch in the existing running times is a research gap rather than a reason to dismiss it.
