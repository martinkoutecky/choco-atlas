What the mirror covers

No result receives a continuous computational mirror; Theorem 1 is an equilibrium-learning guarantee, Theorem D.5 is an existence result, and the agreement and topology results are also non-computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The closest result is Theorem 1 (proved here): for fixed non-atomic private signals and discount factor, every equilibrium on a connected \((d,L)\)-egalitarian countably infinite network learns the state \(S\) almost surely; moreover, for every \(\epsilon>0\), all sufficiently large connected \((d,L)\)-egalitarian finite networks have probability at least \(1-\epsilon\) that all agents learn \(S\), in every equilibrium.

That is a theorem about equilibrium learning, not about computational complexity: the paper gives no algorithm or complexity classification for finding equilibria, deciding whether learning occurs, or computing the probability of learning. Theorem D.5 proves equilibrium existence, also not a computational result. The named results cited from Rosenberg, Solan, and Vieille are agreement results, not complexity results either.

A sympathetic population regime would be a very large social network with bounded neighborhoods and recurring network roles. But a continuous model would need to preserve who observes whom: a distribution over roles or signal types alone does not encode the network topology that drives Theorem 1. The natural question—whether every equilibrium in a continuum-agent network learns—would continue the paper’s analytic question, not provide a continuous version of a named computational-complexity result. Under the required anchor rule, there is therefore no per-anchor continuous problem to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 is the strongest candidate, but it proves an analytic statement about equilibrium learning. Theorem D.5 proves equilibrium existence; Theorem 5.1 and the cited Rosenberg–Solan–Vieille results concern agreement. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no paper result here whose computational population mirror could be the programme’s deliverable.

The best attempt to build one would be: given a finite rationally described network and signal model, decide whether every equilibrium yields learning, or whether a non-learning equilibrium exists. A continuous version might describe a large sparse network by a graphing or a distribution over rooted network roles, then ask for almost-everywhere learning. But that decision problem is supplied by the mirror-maker; it is not a problem this paper formulates or solves. The paper assumes arbitrary non-atomic signal distributions and proves an asymptotic result without giving a finite encoding, an algorithm, or a complexity bound. Making those objects computational would require substantial new choices about how to encode networks, signals, and equilibria.

There is also a real aggregation mismatch. A mass distribution over roles or signal types does not determine which agents observe which others. Preserving that information requires a network object such as a graphing, including its edge structure and connectivity. And the theorem’s “all agents learn” guarantee does not translate directly into an almost-everywhere claim: a continuum measure can ignore exceptional agents that the original claim still covers. These could be sensible research choices, but they change the object being studied and still leave the computational anchor absent.

I would not claim that no worthwhile computational question can ever be built from this model. One could study algorithmic equilibrium learning on encoded network families. The negative case is narrower: that would be a new computational game-theory project, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. Under the programme’s anchor requirement, the proponent’s candidate does not survive.
