The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a result that meets your computational-anchor requirement. The closest is Proposition 4.4, proved in this paper: after writing \(C_{ij}=\min\{L_{ij},L_{ji}\}\), \(\widehat L_{ij}=L_{ij}-C_{ij}\), and defining \(\widehat\Pi\) as the relative net-liability matrix, the system
\[ \widehat P_{ij}=\min\!\left\{\widehat\Pi_{ij}\!\left(z_i+\sum_k\widehat P_{ki}\right),\widehat L_{ij}\right\}, \qquad \widehat P_{ij}\ge -C_{ij} \]
has the unique solution \(b^{pnp}(F)-C\). That is a characterization of clearing payments, not an algorithm or a complexity result; the paper does not analyze its running time. The main named results, Theorem 5.2 and Theorem 7.6, are also proved here, but they are axiomatizations: respectively, they say the pairwise netting proportional rule is the unique bankruptcy rule satisfying net impartiality, invariance to mitosis, claims boundedness, limited liability, priority of creditors, and continuity; and the unique rule satisfying net impartiality, invariance to proportional splitting, claims boundedness, limited liability, and priority of creditors. None asserts computational complexity. So strictly, there are no eligible anchors and no anchor-based continuous problems to hand a prover.

The strongest positive lead I can offer, clearly marked as unanchored, is computing pairwise-netting-proportional clearing for a continuum of highly standardized financial institutions. Let types \(t\in T\) have masses \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and identical per-institution endowment \(z_t>0\) within each type. Let \(Q_{tu}\) be the gross liability from type \(t\) to type \(u\), measured per unit of total population. Require exposures to be uniform across each pair of types: in a population of size \(n\), each \(t\)-to-\(u\) pair has liability \(Q_{tu}/(n\mu_t\mu_u)\). This makes aggregate bilateral netting exact in the symmetric finite replicas. The regime is \(n\gg |T|\): many institutions, relatively few complete financial types.

Given rational \((T,\mu,z,Q)\), set \(E_t=\mu_tz_t\), \(\widehat Q_{tu}=\max\{Q_{tu}-Q_{ut},0\}\), and \(\widehat\Pi_{tu}=\widehat Q_{tu}/\sum_v\widehat Q_{tv}\), taking \(\widehat\Pi_{tu}=0\) when the denominator is zero. The problem, Continuum-Type PNP Clearing, is to output the aggregate payment matrix \(G\), where \(G_{tu}=\min\{Q_{tu},Q_{ut}\}+X_{tu}\), and \(X\) is the unique nonnegative matrix satisfying
\[ X_{tu}=\min\!\left\{\widehat\Pi_{tu}\!\left(E_t+\sum_v X_{vt}\right),\widehat Q_{tu}\right\}. \]
The solution is the payment matrix; there is no optimization objective. My provisional expectation is tractability in \(|T|\) and the input bit length, but proving that is new work. This mirrors the computation of the rule characterized by Proposition 4.4, not the paper’s axiomatic theorems. The authors would plausibly recognize it as a high-multiplicity version of their clearing problem.

The weak point is the symmetry assumption: pairwise netting is counterparty-specific, and uniform exposures across type pairs are a demanding regime for real financial networks. Relaxing that structure may make the number of relevant types grow with the number of institutions. More fundamentally, even a good result for this model would not supply the missing computational anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for has no eligible computational anchor. Proposition 4.4 proves uniqueness of a system describing the pairwise netting proportional payments; it gives no algorithm or complexity bound. Theorems 5.2 and 7.6 characterize the rule by axioms, and Proposition 2.6 establishes a solvency property. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s axiom of continuity concerns how payments respond to changes in endowments and liabilities while the finite agent set is fixed; its splitting axioms concern finite networks. Those are not continuous-population results.

The proposed “Continuum-Type PNP Clearing” is a sensible lead for financial-network computation, but it has no theorem in this paper to mirror. Its type compression also depends on uniform exposure between every pair of types. Pairwise netting is performed edge by edge, and in general aggregating first loses information: the total net exposure need not equal the net of the two aggregate gross exposures. A type description rich enough to retain counterparties’ differing exposures may need as many types as the original network has agents. The block-symmetric case avoids that problem by assumption.

In that block-symmetric case, the continuum equations reduce to the ordinary finite clearing equations on the \(\tau\) type nodes, with liabilities and endowments expressed per unit population. The paper’s own mitosis and proportional-splitting axioms say that splitting an agent while preserving its financial relationships leaves other agents’ payments unchanged. So the growing population of clones supplies no new computational dimension; the remaining task is clearing a finite type-level network. That could be a worthwhile question in computational finance, but it does not turn this axiomatic paper into a computational-social-choice mirror.

The negative case is strongest on that boundary: this paper offers no computational result to anchor, and the proposed follow-up belongs to network clearing rather than the programme’s population-continuized social-choice problems. I would not claim that every homogeneous financial-network model is meaningless; the proponent’s symmetric regime is coherent. Its weakness is that it makes the population limit collapse to the finite network of types, while the paper itself studies rules and their axioms rather than computational tasks.
