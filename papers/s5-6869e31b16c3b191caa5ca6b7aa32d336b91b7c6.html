The continuous mirror question

Fix rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Given a finite support \(T\) of valuation types, positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and an explicitly encoded piecewise-constant probability measure \(\nu_t\) for each type, together with a cut bound \(K\), decide whether the interval \([0,1]\) can be partitioned into alternating \(+\) and \(-\) intervals using at most \(K\) cuts so that \(\nu_t(A_+)=\alpha\) for every \(t\in T\). Equivalently, compute the minimum number of cuts.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete valuation types, each carrying a piecewise-constant probability measure \(\nu_t\). The decision variables are the cut locations and interval labels; minimize the number of cuts subject to \(\nu_t(A_+)=\alpha\) for every supported type. The masses determine which types are represented, while each represented type imposes the paper's unanimous consensus constraint.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1 for exact consensus at fixed rational \(\alpha\); it leaves the cut bounds and the PPA-\(k\) containment results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does minimum-cut computation admit an FPT algorithm parameterized by the support size \(\tau\)?
  • Can the minimum cut count be approximated efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper. It states that, for every fixed \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), deciding the minimum number of cuts needed for an \(\alpha\)-Imbalanced-Consensus-Division instance is NP-hard. The paper proves this by reducing from Exactly-1-3Sat; for \(\alpha=\tfrac12\), it builds on an earlier result that deciding whether consensus-halving is possible with \(n-1\) cuts is NP-complete [FFGZ18]. I would use the rational-\(\alpha\) cases of the theorem for the mirror below.

Call the mirror Type-Consensus Min-Cut. Fix rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). An instance is a society \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of valuation types, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) is a complete, explicitly encoded, piecewise-constant probability measure \(\nu_t\) on \([0,1]\). The question, given a cut bound \(K\), is whether there is a partition of \([0,1]\) into alternating \(+\) and \(-\) intervals using at most \(K\) cuts such that
\[ \nu_t(A_+)=\alpha \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
Equivalently, compute a valid partition with the fewest cuts. A solution is the cut locations and interval labels. This is the paper’s exact consensus condition: it requires agreement for every represented type, not merely for the population’s average valuation.

A plausible regime is a large jurisdiction dividing a continuous public resource—such as land, access time, or bandwidth—between two portfolios. Residents’ normalized valuations are specified by standardized use or entitlement profiles. Millions of residents can share a much smaller set of exact profiles, so \(N\), the population, may be far larger than \(\tau=|T|\). The number of cuts measures how fragmented the resource division must be. This is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s task: a finite realization has \(N_t\) residents of type \(t\), with \(\mu_t=N_t/N\).

The transfer from Theorem 5.1 is direct. Given its finite-agent instance, make each distinct valuation function a type and give it mass equal to the fraction of agents having that valuation. Repeating every resident of each type any number of times leaves both feasibility and the minimum cut count unchanged. Thus the continuous problem contains the paper’s hard instances, while also representing societies with arbitrarily many agents per type. I expect hardness transfers (Class B). The result does not suggest that the continuum itself creates the hardness; it says this high-multiplicity version does not erase it.

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s population-continuous analogue: each type’s measure is exactly the information their task uses about an agent, and a type-level constraint is equivalent to requiring the condition for every member of that type. The interval and the cuts are already continuous in the paper, but I am not counting that as continuization; the mirror is the distribution over agents’ valuation types. The scope here is specifically the minimum-cut problem in Theorem 5.1, not the paper’s cut-bound results or its PPA-\(k\) membership results.

The mirror raises further questions: does minimum-cut computation admit an FPT algorithm parameterized by \(\tau\)? How hard is it to approximate the minimum cut count? What changes if a small mass of types may violate the target ratio? The last question would be a new population-sensitive variant, not an anchor of this paper.

The weak point is that, under the paper’s universal consensus condition, type masses affect the answer only through whether a type has positive mass. This mirror therefore makes the population a continuous, high-multiplicity object without making its mass distribution drive the optimization. Also, Theorem 5.1 does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is bounded: its hard instances may need many distinct types. The case still holds because exact type-level consensus is faithful to the paper’s requirement, and the high-multiplicity regime is plausible even when its support grows with the instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.1 is a genuine computational anchor, so the case against cannot rest on the paper having no result to mirror. The strongest objection is that its proposed mirror puts population masses into a problem whose defining condition treats every supported valuation type as a full veto. The condition is “for every \(t\), \(\nu_t(A_+)=\alpha\),” not a condition on the distribution of those types. A rare type and a large cohort impose the same constraint. That makes the finite-support formulation a set of valuation constraints with population weights attached, rather than a computational question driven by an aggregate society.

The type-constraint interpretation is faithful to the paper, but it weakens the claimed continuization. The reduction for Theorem 5.1 constructs separately specified measures with carefully placed value blocks to encode variables and clauses; it gives no hardness result for bounded support or for a regime where population multiplicity is the operative parameter. Cloning those agents can make the headcount arbitrarily large, but it does not make the population distribution part of the consensus condition. That is a concern about what object the mirror studies, not a prediction that the continuous problem will have the same complexity.

The public-resource scenario is plausible if residents have exactly standardized valuation profiles. But under exact consensus, the composition of those cohorts does no work: the profile catalog supplies the constraints, and even a very small positive cohort has the same standing as the largest one. A genuinely mass-sensitive alternative—such as requiring agreement from at least \(1-\delta\) of the population—would make \(\mu\) matter. It would also replace the paper’s unanimous consensus requirement with partial consent, so Theorem 5.1 would no longer be the anchor for that problem.

An atomless population of valuation functions does not resolve the issue cleanly. Requiring consensus for every valuation in its support produces a continuum of constraints over a function space; requiring it only almost everywhere changes how exceptional types are treated. The paper gives no finite encoding for such a population or a canonical way to impose its exact-consensus condition. The finite-support version is computationally definite, but it is the version in which mass is only bookkeeping.

The same objection reaches the paper’s PPA-\(k\) containment results: their conditions and cut bounds are expressed in terms of the listed measures, not their population shares. So I would argue against making this a central ChoCo mirror. The case is not airtight, though. If the programme accepts unanimous, support-based consensus over standardized valuation types as a population model, the proposed mirror is well-posed and the authors could reasonably recognize it. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; the negative case is that the paper offers little evidence that its computational questions become questions about a continuous population.
