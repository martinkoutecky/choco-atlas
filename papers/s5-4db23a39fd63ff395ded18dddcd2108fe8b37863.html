The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support population measure \(\mu\) over quota and valuation-law types \(t=(b_t,F_t)\), with each \(F_t\) satisfying UF over \(K\) item classes, and per-class supply \(s_g=\frac{1}{K}\sum_t\mu_t b_t\), does assigning each observed report mass its top-\(b_t\) bundle define a feasible mass allocation of integral bundles per unit mass, and what is its distortion against the welfare-optimal feasible mass allocation?

The model it lives in

A finite-support measure \(\mu\) over types \(t=(b_t,F_t)\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and UF valuation laws on \(K\) item classes. Each class has supply \(s_g=\frac{1}{K}\sum_t\mu_t b_t\). The mechanism observes only the induced mass over quota and ordinal top-set reports, and assigns report mass to integral bundles; performance is the ratio of expected optimal mass welfare to expected mechanism welfare.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no eligible ChoCo anchor in the proponent’s case. The paper’s named results bound distortion and distortion gap, or establish incentive compatibility; none analyzes the complexity of a computational problem. RS and RSBS are explicit mechanisms, and the paper does not ask whether computing an optimal mechanism or allocation is in P, NP-hard, or tractable under some parameterization. So a continuous version of one of those theorems would be a performance analysis, not a mirror of the computational-complexity results ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed course-allocation setting is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: grouping agents by quota and valuation-generating distribution is the right way to model repeated types. But the most faithful continuum version also removes the scarcity that drives the paper’s distortion results. Suppose there are \(K\) symmetric item classes and a mass \(\mu_t\) of agents of type \(t\), with quota \(b_t\). Under the paper’s unbiased-favorites condition, mass \(\mu_t/\binom{K}{b_t}\) of that type favors each bundle \(S\) of size \(b_t\). Assign each such mass to \(S\). Then demand for every item class is \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t/K\), exactly the symmetric per-capita supply required by balanced \(b\)-matching. Every agent can receive their favorite bundle, so the finite-market allocation conflict disappears. The \(e/(e-1)\) lower bound and the distortion-gap trade-offs rely on random conflicts among finitely many agents competing for indivisible items; those fluctuations vanish in this atomless limit.

That is a continuum degeneracy, not an objection that the continuous theorem would have a different answer. It leaves no comparable allocation problem whose complexity landscape the paper’s results could illuminate. Preserving scarcity would require changing the model—for example, using unequal supplies or dropping the unbiased-favorites condition. Those could be worthwhile new questions, but they are not mirrors of the results here.

So the strongest honest negative case is limited: there is no anchored ChoCo target in this paper, and its natural population limit erases the phenomenon its distortion bounds measure. I cannot establish that every related continuous study would be worthless; an unequal-supply or non-UF model might be interesting. That would be a new direction, though, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results.
