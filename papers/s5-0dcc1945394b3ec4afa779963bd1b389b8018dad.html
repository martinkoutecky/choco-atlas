The continuous mirror question

For a horizon \(T\), consider a fixed population with seller-type masses \(\tfrac16\) at cost \(0\) and \(\tfrac13\) at cost \(a\), and buyer-type masses \(\tfrac13\) at value \(1\) and \(\tfrac16\) at value \(b\), where \(\tfrac14 \le a < b \le \tfrac34\) are hidden. Each round a deterministic policy posts \(p_t \le q_t\), observes accepted mass on each side, and earns GFT from a maximum-mass matching chosen to minimize GFT among maximum matchings. Is the worst-case cumulative regret against the full-information maximum-GFT matching necessarily \(\Omega(T)\) for every such policy?

The model it lives in

The society has four types carrying the stated rational masses and repeats unchanged for \(T\) rounds. The decision variables are the seller and buyer prices \(p_t,q_t\) with \(p_t \le q_t\); accepted mass trades through a maximum-mass matching, with worst-case minimum GFT among ties. The objective is cumulative regret against the full-information maximum-GFT matching. Scaling each trader in the paper's construction to mass \(\tfrac16\) gives the \(\Omega(T)\) lower bound, with per-round regret at least \(\tfrac1{24}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.6's linear-regret lower bound for Two-Price GFT in a three-seller, three-buyer instance. It leaves the paper's other GFT, profit, and contextual regret results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For general finite-support populations with rational type masses, how does minimax Two-Price GFT regret depend on support size and the mass distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an online GFT problem for a high-multiplicity market. It continues the population: a large platform serves fixed, homogeneous cohorts over many rounds, and each cohort’s mass replaces a count of interchangeable traders. Prices and values remain scalar; the matching becomes a flow of mass because a large cohort need not be traded as one indivisible unit.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.6, proved in this paper. It says that for GFT maximization, every online algorithm using Two-Price Mechanisms can be given a three-seller, three-buyer instance on which it suffers \(\Omega(T)\) cumulative regret. The paper’s construction keeps one seller at cost \(0\), two sellers at a common cost \(a\), two buyers at value \(1\), and one buyer at value \(b\), with \(a<b\). Its adversary chooses prices and feedback so the algorithm never finds a price pair that avoids inefficient trade; the proof gives at least \(1/4\) regret per round in the original unit-count model. The preliminary section also says the lower bound holds with uniformly random maximum matching.

Here is a continuous version of that problem. Let the total population mass be \(1\), split equally between sellers and buyers. Its hidden distribution is
\[ \mu_S=\tfrac16\delta_{(S,0)}+\tfrac13\delta_{(S,a)},\qquad \mu_B=\tfrac13\delta_{(B,1)}+\tfrac16\delta_{(B,b)}, \]
where \(1/4\le a<b\le3/4\). The four atoms are the four distinct types after merging the identical traders in the theorem; their masses are rational and fixed. A platform interacts with this same population for \(T\) rounds. Each round it chooses seller and buyer prices \(p_t\le q_t\). A seller type accepts when its cost is at most \(p_t\), and a buyer type accepts when its value is at least \(q_t\). The learner observes the accepted mass on each side. A trade is a maximum-mass matching between accepted seller and buyer mass; among such matchings, use the one with least GFT, as in the paper’s worst-case matching convention. The benchmark is the maximum GFT matching of the full population with its types known.

Call this Continuous Two-Price GFT Regret. Its instance is a horizon and a hidden population from the stated four-atom family; its solution is an online price policy, or a lower-bound proof, for cumulative regret against the full-information benchmark. The question is whether any such policy can guarantee \(o(T)\) regret uniformly over the family. Scaling each original trader to mass \(1/6\) preserves the adversary’s outcomes and scales GFT and regret by \(1/6\). Thus the theorem’s per-round lower bound becomes at least \(1/24\), giving \(\Omega(T)\) regret. Since per-round regret is bounded, the minimax rate for this family is \(\Theta(T)\). I expect the lower bound to transfer directly to this finite-support continuous model.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: imagine a large, repeated marketplace where sellers fall into two reservation-cost classes and buyers into two value classes, with cohort proportions matching the distribution above. The number of traders can grow without increasing the number of types: \(\tau=4\). Cohort members share the same relevant private information and have unit capacity, just as the paper’s traders do. The paper’s retail and platform examples make that setting plausible. Mass matching is a natural co-modification of the population model: it lets the accepted mass trade while preserving the paper’s maximum-matching objective.

The authors could recognize this as their problem because the decision remains online two-sided pricing under hidden values, accept/reject feedback, budget balance, and GFT regret. The mirror also keeps the theorem’s mechanism restriction: one price for all sellers and one for all buyers. Its further questions are whether segmented prices restore sublinear regret for continuous populations; how the answer depends on support size \(\tau\) and type masses; and whether diffuse value distributions become easier when exact accepted mass is observed.

The weak point is that this is an online learnability and regret mirror, not a standard computational-complexity classification such as P versus NP-hardness. Moreover, the lower bound already arises with three traders on each side. In its continuous form, it establishes that a natural large-population version inherits the information barrier; it does not show that making the population continuous creates that barrier. I would therefore present it as a strong, sharply specified mirror of one central result, while being explicit that it sits at the edge of the programme’s complexity focus.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the kind of result being mirrored, not to the proposed mass model. Theorem 4.6 is a regret lower bound: it says that even an algorithm with unlimited computation suffers linear loss over \(T\) rounds under restricted prices and partial feedback. It gives no running-time or computational-complexity bound. The population size is three sellers and three buyers; \(T\) measures repeated interaction, not input size. The other named results have the same character: they analyze online learnability and regret. None asks how hard it is to optimize over a society represented by a distribution over types, or develops a complexity landscape in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proponent’s four-atom construction is nevertheless a strong high-multiplicity mirror of the *market model*. Scaling each trader to mass \(1/6\) preserves the lower bound, and repeated cohorts with shared costs and values are plausible in a large platform. A version allowing arbitrary cost and value distributions would also be a sensible online-learning question. I would not reject either on grounds of identity, matching, or continuous mass.

Those versions still put the central difficulty in hidden thresholds, feedback, and the restricted pricing mechanism. The distribution is the unknown environment; the paper does not study computation on that distribution. Recasting the problem with the distribution known to an optimizer would remove the unknown-type interaction that defines Theorem 4.6 and pose a different question. Theorem 4.7 and the contextual results have the same limitation: their guarantees concern regret, not computational complexity. The continuous-optimization tools motivating ChoCo therefore have no clear target in these results.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope argument: this paper may support a continuous-population *online learning* extension, while offering no computational-complexity result for ChoCo to continuize. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. If the test is whether the paper admits a credible high-multiplicity population model, this anchor survives and the proponent has the stronger case.
