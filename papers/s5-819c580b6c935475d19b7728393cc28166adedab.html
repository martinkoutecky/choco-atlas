The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) alternatives, a finite set \(\mathcal{R}\) of complete rankings, a discrete horizon \(T\), and an unknown sequence of rational population distributions \(\mu_t\) over \(\mathcal{R}\), suppose each comparison samples a user from the current population and returns that user’s preference. With \(P_t(a,j)=\sum_{\pi:\,a\succ_\pi j}\mu_t(\pi)\) for distinct alternatives and \(P_t(a,a)=1/2\), define \(b_t(a)=K^{-1}\sum_j P_t(a,j)\) and dynamic Borda regret as \(\sum_{t=1}^T[b_t(a_t^*)-(b_t(i_t)+b_t(j_t))/2]\), where \(a_t^*\) maximizes \(b_t\). A significant phase ends at the earliest round by which every arm has an interval since the phase began on which its cumulative Borda gap is at least \(K^{1/3}|I|^{2/3}\). Can a policy using only sampled comparisons attain expected regret \(\widetilde O(\sum_i K^{1/3}(\sigma_{i+1}-\sigma_i)^{2/3})\) without knowing the phase boundaries \(\sigma_i\)?

The model it lives in

A discrete-time online learner selects pairs of alternatives and receives Bernoulli comparison feedback from users sampled from a population whose types are complete rankings. The population at round \(t\) is \(\mu_t\), and its pairwise probabilities induce the paper’s Borda scores. The learner does not know the masses and is evaluated by dynamic Borda regret.

The objection that survived

The learner sees comparison samples rather than the distribution’s support or masses, so the guarantee does not compute over the population or use its support size; the proponent acknowledges this limitation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the adaptive Borda dynamic-regret guarantee in Theorem 2 and its aggregate bound in Corollary 3. It makes no claim about the lower bound in Theorem 7, the Condorcet results, or the generic weighted-Borda framework.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper’s Borda regret lower bound remain tight when comparison matrices are restricted to those induced by distributions over complete rankings?
  • Can an adaptive regret guarantee usefully depend on the support size of the ranking distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a population-generated version of the paper’s Borda dueling-bandit problem. Take \(K\) candidate rankers or recommendations. A type is a user’s complete ranking of those \(K\) alternatives. At each round \(t\), a large user population has type distribution \(\mu_t\), with rational masses and support size \(\tau\). The learner chooses two alternatives, and a user sampled from that population says which they prefer. Thus
\[ P_t(i,j)=\sum_{\pi:\,i\succ_\pi j}\mu_t(\pi), \]
with \(P_t(i,i)=1/2\). The observed response is Bernoulli with mean \(P_t(i,j)\), just as in the paper. Here \(N\), the number of users, can be very large while \(\tau\ll N\): many users share each ranking type. The population may change between rounds, but time remains discrete.

Call the resulting problem Adaptive Population Borda Dueling. Its instance specifies \(K,T\), a finite set of ranking types, and a sequence of population distributions \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_T\); the learner sees comparison samples, not the masses, just as the paper’s learner does not know \(P_t\). Define \(b_t(a)=K^{-1}\sum_j P_t(a,j)\), let \(a_t^*\) maximize \(b_t\), and measure regret by
\[ \mathrm{Regret}_B=\sum_{t=1}^T\left[b_t(a_t^*)-\frac{b_t(i_t)+b_t(j_t)}2\right]. \]
Count significant Borda-winner phases using the paper’s Definition 1: an arm has significant regret on \([s_1,s_2]\) when its cumulative Borda gap is at least \(K^{1/3}(s_2-s_1)^{2/3}\), and a new phase begins when every arm has such a witnessing interval since the prior phase began. The question is whether a policy using only sampled comparisons can achieve the corresponding adaptive regret guarantee without knowing the phase boundaries; a solution is such an online policy and its uniform expected-regret bound.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that Algorithm 2 with the fixed-weight specification has
\[ \mathbb E[\mathrm{Regret}_B]\le \widetilde O\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\widetilde S_B} K^{1/3}(\tau_{i+1}-\tau_i)^{2/3}\right), \]
where \(\widetilde S_B\) is the number of significant Borda phases and \(\tau_i\) their boundaries. The guarantee is adaptive: the algorithm does not need to know the non-stationarity. The paper’s Corollary 3 also gives the coarser bound \(\widetilde O(K^{1/3}T^{2/3}\widetilde S_B^{1/3})\). Since the theorem holds for general preference matrices, it applies to the matrices induced by these ranking populations.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the paper’s online-regret sense: the named algorithm and its bound carry over directly. That is not yet a Class A result in ChoCo’s intended computational sense. The paper gives no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar; its named results concern regret. Theorem 7, for example, is an information-theoretic regret lower bound, not a computational-hardness result. So there is no honest basis here for saying discrete computational hardness transfers, or that the continuum is specifically hard.

The scope is just Borda arm selection. This does not mirror the paper’s Condorcet results or its generic weighted-Borda framework, and it does not continuize time. Further questions would be whether the ranking-type restriction changes the paper’s minimax lower bounds, whether guarantees can depend usefully on \(\tau\), and how a finite population with sampling without replacement compares with the mass model.

The soft spot is that the paper’s \(P_t\) already captures the pairwise probabilities the algorithm uses; it never needs the underlying type masses. The proposed population is a plausible and natural source of those probabilities, but the theorem does not compute over the society or exploit its support. An author could reasonably accept this as a population interpretation of their problem while judging it a thin mirror for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and I don’t think it passes ChoCo’s computational gate. It proves an adaptive expected-regret guarantee for an online learner receiving sampled comparisons. It does not classify the complexity of a problem on a society supplied as an input. The paper reports a runtime for its algorithm, but that runtime is for a sequential learning procedure; it is not an algorithmic result about computing an intervention, winner, or robustness measure from population masses. Theorem 7’s regret lower bound has the same mismatch.

The proposed ranking-type population is a plausible source of the pairwise probabilities \(P_t\). But in the paper’s model the learner sees only comparison samples, and its algorithm uses those samples to estimate scores. The population masses and support never enter the policy or its stated guarantee. Recasting \(P_t\) as a mixture over rankings therefore adds a latent description of the feedback distribution, not a computation over a continuous society. There is no role for support size \(\tau\), mass bit length, or a pricing problem of the kind that makes a ChoCo mirror computationally substantive.

The stronger formulation runs into a choice. If the rational masses are supplied to the learner before each round, it can compute the Borda scores and play a current winner, eliminating the exploration problem behind Theorem 2. If the masses are hidden to preserve that learning problem, the model is again an online bandit problem with a population interpretation; any new work would concern learning or regret under a restricted family of feedback distributions, not the computational complexity of the paper’s social-choice problem. One could make that an interesting high-multiplicity online-learning project, but it would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope, but I would not claim that no population-based version could ever be worthwhile. Large user populations make the proposed interpretation sensible, and the obstacle is not multiplicity. It is that the paper’s contribution is statistical online learning, while its population representation neither supplies a new society-level computational task nor appears in the learner’s computation.
