The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its numbered results concern incentive compatibility, convergence, and existence or impossibility of update kernels; none classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

The closest foothold is Theorem 4.15 (proved here): for a regular partition space on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), any prior \(\pi\), and observation \(o\), there exists a continuous update kernel that satisfies the Partition-Expected condition for PTNE and is arbitrarily bounded around \(o\). The proof constructs pyramid kernels and describes a way to compute their placement. That is a useful mechanism-construction result, but the theorem gives no finite input representation or running-time bound for arbitrary \(\pi\). It cannot honestly serve as an “in P” anchor. Theorem 4.14 (proved here) likewise gives an impossibility result about satisfying PI and convergence together for some prior and true distribution in \(\mathbb{R}^2\); it is not a computational hardness result.

There is a plausible population setting for future work: a large crowdsourcing workforce, grouped by complete worker type—such as task expertise, public and private beliefs, update rule, and payment-relevant preferences—with \(\mu_t\) representing each type’s share. Workers report observations \(r\in\mathbb{R}^d\), and the center uses peer reports to pay them. But the paper’s results study the continuous observation and distribution space while the agent population remains finite. A population-level computational question would need a new objective and a finite representation of the worker types and beliefs. That could be a sensible follow-on problem; it is not an anchor-backed continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational problem whose complexity a continuous-population mirror could classify. Its numbered results concern incentive compatibility, convergence, and existence or impossibility of update kernels. Theorem 4.15 is the best foothold, but “computable” there means finding a kernel placement for a given prior; the paper gives no finite input representation or running-time question. Theorem 4.14 likewise rules out a combination of update properties for some prior and true distribution, not an algorithmic task. So neither theorem anchors a complexity mirror.

A large workforce is a plausible setting, but aggregation seems to add little to the paper’s actual questions. In a homogeneous workforce, each worker’s expected payment already averages over a peer-report distribution. Replacing many identical workers by their population mass does not change that expectation or the incentive condition. In a heterogeneous workforce, cohort masses could change the mixture of peer reports. But then the proposed question is a new typewise incentive analysis: its inputs must describe each cohort’s beliefs and update process, and the paper permits arbitrary probability measures. Choosing finite representations for those objects could make a computational problem, but the representation and objective would be new modeling decisions, not a population mirror of a result here.

The best repair to that proposal is to use a finite set of complete worker types, each with a finitely represented prior and update rule, and ask whether PTNE truthfully elicits reports from every positive-mass type. That is coherent, but the mass vector matters only through the peer-report mixture; the theorem’s individual incentive arguments do not become population-complexity results merely by expressing that mixture as shares. One could study the repaired problem, but the paper offers little reason to expect a population continuum to be the source of its computational structure.

This negative case is not airtight. A carefully chosen finite representation and a type-dependent peer pool could yield a worthwhile algorithmic follow-on. The weakness is that it would be a new model and question, while none of the paper’s results identifies that computational target. The clearest verdict against a mirror is therefore not that such a model is impossible or meaningless, but that this paper gives no strong, result-backed reason to prioritize it.
