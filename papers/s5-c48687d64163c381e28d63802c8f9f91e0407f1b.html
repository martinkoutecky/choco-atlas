The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(H\) and a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type has a strict preference order over \(H\) and being unmatched, each school has a strict priority order over types, and each acceptable pair has rational weight \(w_{t,h}\), choose \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) and unmatched masses \(u_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+u_t=\mu_t\). Set each school's capacity to its assigned mass, \(q_h=\sum_t x_{t,h}\), and require stability: for every acceptable \((t,h)\), either \(u_t+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}=0\), or \(\sum_{t':\,t\succ_h t'}x_{t',h}=0\). Maximize \(\sum_{t,h}w_{t,h}x_{t,h}\); what is its complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A continuous population over complete student types, with rational mass per type, type-level school priorities, mass assignments, and school capacities chosen as assigned mass; optimize total edge weight subject to aggregate stability.

The objection that survived

The paper's strict school rankings make individual priority identity part of a fully specified type, while the mirror groups agents into tied priority classes and changes the stability relation; this bounds fidelity, though it does not rule out the tier-based analogue.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Problem 5 of Theorem 9, the single-round weighted stable capacity-design hardness result. It leaves Problems 1–4 and the paper's polynomial mechanisms, lattice results, enumeration result, and incentive-compatibility results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the weighted stable capacity-design problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is it hard even with divisible type mass?
  • How does the answer change when the number of schools or the number of types is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Max-Weight Stable Capacity Design, a mirror of Problem 5 in Theorem 9. Theorem 9 is proved in this paper: it says that Problems 1–5 are NP-hard. Problem 5 is the single-round problem: given students, schools with strict preference lists, and weights on student–school edges, choose school capacities and a stable matching that maximizes total edge weight. The paper proves its hardness by reducing from cardinal Set Cover, using schools for sets and students for elements.

In the continuous mirror, the instance is a finite set of schools \(H\) and a support \(T\) of student types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a strict preference order \(\succ_t\) over schools and remaining unmatched, and a rational weight \(w_{t,h}\) for each acceptable school \(h\). Each school has a strict priority order \(\succ_h\) over types; people within a type are interchangeable, so a school may be indifferent among them. A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) and unmatched mass \(u_t\ge 0\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+u_t=\mu_t\). The chosen capacity of school \(h\) is \(q_h=\sum_t x_{t,h}\): the capacity vector is still a decision, now measured in population mass.

Require stability in the direct blocking-pair sense: for each acceptable pair \((t,h)\), either no mass of type \(t\) is assigned below \(h\) or left unmatched, or no lower-priority mass is assigned to \(h\). In symbols, either \(u_t+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}=0\), or \(\sum_{t':\,t\succ_h t'}x_{t',h}=0\). Then ask for a stable assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}w_{t,h}x_{t,h}\). This preserves the underlying capacity-design and weighted stable-matching question; it makes the population the continuous object. It does not continuize the outcome space in place of the population.

A plausible regime is a large school district planning admissions for tens of thousands of students, where many students share the same school rankings, priority profile, and weight vector. The types are those complete profiles, so \(\tau\) can be far smaller than the number of students even if there are many schools. Strict individual tie-breaking within a profile can determine *which people* get seats, but not the mass assigned to that profile or the objective when its members are otherwise identical. The authors could recognize this as their Problem 5 at high multiplicity: it retains the chosen capacities, stability, and weighted objective.

My first bet is Class A: allowing a type’s mass to split among schools could turn the all-or-nothing choices in the Set Cover reduction into fractional allocations, opening the way to continuous optimization. The exact question is whether this weighted stable capacity-design problem has an efficient algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\). I would not claim it is already an LP: the no-blocking condition above is disjunctive, so finding a tractable stable-allocation formulation is part of the research. Natural follow-ups are whether the problem is polynomial for fixed \(\tau\) or fixed \(m\), and whether any hardness remains when types can split.

The soft spot is that Problem 5 is a single-round, capacity-design variant, rather than one of the paper’s main two-round mechanisms. Also, the Set Cover reduction may still matter when its elements induce many distinct types; fractional mass does not by itself prove that the hardness disappears. Still, this is a precise mirror of a named hardness result, and its tractability question directly tests what continuizing the population changes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Problem 5 is a real anchor: Theorem 9 proves a named computational problem NP-hard, so the negative case cannot dismiss the paper as lacking a result to mirror. The strongest objection is that the paper’s own model makes high multiplicity hard to obtain. A student’s type must include their school priorities and edge weights as well as their preferences. Since every school has a strict ranking of students, no two students have the same complete priority profile. In the literal model, \(\tau=n\): the population has not been compressed, and assigning a fraction of a singleton type would let one student be split across schools.

The proponent’s repair is to group students with the same preference, weight, and priority *class*, and let tie-breaking within a class affect identities but not aggregate mass. That is plausible for a district with large cohorts and coarse priority tiers. But it changes the paper’s strict school rankings into tied priorities and replaces individual stability with a fractional blocking rule. That may be a sensible problem, but it is the mirror’s key modelling step—not something justified by the paper’s stated model. The Set Cover reduction also gives distinct incidence patterns to its element students; those students generally have distinct types, so it does not itself supply evidence that the hard instances have a small support.

Those points make the mirror less direct than the case for suggests, but they do not defeat its better version. Priority tiers with within-class tie-breaking are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and asking whether the resulting weighted stable capacity-design problem is tractable is worthwhile even if its answer is hardness. The fact that it is a one-round problem rather than the paper’s main dynamic setting is not enough to rule it out either.

So the honest negative case is limited: the proposed mirror is not a faithful continuization of the paper’s strict-priority model without a meaningful change to type and stability. I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. The tier-based version survives that objection, leaving the universal negative case weak.
