The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant anchor-based case from this paper: it contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1, proved here, says the Shapley rewards sum to the grand coalition’s value, so the reallocation is efficient. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that if the finite TU game is convex, the Shapley allocation lies in the core and is stable. Proposition 2 gives an additive-reward condition that makes the game convex. These are relevant mathematical results, but none asserts a problem is tractable, hard, or has a particular computational complexity. Algorithm 1 is unnumbered as a theorem and the paper gives no complexity bound for it.

The most promising *future* mirror would concern Theorem 2’s stability claim. Consider a large fleet in which many CAVs share a complete driving type—such as lane, speed and comfort class, reward weights, and communication capability—with \(\tau\) types and rational masses \(\mu_t\). For each coalition mass vector \(x\leq\mu\), let \(W(x)\) be its expected discounted aggregate reward when that mass coordinates and the rest of the traffic follows a specified policy. A Continuous Shapley Reward Reallocation problem could take an explicit representation of \(W\) and ask for the per-unit type payments \(q_t\), using the replica limit of finite Shapley allocations (or a specified Aumann–Shapley extension), and whether they satisfy

\[ \sum_t \mu_t q_t=W(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t x_tq_t\geq W(x)\ \text{for every }x\leq\mu. \]

Those conditions mean the full fleet’s reward is distributed and no coalition mass can obtain more by leaving. This is a precise question about the underlying reward-allocation problem, not about its encoding. Theorem 2 is the motivation for asking whether convexity of the mass-valued game guarantees stability; it is not a qualifying anchor under your stated rule. The expected computational picture is tractability for explicit, structured \(W\), with general core certification potentially hard because it requires checking all coalition masses. That is a proposed research direction, not a result of this paper.

The regime is plausible for large, repeated fleets or platoons with relatively few shared driving types. It is much less convincing for the paper’s particular 30-vehicle experiment: its agents have rich, changing sensor states, and grouping them without losing the coalition values may be difficult. That is also the mirror’s weakest point. In addition, the continuum version needs a defensible extension of Shapley value to a population with divisible mass; choosing Aumann–Shapley is a substantive modeling step.

So the paper offers a credible seed for a continuous-population stability problem, but no eligible named anchor for the requested case. The mirror would cover the finite-game efficiency and stability claims, not the paper’s MARL algorithm’s empirical performance. Continuous vehicle states or episode time alone would not supply the population continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result. Theorem 1 says the Shapley allocation is efficient in the game-theoretic sense: the payments sum to the grand coalition’s value. That is not a claim about an efficient algorithm. Theorem 2 says convexity puts that allocation in the core. Proposition 2 gives a sufficient condition for convexity. None states a complexity bound or poses a computational problem. Algorithm 1 does not fill the gap: its Shapley calculation explicitly ranges over coalitions, and the paper gives no complexity analysis for that step.

The proponent’s best version—large fleets with a few driving types, a coalition worth function \(W(x)\), and a question about computing per-type payments and checking core constraints—is coherent in principle. But it needs a new computational input model. In this paper, a vehicle’s position, local traffic, sensors, and current state affect its actions and rewards. Keep those details in a complete type and the number of types may grow with the fleet. Collapse vehicles into broad classes and the class masses alone may no longer determine coalition values. A mean-field traffic model could address that, but it would supply new dynamics and a new worth function rather than continuize a computational result the paper proved.

Nor does the continuum Shapley extension itself provide the missing computational question: continuum and limiting Shapley values have a substantial earlier literature, including Aumann and Shapley’s work on non-atomic games. That is not a continuous-computational collision; it means the proposed contribution would have to come from the chosen representation and algorithms. Existing computational Shapley work likewise studies complexity for specific game representations, so the answer depends on how \(W\) is encoded. [Aumann and Shapley, *Values of Non-Atomic Games*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Values_of_Non_atomic_Games.html?id=DsCzuwEACAAJ), [Skibski, “Complexity of Computing the Shapley Value in Games with Externalities”](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5601).

This is not an airtight argument that no useful project could emerge: a carefully specified mean-field fleet and succinct game representation might support one. The negative case is that it would be a new computational model built around the paper’s motivation, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results. The paper’s named results concern allocation properties, while the computational task that could make a mirror worthwhile remains to be formulated.
