Why no mirror

The dynamic-convergence corollary has computational character, but its continuous analogue would move from atomic Nash play to a Wardrop equilibrium, where an individual has zero mass and cannot change company loads. The proposed quadratic program is a precise and plausible labor-market extension, but it replaces the paper’s myopic dynamics with centralized welfare optimization, so the anchored result does not carry over as the same question.

What the mirror covers

The proposed model addresses Fair Value welfare and equilibrium efficiency; it does not continue the paper’s finite best-response path or its move-count guarantee, and it leaves the Labor Union and Shapley results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Fair Value model. Its setting already has the right population story: many workers choose among companies, and each company’s profit depends on which workers join it. A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large labor market where workers fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of standardized skill profiles. A type records the skills and qualifications relevant to productivity; the society is the mass \(\mu_t\) of each type. Each person still joins one company. Fractional assignments describe what fraction of a type’s mass joins each company.

The closest computational-style anchor is the first Fair Value corollary in §4.1, proved in this paper from the Fair Value theorem and earlier lemmas. The supplied text does not render a corollary number or give that corollary a label, so I can’t quote its printed number without inventing one. The corollary says, in full: for every Fair Value game and every \(\alpha\ge0\), the \(\alpha\)-price of anarchy is at most \(2+\alpha\), and in particular the price of anarchy is at most \(2\). Also, from any initial state and for every \(\epsilon>0\), the basic Nash dynamic reaches a state with total profit at least \(\mathrm{opt}/2\,(1-\epsilon)\) in at most \(\lceil(n/2)\ln(1/\epsilon)\rceil\) steps; the basic \(\alpha\)-Nash dynamic reaches total profit at least \(\mathrm{opt}/(2+\alpha)\,(1-\epsilon)\) in at most \(\lceil(n/(2+\alpha))\ln(1/\epsilon)\rceil\) steps. These are explicit bounds on dynamics, but the paper gives no P/NP or other complexity classification for a succinctly represented input; it says its results are non-computational because arbitrary cooperative games have exponential descriptions.

Here is a precise continuous problem for the equilibrium-efficiency part of that anchor. Call it Continuous Fair-Value Equilibrium Computation. An instance has companies \(M\), types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and, for each company \(j\), a rational production function
\[ f_j(z)=a_j^\top z-\tfrac12 z^\top Q_jz \]
on \([0,1]^\tau\), where \(Q_j\) is positive semidefinite and entrywise nonnegative, and \(a_{jt}\ge\sum_u Q_{j,tu}\). These conditions make profit increasing, concave, and diminishing in every type’s mass. A feasible allocation is \(x_{tj}\ge0\) with \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\); company \(j\)’s load is \(x_j\), and its profit is \(f_j(x_j)\). A type-\(t\) worker at company \(j\) receives the marginal-profit wage \(\partial_t f_j(x_j)\). A solution is an allocation \(x\) such that every company receiving positive mass of type \(t\) offers that type a wage at least as high as every other company’s.

The expected answer is tractable, with zero equilibrium inefficiency. Maximize \(\sum_j f_j(x_j)\) over the feasible allocations, a concave quadratic program with \(m\tau\) variables. Its optimality conditions are exactly the stated equilibrium conditions. Conversely, concavity implies that any allocation satisfying those conditions maximizes total profit. Thus every continuous equilibrium is optimal, giving price of anarchy \(1\). This is a stronger continuous counterpart to the paper’s factor-\(2\) bound: splitting type mass removes the discrete local inefficiency that the paper bounds.

The mirror is recognizable because it retains the same decision, marginal-productivity pay, and total-profit objective. The continuous derivative is the natural per-unit version of the paper’s discrete marginal contribution. Its scope is specifically the Fair Value result; it does not cover the Labor Union model’s arrival order or the Shapley model’s averaged shares. The discrete dynamic bound also does not transfer directly: an atomless person’s move transfers zero mass, so the paper’s \(O(n\log(1/\epsilon))\) step guarantee needs a separate continuous-time or mass-update formulation. A useful further question is whether a natural continuous best-response dynamic converges efficiently, and what dependence it has on \(\tau\).

The weakest point is the production-function restriction. A general monotone submodular set function need not come with a unique or suitable differentiable concave extension. The proposed mirror therefore supports a meaningful, concrete labor-market regime, but it does not continuize every Fair Value game in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the Fair Value corollary in §4.1 is not a complexity result for computing an equilibrium. Its price-of-anarchy bound is a welfare guarantee; its dynamic bound counts best-response moves but assigns no cost to computing a move. The paper itself says its results are non-computational and points to succinctly represented subclasses as future work. So the proposed quadratic program is a new computational problem: it replaces a path of myopic deviations by a centralized computation of a welfare-maximizing allocation.

That difference matters in the continuum. A single worker has zero mass, so an individual deviation no longer changes company loads or the potential. The proposed wage condition and concave program give a sensible nonatomic market equilibrium, but they do not preserve the paper’s dynamic. To keep a dynamic, one must specify how positive mass moves—its rate, granularity, and scheduling. The corollary supplies no canonical continuous version of those choices.

This does not support a strong objection to high multiplicity. Large labor markets with many workers sharing a modest number of skill profiles are plausible, and count-dependent, diminishing-returns production is a natural model for them. One could extend that production to type masses and study a mass-flow dynamic or equilibrium algorithm. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they are new computational questions rather than mirrors of a complexity result in this paper.

So the negative case is narrow and weak: the proponent’s anchor does not directly yield the proposed computational mirror. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in that labor-market regime.
