What the mirror covers

The paper’s Propositions 1–7 and Corollary 1 characterize equilibrium existence and comparative statics in finite groups; no continuous computational mirror is anchored to them.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the programme’s anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Propositions 1–7 and Corollary 1 are proved here, but they concern equilibrium existence and comparative statics in a finite Bayesian game.

The closest result is Proposition 5 (proved here): for any odd \(g\le n\), a \(g\)-PIE exists if and only if at least \(g\) legislators have challenger quality outside \((\mu_i^g(1),\mu_i^g(0)]\). That is an equilibrium characterization, not a result that a computational problem is in P, is hard, or has another complexity classification.

A continuous population of legislators might be imaginable, but turning this characterization into an algorithmic problem over a distribution would be a new question. In particular, the paper’s strategic mechanism depends on an individual legislator’s report sometimes changing the collective decision; in a nonatomic model, individual reports are not pivotal. So I have no paper-anchored continuous problem to hand to a prover, and presenting one as a mirror of these results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only candidate is Proposition 5, and it does not provide a computational anchor. It characterizes when a finite group of named legislators can sustain a public-interest equilibrium, given their challengers’ qualities. The paper proves equilibrium existence and comparative statics; it does not state a complexity result or pose an algorithmic problem. Turning the condition into “decide whether an equilibrium exists” would be a new computational wrapper around the theorem, not a computational result the paper contributes.

The strongest attempted upgrade would be: given a distribution of legislator types and electoral conditions, find the largest coalition that can sustain a public-interest equilibrium. But the paper’s mechanism is individual accountability: a legislator may misreport because that report can change the group’s policy, which changes the legislator’s own reelection chance. In a nonatomic population, one legislator’s deviation cannot change the aggregate decision. The pivotality that connects the paper’s equilibrium conditions to its central question disappears. A continuum model can allow truthful reporting, but it also leaves each legislator indifferent among reports; recovering a meaningful strategic problem would require an added equilibrium selection or coordinated bloc behavior. Either would be a new model, not a mirror of Proposition 5.

The same problem defeats the other plausible anchors. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize equilibrium existence; Proposition 3 tracks how group size changes voters’ beliefs about an individual representative; Propositions 4 and 6 rely on finite majorities and coalition membership; Proposition 7 depends on the incentive of an individual who can be pivotal. Distributing electoral conditions across a continuum does not preserve those individual-level links. Treating a positive mass of identical legislators as a single strategic bloc could restore them, but that changes the unit of agency.

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s stated gate: there is no named computational result to mirror, and the natural population limit removes the individual pivotality that drives the paper’s main results. It cannot prove that no separately designed computational question about a continuum of legislative types would be worthwhile. Such a question would need its own algorithmic objective and a nontrivial account of strategic behavior; neither is supplied by this paper.
