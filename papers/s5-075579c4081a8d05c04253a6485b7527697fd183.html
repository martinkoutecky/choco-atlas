What the mirror covers

No named computational result is covered; Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 establish axiomatic properties of approval voting and Condorcet winners.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large political party whose members have a small number \(\tau\) of complete preference types over policy and membership alternatives. A continuous version could describe the society by type masses \(\mu_t\) and its decision by a policy \(x\) and retained mass \(q_t\) for each type. Membership is central to this paper, so making retained membership a mass is a natural population continuization.

But the paper contains no named computational result of the kind this task requires. Proposition 1, proved here, says that approval voting with a monotonic tie-break is consistent, efficient, neutral, and satisfies participation, and is anonymous on profiles where no tie-break is needed. Proposition 2, also proved here, gives corresponding properties for the Condorcet winner at resolute profiles. These are results about axioms of social choice rules, not about complexity, algorithms, or a formulation the paper computes with.

So there is no valid computational anchor, and I can’t honestly give a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor* or predict its place in the tractability trichotomy. One could propose computing an approval winner over type-level retained masses, but defining preferences and approvals over fractional membership would add modeling choices the paper does not make. That would be a new computational question, not a mirror grounded in a named result here. The credible positive case is that the population regime makes such an extension worth exploring; the weak point is decisive for this assignment: the paper provides no computational result for the mirror to carry forward.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The large party with a few recurring preference types is a credible high-multiplicity setting. I would not reject it on the ground that membership is individual, or that participation becomes vacuous: a continuous model can keep each type’s individual rationality condition and represent the retained population by masses. That establishes a sensible population regime, but it does not supply the computational question ChoCo is looking for.

The paper’s named results are Proposition 1, an axiomatic characterization of approval voting’s properties, and Proposition 2, an axiomatic result for Condorcet winners at resolute profiles. Neither states a complexity bound, an algorithmic problem, or a finite input representation. Both have natural continuous *axiomatic* counterparts: replace approval counts and pairwise majorities with masses, then ask whether consistency and the other properties still hold. Those are coherent lifts, but they do not turn the paper’s results into computational ones.

The stronger rescue would be to ask for winner determination. For approval voting, if preferences depend only on each agent’s own inclusion and the policy, let \(A_t\) be the outcomes for which type \(t\) accepts membership. The stable alternative for outcome \(x\) retains exactly the types in \(A_t\), and its approval mass is \(\sum_{t:x\in A_t}\mu_t\). Choosing the winner then reduces to comparing these sums across outcomes. That is a sensible continuous computation, but in this natural restricted model it is a direct calculation, not a complexity landscape furnished by the paper. For Condorcet, pairwise majority can likewise be written as a mass comparison; the paper supplies no computational problem for searching the resulting alternative space.

Making either computation richer means allowing preferences to depend on the entire retained composition. Then each type needs a representation of its preferences over a continuum of mass vectors and outcomes. The paper gives no such representation; its preferences rank subsets of named agents. Choosing, for example, linear utilities or piecewise descriptions would define a new computational problem whose complexity depends on that added language. It might be worthwhile research, but it would be an extension motivated by the paper, not a computational mirror of either proposition.

So the negative case is strong on ChoCo’s stated scope: the political-party scenario survives as a population model, and both propositions admit continuous axiomatic translations, but neither yields a paper-grounded computational anchor. The broader claim that no useful computational project could grow from this model is weaker; it cannot be ruled out, and those projects should be described as new questions rather than mirrors.
