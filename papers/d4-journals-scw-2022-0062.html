The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational complexity result. Its theorems concern the reliability of voting schemes and which order maximizes reliability; none classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized-tractable. So there is no eligible anchor for the requested per-anchor mirror questions.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: with two equiprobable states and three jurors of distinct abilities \(0<a<b<c\leq 1\), the unique order maximizing reliability under honest sequential majority voting is \((b,c,a)\). It is a sharp optimization result, but for exactly three jurors; it says nothing about the complexity of finding an order.

A plausible extension would consider a large forecasting organization with many experts in a small number of ability types. Let \(T\subseteq[0,1]\) be the types, with rational mass distribution \(\mu\). For each growing jury, type-\(a\) experts have the paper’s signal law, vote honestly after seeing earlier votes, and are arranged in an order chosen before signals arrive. The question would be to find orders whose majority-verdict reliability approaches the best possible limit as the type proportions approach \(\mu\). This is a recognizable extension of the paper’s ordering problem, but it is not an analogue of Theorem 2’s three-person result.

The weakness is substantial: each individual vote can change later voters’ beliefs, even as that voter’s population mass tends to zero. A continuum schedule therefore does not by itself specify the sequential process; a limiting convention is needed, and its effect on the answer would need to be established. I would call this a plausible research direction for the authors, not a continuous mirror anchored by a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the anchor: this paper proves no computational complexity result. Theorem 2 characterizes the best of six orders for three fixed abilities. The other named results are probability comparisons and structural claims for fixed juries; for larger juries, the paper offers simulations and a conjecture. There is no algorithmic or hardness result here whose continuous-population counterpart the programme can chart.

The best proposed mirror is stronger than a literal continuization of Theorem 2: take a large panel with repeated ability types and ask for an order maximizing the probability of a correct verdict. That is a recognizable extension of the paper’s larger-jury discussion. Its difficulty is that population mass does not scale the influence of a vote. For a first voter of ability \(a\), an \(A\) vote changes the public odds by the likelihood ratio \((2+a)/(2-a)\), regardless of whether that voter represents one third or one billionth of the panel. Yet that vote can change every later voter’s threshold.

So a distribution over ability types, even with a schedule assigning types to positions, does not by itself yield a faithful continuum process. In the finite panels, the first few voters occupy zero mass in the limit while still shaping the verdict through herding. For a fixed finite type set whose abilities are bounded below \(1\), the usual cascade mechanism can leave almost all later voters following a direction established by that vanishing prefix. A continuum that keeps each vote’s full Bayesian effect must retain that finite prefix or supply some other boundary rule; a continuum that scales each vote’s effect by its mass changes the information and threshold dynamics. Neither is simply the paper’s population made continuous.

The boundary cases do not give a uniform escape. A positive mass of ability-\(1\) voters removes the bounded-evidence condition behind that cascade picture and can lead to full learning as the panel grows. A sequence of panels with increasingly strong but vanishingly rare experts may instead depend on those experts’ finite positions, which a limiting mass distribution can lose. These are different asymptotic regimes, not one stable mirror of the fixed-jury ordering theorem.

That leaves a plausible research direction in high-multiplicity *finite* panels: compress repeated abilities and study the complexity of choosing an order. The paper’s discussion of larger juries makes that direction especially recognizable to its authors. But it is a new computational question, and preserving its sequential information dynamics appears to require finite order details that a continuous society does not capture. I would therefore argue against a continuous mirror under ChoCo’s population-continuization gate, while admitting the negative case is not airtight: the finite high-multiplicity ordering problem may be worthwhile on its own.
