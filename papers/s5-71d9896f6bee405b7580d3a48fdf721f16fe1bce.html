Why no mirror

Theorem 1 rules out finite welfare approximation for every deterministic DSIC+IR mechanism, even with unlimited computation; it does not show that computing an outcome is hard or provide a computational guarantee. The proposed atomless, positive-supply market is a plausible large-market extension, but that does not make this structural mechanism impossibility a computational result, so the sole anchor fails bit (a).

What the mirror covers

The proposed question extends only Theorem 1's deterministic online welfare guarantee; it leaves the randomized, stochastic-arrival, and Bayesian directions in Section 4 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does any uniform finite approximation factor exist for this atomless, positive-supply model?
  • If one exists, what is the best factor as a function of \(q_1,q_2\), and can a mechanism be computed from the finite type support and masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a large online ad market with many advertisers but few distinct valuation schedules. Each advertiser values two slot types and wants at most one slot. The platform receives bids before supply arrives, must allocate the first slot type immediately, and may learn only later whether the second type is available. Millions of advertisers may share a small set of valuation types, so the number of agents is large while the support size \(\tau\) is modest. The continuous object is the advertiser population: a type \(t=(v_{t1},v_{t2})\) has mass \(\mu_t\), its fraction of the population.

My anchor is Theorem 1 (Main Theorem), proved in this paper. It says that with two unit-demand bidders, two item types, the first item arriving for sure and the second possibly arriving, there is no deterministic DSIC+IR mechanism with any finite approximation factor for social welfare—even with unbounded computational resources. The allocation of the first item must be irrevocable when it arrives; payments may be computed after the supply is known. This is the paper’s central impossibility, not a result it cites.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Continuous Online Unit-Demand Auction. Fix rational per-capita supplies \(q_1,q_2>0\), with \(q_1+q_2\le 1\). An instance has a finite set \(T\subseteq\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^2\) of advertiser valuation types and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to one. A mass \(q_1\) of type-1 slots arrives first; a mass \(q_2\) of type-2 slots either arrives later or does not arrive. Each advertiser reports both values before the first arrival, receives at most one slot, and has quasi-linear utility. A deterministic direct mechanism must allocate the first supply immediately, may allocate the second only if it arrives, and may set payments after learning the supply outcome. Each individual must find truthful reporting dominant for either supply outcome and be ex-post individually rational.

For type \(t\), write \(x^s_{t,i}\) for the mass of that type assigned slot \(i\) when the supply outcome is \(s\). These are aggregate rates: each advertiser still receives an integral bundle, while a fraction of a type cohort may receive each bundle. In particular, the first-stage allocation must agree across supply outcomes, and the type-level allocations must satisfy the supply limits and unit-demand constraint. Welfare is \(\sum_t(v_{t1}x^s_{t,1}+v_{t2}x^s_{t,2})\); the benchmark is the maximum-weight matching for that population and realized supply. The question is whether, for each fixed \((q_1,q_2)\), there is a uniform deterministic DSIC+IR mechanism and finite \(H\), independent of \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\mu\), whose welfare is at least \(1/H\) of the benchmark for every society and both supply outcomes. A solution is the mechanism, its payment rule, and a proof of those guarantees. The aggregate variables alone would not suffice: the witness must also establish individual incentive compatibility.

This preserves the paper’s central combination—multi-parameter bidders, online supply, irrevocable early allocation, deferred payments, and welfare under dominant-strategy truthfulness. Scaling supply with population is a co-modification that makes the high-multiplicity regime meaningful: the slots represent a positive share of a large market’s inventory rather than vanishingly few units. This is only a mirror of Theorem 1, not of the paper’s later questions about randomized mechanisms or stochastic arrivals.

My prior is Class A: aggregate cohort allocation may permit a finite approximation through thresholds or continuous optimization, and deterministic division of a large population can provide some of the role-mixing that is unavailable in a two-bidder mechanism. The next questions are whether such an \(H\) exists, what the best \(H(q_1,q_2)\) is, and whether a mechanism can be computed efficiently from the type support and masses.

The weakest point is the scale change. Theorem 1 concerns exactly two bidders and one copy of each slot type; its impossibility does not transfer to a population with macroscopic per-capita supply. In fact, the paper itself points to a need for randomness, and a continuum may let a deterministic mechanism divide a cohort across outcomes. That is a real reason the mirror might be tractable, not evidence that it is the same theorem in disguise. But the proposed question keeps the authors’ incentive and timing problem intact, and asks directly whether their impossibility survives in a natural high-multiplicity market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1. It is a substantial approximation impossibility, but a weak anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme: it rules out every finite approximation even with unbounded computation. The theorem has no running-time claim or input-size family; it fixes two bidders and two item types. The proposed mirror asks whether a truthful mechanism with some finite \(H\) exists. That is a coherent question, but it is primarily a large-market mechanism-existence question, not a complexity result inherited from this paper. Adding a requirement to compute the mechanism efficiently would create a further question the theorem itself does not address.

There is also a gap in the proposed continuous model. The mass vector \(\mu\) and aggregate allocations \(x^s_{t,i}\) do not specify which individual advertiser gets a slot or pays what. A single advertiser’s report has zero mass, so it leaves \(\mu\) unchanged; the aggregate variables cannot describe that advertiser’s deviation payoff. A fractional allocation rate therefore needs a rule assigning a deterministic subset of each cohort. The model must say how that rule identifies individuals—through labels, an ordering, or some other structure. That information is absent from the stated support-and-masses input. The analyst acknowledges that aggregate variables alone do not establish individual truthfulness, but the proposed question does not yet supply the missing allocation model.

The strongest repair is a continuum of advertisers with measurable individual identities, a deterministic allocation of measurable subsets, individual DSIC and IR, and an algorithmic input and output representation. That would be a serious research question. It also changes the paper’s setting substantially: its impossibility concerns two individually pivotal bidders, while an individual in the repaired population has zero mass and cannot shift the aggregate market. The positive per-capita supply makes the welfare limit meaningful, but it changes the one-copy supply problem into an extensive-supply market. These changes may be justified; they mean the resulting theorem would be a new large-market mechanism-design result, rather than a close continuization of Theorem 1.

I cannot honestly make the universal case that no scenario is worthwhile. A market with many advertisers sharing valuation schedules is plausible, and the paper itself motivates its model through online advertising. The identity and computation gaps weaken the proposed mirror as stated; a precise repaired model could still be valuable. If the programme counts truthful-mechanism existence and approximation as computational questions, Theorem 1 remains a viable anchor, and this negative case does not defeat it.
