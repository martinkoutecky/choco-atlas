What the mirror covers

The restated question covers subgroup-estimator inference for a fixed index policy; it leaves out the paper’s finite-sample dependence analysis, experimental power comparisons, and extensions for covariate adjustment, multiple timesteps, and sequential allocation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is Continuous \(\alpha\)-Mass Policy-Effect Inference. It treats the population as mass over complete agent types: \(t\) records covariates, the index value, and the agent’s potential reward behavior. A policy assigns treatment to exactly \(\alpha\) mass, choosing the lowest-index types; any mass tied at the cutoff is split according to a specified tie-breaking rule. If \(y_t\) is the treated mass of type \(t\), the target is
\[ \Delta_\alpha(\mu,\Upsilon)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{t\in T}y_t\bigl(R_t(1)-R_t(0)\bigr). \]
The inference problem is: given a fixed index policy, \(\alpha\), and randomized-trial observations sampled from an unknown type distribution \(\mu\), return an estimate of \(\Delta_\alpha\) and a confidence interval with asymptotically correct coverage. The treatment remains binary; the fixed treatment budget becomes an exact mass budget.

A plausible regime is a large service-call programme with recurring, standardized beneficiary profiles. A type would need to capture everything relevant to both the allocation index and reward behavior—for example, a covariate profile and a shared response model—not merely a shared index. With many beneficiaries and substantially fewer such profiles, \(\tau\ll n\) is credible. The mMitra setting gives the policy-evaluation story, while a deployment organized around repeated response-model templates gives the strongest high-multiplicity regime. The authors would recognize the question: their paper already models agents as draws from a population distribution and evaluates fixed-fraction index policies in randomized trials.

There is, however, no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in the paper. The closest named result is Theorem 4.1, presented here as informal and proved in the authors’ full version: under mild assumptions,
\[ \sqrt{n}\bigl(\theta^{\mathrm{SG}}_{n,\alpha}(\pi)-\tau^{\mathrm{new}}_{n,\alpha}(\pi)\bigr)\big/\sqrt{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{SG}}} \;\xrightarrow{d}\;N(0,1), \]
where \(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{SG}}\) is computable in closed form from the RCT results. This is an asymptotic inference guarantee, not a result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized-hard. So it cannot serve as an anchor under the requested rule, and there is no per-anchor case to make.

As a population functional, the proposed question looks tractable: with finite support, its allocation and value can be computed by sorting types by index and summing their masses and rewards. The subgroup estimator and Theorem 4.1 offer support for statistically tractable inference. But the paper does not establish a runtime bound for this continuous problem, nor does it classify its computational complexity. Further questions include how ties affect the target, how to compare two policies on the same continuous population, and how the formulation extends to sequential treatment allocation.

The weak point is fundamental: the paper already uses a distribution \(P\) over agents and develops asymptotic inference as \(n\) grows. The proposed mirror is therefore a population-limit reading of its existing statistical problem, rather than a new computational question obtained by continuizing a finite society. This is a plausible continuous-population formulation, but the paper gives no named complexity result to anchor the ChoCo-style case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named result about computational complexity to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 4.1 establishes asymptotic normality and supports valid confidence intervals; the experiments compare their coverage and width. The paper gives no runtime classification, hardness result, or algorithm for a computational problem over societies. Under the programme’s criteria, that is decisive: the proponent’s own closest anchor is a statistical inference guarantee, not a computational result.

The proposed mass-effect target is also already close to a quantity in the paper. The authors model agents as i.i.d. draws from a distribution \(P\) over covariates and reward functions, define the population quantile \(q_\alpha\), and study the effect among agents with index at most that cutoff, \(\tau^q_\alpha(\Upsilon)\). They then analyze inference for a finite-sample policy target and relate it to that quantile-based population quantity. Interpreting the limit as mass below an index cutoff is a natural description of the paper’s existing statistical model, not a new computational question created by replacing a finite society with a continuous one.

The stronger finite-support version does not supply the missing computational problem. If the masses and complete agent types are known—including the reward behavior needed to evaluate the policy—the target follows by sorting types by index and summing their mass-weighted effects. A fractional split at the cutoff handles ties. If those quantities are unknown and must be estimated from trial data, the task is statistical inference of the kind the paper already develops. Either way, the mirror adds no new complexity landscape for ChoCo to chart.

A large programme with recurring beneficiary profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s mMitra example makes that scenario credible. That supports the *sense* of a mass-based model; it does not create a computational result in this paper for the model to mirror. An even more ambitious question—optimizing over policies, especially across sequential treatment rounds—would be a different problem. The paper evaluates specified policies and their estimators; it does not give a complexity result for policy design.

So the proponent’s anchor does not survive: Theorem 4.1 is outside the required class of results, while the proposed continuous estimand is already implicit in the paper’s distributional and quantile formulation. The negative case is strong for this paper under ChoCo’s computational scope. The universal claim is weaker: a future paper on treatment allocation might prove a computational result whose high-multiplicity mirror is worthwhile. That possibility does not give this paper such an anchor.
