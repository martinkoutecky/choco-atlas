Why no mirror

Theorem 4.4 is an axiomatic impossibility result, and the paper reports no named algorithm, complexity bound, or computational formulation result. A weighted audit over ranking types is statable and follows the paper’s empirical comparisons, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed weighted audit extends Section 5’s clone-proximity and deletion-response comparisons to a distribution over rankings; it does not turn the axiomatic results into computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s empirical audit: represent voters by their complete rankings, and use their population shares to measure clone proximity and track the effect of candidate deletion. The limitation is central, though: the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor this mirror to. Its theorems concern axioms, and its computations are empirical. The cited work on the complexity of identifying approximate clones is prior work, not a result of this paper.

The closest result is Theorem 4.4, proved here: for \(m\ge 4\), IRV, Ranked Pairs, and Schulze are not weakly independent of \(\alpha\)-deletion clones for any \(\alpha>0\), nor of \(\beta\)-swap clones for any \(\beta>0\). That is a worst-case axiomatic impossibility result, not a classification of a computational problem, so it cannot honestly serve as the required anchor.

A plausible scenario is a large ranked-choice electorate with many voters casting a relatively small number \(\tau\) of repeated full rankings—for example, where party guidance or shared voting patterns produce recurring ballots. Each type is a ranking \(\sigma\in S_m\), and \(\mu_\sigma\) is the fraction of voters with that ranking. The regime of interest has \(N\gg\tau\). The paper does not report support sizes for its election data, so this is a plausible setting for the mirror, not an empirical finding from the paper.

For this society, the paper’s two proximity measures become
\[ \alpha_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\sigma:\,|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|>1}\mu_\sigma,\qquad \beta_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma\bigl(|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|-1\bigr). \]
Deleting a candidate projects each ranking onto the remaining candidates and retains its mass.

One precise, but unanchored, candidate is the Minimum Weak-Spoiler Clone Distance for Schulze. Its instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over at most \(\tau\) rankings. For each pair \(x,y\), compute the Schulze winner set before deletion and after deleting \(x\) or \(y\). A deletion preserves the outcome when the paper’s two weak-independence conditions hold: the winners outside \(\{x,y\}\) are unchanged, and whether the pair contains a winner agrees with whether the remaining member wins after deletion. The task is to return a pair for which neither deletion preserves the outcome and whose \(\alpha_\mu(x,y)\) is minimum; report its \(\beta_\mu(x,y)\) and the relevant winner sets as well, or report that no such pair exists. This captures the paper’s empirical comparison between clone proximity and winner changes.

I expect this audit problem to be tractable (Class A): compute the two distances for each pair from the \(\tau\) types, then evaluate Schulze on the original society and the two projected societies. Exact rational arithmetic and the usual polynomial-time beatpath computation suffice; a direct implementation is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). This covers the paper’s empirical proximity and deletion-response analysis, not its full axiom theory. Natural follow-ups include minimizing \(\beta\) instead, counting violations below a threshold, and studying IRV or Ranked Pairs.

The weak point is that this reframes empirical calculations as a compressed-input algorithmic task; it does not expose a complexity question the paper itself studies. A rational distribution can be expanded into a finite election by multiplying through by a common denominator, so the gain is in representing large repeated electorates compactly. Also, the exact-ranking support may be large in real elections. So there is a credible population mirror here, but no anchor-backed computational contribution to claim from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continue. Theorem 4.4 is an axiomatic impossibility statement quantified over profiles; Theorem 4.5 and Theorem 4.6 are also axiomatic results. The empirical section measures clone proximity and winner changes on finite datasets. None asks for an algorithm or classifies a computational problem.

The proposed “Minimum Weak-Spoiler Clone Distance” turns that empirical audit into an optimization task, but the optimization is added by the analyst. Given a distribution over rankings, the paper’s \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) values are weighted sums, and candidate deletion projects each ranking onto the remaining candidates. The task then checks the winner sets for each candidate pair. That is a reasonable way to rerun the audit on weighted data, but the paper’s central operation is deleting a candidate; it does not select or alter voter mass. There is no population-level search problem in the paper for a continuous optimization method to unlock.

A stronger proposal might ask for the least mass transfer among ranking types that makes a pair close to clones and causes deletion to change the winner. That could be a worthwhile voter-control or robustness problem. It adds an intervention on the society that the paper does not study, though. It is a new problem inspired by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The axiomatic theorems do have natural weighted extensions. The one-third bound in Theorem 4.5 can be read as a bound on shares of rankings, and the Smith-set argument in Theorem 4.6 can be expressed using pairwise margins under a distribution. Those extensions may be mathematically interesting, but they continue axiom analysis. Changing finite counts to masses does not turn them into computational questions.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large ranked-choice electorate with many voters sharing a relatively small set of rankings. Voters can be grouped by complete ranking, and the paper’s measures remain meaningful as population shares. That makes a weighted audit sensible; it does not supply the missing computational anchor. The paper’s own datasets also do not establish that the relevant elections have moderate ranking support.

So I would argue against spending the programme’s time on this paper as a computational mirror. The negative case is limited: a weighted empirical audit could be useful in practice, and the paper’s objects survive continuization cleanly. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no scenario could make such an audit worthwhile; the stronger claim is that the paper itself does not pose the kind of computational question the programme is set up to chart.
