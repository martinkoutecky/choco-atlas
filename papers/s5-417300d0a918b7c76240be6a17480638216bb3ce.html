The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) and \(\beta\geq0\) with \(\beta^2\alpha(1-\alpha)<1\), consider a unit-mass workforce split between two complete-bipartite types with masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). The finite model uses attenuation \(\delta_n=\beta/n\), and a coalition with type masses \(x,y\) has productivity per unit baseline population \(F_\beta(x,y)=\frac{x+y+2\beta xy}{1-\beta^2xy}\). Compute the LRP productivity densities \((w_K,w_M)\), equal within each type, that satisfy efficiency \(\alpha w_K+(1-\alpha)w_M=F_\beta(\alpha,1-\alpha)\), link balance \(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}(w_K-1)=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}(w_M-1)\), and core stability \(xw_K+yw_M\geq F_\beta(x,y)\) for all \(0\leq x\leq\alpha\), \(0\leq y\leq1-\alpha\).

The model it lives in

Use a continuum of total mass \(1\), divided into two worker types with masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). Coalition productivity is \(F_\beta(x,y)=\frac{x+y+2\beta xy}{1-\beta^2xy}\). The LRP rule assigns densities \(w_K=1+\frac{\beta(\beta+2)(1-\alpha)^2}{1-\beta^2\alpha(1-\alpha)}\) and \(w_M=1+\frac{\beta(\beta+2)\alpha^2}{1-\beta^2\alpha(1-\alpha)}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the LRP allocation formula of Proposition 5.4 and its limiting core stability, supported by Theorem 5.5. It leaves the Shapley-value calculation, the other game properties, and extensions beyond complete bipartite networks aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s computation of the Link Ratio Productivity distribution for a large, two type workforce. The regime is concrete: a logistics network has two large cohorts, such as producers and retailers. Workers in each cohort share the same role, intrinsic productivity, and links to the other cohort. If there are \(n\) workers and only two types, the number of agents grows while the number of types stays fixed.

My anchor is Proposition 5.4, proved in this paper. For a complete bipartite network with parts \(K,M\), on the attenuation range where the limiting AN game is defined, it gives the LRP allocation explicitly. Each worker \(i\in K\) receives
\[ \omega_i(\delta)=1+ \left(\frac{|M|}{|K|}\delta+ \frac{2|M|}{|N||K|}\right) \frac{|K||M|\delta}{1-|K||M|\delta^2}, \]
and each worker \(i\in M\) receives
\[ \omega_i(\delta)=1+ \left(\frac{|K|}{|M|}\delta+ \frac{2|K|}{|N||M|}\right) \frac{|K||M|\delta}{1-|K||M|\delta^2}. \]
The paper describes this distribution as easier to compute than the Shapley value.

Here is a continuous problem that preserves that question. Let \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) be the mass of type \(K\), and let \(1-\alpha\) be the mass of type \(M\). Given rational \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\geq 0\) with \(\beta^2\alpha(1-\alpha)<1\), compute the continuous LRP allocation: one productivity density \(w_K\) for each unit of type \(K\) mass and \(w_M\) for each unit of type \(M\) mass.

The attenuation scaling is \(\delta_n=\beta/n\) in finite populations. It keeps the aggregate peer effect finite as the workforce grows. A coalition with type masses \(x,y\) then has productivity per unit baseline population
\[ F_\beta(x,y)=\frac{x+y+2\beta xy}{1-\beta^2xy}, \qquad 0\leq x\leq\alpha,\quad 0\leq y\leq1-\alpha. \]
The decision is the pair \((w_K,w_M)\). It is not a welfare maximization: total productivity is fixed, and the task is to allocate it by the paper’s link ratio principle. A solution must give equal shares within each type, satisfy efficiency
\[ \alpha w_K+(1-\alpha)w_M=F_\beta(\alpha,1-\alpha), \]
and satisfy the continuous link balance condition
\[ \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}(w_K-1) = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}(w_M-1). \]
It should also be coalitionally stable: \(xw_K+yw_M\geq F_\beta(x,y)\) for every allowed pair \(x,y\).

Taking the stated finite formula to this limit gives the exact answer
\[ w_K=1+\frac{\beta(\beta+2)(1-\alpha)^2} {1-\beta^2\alpha(1-\alpha)},\qquad w_M=1+\frac{\beta(\beta+2)\alpha^2} {1-\beta^2\alpha(1-\alpha)}. \]
Thus this mirror is tractable: the allocation takes a constant number of rational arithmetic operations from the input masses and attenuation parameter. Its stability constraints are the limit of the finite coalition constraints. The paper’s result supplies a strong anchor for this question, though the continuous statement and limit are the proposed mirror.

The authors would plausibly recognize it: the mirror retains their two cohorts, complete bipartite interaction, peer productivity, and link based allocation. It covers the LRP calculation for AN games, not the whole paper. It also raises natural follow ups: whether the same mass allocation principle works for more than two types, and how it compares with the continuum limit of the Shapley allocation.

The soft spot is that the paper has no named complexity classification—no theorem asserting membership in \(P\), hardness, or a parameterized class. Proposition 5.4 is a named explicit computation result, which is why it can anchor this case under the prompt’s formula allowance; the tractability classification above is an inference from its formula. Also, this is a narrow, highly symmetric allocation problem. It supports a credible continuous mirror, but it does not by itself create the broader complexity landscape ChoCo seeks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s computational agenda. Proposition 5.4 gives a closed-form allocation rule; the paper specifies no input encoding, search problem, running-time bound, or complexity classification for computing it. In the proposed mirror, the unknowns are just two per-type productivity densities. Efficiency and link balance determine them, while core stability follows from the finite result. That makes the mirror a translation of an allocation formula, rather than a new complexity problem.

That objection has a clear limit: it does not defeat the proposed scenario. The authors would likely recognize it as their productivity-sharing question.
