The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see a named computational complexity result in this paper that qualifies as an anchor, so I can’t honestly state a continuous problem per anchor.

The closest result is Theorem 4.2, proved here: Mechanism 4.1 is universally truthful for a player whose privacy utility satisfies Assumption 3.1, provided that the utility advantage of the player’s preferred candidate is at least \(2F_i(e^\epsilon)\). Proposition 4.3, also proved here, bounds that mechanism’s welfare loss: the probability of falling at least \(\Delta\) below the best candidate’s vote count is less than \(e^{-\epsilon\Delta}\), and its expected loss is less than \(1/\epsilon\). These are mechanism and welfare guarantees, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified by computational complexity. The paper gives no such classification or computational formulation that it analyzes.

A continuous election over a mass \(\mu_A,\mu_B\) would be easy to write down, but it would not preserve the paper’s central incentive question. In a nonatomic population, one voter’s report does not change the distribution, so individual influence—and the privacy cost tied to that influence—vanishes. Giving an agent positive mass, or treating a whole group as one strategic player, changes the model. That may be a research question, but it is not a convincing continuous mirror of an eligible computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper proves no computational complexity result to mirror. Theorem 4.2 gives a truthfulness guarantee for a particular mechanism under assumptions on privacy utilities; Proposition 4.3 bounds that mechanism’s welfare loss. The facility-location and VCG results have the same character. A continuous question about mechanism design could be interesting, but it would be a new question, not a continuous version of a complexity problem this paper studies.

Even treating Theorem 4.2 as an anchor, a society described by masses over \(A\)- and \(B\)-voters loses the deviation the theorem analyzes: one voter changing their report while everyone else’s reports stay fixed. In a nonatomic population, that change leaves the distribution unchanged. The mechanism’s outcome is therefore unchanged under the same random coins, and the voter’s privacy utility is unchanged too. Truthfulness collapses to indifference. Giving a cohort positive mass restores influence, but then the cohort is a strategic bloc with group privacy concerns, not many interchangeable individual agents. This is not an objection to high multiplicity in elections; it is specific to a result whose central object is one person’s influence and privacy.

Proposition 4.3 also degenerates in the population limit. With per-voter differential privacy, the mechanism’s noise has constant scale in vote counts, or scale \(1/n\) in population shares. Its expected welfare loss is less than \(1/\epsilon\) votes, hence a vanishing fraction of the electorate; a fixed positive share margin is overcome with probability at most \(e^{-\epsilon\delta n}\). The limit is deterministic majority, and the stated welfare bound loses its content as a fractional guarantee. Keeping noise at constant scale in shares could preserve a nontrivial welfare tradeoff, but a single voter’s report still has no effect, so it does not preserve the theorem’s privacy-incentive question.

One could study a two-scale limit that retains \(n\) and the \(1/n\) granularity, or a continuum of cohorts that can deviate together. Those may be worthwhile adjacent projects. The first keeps the individual-level discrete structure; the second changes the unit of agency and privacy. Neither supplies a convincing continuous-population mirror of these results. I would argue against a mirror on this paper, while conceding that this is a case about fit to the programme, not a proof that no related continuum mechanism-design project could be valuable.
