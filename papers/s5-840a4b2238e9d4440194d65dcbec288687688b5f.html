The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find an eligible anchor for a continuous mirror. The paper’s main numbered result, Proposition 1, says that in any pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, two active voters with the same preference order vote for the same candidate. That is an equilibrium property, not a result about computational complexity. The mixed-strategy counterexample is not stated as a numbered theorem, and Appendix Claim 2 establishes a particular regular equilibrium; neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

The closest candidate would describe a society by masses of voter types, where each type specifies a utility vector and voting cost, and ask for equilibrium action probabilities over candidates and abstention. But in an atomless population, an individual vote changes no vote share. With positive voting costs, abstention is then optimal, so the model loses the pivotal incentives behind the paper’s results. Treating each type’s entire mass as a strategic bloc would restore influence only by changing the game from individual voting to bloc voting. I don’t see a version that both preserves the paper’s strategic question and supplies one of the required named computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is more basic than the atomless limit: this paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is a structural statement about pure Nash equilibria. The mixed-strategy example and Appendix Claim 2 establish an equilibrium and its regularity. None asks whether a computational problem is tractable or hard. Turning equilibrium computation into such a problem would be a new question motivated by the paper, not a continuous version of one of its computational results.

The best candidate would give the population a finite support of complete voter types—utility vectors and voting costs—and ask about equilibrium behavior by type. That makes high multiplicity plausible: many voters can share a type. But in an atomless plurality game, one voter’s action changes no vote share and therefore no election lottery. With positive voting costs, abstention is strictly better than voting. Proposition 1 then has no active voters to compare, and the mixed-strategy asymmetry disappears. With zero costs, voters are indifferent among actions, so the resulting equilibrium question is vacuous.

One could preserve pivotality by treating each type’s mass as a strategic bloc. But that gives a type coordinated influence over the election; it is not a population of indistinguishable individual voters. Or one could retain a finite electorate of size \(N\), where a vote changes the outcome at ties, and study a sequence of such games as \(N\) grows. That may be worthwhile, but its strategic content depends on finite-\(N\) fluctuations and costs; the exact continuum limit removes the pivotal incentives. It is an asymptotic analysis of finite games, not the paper’s continuous-population mirror.

So I would reject a ChoCo mirror of this paper. The limitation is that this supports a screening judgment, not the literal universal claim that no related continuous research question could ever be valuable. A computational study of high-multiplicity finite voting games might be valuable and might interest these authors. It would still need its own computational question and results; this paper does not supply them.
