The continuous mirror question

Given a finite outcome set \(K\), a finite support \(S \subseteq L(K)\) of true-ranking types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a formula \(\varphi\), decide whether there exist a feasible state \(X=(x_{t,r})\), where \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t\), and an anonymous deterministic rule \(F:\Delta(L(K))\to K\) such that \(\varphi\) holds. Take formulas to be generated from outcome atoms, rational linear inequalities in \(X\), Boolean connectives, a modality for reaching a state by changing at most specified mass \(q_t\) of each type, and a type-preference modality for reaching a state with a more preferred outcome.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic electorate has types \(t\in S\), each with mass \(\mu_t\); \(x_{t,r}\) records how much type \(t\) reports ranking \(r\). An anonymous rule \(F\) maps the resulting report distribution to an outcome. Strategic reachability permits changes to a bounded mass of each type, and type preferences compare the current outcome with outcomes at reachable states.

The objection that survived

Positive-mass group deviations change the paper's individual-agent strategic interpretation; the proponent acknowledges this, and whether the authors would treat it as their logic problem is the main remaining concern.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers satisfiability and decidability for a distributional version of the logic. It leaves aside the paper's full named-agent model, its axiomatization and completeness results, and its strategy-proofness characterization.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does satisfiability in a fixed, effectively encoded version of this mass logic have finite or rational witnesses?
  • Is satisfiability decidable, and what is its complexity in \(|K|\), \(|S|\), the input bit length, and formula size?
  • Under what conditions can its mass deviations be approximated by deviations in finite elections?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I see is narrow: continuize the population in the paper’s logic satisfiability result, rather than claim that its individual strategy-proofness result carries over unchanged.

The anchor is the paper’s unnumbered Theorem (Decidability), proved here: for finite \(N\) and \(K\), deciding whether a formula \(\varphi\in\mathcal L^{scf}[N,K]\) is satisfiable is decidable. The proof enumerates every model over \(N,K\) and checks whether \(\varphi\) holds at a state. The paper gives no complexity bound such as membership in P, and no hardness classification. This theorem is still a computational anchor: its subject is a decision problem, and the paper’s enumeration argument depends directly on the finite population.

A continuous version could be called Continuum SCF Logic Satisfiability. An instance consists of a finite outcome set \(K\), a finite list \(S\) of possible true-ranking types, binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(t\in S\) summing to one, and a finite formula \(\varphi\). Let \(R=L(K)\) be the set of possible reported rankings. A state is a matrix \(X=(x_{t,r})\) with \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_{r\in R}x_{t,r}=\mu_t\): \(x_{t,r}\) is the fraction of the whole population that has true type \(t\) and reports ranking \(r\). The society’s reported distribution is \(\nu_r(X)=\sum_{t\in S}x_{t,r}\).

A model includes a deterministic anonymous social choice rule \(F:\Delta(R)\to K\), so the outcome at \(X\) is \(F(\nu(X))\). Types retain the strict rankings that define them. The finite logic can have outcome atoms, rational linear tests on the masses \(x_{t,r}\), Boolean operators, and the paper’s two strategic ideas in mass form. For example, \(\Diamond_{\mathbf q}\varphi\) says that some state \(X'\) satisfying \(\varphi\) can be reached by changing at most \(q_t\) mass of each true type \(t\), where the amount changed is \(\frac12\sum_{r\in R}|x_{t,r}-x'_{t,r}|\). A preference operator for type \(t\) says that some state satisfying \(\varphi\) yields an outcome that type ranks above the current one.

The question is: does there exist such a rule \(F\) and a feasible state \(X\) at which \(\varphi\) holds? The input is finite; the rule is existentially quantified, just as the paper’s models quantify over outcome functions. The natural complexity parameters are \(|K|\), \(|S|=\tau\), the bit length of \(\mu\), and \(|\varphi|\). This gives a prover a specific question: establish decidability or a complexity classification for satisfiability over distributional SCFs.

The regime is an election with many voters, a fixed ballot language, and relatively few distinct true rankings. Voters of a type share their relevant preference information, and mass records what fraction of the electorate has each true type and report. Such an anonymous rule is a natural class of the paper’s SCFs, which are otherwise allowed to depend on every named agent’s report. The authors’ stated aim—automated reasoning about social choice procedures—makes a logic over population distributions a plausible extension of their problem.

I would tentatively expect Class C, continuum-specific difficulty, for the unrestricted version. The finite enumeration proof no longer applies: states range over a real-valued simplex, and the strategic operators quantify over mass reallocations. There is no obvious hardness transfer from population size alone. That is a research bet, not a result. The first questions are whether satisfiable formulas have finite or rational witnesses, whether the mass geometry admits an effective abstraction, and what the complexity is in \(|K|,\tau\), bit length, and formula size. A bridge to finite elections would also need to show when arbitrary-mass moves can be rounded to multiples of \(1/n\).

The weakest point is the strategic interpretation. In a nonatomic society, one person has zero mass and cannot change an aggregate rule’s input. The mass-budget modality therefore captures deviations by subpopulations, not the paper’s original unilateral deviations. The rule is also anonymous, whereas the paper permits arbitrary labeled SCFs. Those are substantial co-modifications, but they fit a large-election regime and preserve the paper’s central computational object: reasoning about how reported preferences affect a social choice rule. This case covers the decidability theorem and its logic-satisfiability problem; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s strategy-proofness characterization without changing its meaning.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found the paper’s one credible computational anchor: its Theorem (Decidability). I would not deny that this is a decision result. Its mirror is weak, though, because the theorem decides satisfiability in a finite logic whose states are profiles of named agents’ rankings and whose social choice functions are arbitrary tables on those profiles. The proof enumerates that finite model space. It gives no complexity analysis for an election supplied as input, and no algorithmic result whose running time depends on population size or the number of voter types. The proposed continuum problem therefore changes the subject from finite model enumeration to satisfiability over a real state space with a newly defined language.

The most faithful way to aggregate profiles is to represent them by the matrix \(X=(x_{t,r})\). That works only if the rule depends on the aggregate report distribution: the paper allows an SCF to distinguish two named profiles with identical histograms. Restricting to anonymous SCFs is sensible for some large elections, but it is already a restriction of the model. Keeping the paper’s full class by putting identities into types defeats the proposed compression: in the general case, the number of types grows with the population.

More fundamentally, aggregation removes the paper’s individual strategic move. In an anonymous rule on a nonatomic population, one person’s report changes no mass, so it leaves the aggregate input and outcome unchanged. The paper’s \(\Diamond_C\) modality lets a specified coalition change its members’ reports; it does not give a single agent a positive mass budget. The proposed \(\Diamond_{\mathbf q}\) instead permits changes by a fraction of a type. That is a coherent way to study deviations by subpopulations, but it changes the strategic question that gives the paper’s logic much of its purpose. Restoring individual deviations requires retaining finite agents or adding information about how rules react to vanishingly small changes—information the aggregate state \(X\) does not contain.

The best rescue is a new logic for anonymous distributional rules: specify mass based deviations, allow rational linear tests on distributions, and perhaps restrict rules to an effective finite representation. That could be worth studying, especially given the authors’ interest in automated analysis. But the mass operators and quantitative tests create the new problem; they are not a continuization of the theorem’s finite satisfiability problem. Keeping the rule unrestricted instead leaves satisfiability over arbitrary labelings of a continuum, which needs its own effective semantics and justification.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper’s anchor, not as proof that no continuous logic could ever be valuable. I see no version that both preserves the paper’s individual strategic content and makes the society itself a distribution. The proposed version may be a worthwhile new project, but the paper does not provide a convincing computational result to mirror into it.
