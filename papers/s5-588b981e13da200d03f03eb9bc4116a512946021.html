The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(A\) and two finitely supported distributions \(\mu^{(1)},\mu^{(2)}\) over complete rankings of \(A\), with binary-rational masses summing to one in each panel, does there exist a monotonic positional scoring vector \(s\), where \(1=s_1\ge\cdots\ge s_m=0\), such that both panels induce the same strict ranking? For each panel \(j\), define \(S_j^s(a)=\sum_{\rho}\mu_\rho^{(j)}s_{\operatorname{rank}_\rho(a)}\); the condition is \((S_1^s(a)-S_1^s(b))(S_2^s(a)-S_2^s(b))>0\) for every distinct \(a,b\in A\).

The model it lives in

A population has two equal-mass panel strata, with voter types \((j,\rho)\) and panel-conditional masses \(\mu_\rho^{(j)}\). The decision variable is one normalized monotonic positional scoring vector \(s\); ask whether it gives both strata the same strict ordering of alternatives. The input is the supported rankings and binary-rational masses, with parameters \(m\), combined support size \(\tau\), and bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s fixed-split perfect-agreement decision problem. It leaves the expected-disagreement objective, axiomatic results, experiments, and other candidate-rule families outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist when the combined support size \(\tau\) is bounded or small relative to population size?
  • What parameterized algorithms are possible in \(m\) and \(\tau\)?
  • How does the complexity change for minimizing expected disagreement over random splits of a mass society?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one narrow case, with Theorem 3 as the lead. It is proved in this paper, by a reduction from 3SAT in Section 6 and Appendix A.3. In full: PerfPos is NP-complete. Its input is a profile of complete strict rankings and a fixed partition of the voters into two equally sized panels. It asks whether there is a monotonic positional scoring rule whose output rankings on the two panels have Kendall–Tau distance zero.

A plausible population setting is a large municipal or association election with a short, common slate. Split the electorate into two equal, independently constituted panels. Let a type be the panel label together with a complete ranking of the alternatives; agents with that type submit the same ballot. The mass of a type is its fraction of that panel. This is most convincing where many voters share recurring ballot patterns, so the number of voters \(n\) is much larger than the total number of supported types \(\tau\). The paper’s political-election setting makes this scenario recognizable, though the paper does not establish that its datasets have small \(\tau\).

Here is the mirror, Perfect Positional Agreement for Two Mass Panels. The input is a finite alternative set \(A\), with \(|A|=m\), and two finitely supported distributions \(\mu^{(1)},\mu^{(2)}\) over complete rankings \(\mathcal L(A)\). Their masses are binary rationals and each distribution sums to one; write \(\tau\) for their combined support size and \(L\) for the input bit length. For a scoring vector \(s\) with \(1=s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge s_m=0\), define the score of alternative \(a\) in panel \(j\) as
\[ S_j^s(a)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal L(A)}\mu^{(j)}_\rho\,s_{\operatorname{rank}_\rho(a)}. \]
The question is whether there exists such an \(s\) for which, for every pair of distinct alternatives \(a,b\),
\[ \bigl(S_1^s(a)-S_1^s(b)\bigr)\bigl(S_2^s(a)-S_2^s(b)\bigr)>0. \]
A solution is a scoring vector that induces the same strict ranking in both panels. This is a feasibility question: the target is perfect agreement, not minimum cost. In the paper’s tie-aware Kendall–Tau distance, zero distance requires both outputs to be strict and identical, so this condition preserves the anchor’s success criterion.

I expect this mirror to be NP-complete, by hardness transfer (Class B). For NP-hardness, turn each finite panel in a PerfPos instance into its ballot histogram: assign each ranking mass equal to its count divided by the panel size. This is a polynomial-size transformation, and normalization only scales every score in a panel by the same positive factor, preserving its output ranking. Theorem 3’s hardness therefore applies directly. For membership in NP, guess the common strict order and a rational scoring vector. For that order, the required score gaps are rational linear inequalities. If a real scoring vector satisfies all the strict inequalities, their minimum gap is positive; maximizing a margin variable gives a rational LP witness of polynomial bit length. Clearing denominators also gives the mass instance an exact finite-clone interpretation, though the expanded voter list can be exponentially long in \(L\), so that fact alone should not be used as a polynomial-time reduction in that direction. The reduction’s hardness is carried by the alternatives and the scoring-vector choices, not just by the number of voters.

The mirror is plausible to the authors because it asks the same rule-selection question their computational section isolates: can one scoring rule make two panels agree perfectly? It deliberately matches the paper’s fixed-split PerfPos problem. It does not claim to mirror AbC’s expected disagreement over random splits, which the paper identifies as a distinct problem. Its scope is Theorem 3 only; the axiomatic results, experiments, and other candidate-rule families are outside this mirror.

The main follow-up questions are whether the hardness persists with bounded or small \(\tau\), what parameterized algorithms are possible in \(m\) and \(\tau\), and whether the fixed-panel result extends to minimizing expected disagreement over random splits of a mass society. The paper also observes that multiplicative approximation of the fixed-split minimum disagreement is hard; the zero-agreement instances suggest the corresponding mass version as a further question.

My weakest point is the fit between the anchor and the most natural empirical regime. The mirror needs complete rankings, and real peer-review data may have partial rankings with few repeated types—or nearly every reviewer may have a distinct ranking. Nor does Theorem 3 establish NP-hardness when \(\tau\) is small relative to \(n\). So this is a credible, tightly anchored high-multiplicity mirror, but its hardness result should not be presented as already covering the small-support regime that makes the population interpretation most compelling.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat the proponent’s sole anchor. Theorem 3 gives a direct, well-posed continuous-population question. Take two rational mass distributions over complete rankings, one for each panel, and ask whether a common positional scoring vector gives both panels the same strict ranking. The masses are legitimate voter types, and the panel label can be represented as a population stratum. In a high-multiplicity election, many voters can share each ballot type.

The discrete reduction transfers exactly: replace each panel’s ballots by its normalized histogram. Dividing every score in a panel by its size preserves that panel’s ranking, so perfect agreement is unchanged. This is not a strained interpretation of the theorem, and the paper’s tie-aware distance makes zero distance equivalent to identical strict rankings. Allowing binary-rational masses also gives a natural succinct representation of large populations.

There is a real degeneration in a different proposed mirror: if one takes a single atomless population and randomly splits it, each type’s mass is divided in half almost surely, so the two resulting panels agree automatically. That objection applies to a literal continuum limit of AbC’s random-split experiment. The proponent’s fixed-panel mirror avoids it by comparing two distinct panel distributions.

The honest negative case is therefore limited to scope. This mirrors one feasibility result, not AbC’s expected disagreement objective; the paper also does not show that its empirical settings have small support or establish the mirror’s complexity in a small-\(\tau\) regime. Those limits narrow the claim. They do not make the fixed-panel problem an unworthy mirror. On the supplied evidence, the universal case against has to concede that this anchor survives.
