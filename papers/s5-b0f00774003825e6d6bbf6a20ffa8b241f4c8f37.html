The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and position type sets with rational mass vectors \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), public utilities, independent symmetric mean-zero shock laws, and parameters \(B\) and \(\delta\), can one compute a finite description of an adaptive measurable interview policy and matching rule that uses at most \(B\) interviews per agent almost everywhere and, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), yields a measure-preserving bijection supported on interviewed pairs whose blocking-pair set has product measure zero?

The model it lives in

Two atomless populations are partitioned into finitely many types with masses \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). A measurable adaptive interview relation has degree at most \(B\) almost everywhere; independent shocks are revealed on its edges; and an outcome-dependent, measure-preserving matching must use interviewed edges and be stable almost everywhere.

The objection that survived

The mirror's almost-every-pair stability can ignore a null set of blockers, whereas the finite theorem rules out every blocking pair; this narrows transfer to an aggregate stability notion.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's adaptive interview bound and also admits the tiered non-adaptive setting of Theorem 2. It does not cover the paper's incentive properties.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite ordered-proposal argument be replaced by a measurable adaptive policy for finite-support populations while keeping \(B\) finite?
  • How does the minimum feasible \(B\) depend on the type masses, shock laws, and required success probability?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s interim-stability problem itself, with interviews still between individual applicants and positions. Let the population be a nonatomic mass-\(N\) cohort on each side. The input gives finite sets of applicant and position types, with rational type fractions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\); a type specifies its public utility and its interview-shock law. Thus a type-\(t\) applicant cohort has mass \(N\mu_t\), and similarly for positions. Take the shocks to be independent draws made when an interview occurs, as the paper’s information structure permits: only interviewed shocks affect observed utilities.

An interview schedule is a measurable relation between applicants and positions. Its burden is the essential maximum number of interviews assigned to any one applicant or position. A matching is a measure-preserving bijection, so each person still gets one whole position; only the aggregate type flows are fractional. A successful schedule must interview every matched pair and, with the required probability, yield an interim-stable matching almost everywhere: almost every applicant–position pair must fail to be a blocking pair under observed utilities. This keeps the paper’s scarce resource, information gathering, and stability condition intact while making the population a distribution over types. A plausible regime is a very large labor or residency market with many agents but relatively few utility or tier classes.

Lead: Adaptive mass-interview stability. The instance is \(N\), the two rational type distributions, public utilities, independent symmetric mean-zero shock laws, a budget \(B\), and a failure tolerance \(\delta\). The question is whether one can construct a causally adaptive interview policy, with a finite description in terms of types and mass, that interviews at most \(B\) partners per agent almost everywhere and produces the required matching with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The solution is the policy and its outcome-dependent matching rule. The computational question is to find such a policy, with complexity measured in the type supports, input bit length, \(\log N\), and \(1/\delta\).

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that Algorithm 1 finds an interim-stable matching between \(n\) applicants and \(n\) positions and, with high probability, gives every applicant and position at most \(O(\log^2 n)\) interviews. This is the strongest anchor: it covers arbitrary public utility values and adaptivity, and its interview budget is the paper’s main adaptive result. It is an algorithmic interview bound, not a claim that the problem is in P.

I would expect the fixed-support adaptive mirror to be Class A, through a constructive quantile-based policy and measurable matching. The paper’s proof suggests useful order structure and concentration, rather than hardness that depends on having many distinct agents. The questions for a prover include whether a bound of \(O(\log^2 N)\) survives in the mass model, whether it can be expressed in terms of \(\delta\), \(\tau\), and type masses, and whether the policy can be computed from the rational input without expanding the population into individuals.

Specialized mirror: Nonadaptive tiered mass-interview stability. Use the same instance and solution notions, but require the interview relation to be fixed before any shocks are revealed. Applicant and position types now have tier labels: members of a tier share their public value, higher tiers dominate lower tiers regardless of interview outcomes, and the shocks satisfy the bounded, symmetric assumptions used for the tiered result. The question is whether a finitely described, fixed interview schedule with burden at most \(B\) can support an interim-stable matching with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

The anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. For tiered markets, it gives a non-adaptive algorithm that finds an interim-stable matching with high probability while assigning at most \(O(\log^3 n)\) interviews to each applicant or position. This is a separate, narrower mirror, not a strengthening of Theorem 1. I would also expect Class A here: the theorem’s tier structure and overlapping interview sets look amenable to a mass or quantile construction. Further questions are whether the bound improves to \(O(\log^2 N)\), and how the necessary burden depends on the tier masses.

The authors could plausibly recognize both questions: they retain one-to-one matching, revealed preferences, interim stability, and the maximum interviews per agent. The type definition is also defensible here. Since only revealed shocks enter the algorithm and stability test, the independent shocks can be viewed as generated at interview time. Agents with the same public utility and shock law are then indistinguishable for the problem’s decisions. This is not a mirror of fractional matching as an outcome; fractional type flows summarize a continuum of whole matches.

My weakest point is that the paper proves finite-market guarantees, not these measurable-policy results. Its \(O(\log^2 n)\) and \(O(\log^3 n)\) bounds control individual degrees in finite random graphs; they do not automatically define a finite-degree relation on a continuum population. In particular, the algorithms use ordered agents and finite-size concentration bounds, while the normalized interview density tends to zero as \(N\) grows. Whether the required sparse relation and almost-everywhere stability survive the continuous limit is the central open issue. That gap limits what can be claimed from the anchors, but it also makes the mirrors worthwhile questions about the same interview-and-stability problem.

These mirrors cover Theorems 1 and 2, not the paper’s incentive properties: the authors explicitly note that their algorithms are not DSIC.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that interview stability is a property of specific pairs, while a continuous society records mass by type. The applicants and positions can certainly come from a large cohort with few public utility classes. The difficulty is preserving what the paper’s results actually guarantee when each person has measure zero.

There are two named algorithmic results here, so “no computational result to mirror” is not a sound dismissal. Theorem 1 gives an adaptive procedure with \(O(\log^2 n)\) interviews per agent, and Theorem 2 gives a non-adaptive procedure for tiered markets with \(O(\log^3 n)\). A stronger mirror than simply translating those bounds would ask for the minimum interview burden needed to produce an interim-stable matching for a cohort of mass \(N\), given rational type masses and shock laws.

That formulation runs into a stability problem. With finitely many interviews per person, the interview schedule connects individual agents, and the matching does too. In a nonatomic model, those relations have zero product measure. The proposed condition that almost every applicant–position pair is nonblocking can therefore ignore precisely the exceptional pairs that matter in the paper: a single un-interviewed pair that blocks the matching invalidates finite-market stability. It also ignores the interviewed pairs on which shocks were revealed. Under this almost-everywhere notion, the interview process can disappear from the stability test. A condition requiring *every* pair to be nonblocking preserves the finite problem’s force, but it needs the identities and pair-specific realized utilities that a distribution over types omits.

For Theorem 1, this is not fixed by making the policy adaptive. Its decisions depend on observed shocks from particular interviews, and its proof controls how each individual moves through an ordered set of positions. A type-level mass flow does not specify those interview histories. One could attach labels and a realized preference profile to every agent; then the type must include that agent’s shocks toward particular counterparts. Under the paper’s independent pairwise shocks, that description no longer has the finite-support, high-multiplicity form that makes the proposed mirror attractive. Treating shocks as fresh random draws at interview time avoids putting them in the type, but leaves the model needing an identity-level random process and matching rule.

Theorem 2 is an especially natural candidate because its interview graph is chosen in advance. A schedule based on tier masses and quantiles could reproduce its broad design: random links within large tiers, overlapping links near tier boundaries, and special treatment of small tiers. But its guarantee still concerns each person’s interviews and the realized stability of particular pairs. An almost-everywhere stability condition loses that guarantee; an exact pointwise condition again requires individual labels and pair-level random outcomes. Moreover, the bound grows with \(n\), so the literal limit as \(N\) tends to infinity does not supply a finite interview budget per person.

One could define stability through deviations involving a positive mass of agents, or keep quantile labels and a detailed random interaction process. Either might produce a worthwhile model. But the first replaces pairwise interim stability with a different condition, and the second gives up much of the compression to a distribution over types. The paper’s related large-market work does not settle the issue: an asymptotic or analytic treatment is not already a continuous computational classification.

So the negative case is real but not airtight. It defeats the proposed finite-support, almost-everywhere mass mirror as a faithful translation of both theorems. It does not establish that every labeled continuum or positive-mass deviation model would be unworthy; the paper’s constructive results provide genuine anchors for those further formulations.
