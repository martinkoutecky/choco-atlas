The continuous mirror question

Given binary issues, a target outcome \(p\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over complete unconditional approval-ballot types, and a rational deletion budget \(\beta\), is there a vector of deletion masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\) such that \(p\) uniquely minimizes the remaining population’s total dissatisfaction?

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) is a complete approval ballot over the issues. Choose deletion masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with total at most \(\beta\); require that, on every issue \(j\), the target value \(p_j\) has strictly less remaining disagreement mass than its opposite. A shared margin \(\varepsilon>0\) encodes these strict inequalities in a polynomial-size LP.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror here is the paper’s delete-voters-for-all-issues result.

The anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper: cdv-all is NP-hard even for Unconditional Minisum when every issue has a binary domain. In full, the input is an election, a deletion quota \(q\), and a target outcome specifying one alternative for every issue. The question is whether deleting at most \(q\) voters can make that target the unique optimal Conditional Minisum outcome. The theorem proves hardness by reducing from Vertex Cover.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large electorate voting on a shared package of binary public projects. The voter type is the voter’s complete unconditional approval ballot across all issues; its mass is that ballot’s fraction of the electorate. A citywide electorate might have millions of residents but far fewer recurring ballot types, for example because preferences cluster by constituency or interest group. The controller chooses how much mass \(x_t\) of each type to delete, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). For a finite profile with \(n\) voters, the corresponding masses are \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the discrete quota \(q\) becomes a mass budget \(\beta=q/n\).

Call the resulting decision problem Continuous Unconditional Minisum Control. Given binary issues, supported ballot types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a target outcome \(p\), and a rational deletion budget \(\beta\), ask whether there are deletion masses \(x_t\) summing to at most \(\beta\) such that \(p\) is the unique outcome minimizing the remaining population’s total dissatisfaction. The outcome objective is the expected number of dissatisfied issues per voter; the controller’s solution is a feasible vector of deleted masses witnessing the target’s unique-winner status.

This mirror is likely tractable (Class A). With unconditional ballots, the dissatisfaction objective separates by issue. For every issue \(j\), the target value must have strictly lower dissatisfaction mass than its alternative. Let \(g_{tj}\) be the type-\(t\) difference between dissatisfaction with the alternative and dissatisfaction with the target on issue \(j\); then \(g_{tj}\in\{-1,0,1\}\). The required strict inequalities are

\[ \sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)g_{tj}>0 \qquad\text{for every issue }j. \]

Introduce a shared margin \(\varepsilon\), replace each strict inequality with \(\sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)g_{tj}\ge\varepsilon\), and maximize \(\varepsilon\), subject to the deletion bounds and budget. The target is uniquely optimal exactly when the LP has optimum \(\varepsilon>0\). It has \(O(\tau)\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints, so this gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length.

That is a credible continuous form of Theorem 8’s underlying control question: the controller still changes who participates, the electorate still judges the same outcomes by the same dissatisfaction rule, and the target must still win uniquely. The substantive change is the high-multiplicity relaxation from selecting indivisible voters to selecting mass within a voter type. The Vertex Cover choice that makes the discrete result hard may therefore disappear. This covers Theorem 8 specifically; it makes no claim about the paper’s conditional-ballot control results or its other results.

The weakest point is that the anchor concerns the unconditional special case, where issues separate, and that this mirror deliberately permits fractional deletion within a type. It may not preserve the answer to a particular finite election: continuous deletion allows quantities that are not multiples of \(1/n\). But that is a genuine population continuization, not outcome-space or probability continuization, and it is precisely the relaxation whose computational effect the programme is meant to investigate.

The mirror generates concrete follow-up questions: how well can a feasible continuous deletion plan be rounded to a finite electorate; what additive or multiplicative loss does rounding incur as type counts grow; and does the LP picture survive for conditional ballots, where the issues no longer separate?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong negative case against the one anchor the proponent raised. Theorem 8 is a named computational result, and its continuous mirror is well formed: types are complete approval ballots across the issues, deletion is represented by mass \(x_t\), and the target is unique exactly when it has a strict advantage on every issue. Maximizing a shared margin \(\varepsilon\) turns those strict inequalities into a polynomial-size LP. Normalizing the remaining population does not change which outcome wins, provided some mass remains.

The best objection is that Theorem 8’s Vertex Cover construction gives voters different incidence-based ballots, so its hard instances may have nearly one type per voter. That limits how well this mirror explains the theorem’s hardness in the regime the programme cares about. But it does not defeat the mirror: a large electorate over shared public projects can plausibly have many residents per recurring ballot type. The paper’s own lack of public data for these elections is a practical limitation, not evidence that such a population regime is incoherent.

The other plausible objections fare no better. Fractional deletion can change answers, and strict uniqueness can hinge on a small margin, but those are features of the population relaxation; finite-profile equivalence and rounding are follow-up questions, not prerequisites for asking it. And the fact that unconditional Minisum separates by issue makes the mirror tractable. Under the programme’s stated standard, that is a result worth learning, not a reason to reject the question.

So the honest conclusion is that the negative case is weak here. The reduction’s dependence on voter-specific ballots is a real limitation on the connection to its hard instances, but it does not rule out a sensible high-multiplicity regime or a worthwhile continuous control question.
