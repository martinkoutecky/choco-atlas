What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers finite-horizon average-price prediction under a coalition-size cap; it leaves uncapped coalition dynamics, equilibrium behavior, entropy, and the paper's simulation findings without computational guarantees.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of approximating the horizon-\(T\) price pair as \(K\), the rate parameters, and \(1/\varepsilon\) vary?
  • Can the uncapped model be given a useful mass-based limit with guarantees for large-coalition behavior?

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no named computational-result anchors in this paper. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its results are a physics-motivated rate equation and simulation findings about coalition sizes, entropy, and average prices. I can’t honestly claim that any of those is a hardness or algorithmic anchor.

The strongest positive case is nevertheless unusually natural as a *model mirror*. Consider an online retailer with very many buyers, all of whom want one of two complementary items. Buyers who want item \(a\) share type \(A\); buyers who want item \(b\) share type \(B\). Let their population fractions be \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), with \(\alpha+\beta=1\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: \(N\) grows while the number of distinct buyer types stays at \(\tau=2\), and members of each type have the same demand and face the same price rules. It is also close to the paper’s own scenario.

A concrete candidate is Finite-Horizon Continuum Coalition-Price Prediction. An instance gives rational \(\alpha,\beta\); the paper’s base prices, discount and ratio-penalty parameters, joining and escape rates; an initial coalition distribution; a time horizon \(T\); and an error tolerance \(\varepsilon\). To keep the state finite, include a maximum bundle size \(K\), as a retailer might impose operationally. Let \(x_{ij}(t)\) be the number of coalitions containing \(i\) type-\(A\) buyers and \(j\) type-\(B\) buyers, divided by the total population. The initial distribution is nonnegative, supported on \(1\le i+j\le K\), and satisfies
\[ \sum_{i,j} i\,x_{ij}(0)=\alpha, \qquad \sum_{i,j} j\,x_{ij}(0)=\beta. \]
The singleton states include the pools from which buyers join coalitions. Coalition transitions move between neighboring compositions when one buyer joins or leaves; their fluxes follow the paper’s price-change-based joining rates and escape rates, with transitions beyond \(K\) disallowed. The task is to compute the two average buyer prices at time \(T\), within additive error \(\varepsilon\):
\[ \bar p_A(T)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i,j} i\,x_{ij}(T)p_A(i,j), \qquad \bar p_B(T)=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i,j} j\,x_{ij}(T)p_B(i,j). \]
The output is an approximate price pair, not a solution to an encoding. This asks a computational question about the same dynamics and effectiveness measure the paper studies.

My provisional expectation is tractable for fixed \(K\) and bounded rates: the mirror is a finite-dimensional trajectory-approximation problem. That expectation is not a result of this paper. With \(K\) growing, or with an unbounded coalition-size space and an equilibrium-time query, the complexity and even the needed limit and truncation guarantees remain open. Nothing in the paper supports a claim that discrete hardness transfers or that the continuum is specifically hard.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper already works at a macroscopic level and simulates aggregate dynamics. Normalizing its coalition counts and asking for the computational complexity of price prediction may be a small extension of its existing model, rather than a new computational result with a strong anchor. The bundle cap also narrows the paper’s unrestricted coalition-size setting. Still, the two-type, very-large-market regime is convincing, and the proposed question keeps the paper’s price-driven coalition transitions and price outcomes intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. It proves no complexity bound and defines no computational decision or optimization problem. Its contribution is a rate equation for aggregate coalition dynamics, followed by simulations of coalition sizes, entropy, and average prices. A new complexity question could be built around those simulations, but that would be a question added to the paper, not a continuous version of one of its computational results.

The proponent’s buyer population is credible: two demand types, many buyers of each, and prices that depend on coalition composition. But the paper already aggregates those buyers into counts of coalitions of each composition and studies their macroscopic evolution. Its proposed state \(x_{ij}(t)\) is essentially those same counts divided by population size. That normalization may be useful, but it does not uncover an unmodelled population structure; the paper has already removed individual identities in the way the mirror would need.

The proposed finite-horizon price prediction is the most plausible candidate, and it does preserve the paper’s price outcomes. Its weakness is that it makes numerical trajectory evaluation the computational problem. With a fixed coalition cap \(K\), the model is a finite system with \(O(K^2)\) state coordinates. That is a reasonable simulation task, but it is not a high-multiplicity relaxation of an existing combinatorial problem. A mirror of the paper’s actual contribution would ask about the same aggregate equations the authors already simulate.

Removing the cap does not supply a stronger match. Coalition size then grows with the population, so the state space is no longer a fixed finite set of agent types. A useful limit would need to account for how coalition sizes scale and whether mass concentrates in very large coalitions. The paper gives no computational task over such a limit, and adding one would require more than normalizing its variables. Conversely, keeping \(K\) fixed makes the problem well specified by excluding the large-coalition behavior that features in the paper’s results.

I would not argue that multiplicity is implausible, or that the price objective loses meaning in a continuum; neither claim holds. The negative case is narrower: the proposed mirror is a plausible new numerical-analysis problem, but this paper offers no computational result to anchor it, and its central model is already macroscopic. That is a substantial reason to spend ChoCo’s time elsewhere, though not an airtight argument that no useful algorithmic question could ever be built from this model.
