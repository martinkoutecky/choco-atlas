The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\), recurring chore types \(J\), rational population masses \(\mu\), rational per-capita supplies \(\lambda\), and binary incidences \(a_{t,j}\in\{0,1\}\), decide whether there exists a finite-support rational distribution \(\nu_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{J}\) satisfying \(\sum_b\nu_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}\nu_{t,b}b_j=\lambda_j\), and \(u_t(b)\ge u_t(b')\) for every occupied \(b\) of type \(t\) and every occupied bundle \(b'\), where \(u_t(b)=-\sum_j a_{t,j}b_j\); if feasible, output such a distribution.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity complete allocation of recurring indivisible chores: types \(T\) carry mass \(\mu\), chore types \(J\) have per-capita supply \(\lambda\), agents receive integral bundle configurations \(b\), and \(\nu_{t,b}\) records mass assigned to each configuration; the objective is feasibility, with no welfare optimization.

The objection that survived

The common support of integral bundles may have an intractable pricing problem; the proponent acknowledges but does not resolve this, so only the predicted Class \(A\) outcome remains vulnerable.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers the exact envy-free existence theorem for binary additive chores and also supplies a repaired \(\mathrm{EF1}\) configuration mirror. It leaves the mixed-resource \(\mathrm{EFM}\) results aside because their continuity concerns the resource rather than the population.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact envy-free feasibility admit a polynomial-size configuration or column-generation formulation for bounded \(|T|\) and \(|J|\)?
  • What is the pricing complexity over integral bundles, and does the binary Set Splitting hardness dissolve, transfer, or become continuum-specific?
  • Can a finite-encoding compressed algorithm for \(\mathrm{EF1}\) replace named-agent top-trading cycles?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s exact envy-free-existence problem for binary chores.

Take a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types and a finite set \(J\) of recurring chore types. A type \(t\) is specified by \(a_{t,j}\in\{0,1\}\), where \(a_{t,j}=1\) means that type dislikes one copy of chore \(j\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Chore supply is specified per capita by \(\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{J}\): in a finite \(N\)-replica realization there are \(N\lambda_j\) indivisible copies of chore \(j\).

An individual receives an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{J}\), with utility \(u_t(b)=-\sum_j a_{t,j}b_j\). The decision variable is a finite-support distribution \(\nu_{t,b}\), meaning that fraction \(\nu_{t,b}\) of the society consists of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(b\). It must satisfy \(\sum_b\nu_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}\nu_{t,b}b_j=\lambda_j\) for every chore type \(j\).

Call \(\nu\) envy-free if every type-\(t\) agent receiving \(b\) weakly prefers \(b\) to every bundle \(b'\) appearing anywhere in society: \(u_t(b)\ge u_t(b')\). Thus the bundles remain indivisible for each agent; only the population and the supply of repeated chore copies are continuous. The problem is to decide whether such a \(\nu\) exists and, if so, output it. There is no welfare objective: this is a feasibility problem.

This is a credible regime for the paper’s chores model. Think of a large population of households, work teams, or residents repeatedly assigned a finite vocabulary of cleaning, maintenance, or emissions-reduction duties. Agents with the same role, ability, and burden profile are one type. The intended regime has \(N\) very large, while \(|T|\) and \(|J|\) are moderate. A rational continuous instance has an exact finite dictionary: for a common denominator \(K\), \(\mu_t\) becomes \(K\mu_t\) agents and \(\lambda_j\) becomes \(K\lambda_j\) chore copies. Conversely, any such replicated finite instance yields \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\). This is a genuine high-multiplicity bridge, not merely an analogy.

The anchor is the paper’s Theorem \(\ref{thm:EF_NP-complete_Chores_Binary}\), restated as \(\NPhardness\). It is proved in this paper, not cited: deciding whether a chores instance admits an envy-free allocation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even when every valuation is additive and every item has value in \(\{-1,0\}\). The proof reduces \(\mathrm{SetSplitting}\): dummy chores force edge agents to receive one dummy each, while vertex chores assigned to two colour agents encode a partition in which every hyperedge receives both colours.

The continuous question above asks exactly what happens to that result after high multiplicity. The reduction’s finite agents become many copies of a small collection of edge and colour types, and each chore becomes many indistinguishable copies. The crucial discrete forcing can disappear: copies of a vertex chore may be divided in mass between the two colour populations, producing fractional incidence constraints rather than a single integral colour choice. In the reduction, those constraints resemble linear conditions saying that every hyperedge receives enough mass on both sides.

I would therefore tentatively expect this lead problem to be Class A in the bounded-vocabulary regime, probably through a configuration or column-generation formulation. I would not claim that as a theorem: the remaining difficulty is selecting a common support of integral bundles, and succinctly encoded large per-capita supplies could conceal a continuum-specific hardness result. That uncertainty is precisely a worthwhile boundary question. The paper’s \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness does not automatically transfer, and testing whether it dissolves is a direct computational question about the paper’s problem.

A second, weaker anchor is Theorem \(\ref{thm:Monotone_Chores}\). It is also proved here: for monotone non-increasing valuations over indivisible chores, the paper’s top-trading envy-cycle elimination algorithm returns an \(\mathrm{EF1}\) allocation. My corresponding problem is Continuous Monotone-Chore-\(\mathrm{EF1}\). The input is the same \((T,J,\mu,\lambda)\), except that each type has a monotone non-increasing valuation \(v_t:\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{J}\to\mathbb{R}\). The output is a bundle distribution \(\nu\) satisfying the supply equations and, for every type \(t\), every bundle \(b\) assigned to that type, and every occupied bundle \(b'\), the existence of a chore type \(j\) such that removing one copy of \(j\) from whichever bundle contains it gives \(v_t(b\setminus\{j\})\ge v_t(b'\setminus\{j\})\).

This should be Class A, at least when the type and chore vocabularies are finite and valuations admit efficient bundle comparison. The paper’s structural insight—top-trading cycles preserve the fairness invariant—ought to admit compression from named agents to type-and-bundle masses, although extending the cycle argument to a continuum is nontrivial. The resulting questions are whether such a compressed algorithm exists, whether its pricing problem is polynomial, and whether arbitrary monotone valuations introduce a Class C obstruction.

I deliberately do not use the paper’s cake results as anchors. Cake is outcome-space continuity, whereas the proposed mirrors continuize the population and retain integral bundles. The paper’s mixed-resource results may provide useful machinery later, but they do not themselves constitute a continuous-population mirror.

The weakest point is that without scaling chore supply with population, the mirror degenerates: almost everyone receives the empty bundle, and any agent receiving a disliked chore envies an empty agent. Per-capita replication of chores is therefore essential. A referee could regard that as a substantial co-modification of the model. I think it is justified by the paper’s own recurring-task interpretation and by the high-multiplicity programme: the unit of mass receives an integral bundle, while repeated copies of the finite chore vocabulary scale with the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is weaker than the positive one, and I would not honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.

The lead anchor, Theorem \(\ref{thm:EF_NP-complete_Chores_Binary}\), is a genuine computational result: the paper proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness, not merely an axiomatic or analytic statement. The proposed repeated-chore model is also a legitimate high-multiplicity regime. Edge agents and colour agents can be recurring role types, while dummy and vertex chores can be recurring task types. Replicating the construction by a factor \(K\) gives exactly the rational mass/supply dictionary the programme wants.

The strongest objection is that this is more than replacing agents by masses. It also introduces per-capita chore supplies and permits a type’s population to split across several integral bundles. The original Set Splitting gadget depends on each vertex chore being assigned wholly to one of two named colour agents; the mass version instead becomes a configuration problem in which every positively occupied colour bundle must satisfy the relevant envy constraints. Thus the proposed claim that the reduction simply becomes fractional linear constraints is not justified. The support of integral bundles may retain substantial combinatorial complexity, and a column-generation formulation could have an NP-hard pricing problem.

That is, however, an objection to the proponent’s predicted classification, not to the mirror itself. The modified resource model is explicitly permitted by the programme, is subject-matter plausible, and has a precise finite-replica interpretation. Whether the Set Splitting hardness dissolves, survives, or becomes continuum-specific is exactly the computational question the programme is designed to ask. I cannot defeat this anchor without incorrectly treating the necessary scaling of recurring chores as an impermissible scope change.

The second anchor, Theorem \(\ref{thm:Monotone_Chores}\), is less compelling. The paper does not specify a finite encoding or oracle model for arbitrary monotone valuations, so a claim of polynomial-time computation in the continuous representation is initially undefined. Moreover, top-trading cycles do not compress directly: one population type may occupy several different bundles, and therefore has no single favourite bundle or single vertex in an envy graph. A valid compressed algorithm would need a configuration-level representation, not merely the paper’s cycle argument with names replaced by masses.

But the repaired version remains meaningful. Restricting to rational additive or binary valuations gives a well-defined high-multiplicity search problem; requiring every occupied bundle to satisfy the \(\mathrm{EF1}\) comparison is a natural atomless analogue; and rational solutions lift to finite replicated allocations. So this anchor is best criticised as underdeveloped, not dismissed as incoherent.

The mixed-resource cake theorems would not help the positive case, since they continuize the resource rather than the population, and the proponent was right not to use them. Overall, the strongest honest negative conclusion is therefore only that the second mirror is underspecified and that the first may conceal a difficult configuration-pricing problem. A universal “no worthwhile continuous mirror” verdict would not survive: the binary-chores existence theorem supplies a natural, computationally substantive population mirror.
