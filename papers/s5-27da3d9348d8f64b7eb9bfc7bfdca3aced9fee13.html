The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), per-capita object-class supplies \((b_q)_{q\in Q}\), and a partial top-\(k\) ranking \(P_t\) for each type, decide whether there is an assignment giving each applicant one seat, represented by masses \(x_{tq}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_q x_{tq}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tq}=b_q\), that is Pareto optimal under every completion of every applicant’s ranking consistent with their revealed prefix. For every such completion, no feasible reassignment may weakly improve all applicants and strictly improve a positive mass of them.

The model it lives in

An atomless cohort with finitely many partial-ranking types and rational masses, assigned individual indivisible seats from finitely many interchangeable classes with proportional supplies. The decision variables \(x_{tq}\) record the mass of type \(t\) receiving class \(q\); the question is whether such an assignment is necessarily Pareto optimal across all consistent preference completions.

The objection that survived

The type-level allocation matrix may need a precise treatment of how preference completions vary within a type to express every possible Pareto improvement; the opponent’s proposed independent completions make that modeling choice plausible.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s NPO existence and construction result; it leaves the NPO checking and elicitation results and all NRM results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What type-level condition characterizes necessary Pareto optimality for these assignments, including assignments to unrevealed classes?
  • Can existence be decided efficiently in the number of types, object classes, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the paper’s existence problem for necessarily Pareto-optimal matchings. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that a top-\(k\) preference profile admits an NPO matching exactly when there is a matching of size at least \(n-1\) using only revealed agent–object pairs; it also gives a polynomial-time algorithm to decide existence and compute such a matching.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large allocation of school seats or housing units. Treat interchangeable seats or units as object classes \(Q\), with each class \(q\) having per-capita supply \(b_q\), where \(\sum_{q\in Q}b_q=1\). A type \(t\) is an observed top-\(k\) ranking of those classes, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants with that information. The regime is one with many applicants but relatively few distinct observed rankings: \(\tau\ll N\). Each applicant still receives one indivisible seat; the aggregate allocation is fractional because a type’s mass can be split among classes. This is the permitted co-modification of object supplies that makes the population limit meaningful.

I would ask the following. Continuous NPO Existence: Given rational type masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), rational class supplies \((b_q)_{q\in Q}\), and a partial top-\(k\) ranking \(P_t\) for each type, decide whether there is an assignment of the atomless population to individual seats that is Pareto optimal under every completion of every applicant’s ranking consistent with their revealed prefix. If one exists, return it, represented by rational masses \(x_{tq}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_q x_{tq}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tq}=b_q\). The output must be realizable as an assignment in which each person gets one seat. For each allowed completion profile, there must be no feasible reassignment that makes everyone weakly better off and a positive mass of applicants strictly better off.

I expect this problem to be Class A, but that is a conjecture about this mirror, not a result of the paper. Its feasibility constraints are a transportation problem, and the paper’s finite NPO test itself relies on an exchange-cycle characterization. Those features make a compact aggregate certificate plausible. The exact \(n-1\) threshold should not simply be carried over: in an atomless society there is no single residual applicant, and interchangeable seats change the exchange structure. A useful follow-up is whether the maximum revealed-edge flow must cover all mass for an NPO allocation to exist. I would not assume so; identical seats can make some unrevealed assignments harmless.

The authors would, I think, recognize this as their problem: it is still one-sided assignment from partial ordinal preferences, and the paper itself cites student-to-school allocation as an application. The mirror covers only NPO existence and construction, not the paper’s NRM results or online elicitation guarantees.

The soft spot is the object-class coarsening. The paper ranks distinct objects with strict orders, while this scenario treats genuinely interchangeable seats as equivalent. That change is natural for high-multiplicity school or housing allocation, but it means Theorem 1’s particular \(n-1\) characterization is not automatically preserved. The case rests on the continuous existence question remaining a sensible version of the authors’ NPO problem, not on claiming their theorem already solves it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s anchor is that the proposed mirror changes the assignment model in ways that matter to the paper’s NPO theorem. Theorem 1 relies on a one-to-one matching of distinct objects: at most one assigned pair can be unrevealed. In the proposed model, a school or housing class has capacity, seats in that class are interchangeable, and a type’s mass can be split across classes. A positive mass of people can then receive unrevealed assignments to the same class without the pairwise exchange argument from the paper applying. So the \(n-1\) characterization does not carry over as stated. The matrix \(x_{tq}\) also needs a precise account of how Pareto improvements and preference completions work across submasses.

That is a real modeling burden, but it does not defeat the best version of the mirror. An atomless cohort of applicants, a finite set of school types with capacities proportional to cohort size, partial rankings over those types, and completion uncertainty chosen independently for each applicant gives a coherent robust NPO existence problem. A measurable assignment can still give each applicant one indivisible seat; its type-level representation is fractional. This is close to the paper’s one-sided assignment problem, and the paper itself cites student-to-school assignment. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their NPO question.

The finite theorem’s exceptional single unrevealed assignment becomes especially interesting here: one has to determine what replaces it when mass can be split and seats are interchangeable. The answer might be simple, but that cannot itself disqualify the question under the programme’s criteria. I therefore cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case is weak precisely at its strongest point: the proposed mirror needs a careful continuum definition, but there is a natural, plausible regime in which to ask the question.
