The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of additive valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), goods categories \(G\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and nonnegative values \(a_{t,g}\), each unit of mass must receive an integral bundle \(\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{G}\). Let \(x_{t,\mathbf{k}}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(\mathbf{k}\), with \(\sum_{\mathbf{k}}x_{t,\mathbf{k}}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,\mathbf{k}}x_{t,\mathbf{k}}k_g=q_g\). Can a polynomial-time algorithm, for fixed \(\alpha>0\), return such an allocation and nonnegative payments that give every recipient the same post-payment value and make each type weakly prefer its own bundle-and-payment to every occupied recipient class, while attaining population NSW \(W=\exp(\sum_{t,\mathbf{k}}x_{t,\mathbf{k}}\log v_t(\mathbf{k}))\ge\alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is unrestricted maximum NSW for the same supplies?

The model it lives in

A continuous population of valuation types with mass \(\mu_t\), integral bundles for each recipient, and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\). The decision variables \(x_{t,\mathbf{k}}\) allocate mass to bundles; nonnegative class payments must equalize post-payment utility and satisfy envy constraints across types. Measure welfare by the mass-weighted geometric mean of recipients' goods values.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 7's incompatibility between joint EQ-and-EF fairness and positive NSW approximation. It leaves the subsidy bounds and the algorithmic guarantees in Theorems 1–5 unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of maximizing NSW among jointly fair allocations over a finite set of valuation types, relative to the best jointly fair NSW?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Proposition 7, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\alpha>0\), there is a goods instance with additive valuations in which no allocation that is both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable is \(\alpha\)-maximal for Nash welfare. The paper’s witness has one agent value every item at \(1\), while each other agent values every item at \(1-\varepsilon\). By the paper’s characterization of joint equitability and envy-freeness, all items must go to the first agent. The resulting NSW is zero, while an unconstrained allocation can give every agent positive value. This is an inapproximability result proved here, not a result cited from elsewhere; it is a welfare-guarantee impossibility, rather than an NP-hardness theorem.

A natural continuous mirror keeps the goods indivisible for each recipient and makes the *population* continuous. Let \(T\) be a finite set of additive valuation types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(G\) be a finite set of categories of identical goods, with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\). An individual receives an integral bundle \(\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\), valued by type \(t\) at
\[ v_t(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{g\in G}a_{t,g}k_g. \]
The decision variable \(x_{t,\mathbf{k}}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(\mathbf{k}\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{\mathbf{k}}x_{t,\mathbf{k}}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,\mathbf{k}}x_{t,\mathbf{k}}k_g=q_g\quad(g\in G). \]
Thus each person still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of people assigned each bundle can be fractional.

For every occupied type-and-bundle class, allow a nonnegative per-person payment \(p_{t,\mathbf{k}}\). Require a common post-payment value \(U\), so \(v_t(\mathbf{k})+p_{t,\mathbf{k}}=U\) whenever \(x_{t,\mathbf{k}}>0\), and require envy-freeness: for every observer type \(r\) and every occupied recipient class \((t,\mathbf{k})\), \(v_r(\mathbf{k})+p_{t,\mathbf{k}}\le U\). As in the paper, measure NSW from the goods allocation, excluding subsidy:
\[ W(x)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t,\mathbf{k}}x_{t,\mathbf{k}}\log v_t(\mathbf{k})\right), \]
with \(W(x)=0\) if positive mass receives zero value. The computational question is: for fixed \(\alpha>0\), can an algorithm, polynomial in the type and goods-category description and the rational input bit length, return a jointly equitable and envy-free allocation with \(W(x)\ge\alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is unrestricted maximum NSW? A solution consists of the mass allocation and its payment schedule.

The high-multiplicity regime is credible with just two types. For example, take two large cohorts with \(\mu_A=\mu_B=\tfrac12\), one category of goods with \(q=1\), and per-unit values \(a_A=1\), \(a_B=1-\varepsilon\). This is a large population with two complete valuation types; finite realizations have \(N/2\) agents of each type and \(N\) identical, still-indivisible goods. Because type \(A\) values every nonempty bundle more than type \(B\), the joint fairness condition forbids assigning any goods to type \(B\). Yet joint fairness is feasible: assign two goods to each unit of type \(A\), none to type \(B\), and use payments \(0\) and \(2\), respectively. Everyone then has post-payment value \(2\), and neither type envies the other’s bundle. Unrestrictedly, one good per person gives \(W=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}>0\); every jointly fair allocation has \(W=0\). So no positive \(\alpha\) guarantee is possible, even with \(\tau=2\).

The paper’s stated witness singles out one high-valued agent. The positive case here relies on a direct replication of its proof mechanism: populate both valuation types in large numbers, then apply the same joint-fairness characterization. That cloned family is my extension, not an additional theorem claimed by the authors. The authors should still recognize the mirror: the objects, additive valuations, subsidies, and EQ-plus-EF constraints are the same; the mass variables and per-capita supply express a large cohort allocation.

The expected result is the zero-approximation barrier transferring to the continuum. It is structural: no algorithm can achieve the requested ratio because no qualifying allocation can. I would not present it as a computational hardness classification or as continuum-specific hardness. The scope is Proposition 7’s additive-goods, joint-EQ-and-EF NSW guarantee; it does not mirror the paper’s other results.

The weak point is precisely that this does not show continuous optimization technology resolving or creating a complexity question: the welfare gap is already forced by the fairness constraints. A useful follow-on would be to classify the computational complexity of maximizing NSW *among* jointly fair allocations, and to identify valuation or type-distribution conditions under which joint fairness can retain a positive fraction of unrestricted NSW.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 7 is the strongest anchor, and its population version is coherent. With two types of mass \(1/2\), one good per capita, and values \(1\) and \(1-\varepsilon\), joint EQ and EF force the lower-valued type to receive no goods. A positive mass then has zero value, so population NSW is zero; unrestricted NSW is positive. This defeats the anchor as a computational result: the barrier comes from which allocations are feasible, independent of any algorithm or running time.

That distinction matters for ChoCo’s stated aim. Proposition 7 proves an allocation incompatibility, not a complexity bound. Asking whether an algorithm can guarantee a positive fraction of unrestricted NSW has no algorithmic content here: no qualifying allocation attains that fraction, even with unlimited computation. The mass formulation therefore supplies no complexity landscape result for the programme.

A more computational question would optimize NSW *among* jointly fair allocations and study its complexity in the type count, goods description, and input bit length. That is well posed, but it changes Proposition 7’s target: the proposition says nothing about the best fair allocation’s welfare relative to the fair optimum. It could be a separate mirror of the paper’s algorithmic results.

So I can make a case against treating Proposition 7 as a worthwhile ChoCo anchor. I cannot honestly defend the broader claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario: its polynomial-time allocation results, including Theorem 5, leave plausible computational mirrors. The negative case is weakest precisely at that universal claim.
