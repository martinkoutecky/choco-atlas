The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) player types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(\rho\) integral item types with rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\), and value-oracle access to normalized monotone submodular valuations \(f_t\), can one compute a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq\mathcal R\) that approximates the maximum \(W(x)=\min_{x_{t,B}>0} f_t(B)\)? In particular, can one obtain a \(D^\epsilon\)-approximation in \(D^{O(1/\epsilon)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time for fixed \(\epsilon>0\), and a polylogarithmic approximation in \(D^{O(\log D/\log\log D)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time, where \(D=2+\tau+\rho\) and \(L\) is the input bit length?

The model it lives in

Represent each type by its mass \(\mu_t\) and each integral bundle \(B\) by assigned mass \(x_{t,B}\). Require \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B\ni r}x_{t,B}\le q_r\); maximize the least \(f_t(B)\) among bundles assigned positive mass.

The objection that survived

The paper's running time in \(n\) gives no bound in \(D=2+\tau+\rho\), and its recursive LP analysis does not establish efficient pricing for the mass model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper's max-min allocation objective and its monotone submodular valuation setting. It leaves the paper's augmentation problem and technical LP and rounding results as supporting machinery, and it does not transfer the paper's approximation guarantees to the continuous dimensions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP over type and integral-bundle masses be approximately separated in time polynomial in the type and item dimensions under value-oracle access?
  • What approximation guarantees are possible as functions of \(\tau\), \(\rho\), and the rational input length?
  • How closely does the mass optimum relate to finite populations with repeated valuation types and inventories scaled with population?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s max-min allocation problem. Imagine a large public agency distributing standardized, indivisible aid items to millions of households. Households fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete need-and-valuation types: two households of the same type value every bundle identically. There are \(\rho\) standardized item slots, with inventory scaling in proportion to the population. Thus the regime has \(N\gg\tau,\rho\), rather than a small committee whose members are effectively all distinct.

For a continuous instance, let \(\mathcal T\) be the player types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(\mathcal R\) be the finite set of item slots, with rational supply \(q_r\) per unit of population. Each type has a normalized, monotone submodular valuation \(f_t:2^{\mathcal R}\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\). The bundles remain integral: an individual receives a subset \(B\subseteq\mathcal R\), never a fractional item. Let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). A feasible allocation satisfies
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq\mathcal R}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{B\ni r}x_{t,B}\le q_r \quad\text{for every }r\in\mathcal R. \]
Its welfare is the least value received by any positive-mass group assigned a bundle:
\[ W(x)=\min\{f_t(B):x_{t,B}>0\}. \]
The continuous Submodular Santa Claus problem is to maximize \(W(x)\). This is not average utility by type: every individual in the continuum receives an integral bundle worth at least the achieved welfare. Only the mass assigned to each bundle is continuous.

The anchor is the main theorem in §1, which is printed as “Theorem” without a numeral in the supplied text; I would not invent one. It is proved in this paper. It states that Submodular Santa Claus has a polylogarithmic-approximation algorithm running in \(n^{O(\log n/\log\log n)}\) time, and, for every constant \(\epsilon>0\), an \(n^\epsilon\)-approximation algorithm running in \(n^{O(1/\epsilon)}\) time. The paper also gives a separate technical algorithmic theorem for its augmentation problem, but I would use the main theorem as the anchor for this mirror. The paper contains no named hardness theorem of its own; its cited lower bounds are background, not anchors proved here.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is: Type-Continuous Submodular Santa Claus Approximation. Given \((\mathcal T,\mu,\mathcal R,q,(f_t)_{t\in\mathcal T})\), with the valuations available through value oracles, let \(D=2+\tau+\rho\) and let \(L\) be the bit length of the rational masses and supplies. For every fixed \(\epsilon>0\), can one return a feasible mass allocation \(x\) with \(W(x)\ge \mathrm{OPT}/D^\epsilon\) in \(D^{O(1/\epsilon)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time and value-oracle calls? Can one also obtain a polylogarithmic-in-\(D\) approximation in \(D^{O(\log D/\log\log D)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time? The required solution is the allocation table \(x\), not a fractional allocation of goods to an individual.

I would tentatively expect the approximation question to be Class A. Its configuration variables are exactly masses assigned to integral bundles, so the threshold feasibility problem is an LP with potentially many bundle configurations; column generation and separation are natural tools. The paper’s recursive configuration-LP machinery gives a concrete reason to take that route seriously. The continuous setting also removes the need to round a finite collection of players: mass can be split among whole-bundle configurations while every recipient still gets an integral bundle. The further questions are whether the relevant pricing problem has efficient approximate separation under the valuation-oracle model, whether the finite-population optimum converges to this mass optimum as \(N\) grows, and whether the continuous problem admits a better approximation than the paper’s tradeoff.

This mirror covers the paper’s max-min welfare problem and its submodular valuation assumption, not every result or technical lemma. Its soft spot is substantial: the theorem’s guarantee in \(n\) does not imply the proposed guarantee in \(D\). Expanding a rational mass instance into \(N\) households only gives an algorithm whose time may depend on \(N\), which is precisely what the high-multiplicity question is meant to improve. The mirror also assumes shared valuations within types and standardized item slots. Those are real restrictions on the regime, but they define a plausible allocation setting while preserving the paper’s objective, indivisible bundles, and submodular preferences.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat the proponent’s one anchor. The paper’s main theorem is a genuine computational result: it gives approximation algorithms with stated running times. Its lack of a theorem number in the supplied text does not weaken it.

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror narrows a problem with arbitrary player-specific valuations to one with only \(\tau\) recurring valuation types and a finite catalogue of reusable resources. The paper’s running time in \(n\) does not translate into one in \(D=2+\tau+\rho\): expanding rational masses into individual players can require a population \(N\) far larger than \(D\), and the paper’s layered-flow argument offers no compression guarantee. The proposed class-A rationale is therefore a research lead, not a consequence of the theorem.

That is a real gap, but it does not make the mirror unworthy. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, a plausible high-multiplicity regime is enough; it need not preserve every arbitrary valuation profile. The stronger formulation retains complete valuation types, integral bundles for each recipient, and per-capita supplies. Its minimum over bundles receiving positive mass is well-defined, and its threshold version naturally yields a configuration LP. Whether that LP can be separated efficiently is precisely a computational question. The fact that bundle and item combinatorics remain difficult is not a disqualifier under the programme’s rules.

Nearby work on identical-valuation submodular max-min allocation gives a \(2/5\)-approximation and studies the configuration LP, but it treats a finite set of players; it does not answer the heterogeneous-type mass-allocation question. That work makes the allocation regime more credible, not less. [The 2026 paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol370-swat2026/html/LIPIcs.SWAT.2026.8/LIPIcs.SWAT.2026.8.html)

So the negative case is weak: it shows that the proposed complexity bounds need independent justification, but it does not show that the mirror lacks a worthwhile question. The anchor survives.
