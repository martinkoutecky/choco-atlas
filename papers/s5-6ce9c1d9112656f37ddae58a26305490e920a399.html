Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5 — survived: Theorem 4 connects local kernels to locally stable exchanges, and the proposed mass-based KEP question is a concrete computational extension, though Theorem 5's hardness is not shown to transfer to it.

The continuous mirror question

Given fixed \(K\), a finite set of patient-donor types with compatibility and rankings, and rational masses \(\mu_t\), maximize the matched mass over cycle-template allocations satisfying \(\sum_r N_t(r)z_r\leq\mu_t\), subject to there being no feasible template that can form a positive-mass local block using unmatched agents or agents who strictly prefer the proposed donor type. Is this optimization problem tractable, and how does its complexity depend on \(K\) and the number of types?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of patient-donor pairs divided into finitely many complete clinical and preference types, each with mass \(\mu_t\). Decision variables assign mass \(z_r\) to feasible cycles of length at most \(K\), with type-mass capacity constraints; maximize transplanted mass while excluding any positive-mass locally blocking cycle.

The objection that survived

A discrete local block may intersect an exchange at only one pair, while the proposed rule requires a positive mass of already matched agents; the paper gives no discrete-to-continuous dictionary showing when such blocks persist.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers maximum locally stable exchange and its nonemptiness question for type-based high-multiplicity pools. It leaves the transfer of Theorem 5's arbitrary-digraph hardness, ordinary stability, and local strong stability unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the arbitrary-digraph SAT gadgets from Theorem 5 be represented by blocking digraphs of type-based kidney exchange instances?
  • How does the continuous problem's complexity vary with \(K\) and the number of types?
  • When does positive-mass local blocking agree with local blocking in discrete high-multiplicity pools?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a continuous maximum locally stable kidney exchange. It carries one result emphatically: Theorem 5, proved in this paper (its proof is in Appendix A), says that for an arbitrary digraph \(G=(V,A)\), deciding whether \(G\) has a nonempty local kernel is NP-complete. The paper also observes that computing a maximum-size local kernel is therefore NP-hard.

The bridge to the kidney exchange problem is Theorem 4, also proved here: for a compatibility digraph \(G\), its locally stable exchanges are exactly the local kernels of its associated blocking digraph \(G^*\). In \(G^*\), vertices represent feasible exchange cycles, and arcs encode the blocking and friendship relations defined in the paper. This makes local-kernel complexity a natural anchor for a continuous version of the paper’s exchange problem. It does not, by itself, establish hardness for kidney exchange: Theorem 5 concerns arbitrary digraphs, and the paper does not show that its reduction produces blocking digraphs of the required form.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large kidney exchange program in which many patient-donor pairs share the same clinical profile. Define a type by the pair’s compatibility to donor categories and the patient’s complete ranking of those compatible categories, along with any other feature the exchange model uses. This is a complete description for the problem, rather than a claim that patients with different profiles are interchangeable. If preferences are driven by a finite set of donor quality categories, a large pool can have \(n\gg\tau\), where \(n\) is the number of pairs and \(\tau\) the number of distinct types. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of pairs of type \(t\), with rational \(\mu_t\geq0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Fix a cycle limit \(K\). An instance gives the type set \(T\), the masses \(\mu_t\), compatibility between donor and patient types, and each type’s ranking of compatible donor types. A cycle template \(r=(t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\), for \(2\leq\ell\leq K\), is feasible when each donor type \(t_i\) is compatible with the patient type \(t_{i+1}\), with indices taken cyclically. A type may occur more than once in a template: its positions use distinct pairs from that type’s mass.

Choose \(z_r\geq0\), the mass of cycles assigned to each template, subject to
\[ \sum_r N_t(r)z_r\leq\mu_t\qquad(t\in T), \]
where \(N_t(r)\) counts occurrences of \(t\) in \(r\). The remaining mass is unmatched. The objective is to maximize
\[ \sum_r |r|z_r, \]
the fraction of the population transplanted. Each individual still receives an integral assignment to one cycle or to no cycle; mass is split across cycle patterns because the population has been made continuous.

Use the paper’s local-blocking condition at positive mass. Let \(a_{t,s}\) be the mass of type \(t\) currently matched to donor type \(s\), and \(a_{t,\bot}\) its unmatched mass. A proposed template \(r=(t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\) blocks locally if some \(q>0\) can be formed by choosing, for each position \(i\), mass \(b_{i,s}\) from current states \(s\in T\cup\{\bot\}\), such that
\[ \sum_s b_{i,s}=q,\qquad \sum_{i:t_i=t}b_{i,s}\leq a_{t,s}. \]
Only unmatched agents or agents who strictly prefer the proposed predecessor type \(t_{i-1}\) to their current donor type \(s\) may be chosen. The proposed cycles must also use positive mass of already matched agents, so that they intersect the current exchange in the continuous sense. An allocation is L-stable exactly when no feasible template and choices \(b_{i,s}\) give such a positive-mass block.

The lead question is: given the rational type data, find an allocation maximizing transplanted mass subject to this continuous L-stability condition. Its nonemptiness decision version asks whether the optimum is positive. A solution is the cycle-mass allocation \(z\); the blocking test is part of the definition, and can be checked template by template through finite linear feasibility problems. This is recognizably the paper’s problem: the same K-way cycles, compatibility, preference comparisons, local intersection requirement, and transplant objective are retained. Population mass and fractional type-level averages are the co-modification that lets identical pairs be split across cycle patterns.

I would provisionally expect hardness to persist when the number and structure of types are part of the input. The relevant combinatorics appear to lie in the pattern of compatible cycles and their blocking relations, not in the raw number of copies of each type. But that is a conjectural direction, not a consequence of Theorem 5: its SAT reduction is for arbitrary digraphs, and this paper leaves the complexity of maximum L-stable exchange open. Showing that the reduction’s combinatorial gadgets can be represented by type-based kidney exchanges would be a first major step toward a hardness-transfer result. If they cannot, continuum-specific hardness remains possible; with fixed \(K\) and small \(\tau\), the finite set of cycle templates also leaves room for tractable algorithms.

The scope is the paper’s local stability and maximum-transplant results. It does not continuize only the possible outcomes: the population of patient-donor pairs itself is represented by \(\mu\). Nor does it claim that classical stability or local strong stability has the same continuous answer. Useful follow-up questions include whether the positive-mass existence decision is hard on realizable blocking digraphs, how complexity varies with \(K\) and \(\tau\), and how the continuous optimum compares with discrete high-multiplicity pools having the same proportions.

The weakest point is the regime assumption and its link to the anchor. Clinical profiles and rankings may be so individual-specific that \(\tau\) approaches \(n\); and Theorem 5’s arbitrary-digraph hardness may have no faithful kidney-exchange realization. Those gaps prevent a hardness claim today. They do not erase the mirror: the question above is a precise, population-continuous version of the paper’s own maximum L-stable exchange problem, and the unresolved representability question is exactly where the complexity investigation should begin.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 5 is not a complexity result about kidney exchanges. Its vertices are cycles in an arbitrary digraph, and its SAT reduction constructs arbitrary digraphs. Theorem 4 says that a kidney exchange’s stable outcomes correspond to local kernels of a *particular* blocking digraph; it does not show that the digraphs in Theorem 5’s reduction can arise that way. The paper leaves the complexity of maximum L-stable exchanges open. So Theorem 5 cannot support a hardness claim for the proposed continuous kidney-exchange problem. A continuous local-kernel problem over cycle vertices would continuize the outcome structure, not the patient population.

The proposed type model also requires a substantial restriction. To preserve the paper’s instance exactly, a type must capture compatibility and preferences across the relevant donors. If those refer to named individuals, types may be nearly unique. Grouping donors into clinical categories instead imposes shared rankings and block-structured compatibility: pairs in a type interact with another type alike. That may describe a useful stylized exchange program, but it is a narrower model, not a mirror of arbitrary compatibility digraphs. The proponent’s large, categorical cohort is plausible enough to keep the question alive; the paper does not establish that it is a natural regime for the instances it studies.

The positive-mass blocking rule is a reasonable atomless analogue, but it needs a discrete-to-continuous dictionary. A discrete local block can intersect the exchange at one pair; in a growing population that intersection may have mass \(1/n\) and disappear. With fixed types of positive limiting mass, blocks can often be scaled to positive mass, but that is an additional regime assumption. It should be proved for the chosen model rather than inferred from Theorem 4.

There is also no clear sign that continuization unlocks the programme’s distinctive optimization machinery here. For fixed \(K\), the type-level cycle templates number \(O(\tau^K)\); the model is a finite cycle-packing problem with stability conditions that remain disjunctive. That could still yield interesting complexity results, but the paper gives no reason to expect a column-generation or convex-optimization breakthrough.

So I would reject the claim that this paper already provides a strong, theorem-backed continuous mirror. But the better type-based model is coherent and asks a genuine computational question. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no scenario is worthwhile: the defensible negative is that its natural regime and its connection to the paper’s only complexity theorem remain unestablished.
