The continuous mirror question

Given \(N \ge 2\), a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), and rational geometric discount factors \(\delta_\theta \ge (2N-3)/(2N-2)\), decide whether there are Borel probability measures \(\lambda_\theta\) over integral schedules \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) such that \(\lambda_\theta\)-almost every \(S\) has \(u_\theta(S) \ge 1/N\) and, for every date \(t\), \(\sum_{\theta \in \Theta}\mu_\theta\lambda_\theta(\{S:t\in S\})=1/N\).

The model it lives in

Each type \(\theta\) is an atomless cohort of mass \(\mu_\theta\) sharing utility \(u_\theta\); its kernel \(\lambda_\theta\) assigns people integral schedules. The objective is feasibility: each person receives utility at least \(1/N\), while each date is assigned to total mass \(1/N\).

The objection that survived

The proposed feasibility problem gives no finite or succinct representation for its measure-valued witnesses, and the utility floor couples dates, so the claimed tractable pricing route is unsupported.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 1's proportional-allocation guarantee; it leaves Theorem 2's envy-free guarantee, the efficiency corollary, and the auxiliary lemmas outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can every feasible family of type kernels for rational input be represented by finite or succinctly described schedules, and can feasibility be decided effectively?
  • Does the discrete construction in Theorem 1 extend to unaligned type masses under per-date capacity \(1/N\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mass version of Theorem 1, with a qualification: the paper has no named computational-complexity result in the sense your anchor rule requires. It proves constructive existence results, but states no running-time bound or complexity classification for a finitely encoded problem. Its procedures can scan an infinite sequence of slots, and the input utilities need not have finite descriptions. So Theorem 1 is the closest named result to anchor on, but it is not a formal “in P” anchor.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for an economy with \(n\ge 2\) agents, if every agent’s utility is \((2n-3)\)-Kakeya—meaning the value of the future after each slot is at least \(2n-3\) times the value of that slot—then a proportional allocation exists. Proportionality means every agent values their assigned schedule at least \(1/n\). The proof is constructive: it uses Iterative Cycle Apportionment, alongside monotonic reordering of utilities.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large cooperative whose members take turns using a facility on dated slots. Members fall into a small number \(\tau\) of temporal-preference types, each sharing the same utility function; \(N\), the effective population scale, is much larger than \(\tau\). For a finite encoding, restrict each type \(\theta\) to a rational geometric discount factor \(\delta_\theta\), with utility \(u_\theta(S)=(1-\delta_\theta)\sum_{t\in S}\delta_\theta^{t-1}\). Type \(\theta\) has rational mass \(\mu_\theta\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). The schedule each person receives remains an integral subset of dates.

The lead candidate is Mass-Proportional Turn Feasibility. Its instance consists of \(N\ge2\), the finite type set \(\Theta\), the rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), and the rational discount factors \(\delta_\theta\), with each \(\delta_\theta\ge (2N-3)/(2N-2)\). The population is atomless, and each dated slot has capacity \(1/N\) of the population: this normalizes the original rule that one out of \(N\) agents receives each slot. The question is whether there are Borel probability measures \(\lambda_\theta\) over integral schedules \(S\subseteq\mathbb N\) such that \(\lambda_\theta\)-almost every schedule satisfies \(u_\theta(S)\ge 1/N\), and, for every date \(t\), \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\lambda_\theta(\{S:t\in S\})=1/N\). A solution is the family \((\lambda_\theta)_{\theta\in\Theta}\). Each measure describes how an atomless cohort is divided among people receiving different whole schedules; it is not a lottery giving any individual a fractional schedule.

I would bet on Class A for this restricted problem. When the masses align with \(N\) discrete agents, Theorem 1 supplies a feasible allocation directly. For unaligned masses, allowing cohorts to split by mass may remove integer-count obstacles, while the cycle structure in the proof offers a route to a construction. With geometric utilities, pricing a candidate schedule against per-date values also separates slot by slot. Those observations motivate the tractability conjecture; they do not establish it. Further questions are whether the construction extends to unaligned masses, whether feasible kernels have finite succinct descriptions, and how much the patience threshold can be lowered.

This case covers Theorem 1 only. It does not claim a mirror of Theorem 2’s envy-free allocation result. The weakest point is the continuous model’s per-slot capacity: \(1/N\) keeps the effective population scale in the instance even though people are represented by mass. That capacity scaling is a plausible co-modification of the original taking-turns problem, and individual bundles remain integral, but the paper neither studies mass cohorts nor proves that its construction extends to them. The proposed mirror therefore rests on a specific, testable extension of its proportional-allocation result—not on an established computational theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 1, and its weakness is fundamental: it is an existence theorem, not a complexity result for a finitely encoded problem. The utilities range over arbitrary countably additive measures on infinitely many dates, and the allocation is an infinite partition of those dates. The proof is constructive in the mathematical sense, but it scans an unbounded schedule and supplies no running-time bound or finite output representation. So the paper gives no computational result for a continuous-population version to inherit.

The proponent’s Mass-Proportional Turn Feasibility question repairs that gap by adding rational geometric utilities and a finite type list. But it also adds the central computational problem: feasibility over measures on an infinite space of schedules, subject to infinitely many date-capacity constraints. A finite input alone does not make that a finite algorithmic problem; one needs a representation for feasible measures or a theorem that bounds the schedules needed. And the suggested pricing intuition does not provide one: choosing a schedule subject to a minimum discounted value couples its dates, rather than separating slot by slot. This does not show the question is intractable. It shows that its tractability is a conjecture about a newly specified problem, not a computational consequence of Theorem 1.

There is a genuine modeling tradeoff in that specification. With one recipient per date, an atomless population gives each individual zero mass, so retaining a positive proportional share requires another scale. The proposal sets each date’s capacity and each person’s guarantee to \(1/N\). That is a coherent per-capita supply model, and may fit a large cooperative with many parallel users. But a date now serves positive mass, whereas the paper’s timeshare and custody examples allocate each date to one agent. The capacity scaling is permitted as a co-modification in the programme, so this is not a knockdown objection; it does mean the strongest version is a new model whose fit to the authors’ problem needs to be established.

The limit does not resolve that modeling question for free. As \(N\) grows, the guarantee \(1/N\) vanishes and the required geometric discount factors approach \(1\). A nondegenerate asymptotic question would need to say how to scale utility and time as well as population. Keeping \(N\) finite avoids that limit issue, but then the proposal is best understood as an atomless cohort extension at a chosen capacity scale, not as the large-population limit of the paper’s theorem.

I would therefore resist counting this as a strong continuous mirror on the evidence offered. The paper supplies no named computational result, and the proposed mirror still needs a finite witness model and a convincing bridge from one-recipient turns to mass-capacity schedules. But I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile version: cohorts of many identical users with per-capita slot supply are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting feasibility question could be computationally interesting. The negative case is strongest against treating the mirror as already well grounded; it is weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists.
