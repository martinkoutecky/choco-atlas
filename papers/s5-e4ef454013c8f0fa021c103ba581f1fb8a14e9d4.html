The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 2.1, proved here, characterizes a parametric-divisor allocation through pairwise transfer stability and an equivalent inequality. Theorem 2.2 is attributed to Balinski and Young and compares the relative bias of divisor methods; Corollary 2.3 and Theorems 2.4–2.5 give further comparisons and allocation properties. None classifies a problem as tractable or hard, or gives a computational formulation that the paper analyzes. The recursive divisor-method procedure and the census calculations are computational work, but they are not numbered results of the required kind.

The most sympathetic possible regime would be a large federation with many districts drawn from a small set of population types. A type would specify a district’s population, and its mass would be the fraction of districts of that type; a type-level average seat allocation could then be fractional even though each district receives an integer number of seats. One could study the aggregate bias of a divisor method on that distribution. But the paper’s results concern allocations to a finite set of constituencies and do not establish a computational result about this continuous question. It would be a proposed new problem, not a mirror anchored in the paper’s named results.

So there are zero anchors, and no per-anchor prover-ready continuous problem to state honestly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s zero-anchor conclusion holds. Theorem 2.1 characterizes when a pairwise seat-transfer rule yields the parametric divisor allocation; Theorem 2.2, Corollary 2.3, and Theorems 2.4–2.5 establish comparative properties of divisor methods. These are structural results, not complexity results about a computational problem. The paper gives a recursive way to produce allocations and reports numerical experiments, but neither supplies a complexity theorem to continuize.

The strongest plausible mirror would use voters in a large federation: let \(\mu_i\) be the population share in constituency \(i\), retain integer seat allocations \(d_i\), and study the divisor method or its bias as a function of these shares. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But constituency affiliation must remain part of the type, since the rule assigns seats to named constituencies and the transfer characterization compares them pairwise. Keeping those identities leaves a finite allocation problem over the same constituency coordinates; merging them into constituency types loses the allocation to each district. Either way, this suggests a related continuous-input question, but does not turn any result in the paper into a computational result.

A model that instead puts mass over constituency types and allocates fractional seat mass would change the object under study. It might motivate a new project, but it is not a stronger mirror of these theorems. The negative case is therefore strong for excluding this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile research question could be built around apportionment: the paper cannot rule out such a question; it simply does not provide the named computational result needed to anchor one.
