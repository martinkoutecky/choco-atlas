The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. The closest result is the cited finding by Barbanel, Brams, and Stromquist [3] that an allocation of a two-player pie exists that is envy-free, undominated, and equitable. This paper reports that result as prior work; it is an existence claim, not a complexity classification.

The paper’s own gap procedure produces an envy-free allocation that is \(\varepsilon\)-undominated and \(\varepsilon\)-equitable, while its exact procedure requires continuous calculations. The authors explicitly distinguish such moving-knife procedures from finite algorithms. Neither procedure is a numbered computational result suitable as an anchor.

The main obstacle to a ChoCo mirror is that the continuity here belongs to the pie and its nonatomic valuation measures, while the population remains exactly two players. Treating those players as high-multiplicity types would change the allocation problem, and the paper itself warns that its desired properties need not hold for three or more players. So the paper offers a useful neighboring example of continuous fair division, but no honest, anchor-backed computational question about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest mirror is a finite computation question about the paper’s constructive results. The gap procedure promises an \(\varepsilon\)-fair partition, and the exact procedure promises an exact one while explicitly requiring infinitely many calculations. Given finitely encoded valuation measures, what is the complexity of finding those cuts? The cited existence result of Barbanel, Brams, and Stromquist [3] makes the target precise; it does not answer how to compute a witness. The absence of a numbered complexity theorem is therefore a gap in the paper that a computational mirror could fill, not evidence that there is no question to ask.

There is also a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Imagine two large constituencies dividing access to a shared public amenity whose locations lie around a circle. Members have valuation types over those locations, and each constituency evaluates its allotment by its mass-weighted per-capita average. The society is a distribution over finitely represented valuation types; its masses determine the two aggregate measures. Computing an exact or \(\varepsilon\)-fair division for those measures is a population-continuous version of the paper’s problem, with the gap and exact procedures as direct anchors.

The limitation is that this setting gives fairness to the constituencies in average, not necessarily to every member. If the authors regard collective fairness as a sensible objective for shared public resources, the mirror is worth asking about. If they require guarantees to every heterogeneous individual, the case weakens sharply. But the paper’s fixed count of two players does not rule out a high-multiplicity regime: it can describe two constituencies whose members’ type distribution determines their collective valuations.
