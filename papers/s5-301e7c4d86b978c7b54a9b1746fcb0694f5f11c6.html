The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with integer entitlements \(w_t>0\), binary item-category values \(a_{tk}\in\{0,1\}\), rational society masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_k\), and integral bundle vectors \(B\) from a specified family \(\mathcal B\), where \(v_t(B)=\sum_k a_{tk}b_k(B)\), compute masses \(x_{tB}\) and nonnegative subsidies \(p_{tB}\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{tB}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}b_k(B)x_{tB}=\rho_k\), weighted envy-freeness \(\frac{v_t(B)+p_{tB}}{w_t}\geq\frac{v_t(C)+p_{uC}}{w_u}\) for every pair of occupied cells \((t,B)\) and \((u,C)\), \(p_{tB}\leq w_t/w_{\min}\), and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{tB}p_{tB}\leq(\sum_t\mu_t w_t)/w_{\min}\).

The model it lives in

A typed high-multiplicity fair-division model in which \(T\) is finite, \(\mu_t\) is population mass, bundles remain integral, \(x_{tB}\) are assignment masses, \(\rho_k\) are per-capita item supplies, and \(p_{tB}\) are cell-level subsidies.

The objection that survived

The finite proof is atomistic: a continuum algorithm must define simultaneous mass transfers, cell capacities, and tie handling while preserving non-redundancy and the subsidy invariant.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the binary-additive General Yankee Swap algorithm and its WEF-able subsidy guarantee. It leaves the external hardness citation and the general monotone, superadditive, matroidal, identical-item, dynamic-programming, and limited-budget results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time fluid analogue of the General Yankee Swap algorithm exist for finite binary-additive types and rational \(\mu\) and \(\rho\)?
  • Can configuration-LP or column-generation methods handle support selection when the integral bundle family \(\mathcal B\) is implicit?
  • Does the continuous problem remain tractable for fixed type signatures, or does hardness survive through item-category and configuration structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here. My lead is the paper’s hardness anchor: the paper invokes Corollary 1 of Halpern and Shah, cited rather than proved here, which says that computing the minimum total subsidy needed for an envy-free allocation is NP-hard even with binary additive valuations when the allocation is required to be non-wasteful. Equal weights are a special case, so this is also a hardness statement for weighted envy-freeness.

The natural continuous version is the following.

Take a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types. A type \(t\) consists of an entitlement \(w_t>0\) and a binary additive valuation \(a_{tk}\in\{0,1\}\) for each item category \(k\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). Item supplies also scale with population: \(\rho_k\) denotes the number of copies of category \(k\) per unit population. An individual still receives an integral bundle \(B\), not a fractional bundle; only the mass of agents receiving each bundle is fractional.

Let \(x_{tB}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\). Thus

\[ \sum_B x_{tB}=\mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t,B} b_k x_{tB}=\rho_k \]

for every item category \(k\), where \(b_k\) is the number of copies of \(k\) in \(B\). Write \(v_t(B)=\sum_k a_{tk}b_k\). For each occupied type–bundle cell \((t,B)\), choose a nonnegative subsidy \(p_{tB}\).

The problem I would hand to a prover is:

Continuous Minimum-Subsidy WEF. Given \(\mu\), the type data \((w_t,a_t)\), per-capita item supplies \(\rho\), and the allowed integral bundle configurations, find a feasible \(x\) and subsidies \(p\) minimizing

\[ \sum_{t,B}x_{tB}p_{tB} \]

subject to weighted envy-freeness between every two occupied cells:

\[ \frac{v_t(B)+p_{tB}}{w_t} \ge \frac{v_t(C)+p_{uC}}{w_u} \]

for every occupied \((t,B)\) and \((u,C)\). In the binary-additive version, non-wastefulness requires that positive mass of an item category be assigned only to types that value it whenever some other type values it.

This is a genuine population continuization of the cited NP-hard problem. It does not fractionalize bundles or utilities at the individual level. A finite realization with \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(N\rho_k\) copies of item \(k\) has exactly this interpretation. The quantities \(x_{tB}\) are empirical frequencies of integral assignments. Type-level averages may be fractional because the population is large, not because an agent receives half an item.

The regime is plausible in a large cooperative, employee-benefit pool, or shareholder settlement with many agents in a small number of entitlement and valuation classes. For example, millions of participants may fall into a dozen standardized entitlement–preference types, while the number of item categories is moderate. The paper’s authors should recognize this as the same weighted-envy-with-subsidies problem: same normalized utilities, same entitlements, same indivisible bundles, same total-subsidy objective. The only added structure is the high-multiplicity regime.

My expectation is that this problem is Class A for fixed or moderately structured type and bundle signatures: it becomes a configuration-allocation problem with linear mass constraints, and the subsidy feasibility of a fixed configuration is governed by the same longest-path/no-positive-cycle structure used in the paper. If the bundle family is exponentially represented, the interesting question becomes whether its pricing problem admits column generation. If the hardness reduction in Corollary 1 relies on many distinct item or valuation patterns rather than on the number of agents, hardness may transfer instead. That would not invalidate the mirror; it would place the problem in Class B.

The paper’s own positive anchor is Theorem 21, proved in the paper and cross-referenced in the supplied text as \(\Cref{app:theorem_21}\). It states that for additive binary valuations, the paper’s General Yankee Swap variant computes, in polynomial time, a WEF-able allocation in which agent \(i\) receives subsidy at most \(w_i/\wmin\), and the total subsidy is at most \(W/\wmin-1\). The proof uses transfer paths, non-redundancy, and the weighted gain rule.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Binary-Yankee Allocation. Given a typed society \(\mu\), binary valuations \(a_t\), weights \(w_t\), and per-capita item supplies \(\rho\), compute mass assignments \(x_{tB}\) and subsidies \(p_{tB}\) such that every agent receives an integral bundle, all supplies are exhausted, the outcome is WEF, and every occupied cell satisfies

\[ p_{tB}\leq \frac{w_t}{\wmin}. \]

If \(\bar w=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\) is average entitlement, require additionally that the total subsidy per unit population satisfy

\[ \sum_{t,B}x_{tB}p_{tB} \leq \frac{\bar w}{\wmin}. \]

The finite theorem’s \(-1\) disappears after normalization by population: one zero-subsidy agent has vanishing mass in the continuum limit. A fluid version of the paper’s transfer-path algorithm would repeatedly move an infinitesimal mass block along a path to an unallocated item, while types with identical gain values move together. This looks like a Class A problem, plausibly expressible through flow and configuration-LP machinery. It is not already contained in Theorem 21: that theorem treats named finite agents, whereas this question asks for the mass-level algorithm and its complexity in \(|T|\), the item signature, and the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(\rho\).

The mirror deliberately covers only the binary-additive, non-wasteful/minimum-subsidy part of the paper and the binary-additive polynomial algorithm. It does not claim to continuize every result about general monotone, matroidal, superadditive, or identical-item valuations.

The weakest point is the exact minimum-subsidy mirror. The WEF inequalities depend on which type–bundle cells receive positive mass, so the joint optimization is not automatically one plain LP; support selection may itself be difficult. Thus I would not claim that the continuous problem is already proved tractable. The positive case is that this is a sensible high-multiplicity problem, with a clear Class A regime and a clear boundary question: does the cited discrete hardness disappear when only a few complete agent types remain, or does the combinatorics survive in the item/configuration side?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither proposed anchor yet establishes a worthwhile population continuization, but it is not airtight.

The first anchor is already procedurally weak: the cited Corollary 1 of Halpern and Shah is not a result of this paper. More substantively, its hardness concerns arbitrary agent–item incidence patterns. If those patterns are retained, agents will generally have distinct valuation types, so the number of types grows with the instance and there is no high-multiplicity regime. If they are compressed into finitely many valuation types, while item supplies are simultaneously scaled into repeated categories, the resulting problem is a new capacitated configuration-allocation problem. That may be a sensible problem, but the proponent has not shown that it mirrors the cited hardness rather than replacing its source of combinatorial difficulty.

The repaired version is mathematically coherent. For fixed type and bundle signatures, the mass variables \(x_{tB}\) form a finite transportation/configuration model; for a fixed support, WEF subsidies are still obtained from the paper’s longest-path inequalities. If the signatures are variable, the difficulty lies in choosing configurations or item patterns. This is a legitimate Class A or Class B question under the programme, so it cannot be rejected merely because it may be easy or because hardness may survive. It does, however, mean that the proposed mirror has not demonstrated a population-specific phenomenon: the population measure mainly becomes a right-hand side in a resource-allocation problem, while the WEF constraints remain a finite graph over occupied bundle cells.

Theorem 21 is a stronger anchor, but its proof is markedly atomistic. It repeatedly selects one named agent, increases that agent’s utility by exactly one, follows a transfer path through unit-capacity bundles, and uses the fact that every allocated item contributes one unit to the owner’s binary valuation. A fluid operation moving mass \(\delta\) along a path is not automatically the same algorithm: one must define capacities for every type–bundle cell, preserve integral bundles for every individual, and prove that simultaneous transfers and ties preserve non-redundancy and the subsidy invariant. That would be a new theorem, not a continuization obtained by taking a limit.

The sharpest finite guarantee also does not survive. The proof obtains total subsidy \(W/\wmin-1\) partly because at least one named agent receives zero subsidy. After normalization by population,

\[ \frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{W}{\wmin}-1\right) = \frac{\bar w}{\wmin}-\frac{1}{N} \longrightarrow \frac{\bar w}{\wmin}. \]

A zero-subsidy cell in the continuum may have arbitrarily small mass, so the finite “minus one” yields no macroscopic saving. The proposed bound \(\bar w/\wmin\) is therefore only the aggregate of the pointwise bound \(p_{tB}\leq w_t/\wmin\), not a genuine population-level analogue of the theorem’s strongest conclusion.

Those objections defeat the proponent’s claim that the two anchors straightforwardly supply compelling continuous mirrors. They do not defeat the best repaired model. A large standardized pool with repeated indivisible benefit packages, finitely many entitlement–valuation types, and per-capita supplies is a plausible high-multiplicity fair-division regime, and the authors would likely recognize it as their problem. Under ChoCo’s rules, its being a configuration problem, or even turning out to be tractable, is not an objection.

So the honest negative verdict is weak: the paper does not support a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed mirrors need substantial remodelling and new proofs, but the binary-additive high-multiplicity model remains a credible continuous mirror.
