The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(t\in[\tau]\) with masses \(\mu_t\), scores \(v_t\), fits \(e_{it}\), acceptance probabilities \(p_t\), uncertainty levels \(\delta_t\), remaining quota per capita \(r_k\), and parameters \(\gamma_i,\eta_{i,k}\), choose the approached fraction \(x_t\in[0,1]\) of each type to minimize \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t(v_t+e_{it})(\eta_{i,k}\delta_t-p_t)+\gamma_i\max\{\sum_t\mu_t x_t p_t-r_k,0\}\). Return an optimal \(x\) and its objective value.

The model it lives in

A fixed college’s stage-wise choice over a population of applicant types: type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) and fixed attributes and acceptance behavior; the decision \(x_t\) is the fraction approached. Minimize the paper’s variational loss, with expected acceptance and over-quota penalty weighted by type mass.

The objection that survived

The paper already proves zero additive loss for a continuum of arms, so the finite-support mass formulation is largely a restatement of a solved case; this limits novelty but does not defeat a mirror that the paper itself establishes.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s stage-wise choice of which applicants a college should approach. Applicants are the large population: in a high-multiplicity admissions market, many applicants could share the same complete profile—scores and fits relevant to the colleges, preferences, and offer-response behavior. A market with \(N\) applicants and \(\tau\) such profiles, where \(\tau\ll N\), is a plausible regime. Seats scale with the cohort, so quotas can be expressed per applicant. This gives a concrete meaning to mass while leaving each match individual and integral.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, the greedy near-optimality result, proved in this paper in Appendix B.2.2. For a fixed true state \(s^*_{i,k}=s_{i,k}\), it says the greedy arm set \(\widehat{\mathcal B}_{i,k}(s_{i,k})\), formed by ranking arms by their variational expected utility per unit of acceptance probability and applying a cutoff, satisfies
\[ \min_{\mathcal B_{i,k}}\mathcal L^\dagger_{i,k}[\mathcal B_{i,k}] \leq \mathcal L^\dagger_{i,k}[\widehat{\mathcal B}_{i,k}(s_{i,k})] \leq \min_{\mathcal B_{i,k}}\mathcal L^\dagger_{i,k}[\mathcal B_{i,k}] +\mathrm{UE}^\dagger. \]
The additive term \(\mathrm{UE}^\dagger\) is nonnegative and equals zero when there is a continuum of arms and \(\pi_{i,k}(\cdot,v)\) is continuous in \(v\). Thus the greedy policy is exactly optimal in that continuum case. Theorem 1 supplies the connection to the paper’s main objective: for suitable \(\eta_{i,k}>0\) before the final stage and \(\eta_{i,K}=0\), minimizing the variational loss gives a maximizer of total expected payoff with high probability.

Here is the corresponding problem, Mass-LUB Stage Strategy. At stage \(k\), take the currently available applicant types \(t\in[\tau]\), with masses \(\mu_t\), college score \(v_t\), fit \(e_{it}\), acceptance probability \(p_t=\pi_{i,k}(s_{i,k},v_t)\), and uncertainty \(\delta_t=\delta_{i,k}(v_t)\). Let \(r_k\) be the college’s remaining quota per applicant, and let \(\gamma_i,\eta_{i,k}\) be the over-quota penalty and uncertainty weight. The input gives these quantities as binary rationals. Choose \(x_t\in[0,1]\), the fraction of type \(t\) the college approaches, to minimize
\[ \mathcal L^\dagger_\mu(x) = \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t x_t(v_t+e_{it})(\eta_{i,k}\delta_t-p_t) + \gamma_i\max\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t x_t p_t-r_k,\,0\right\}. \]
A solution is a rational vector \(x\) and its objective value. The fraction \(x_t\) means that this share of indistinguishable applicants receives an offer; each applicant who accepts still receives an integral match. The expected-payoff objective is the same expression with \(\eta_{i,k}\delta_t\) removed from the first sum and a minus sign on the over-quota penalty, as in the paper.

The expected direction is tractable (Class A). Introducing a slack variable for over-quota mass turns the problem into a linear program with \(O(\tau)\) variables and constraints. Its arithmetic running time is polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The paper’s cutoff theorem independently supports that conclusion: under its continuum conditions, the greedy rule has zero additive loss. Rational solutions can be realized by sufficiently replicating the cohort so the selected type masses and quota align with integer counts.

This is a close mirror of the authors’ problem: it keeps the same college decision, applicant attributes, acceptance model, utility, quota penalty, and stage-wise scope. The only population-side change is to replace sums over applicant individuals with sums over type masses and to allow contact at mass granularity. The continuous result is already partly present in the paper’s theorem, which explicitly treats a continuum of arms. That makes this a credible, author-recognizable mirror and a useful tractability boundary, though it leaves limited room to claim a new continuous algorithm.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s stage-wise strategy optimization and its expected-payoff interpretation through Theorem 1. It does not cover the paper’s statistical learning and state-calibration procedure, fairness propositions, or comparisons between multi-stage and centralized matching. Natural follow-up questions are how rounding performs for a fixed finite cohort, how many complete applicant types real markets actually have, and how to model the coupled evolution of applicant mass across all colleges and stages.

The weakest point is type compression. The paper’s simulations give colleges applicant-specific random fits; with such variation, exact full profiles may yield \(\tau\) close to \(N\). The positive case rests on markets with standardized profiles and shared response behavior, such as large admissions platforms using common credential and preference categories. That regime is plausible, and the paper’s own continuum-arm result supports it, but the paper’s examples do not establish that it is common. Also, the paper has no numbered NP-hardness or classical complexity-classification result; the eligible anchor here is its named algorithmic guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The offered anchor is the greedy stage-wise strategy result, with the variational theorem linking it to expected payoff. Its proposed population mirror is already present in the paper’s continuous-arms case. Here the arms are applicants, so a continuum of arms is a nonatomic applicant population. The paper says the greedy strategy’s additive loss is zero in that case when acceptance probability is continuous in score. Its cutoff selects the same kind of measurable set that the proposed mass model describes.

The stronger finite-type version does not create a new computational question. Replace each applicant sum by a mass-weighted sum, and allow the college to approach a fraction of a type. That yields the proposed piecewise-linear optimization; a partial choice from a boundary type handles cases where an atomic type’s full mass would cross the quota. This is the same cutoff computation expressed in type-mass notation. The variational theorem already connects that choice to the payoff objective. The point is not that the continuous answer is easy; it is that the paper has already analyzed this continuous-population version of the anchor.

Making applicant types more complete—adding their preferences or response behavior—does not improve the case if those attributes simply enter each applicant’s value and acceptance probability: the stage-wise choice remains a cutoff over arms. A broader model that makes colleges’ strategies jointly endogenous and tracks applicants across colleges and stages could pose a new computational problem. But that adds coupled market behavior beyond the anchored result, which optimizes one college’s stage-wise choice given acceptance probabilities. It is a possible extension, not a stronger mirror of this theorem.

The negative case is strongest for this anchor, rather than airtight for every possible extension of the paper. A new coupled-flow or equilibrium problem might be worthwhile; the supplied case does not establish one. It does, however, fail to establish a fresh continuous computational question for the result it chose.
