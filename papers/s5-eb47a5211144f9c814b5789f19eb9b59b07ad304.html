The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest anchor-based case for a continuous mirror here. The paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally complex.

The closest candidates do not meet that requirement. Theorem 10 states that, under its conditions on informational externalities and welfare-maximizing alternatives, a welfare-maximizing direct mechanism cannot be incentive compatible; it is attributed to Jehiel and Moldovanu (2001), with a proof given in the text. Theorem 16 states that with two agents who each have multidimensional signals, generic preferences permit only trivial ex-post implementable choice rules; it is attributed to Jehiel et al. (2005). Both are mechanism-design impossibility results, not results about the computational complexity of solving a problem.

A possible new question would put mass \(\mu\) over complete bidder types and ask whether a welfare-maximizing, incentive-compatible allocation and payment rule exists, or how to compute one. But that is not what these theorems establish: the paper studies a finite number of strategic agents whose individual signal spaces may be continuous. Recasting that as a continuum population would change the problem, and would not supply the computational anchor you require.

So there is no lead anchor, no anchor-specific continuous problem to hand a prover, and no basis here for predicting tractability, transferred hardness, or continuum-specific hardness. The weak point is decisive: the paper may inspire a new population model, but its named results do not support a computational continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no anchor in the case for to defeat: it raises no continuous question as a candidate mirror. Its strongest observation is also the strongest case against including this paper in ChoCo: Theorems 10, 11, and 16 are implementation and incentive-compatibility results, not computational-complexity results.

The best candidate would be to ask whether, or how efficiently, a welfare-maximizing incentive-compatible mechanism can be found for a population distribution of bidder types. But the paper’s results concern finitely many strategically significant agents, whose individual signals affect allocations and whose deviations must be deterred. Replacing them with an atomless population changes that strategic structure: a single report does not change the aggregate distribution. To preserve meaningful incentives, one would need to specify how each person’s report affects their own allocation, as well as how goods or licenses scale with the population. That could be a legitimate new model, but it would not be a direct mirror of the paper’s impossibility theorems.

There is also no computational input specified by those theorems. The paper allows continuous signals and general valuation functions; it does not give a finite representation from which an algorithmic problem and its complexity could be defined. Restricting the model to finitely encoded distributions and valuations might produce a computational mechanism-design problem, but that would require substantial new choices about the domain, allocations, and payment rules. The paper does not supply a natural formulation whose complexity landscape is waiting to be charted.

This is a strong reason against treating the paper as a ChoCo mirror candidate, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question could arise from auctions with externalities. Large-market extensions may be interesting on their own. The honest negative case is narrower: the paper’s named results do not anchor such a question, and the most plausible reformulation changes the strategic object enough that it is better described as a new project than as a continuization of this paper.
