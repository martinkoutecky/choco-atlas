Theorem 1

The continuous mirror question

Given finite resident-type and hospital-type sets with rational masses \((\alpha_a)\) and \((\beta_b)\), quotas \(q_b\), truncated strict rankings over the opposite-side types, and a target pair \((a_0,b_0)\) with initial tentative flow \(x_{a_0b_0}>0\), decide whether every completion preserving the submitted prefixes yields final DA flow on that pair of at least \(x_{a_0b_0}\). In type-level resident-proposing deferred acceptance, each type’s mass proposes in ranking order and each hospital type holds its highest-ranked incoming mass up to capacity \(q_b\beta_b\), splitting mass at cutoffs.

The model it lives in

Residents and hospitals are exchangeable cohorts with rational masses and common rankings within each type. The decision variable is the mass flow between type pairs under deferred acceptance; hospital-type capacity is per-hospital quota times hospital-type mass, and completions supply the undisclosed ranking tails.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and the quota-two singleton-list subcase of Theorem 5; it leaves the quota-one resident-minimal algorithm, the general sufficient condition, and the simulations unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does continuous FTM over rational type masses remain coNP-complete for unbounded support \(\tau\)?
  • What is the parameterized complexity of continuous FTM as a function of support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest mirror is a mass version of FTM. Imagine a large residency or hiring market with many applicants and many programs, but with repeated preference profiles: applicants of one type submit the same ranked list of program types, and members of each program type have the same ranking of applicant types and the same quota. A type includes every attribute the matching rule uses, so people in a type are interchangeable for this problem. There may be \(K\) people in each type while the number of types is \(\tau\); \(K\) can be much larger than \(\tau\).

Formally, give each resident type \(a\) a positive rational mass \(\alpha_a\), and each hospital type \(b\) a positive rational mass \(\beta_b\). Normalize their combined mass to one. A hospital of type \(b\) has per-hospital quota \(q_b\), so that type has total capacity \(q_b\beta_b\). Each type has a truncated strict ranking of the opposite-side types. Complete rankings must preserve the submitted prefixes. Run resident-proposing DA on these divisible masses: a type’s mass proposes in list order, and a hospital type holds its highest-ranked proposals up to capacity, splitting mass at a cutoff if needed. Missing hospital rankings use the paper’s pending-proposal rule.

The lead question is Continuous FTM. Its input is those masses, quotas and truncated lists, plus a type pair \((a_0,b_0)\) with positive tentative flow \(x_{a_0b_0}\). For each completion \(J\) of all type lists, let \(y^J_{a_0b_0}\) be the flow on that pair in the completed DA outcome. Compute the worst-case retained flow
\[ \Phi=\min_J y^J_{a_0b_0}. \]
The answer is yes exactly when \(\Phi\ge x_{a_0b_0}\): the entire tentative amount for that type pair is guaranteed to remain matched together. A no-instance has a concrete witness, a completion that lowers the flow below \(x_{a_0b_0}\). This is the mass-level counterpart of asking whether a tentative match survives every completion.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1 of Tamaki’s paper: “1-FTM, and hence FTM, is coNP-complete.” The theorem is proved in the paper, by reducing SAT to the complement of 1-FTM. In full, deciding whether a specified tentative resident–hospital pair occurs in the resident-optimal stable matching for every completion of the submitted lists is coNP-complete even when every hospital quota is one, that is, in stable marriage. [Tamaki’s paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.04381)

A second useful anchor is Theorem 5, also proved there: “2-FTM-RM is coNP-complete.” Here the question is the same finalizability question, restricted to resident-minimal instances—where residents have disclosed no unused tail beyond their tentative match—and to hospital quotas of at most two. The proof reduces from 2-DAG-FIRING. This restriction is particularly relevant to the paper’s incremental-list setting, where resident-minimal instances arise naturally.

The corresponding second question is Continuous FTM-RM-2: use the same mass-flow input and target, restrict quotas to \(q_b\le2\), and require that every tentatively held resident mass has exhausted its disclosed list through its tentative partner (unmatched mass has exhausted its submitted list). Ask whether \(\Phi\ge x_{a_0b_0}\). In the paper’s Theorem 5 reduction, nonempty resident lists have length one, so the resident-minimal condition carries over directly.

I expect both mirrors to be Class B: the discrete hardness transfers. In the reductions, treat each logical resident and hospital as a type of mass \(\epsilon=1/(|R|+|H|)\). For an explicit large-population regime, make \(K\) interchangeable copies of every logical type; after normalization each type still has mass \(\epsilon\), and a hospital type’s capacity is an integer multiple of \(\epsilon\). Proposal and rejection flows in these instances stay on that same mass grid, so the continuous DA execution reproduces the reduction’s type-level decisions instead of gaining a fractional escape route. Completions over types reproduce the reduction’s preference completions, and the target tentative pair becomes a target type-pair mass. Thus the counterexample completions, and the coNP-hardness, survive. For these finite-support problems, a no-witness is also just a completion of the type lists; mass DA and its rational arithmetic can verify that witness in polynomial time. That gives coNP-completeness for the stated mirrors.

This is a mirror the authors should recognize: it keeps their uncertainty over truncated preferences, their resident-proposing DA process, and their universal finalizability criterion. It changes the unit from a named person to an exchangeable cohort, and asks whether the tentative *mass* for a type pair is guaranteed. That change is what makes population continuization meaningful; the preference lists still describe the matching decision.

My weakest point is the support regime. The reductions use a separate logical type for each participant in the hard instance. Replicating each type gives arbitrarily many agents per type, but \(\tau\) still grows with the reduction. So this establishes hardness for unbounded support; it does not show hardness when the number of types is fixed or naturally small. Nor does a guaranteed type-pair mass certify that particular named applicants will receive particular programs. Those limits matter for applications, but they do not invalidate the stated high-multiplicity problem or its complexity result: the programme tracks \(\tau\), and hardness in \(\tau\) is a meaningful boundary.

The scope here is the general FTM hardness in Theorem 1 and the resident-minimal, quota-two hardness in Theorem 5. I am not claiming mirrors for the paper’s quota-one resident-minimal algorithm, its sufficient safety test, or its simulations. A useful next question is whether that quota-one resident-minimal tractability result extends to arbitrary rational type masses, rather than only the replicated mass-grid instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the hardness reductions do not show what happens when a fixed collection of voter-like types acquires a genuinely fluid population. In Theorem 1, each logical resident and hospital has a distinct role. Replicating each role \(K\) times and scaling capacities makes the mass execution stay on a discrete grid: the reduction’s proposals and rejections happen to whole cohorts. That is a valid high-multiplicity embedding, but it yields no fractional escape and no new continuous phenomenon. The support still grows with the SAT instance.

There is a deeper modeling cost in that embedding. Tamaki’s uncertainty is over completions of named agents’ strict preference lists. The proposed mass version completes rankings over *types*, so everyone in a cohort shares the same hidden tail and rankings within a cohort disappear. If hidden preferences vary within the cohort, those agents are different types under the programme’s definition; if they do not, the model imposes common hidden information. The flow question—whether all tentative mass between two types remains together—is sensible, but it is a new aggregate guarantee, not the paper’s guarantee about a particular match.

The best repair is also clear: define preferences over program and applicant categories, use fluid capacities, and ask whether the tentative flow between a category pair is preserved under every completion of the category rankings. That is recognizable as a mass version of finalizability. The paper’s large-market motivation makes such a scenario plausible. The objection therefore weakens the claim that Theorem 1’s hardness establishes a useful low-support mirror; it does not defeat the repaired mirror.

Theorem 5 has the same limitation. Its reduction uses resident-minimal instances whose nonempty submitted lists have length one, and the logical roles can again be replicated on a mass grid. So it carries coNP-hardness into a high-multiplicity encoding, while saying little about fractional resident-minimal executions. More generally, a type mass can split across hospitals during deferred acceptance; then one type may have tentative flow to multiple hospitals, complicating the paper’s resident-minimal condition that each resident’s list ends at their tentative match. But the reduction’s singleton-list case avoids that complication, so it does not invalidate the anchor.

So the negative case is weak where it needs to be strongest. The two anchors establish hardness in an unbounded-support, grid-preserving regime; they do not establish a new continuous effect in a natural small-support market. That is a legitimate reservation about the proponents’ evidence and the mirror’s practical reach. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the category-level finalizability question remains coherent and close enough to the paper’s problem that I cannot honestly rule it out.
