The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result in this paper that can serve as an anchor. Although it uses the word “incomputability,” the paper defines that term broadly around formal contradictions and develops a correspondence between logic and social choice. It gives no result classifying an algorithmic problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The undecidability results mentioned in the introduction are prior work, not results established here.

The closest anchor is Theorem 3.6.9, whose content is that if a social welfare function \(w:P^N\to P\) satisfies unanimity, IIA, and non-dictatorship, then there are profiles \(q,q'\in P^N\) such that \(w(q)=w(q')=c\), where \(c\) denotes the contradictory preference cycle, and \(q,q'\) are inconsistent. The paper derives this by identifying preference cycles in a result from [27, Theorem 4.3.4]. It is a structural existence statement about Arrow’s conditions, with no computational problem or algorithm attached. Theorem 3.5.9, concerning dictators and diagonalisers, is likewise structural.

A plausible adjacent extension would take types to be weak rankings over a fixed alternative set and a society to be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types. One could ask whether a rule on such distributions, satisfying continuous versions of unanimity and IIA, must produce a cycle somewhere or have a dictator. Large populations with a small number of recurring ballot types make that setting credible. But this is an axiomatic existence question, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper. Moreover, the finite indexed profiles used here retain the identity of a possible dictator; a distribution over types discards that identity, so a continuous formulation would need to decide what dictatorship means.

So the strongest honest positive case is that population continuization is a natural direction for extending the paper’s Arrow analysis. I cannot supply the requested continuous problem per anchor or assign it a complexity-trichotomy outcome: the paper has no eligible computational anchor, and inventing one would go beyond mirroring its named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that neither Theorem 3.6.9 nor Theorem 3.5.9 states a computational result. The paper’s use of “incomputability” is broader than algorithmic complexity: Theorem 3.6.9 is an existence claim about profiles under Arrow’s axioms, and Theorem 3.5.9 characterizes dictatorship through a fixed-point property. The undecidability results in the introduction are prior work, not results this paper establishes. There is no algorithmic problem here whose complexity a continuous version could classify.

The best rescue of Theorem 3.6.9 would take a society to be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types and ask whether a continuous aggregation rule produces a cycle for some \(\mu\). But that requires supplying a rule on distributions and an algorithmic representation of it. The paper supplies neither: its \(w:P^N\to P\) is an arbitrary function on indexed profiles. Without a specified representation of \(w\), “find a cycle” has no defined computational input, and its complexity would be determined by whichever representation a new study chose. That could become a computational social choice project, but it would add the computational problem rather than mirror one in this paper.

There is also a deeper obstacle to preserving the theorem’s meaning. A distribution over rankings forgets which individual has which ranking, while dictatorship is explicitly about a fixed individual across profiles. Put identity into each type and agents no longer repeat; keep types anonymous and a positive-mass type is a bloc, not a dictator. Giving one named person mass \(1/N\) makes that mass vanish as \(N\) grows; keeping it positive changes the question to bloc dictatorship. The same issue defeats the more ambitious mirror of Theorem 3.5.9: its “\(i\)-th indicator” and diagonaliser depend on a distinguished coordinate that a population distribution does not retain. Replacing that coordinate with a type or a mass fraction changes the property being characterized.

A new study could choose a continuous aggregation model and ask algorithmic questions about it. I can’t rule out that such a study would be worthwhile. But it would need a new rule representation, computational task, and interpretation of dictatorship; none is supplied by the paper’s results. The negative case is therefore strongest as a claim about the absence of a worthwhile mirror of this paper, rather than a proof that no related continuous computational project could be worthwhile.
