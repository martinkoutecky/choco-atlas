The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) interval islands, a piece limit \(k\), a rational population scale \(N\), and \(\tau\) types with rational proportions \(\mu_t\) and rational piecewise-constant valuations \(V_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(V_t(C)=1\), assign mass \(N\mu_t\) of each type to bundles that are unions of at most \(k\) intervals. Represent the assignment by a measure \(\pi_t\) over bundles, with \(\pi_t\) having total mass \(N\mu_t\) and aggregate cake use at most one at almost every point. Find the largest \(\alpha\) such that \(\pi_t\)-almost every bundle assigned to each type \(t\) has value at least \(\alpha\); characterize the sharp worst-case guarantee over such instances and determine whether it equals \(\min\{1/N,k/(m+N-1)\}\).

The model it lives in

A population of total mass \(N\) with \(\tau\) complete valuation types in proportions \(\mu_t\), sharing a fixed cake of \(m\) interval islands with unit capacity. Each individual receives an ordinary bundle of at most \(k\) intervals; only the aggregate assignment of bundles within a type is fractional. Maximize the common value guarantee and characterize its worst-case value.

The objection that survived

With fixed cake supply, the guaranteed value per claimant tends to zero as \(N\) grows, and the stated mirror does not scale supply with population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's absolute guarantee, its constructive algorithm, and the matching upper bound; it leaves the relative-value guarantee, envy-freeness, polygon application, and maximin-share questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the sharp worst-case guarantee for the type-mass model equal \(\min\{1/N,k/(m+N-1)\}\)?
  • Can an optimal or suitably approximated assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N\), for a stated finite encoding of the valuations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(m,k,n\geq 1\), the largest fraction of total value that can be guaranteed to every agent in a multicake division with at most \(k\) intervals per agent is
\[ r(m,k,n)=\min\left\{\frac1n,\frac{k}{m+n-1}\right\}. \]
The paper proves both sides: some instance rules out any larger universal guarantee, and a constructive polynomial-time algorithm attains this one. The guarantee and algorithm are the result I would mirror; the paper has no named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question. Its polynomial-time claim is in the paper’s valuation-access setting, rather than a stated bit-complexity bound for rational input.

A credible regime is a large public land-allocation programme: many claimants share a small number of complete valuation profiles over the same \(m\) estates, and each claimant can practically manage at most \(k\) plots. The population scale \(N\) is large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles is much smaller. A type records the full valuation density and any other relevant constraint; two claimants share a type only if they are indistinguishable for the allocation problem. The estates remain a single finite resource. This is a population continuation, not a claim that divisible land by itself makes the paper’s problem continuous.

I would ask the prover to study Mass Multicake Proportionality. An instance has \(m\) interval islands \(C\), a piece limit \(k\), a rational population scale \(N\geq1\), and a finite support of types \(T\), with rational proportions \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Type \(t\) has mass \(N\mu_t\) and a nonnegative, rational piecewise-constant valuation \(V_t\), normalized so \(V_t(C)=1\). A legal individual bundle \(B\) is a union of at most \(k\) intervals.

A solution is a mass assignment \(\pi_t\) over legal bundles for each type. It must assign total mass \(N\mu_t\) to type \(t\), and the mass receiving any point \(x\) of the cake must be at most its available capacity:
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\int \mathbf{1}[x\in B]\,d\pi_t(B)\leq 1 \quad\text{for almost every }x\in C. \]
The allocation’s guarantee is the largest \(\alpha\) such that \(\pi_t\)-almost every assigned bundle satisfies \(V_t(B)\geq\alpha V_t(C)\), for every type. The task is to find that guarantee and a corresponding allocation; the broader theorem is to determine the sharp worst-case guarantee over instances as a function of \(m,k,N\), and the type support. Each claimant receives one ordinary bundle of at most \(k\) intervals. Only the aggregate assignment to a type is fractional.

The natural conjecture is that the sharp guarantee continues to be
\[ \min\left\{\frac1N,\frac{k}{m+N-1}\right\}, \]
with real type masses in place of integer headcounts. I expect this to be Class A, though that is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 1. For a fixed target \(\alpha\), the allocation can be viewed as a mass-flow LP whose columns are qualifying \(k\)-interval bundles. The key question is whether its pricing and capacity structure can be handled in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N\), where \(L\) includes the rational valuation descriptions. A compressed version of the paper’s threshold and matching arguments may provide the route; the configuration LP makes the continuous-optimization tools relevant. The theorem’s formula is a conjectured target for the prover, not a result I am attributing to this paper’s continuous mirror.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s absolute proportional guarantee and its constructive algorithm. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s relative-value guarantee, envy-freeness results, rectilinear-polygon application, or maximin-share questions. Nor does the fact that cake is already divisible count as the proposed continuation: the new object is the claimant population and its type-mass allocation.

The weakest point is whether the authors would accept a mass assignment over bundles as preserving the force of their problem. An average bundle per type would be too weak: it could conceal claimants who miss the guarantee. The definition above avoids that by requiring every bundle receiving positive type mass to meet the target and by retaining integral, \(k\)-interval bundles for individuals. Even so, the original paper’s algorithm reasons directly about a finite list of agents, so replacing that list with type masses is a genuine extension. The mirror is strongest as a question about whether its exact guarantee and algorithm compress to high multiplicity—not as a claim that the paper already contains that answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it gives a sharp guarantee and a constructive polynomial-time algorithm. So the decisive “no named computational result” objection is unavailable.

The strongest negative case is about the proposed model’s scale. With a fixed cake and \(N\) growing, the paper’s guarantee is \(\min\{1/N,\ k/(m+N-1)\}\), which tends to zero. For bounded piecewise-constant valuations, that makes each claimant’s guaranteed value vanish too. The model can still ask a finite-\(N\) question, but its literal large-population limit risks losing the positive per-person fairness guarantee that motivates the paper.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror. One can scale the available land with the population, or study a suitably normalized aggregate guarantee. The programme explicitly allows supply to scale with population when that is part of the model. And a type-mass assignment over ordinary \(k\)-interval bundles is a coherent way to represent an atomless population while enforcing aggregate capacity. It leaves a substantive question: whether the paper’s guarantee and algorithm extend to masses, and how to compute such an allocation from finitely encoded valuations.

The missing bit-complexity model in the paper is a limitation, too, but rational piecewise-constant valuations give a reasonable finite-input version. Neither issue rules out a worthwhile mirror. The honest negative case is weak: the fixed-supply formulation may degenerate asymptotically, but a plausible rescaling repairs that concern, and the paper’s central result survives as a meaningful high-multiplicity question.
