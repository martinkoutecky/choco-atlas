The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(Z\), rational masses \(\mu_z>0\), persistence \(\rho\), and a stationary type-level equilibrium weight matrix \(W\) with signal weights \(w_z^s\), compute each type’s tagged-member social influence in the block continuum, \(\mathrm{SI}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{w_z^s}{\mu_z}\left[\mu^\top\left((I-\rho W)^{-1}-I\right)\right]_z\), and determine when this resolvent is well-defined.

The model it lives in

A continuum block society with types \(z=(k,\tau)\), masses \(\mu_z\), a block observation kernel, and type-level equilibrium weights \(W\). Social influence is the per-tagged-member response obtained by normalizing the type-level aggregate response by source mass, giving a finite resolvent when the type support is finite.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a narrow, finite-type population model. The paper itself studies stochastic block models with fixed numbers of network types and signal types as the number of agents grows. A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization with recurring cohorts of workers in a few roles, each role having a small number of signal-quality classes and a stable pattern of connections. A type would be \((k,\tau)\), its mass \(\mu_{k\tau}=\alpha_k q_{k\tau}\), and the block link probabilities would describe how agents observe one another.

There is a strict limitation, though: the paper has no named computational-complexity result to serve as an anchor. Proposition 1 proves equilibrium existence; Theorem 1 proves good aggregation in signal-diverse random networks; Theorem 2 gives a lower bound under homogeneous signals. These are analytic results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Proposition 5 gives a formula for social influence, but does not establish a complexity result. The introduction’s claim that equilibria can be calculated quickly is not a numbered result. I would not present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The closest concrete mirror I can offer is therefore explicitly unanchored: Finite-Type Stationary Learning Equilibrium. Given rational masses \(\mu_{k\tau}\), block link probabilities, signal variances, persistence \(\rho\), memory \(m\), and tolerance \(\varepsilon\), find a stationary Bayesian linear equilibrium of the continuum block society to additive error \(\varepsilon\). A solution would give type-level updating weights and an error-covariance matrix satisfying the best-response and stationary covariance conditions to that tolerance; the corresponding aggregation errors could then be reported for each type. This is recognizable as computation of the paper’s equilibrium object, and Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 motivate it, but neither anchors a complexity classification for it. Whether it is tractable as the number of types grows is open; fixed-size numerical computation seems plausible, not proved by the paper.

The mirror’s weak point is substantial: replacing realized neighborhoods with type-level masses may suppress individual network variation, while exact Bayesian inference from correlated observations is central to the paper. Also, this is computational social learning rather than a problem in social choice. So the paper supports the *plausibility of a high-multiplicity population regime*, but under the requested anchor rule it supplies no anchored continuous computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. Proposition 1 proves equilibrium existence; Theorems 1 and 2 concern aggregation accuracy; Proposition 5 gives a formula for influence. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The claim that equilibria can be calculated quickly is not a complexity result. So the proponent’s “Finite-Type Stationary Learning Equilibrium” is a newly proposed computational task, motivated by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational results. Under the requested anchor standard, the case for has no qualifying anchor.

The proposed types \((k,\tau)\) also do not capture everything agents use in the finite model. Agents with the same network and signal types can have different realized neighborhoods, and best responses depend on the covariances induced by those neighborhoods. Collapsing them to masses \(\mu_{k\tau}=\alpha_k q_{k\tau}\) therefore requires a mean-field closure. In a dense continuum version, averaging independent signal errors can remove the sampling noise that gives the paper’s \(C/n\) aggregation rate its content; the benchmark and the error both vanish in the limit. Recovering finite-size rates would require retaining a population-size parameter and random graph fluctuations, which defines a different, finite-size computational problem. A sparse version would likewise need more network structure than block masses alone.

There are stronger computational tasks one could formulate: approximate a continuum equilibrium, optimize the distribution of signal quality, or calculate type-level influence. They may be worthwhile research questions. But each needs its own model and algorithmic analysis; citing an existence, accuracy, or influence result does not turn it into an anchored complexity mirror. They also sit in computational social learning rather than the usual social-choice problems of voting, control, or campaigning, which weakens their fit with ChoCo.

I would not defend the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous model. The paper’s growing stochastic block models make large populations with repeated signal and network classes a plausible regime, and a complete-network continuum model is easy to motivate. That is evidence for a sensible population continuum, but it does not cure the central weakness: the paper offers no named computational result for that continuum to mirror.
