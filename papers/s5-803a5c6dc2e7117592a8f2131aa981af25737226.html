The proposed consortium model adds a population limit and a new mechanism-construction task whose finite input and incentive constraints are not specified by the paper. What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational population mirror; Proposition 1, Proposition 2, and Corollary 1 are economic possibility results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires as an anchor. Its numbered results prove economic feasibility claims; they do not classify a computational problem, give an algorithm or running time, or compute with an explicit formulation. The paper also permits continuous *private type spaces* while keeping the set of agents finite. That is not itself a continuous-population model.

The closest results are Proposition 2, proved here, and Corollary 1, also proved here. Proposition 2 says that when the decision space is convex, total surplus is convex in the decision, and a decision rule is implementable in an incentive-compatible mechanism satisfying cross-congruence, the expected equilibrium decision sustains that rule in bargaining. Corollary 1 specializes this to private values: under those convexity assumptions, the expected efficient decision permits efficient bargaining. Proposition 1 gives related interim participation guarantees for a randomized status quo matching the equilibrium allocation’s distribution, or—under stronger convexity assumptions—for its expectation. These are substantive, natural results, but none is a computational anchor under the stated rules.

There is a plausible *follow-on* population model. Imagine a large consortium bargaining over a divisible resource, with agents in a small number of complete valuation and institutional types. Type \(t\) describes everything relevant to incentives and participation; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the population. A mechanism assigns per-capita bundles and transfers, subject to aggregate resource feasibility. One could ask whether the expected efficient allocation can serve as the status quo for an efficient, incentive-compatible, interim-participation, budget-balanced mechanism, and require a mechanism or an impossibility certificate. This is a credible high-multiplicity setting, but it needs a careful continuum-agent formulation—especially for individual incentives and what “expected efficient allocation” means for a mass of agents. It is my proposed extension, not a question anchored by a computational result in this paper, so I would not assign it a Class A/B/C outcome.

The possible scope is only the efficient-bargaining result around Proposition 2 and Corollary 1. The weakest point is decisive for the requested case: the paper’s theorem concerns participation and implementation, while the proposed mirror adds both a population limit and a computational task. The authors might recognize it as a related mechanism-design extension, but the paper does not establish that it is the continuous form of one of its computational problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 gives participation guarantees for a mechanism already in hand; Proposition 2 gives a sufficient condition for a status quo to sustain a decision rule; Corollary 1 applies that result to efficient private-values bargaining. None defines a computational problem, an algorithmic input, or a complexity claim. The general model allows arbitrary measurable type and allocation spaces and bounded measurable utilities, with no finite encoding or input length.

Proposition 1 is especially easy to misread as a continuization result: its status quo may be randomized, and its deterministic version uses an expected allocation. Those are changes to the outcome, while the set of agents remains finite. The paper also allows continuous private-type spaces, but again keeps the agents finite. Neither gives the population distribution over complete voter or agent types that ChoCo’s mirror requires.

The consortium proposal is the strongest possible extension. One could specify finitely many complete agent roles, give each role a population mass and a private-type distribution, and study efficient mechanisms with interim participation and budget balance. That is a coherent large-population mechanism-design question. It still needs a new finite input model: encodings for the distributions, utilities, decision space, and mechanism; a definition of individual incentives and feasibility in the population limit; and a computational task such as constructing a mechanism. Proposition 2 starts with an implementable mechanism and supplies a status quo. It does not formulate that construction problem. In the atomless version, an individual report also has no effect on the population distribution, so the incentive constraints must be specified for the new model rather than read off from the paper.

A finite-support, finite-agent version could make the new task computationally precise. Its complexity would depend on how the mechanism and distributions are represented and what the task asks the algorithm to compute. Those choices may lead to worthwhile research, but the paper’s results do not select among them. They would create a follow-on problem rather than identify a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the paper’s eligibility as a ChoCo mirror and weaker as a universal claim about the underlying economics. Large-population bargaining is a plausible research direction; I cannot honestly rule out a useful model of it. What I can say is that even the best version of the proposed extension supplies a new computational target rather than continuing one established by Proposition 1, Proposition 2, or Corollary 1.
