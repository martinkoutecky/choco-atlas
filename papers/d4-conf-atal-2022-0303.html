The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a qualifying anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. The closest claim is that the welfare-maximizing MAPF allocation problem is NP-complete, cited to [8]; it is not presented here as a numbered result. The paper’s own contribution is a heuristic auction evaluated experimentally. So there is no honest way to give the required result-anchored case.

The strongest candidate mirror is the welfare-maximizing MAPF allocation problem underlying the auction. A plausible regime is a large warehouse fleet operating across repeated aisles or parallel lanes: many robots share a small set of task and cost types, while capacity at each vertex and time scales with fleet size. A type \(t\) specifies a robot’s origin and destination classes and its complete path-cost function. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu_t\) over those types. For each feasible discrete path \(p\) of type \(t\), let \(x_{t,p}\) be the mass routed along it. The constraints are \(\sum_p x_{t,p}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,p:(v,k)\in p}x_{t,p}\leq q_{v,k}\) for each vertex-time resource, where \(q_{v,k}\) is capacity per unit of population. Minimize \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\ell_t(p)\), with \(\ell_t(p)\) the path cost; a solution is a feasible mass assignment and its objective value.

I expect this mass-allocation problem to be tractable: it is a path-variable LP, and with additive path costs its pricing step is a shortest-path computation in a time-expanded graph. That would make it a plausible Class A mirror of the paper’s social-welfare optimization, not of its entire mechanism. Further questions include whether the LP has an integral solution at finite population sizes, how its optimum approximates finite MAPF, and whether any of the paper’s auction and participation properties survive for a continuum of agents.

The soft spot is substantial: this formulation gives a central planner the aggregate allocation problem, while the paper’s distinctive contribution is a non-cooperative auction that preserves each agent’s autonomy and studies incentives. Also, fixed shared infrastructure may not support a nontrivial high-multiplicity limit; the regime needs capacity to grow with the fleet. Those caveats are why this is a credible candidate mirror, but not a positive case anchored in a named result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s opening observation is the strongest objection to their case: the paper proves no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about computational complexity. Its NP-completeness statement concerns the general combinatorial-auction winner-determination problem and cites prior work; the paper’s own contribution is a heuristic mechanism and experiments. That gives no named computational result to anchor the proposed mirror.

The best rescue is the warehouse path-flow model. With repeated robot types, additive path costs, and vertex-time capacities scaled with fleet size, its variables \(x_{t,p}\) describe how much type-\(t\) mass takes path \(p\). This is a coherent continuous allocation problem, and its pricing step can be a shortest-path computation. The warehouse story makes the regime plausible; scaled capacities are an allowed co-modification. So I would not reject this model merely because it is fractional or potentially tractable.

Its limitation is that it does not preserve the paper’s distinctive computational question. The flow LP chooses a centrally planned aggregate allocation. PKA and VCG instead allocate indivisible paths among named agents with private valuations, calculate exclusion-based payments, and try to preserve agent autonomy. In an atomless population, removing one agent changes aggregate welfare by zero, so the VCG externality payment vanishes; an individual’s acceptance or deviation likewise has no effect on the aggregate allocation. Modeling strategic choices by positive-mass cohorts could restore influence, but it adds a group-deviation model the paper does not study. The path-flow problem remains a plausible neighboring question, while a continuous mirror of the mechanism’s incentive and payment claims needs a substantially different formulation.

There is also a support-size obstacle for the paper’s own MAPF regime. Its agents have distinct starts and goals on a graph with unit-capacity vertex-time resources. On a fixed graph, exact endpoint and path-cost types therefore tend to proliferate with the fleet. A bounded-support limit needs a repeated or symmetric environment, with resources scaled to match. That is credible for some warehouses, but it is a constructed regime rather than the regime established by the paper’s experiments.

My negative case is strongest on the requested standard: no result-anchored mirror has been identified, and the best proposed rescue is an aggregate planning problem that drops the paper’s strategic mechanism. It is weaker as a universal claim. The scaled-warehouse path-flow problem is coherent and could be worth studying; I cannot honestly show that no scenario makes it worthwhile.
