The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper does not provide a compliant anchor for a continuous mirror. Although its LaTeX preamble declares theorem environments, the body contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition, and no formal complexity result proved by the authors. Statements such as “the problem is computationally very expensive” and “almost insurmountable obstacle” are forecasts or informal assessments, not named results. The relevant formal results are attributed to other papers—Alon and Edelman (2010), De et al. (2012), Kurz et al. (2014b), and others—but are not reproduced as numbered results here.

The best positive case would therefore be conditional on accepting Section 4’s inverse-power discussion as an anchor. Its most promising mirror is a high-multiplicity inverse power-design problem.

Consider a large two-tier organization with a finite set \(T\) of voter types. A type records everything relevant to the voting rule: constituency, representation status, and per-capita voting weight. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta^{T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members of type \(t\). Thus \(N\mu_t\) members are interchangeable type-\(t\) voters in an \(N\)-member realization. This is plausible for a large federation, union, shareholder organization, or electoral system with millions of members but only a small number of constituency classes.

The decision variable is a weighted majority rule: nonnegative per-capita weights \(a_t\) and quota \(q\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=1\). Given \(N\), form the weighted voting game in which a coalition \(S\) wins exactly when

\[ \sum_{i\in S}a_{\operatorname{type}(i)}\ge q. \]

For \(I\in\{\mathrm{PBI},\mathrm{SSI}\}\), let \(P^I_{N,t}(a,q)\) be the normalized aggregate power of all type-\(t\) voters—equivalently, the sum of their individual power indices. Define the continuum type-power vector by the high-multiplicity limit

\[ \Pi^I_t(a,q;\mu) = \lim_{N\to\infty}P^I_{N,t}(a,q), \]

along integers for which every \(N\mu_t\) is integral.

The continuous problem is:

Given \((T,\mu,r,\varepsilon)\), where \(r\in\Delta^T\) is a target distribution of aggregate power, choose \((a,q)\) minimizing
\[ > \|\Pi^I(a,q;\mu)-r\|_1, > \]
or decide whether some rule achieves distance at most \(\varepsilon\).

This is recognizably the paper’s inverse Banzhaf/Shapley-Shubik question: choose a voting rule whose induced power distribution approximates a prescribed target. The only co-modification forced by continuization is that individual power is aggregated by type; otherwise every individual’s limiting pivotality is zero. The paper’s discussion of two-tier systems, population sizes, weighted voting, and large voting bodies gives the authors a plausible reason to recognize this as their problem rather than as an unrelated optimization task.

I would tentatively expect the fixed-\(|T|\) approximation version to be Class A: coalition enumeration may collapse into a finite-dimensional limit calculation, while the number of distinct types remains small. Exact unrestricted optimization could instead be continuum-specific hard, especially near quota thresholds. The resulting questions include whether the limit exists uniformly, how rapidly finite elections converge to it, which target vectors are attainable, and whether the known finite-population obstructions of Alon and Edelman survive.

The weakest point is decisive: this mirror is not anchored to a numbered result in the paper, and it changes individual power into aggregate type power. A referee could reasonably say that it is a natural extension of the paper’s inverse-power agenda, but not a continuous version of a theorem actually stated there. Thus the paper supports a plausible mirror, but not the anchor-based positive case requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no compliant anchor. It is a forecast essay, not a theorem paper: the preamble declares theorem environments, but the body contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or formal complexity result. “Computationally very expensive” and “almost insurmountable obstacle” are informal forecasts. The actual inverse-power and asymptotic results are attributed to other papers. Under the programme’s anchor rule, there is therefore no result here whose continuous mirror can be graded.

The proposed inverse-power mirror is the strongest rescue, but it is not actually the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem without several substantive choices. In the finite problem, the target is a power vector over individual players. If \(N\mu_t\) players are identical copies of type \(t\), symmetry forces them to have equal power. Preserving the original target requires distinguishing individuals, which makes the number of types grow with \(N\) and removes the high-multiplicity regime. Aggregating their power into a type-level quantity,

\[ \Pi_t^I=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i:\operatorname{type}(i)=t} I_i^I, \]

is perfectly conceivable, but it changes the observable and hence the inverse problem. It is a new type-level power index, not merely the paper’s inverse problem with a continuous population.

The limit is also not canonical. With per-capita weights \(a_t\), the proposed coalition weight is of order \(N\), so a nondegenerate quota must be scaled as \(Nq\), not held fixed. One must then choose how finite games approximate real weights and quotas, how ties are handled, and whether the continuum object is a limit of sums of individual Banzhaf or Shapley–Shubik indices, a type-level pivotality measure, or a nonatomic cooperative game. For Banzhaf power, an individual in an atomless population has zero pivotality; for Shapley–Shubik power, a uniform random ordering of a continuum is not supplied by the original definition. These are not minor formal inconveniences: they determine what problem is being studied.

The paper’s discussion of large voting bodies does make a high-multiplicity scenario plausible. That is the proponent’s strongest point, and it prevents an honest claim that no worthwhile related research could ever be designed. But the resulting project would be a new asymptotic theory of aggregate power and inverse design, with no named computational result in this paper to mirror. If the programme accepts such forward-looking extensions, this is a plausible research seed; under the stated anchor discipline, it is not a successful continuous mirror.
