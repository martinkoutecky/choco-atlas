The continuous mirror question

Given rational per capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^d\) and supported types \(t\) with rational additive weights \(w_{t,e}\), compute each type’s largest threshold \(s_t\) such that, for every \(\alpha\in[0,1)\) and every mass distribution \(r\) of total mass \(\alpha\) over integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^d\) with \(w_t\cdot z<s_t\) and aggregate use \(\sum_z r_z z\le q\), the residual inventory \(q-\sum_z r_z z\) can be assigned to mass \(1-\alpha\) as a distribution \(y\) over bundles satisfying \(w_t\cdot z\ge s_t\), with \(\sum_z y_z=1-\alpha\) and \(\sum_z y_z z=q-\sum_z r_z z\). Return \(s_t\) and a full mass partition at \(\alpha=0\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of each valuation type, with indivisible bundles drawn from \(d\) replicated good categories and per capita inventory \(q\). The society’s type distribution \(\mu\) supplies the supported types, while each share remains a per-type benchmark, as in the paper.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 21’s additive RMMS value and feasible partition tasks; it leaves Proposition 20’s completion algorithm and the paper’s fairness existence and share comparison results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the weak NP-hardness in Proposition 21 survive when bundle allocations can mix across population mass?
  • Can the residual threshold and an \(\alpha=0\) mass partition be computed in polynomial or pseudo-polynomial time?
  • How do these continuous values relate to finite RMMS instances under population replication?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s computation of the residual maximin share itself. I would make it a continuous RMMS benchmark for a large population facing a standardized catalogue of indivisible goods.

Let the society have unit mass, with type distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete valuation types. A type \(t\) specifies additive values \(w_{t,e}\) for \(d\) good categories. Category \(e\) has \(q_e\) copies per capita in the inventory. An individual bundle is still integral: a vector \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^d\), worth \(v_t(z)=\sum_e w_{t,e}z_e\). The population can be divided across bundle types by mass, so its average bundle may be fractional even though every individual’s bundle is integral.

For each valuation type \(t\), define \(s_t\) to be the largest threshold with the paper’s residual self-feasibility property. Explicitly, for every \(\alpha<1\) and every mass distribution \(r\) of total mass \(\alpha\) over bundles worth less than \(s_t\), if those bundles use at most the available inventory \(q\), the remaining inventory must be partitionable among mass \(1-\alpha\) into bundles each worth at least \(s_t\). The partition is a distribution \(y\) over integral bundles with total mass \(1-\alpha\), using exactly the residual inventory. The objective is to maximize \(s_t\). At \(\alpha=0\), \(y\) is a full mass partition witnessing the threshold.

This is a per-type share benchmark, as RMMS is in the paper: each type evaluates the same inventory using its own valuation. In a downstream allocation, a variable \(x_{t,z}\) would assign mass of type \(t\) to integral bundles \(z\); its type-level average value may be fractional.

The regime is large-scale distribution of standardized goods: \(N\) households, \(Nq_e\) copies of each category, and roughly \(N\mu_t\) households of type \(t\). The catalogue and number of valuation types can stay small as \(N\) grows. This is a natural high-multiplicity setting: households sharing a type have the same preferences, and item supplies scale per capita. In finite approximants, copies within a category are distinct indivisible goods with identical values.

My anchor is Proposition 21, proved in this paper. It says that computing the RMMS value for additive valuations is weakly NP-hard, and that finding feasible \(n\)-partitions is likewise weakly NP-hard. The proof reduces from the weakly NP-hard Partition problem using \(n=2\), where RMMS equals MMS; it extends the result to larger \(n\) by adding \(n-2\) exceptionally high-value items. The continuous problem above mirrors both tasks: compute \(s_t\), and return a full mass partition at that threshold.

I expect this to be a Class B candidate: Proposition 21’s reduction is driven by item values and bundle choices, not by a large number of agents. That makes hardness transfer plausible. The paper’s proposition does not prove transfer, though. In the continuous problem, mass can be split among different integral bundles, potentially relaxing the very partition constraint that gives the discrete reduction its force. A prover should settle whether the reduction survives that relaxation; if it does not, an exact LP or a pricing algorithm may uncover a tractable case.

The authors should recognize this as their RMMS question: it keeps additive valuations, integral individual bundles, and the defining universal condition over removed low-value bundles. It changes the finite number of agents and goods into population mass and per-capita stock. The named continuous problem is therefore C-RMMS Value-and-Witness: given \(\mu\), \(q\), and the valuation vectors, compute every \(s_t\) and give a full mass partition witnessing each value. The further questions are whether this is polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and the input bit length; whether pseudo-polynomial algorithms exist; and how its answers relate to finite RMMS under \(N\)-fold replication.

The weak point is that the paper studies a finite catalogue of distinct goods, while this mirror needs repeated goods categories and inventory that scales with population. That is a plausible large-scale allocation regime, and the per-capita supply is part of the mirror because it keeps the benchmark meaningful as the population grows. The second weakness is mathematical: mass mixing may soften the Partition-based hardness. That is exactly the substantive question this mirror puts on the table.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to Proposition 21 as an anchor is the gap between its reduction and the proposed population regime. Its weak NP-hardness proof starts from \(n=2\) and a collection of one-off goods: the Partition instance is encoded in which distinct goods go into each bundle. To carry that instance unchanged into a population of \(N\) agents, each category has per-capita supply \(1/N\), so the stock per person vanishes as \(N\) grows. To keep a nonzero per-capita supply \(q_e\), the natural construction instead gives the population \(Nq_e\) copies of each category. That changes the partition structure: agents can receive multiple copies, and a mass allocation can mix across integral bundle types. The paper’s reduction therefore supplies no direct hardness transfer to C-RMMS. Nor does a full mass partition at \(\alpha=0\) witness the continuous RMMS condition by itself; the latter must hold for every permitted removal distribution.

That weakens the claim that C-RMMS is *the same computational problem* as Proposition 21. But it does not show that the better formulation is unworthy. Repeated standardized goods and large populations are a plausible fair-division regime, and indivisible bundles for individuals can coexist coherently with fractional mass across bundle types. The fact that each \(s_t\) depends on the type’s valuation and the stock \(q\), rather than on \(\mu\), also narrows the mirror: it makes this a per-type benchmark problem, not a computation driven by the society’s mix of types. Still, a per-type share is what the paper defines, and that limitation alone does not disqualify it.

I cannot make a strong negative case that survives the programme’s test. The paper has a named computational result, and the proposed continuous RMMS question is recognizable and nondegenerate. The honest objection is that Proposition 21’s particular reduction does not validate the proposed mirror; establishing that connection would require new work. Since a new, well-posed continuous problem can be worthwhile even when hardness fails to transfer, that objection does not defeat the anchor. The proponent’s case is therefore stronger.
