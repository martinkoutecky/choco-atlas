The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) divisible items, positive per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), protected labels \(z_t\in\{0,1\}\), and positive rational linear valuations \(v_t\), with both groups having positive mass and group \(1\) having lower average utility at equal budgets, find a multiplier \(B\geq 1\) and a competitive equilibrium allocation \(a_t\) for budgets \(b_0=1\), \(b_1=B\), whose group average utilities differ by at most \(\epsilon\). For each \(B\), the allocation is the Eisenberg–Gale solution maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t b_{z_t}\log(v_t\cdot a_t)\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{tj}\leq q_j\) for every item \(j\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support population with types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), group labels \(z_t\), and valuations \(v_t\); decision variables are the group-1 budget multiplier \(B\) and per-member bundles \(a_t\). The objective is the mass-weighted, budget-weighted Nash welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t b_{z_t}\log(v_t\cdot a_t)\), with per-capita item constraints and equal average utility across groups to tolerance \(\epsilon\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers CEEqI's equalizing-budget computation, using Theorem 3 for existence and Corollary 1 for approximate computation. It leaves EqEEI's allocation-distribution constraint, the SP-L results, the mechanism-property theorems, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the total bit complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), input length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), including the equilibrium solves?
  • How does this mass formulation correspond to finite markets when supplies scale with population size?
  • How does the complexity change as the support size \(\tau\) grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: mirror the paper’s utility-fair mechanism, CEEqI. The lead anchor is Corollary 1, proved here: for every \(\epsilon>0\), one can compute a group budget \(\bar B_\epsilon\) such that the corresponding Eisenberg–Gale equilibrium has average utilities in the two protected groups within \(\epsilon\), using \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) CEEI solves. The preceding Theorem 3 proves that an exactly equalizing budget exists; Corollary 1 gives the approximate computation. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result: the paper states no named hardness theorem to pair with a continuum version.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Finite-Support CEEqI. An instance has \(m\) divisible items, positive per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a finite list of types \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), protected-group label \(z_t\in\{0,1\}\), and strictly positive linear valuation vector \(v_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\); the masses are nonnegative binary rationals summing to one, and both groups have positive mass. A type is complete: its members share the same protected label and valuation vector. Assume that at equal budgets group \(1\) has lower average utility; otherwise exchange the group labels.

Choose a budget multiplier \(B\geq 1\) for group \(1\), leaving group \(0\)’s budget at \(1\). For each \(B\), an equilibrium allocation \(a_t\in\mathbb R_+^m\) maximizes
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t b_{z_t}\log(v_t\cdot a_t) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t a_{tj}\leq q_j\quad(j=1,\ldots,m), \]
where \(b_0=1\) and \(b_1=B\). The question is to return such a budget and equilibrium allocation (and supporting prices) for which
\[ \left| \frac{\sum_{t:z_t=0}\mu_t(v_t\cdot a_t)}{\sum_{t:z_t=0}\mu_t} - \frac{\sum_{t:z_t=1}\mu_t(v_t\cdot a_t)}{\sum_{t:z_t=1}\mu_t} \right|\leq\epsilon. \]
Thus the action is a per-member bundle for each type, the item constraint uses total mass, and the objective is the paper’s budget-weighted Nash welfare for fixed \(B\). The requested solution is an equal-utility competitive equilibrium to the stated tolerance.

A plausible regime is a large recommendation or advertising market allocating divisible exposure across a fixed catalog. Millions of users may fall into a modest number of standardized preference-profile cohorts, with each type recording both the profile and protected-group label. The number of users can then be much larger than \(\tau\), and supplies can scale with population size as in the paper’s large-market model. Equal average utility across protected groups is the paper’s own fairness question; this mirror keeps its linear valuations, CEEI market, budget adjustment, and group utility criterion. It compresses repeated, identical agents into masses rather than changing the question. The authors should recognize that as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime.

I expect Class A for this finite-support version. Its equilibrium program has \(\tau m\) allocation variables, and the remaining choice is a one-dimensional budget search; the paper’s corollary gives a logarithmic number of CEEI solves. A full complexity result should still bound the bit complexity of each solve in \(m,\tau\), the rational input length, and the requested precision. The paper counts equilibrium solves, not their total running time, so its corollary alone does not establish that bound. Further questions include exact equalization, the dependence on support size when \(\tau\) grows, and how this mass formulation maps back to finite markets. I would not claim that Theorem 1’s SP-L rate transfers: that result uses a different stochastic model. Nor would I extend this case to EqEEI’s allocation-distribution constraint.

The weakest point is that the paper’s named computational result concerns a finite-agent mechanism and counts CEEI solves; it does not analyze a type-compressed input or prove polynomial bit complexity for it. The proposed scenario also needs genuinely repeated valuation profiles, which will fit some cohort-based markets better than individualized latent-vector data. Still, it is a credible mirror of the paper’s central CEEqI question, and the paper’s own budget search makes the computational version concrete.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper has already done the continuous-market work it would motivate. It studies valuations drawn from a distribution \(F\) with supplies growing as \(c_j n\), and explicitly describes its finite-market results as sharpening continuous-market limit results with convergence rates. For CEEqI, Theorem 3 establishes an equalizing budget and Corollary 1 gives the budget search using \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) CEEI solves.

The proposed finite-support model is an exact rewriting of that same equilibrium problem. If \(n_t\) individuals share type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and give them all bundle \(a_t\). Then
\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i b_{z_i}\log(v_i\cdot x_i) =\sum_t \mu_t b_{z_t}\log(v_t\cdot a_t), \]
and dividing the supply constraints by \(n\) gives \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{tj}\le c_j\). The group utility averages and the budget that equalizes them are unchanged. Collecting identical agents into masses therefore adds no new algorithmic question to the paper’s CEEqI result.

A fuller bit-complexity analysis could still be useful: the corollary counts equilibrium solves but does not bound their total running time. A model with an arbitrary continuous valuation distribution would also need a precise finite input or oracle model. Those are possible follow-up questions, though the first sharpens the complexity accounting for the same convex program, and the second depends on a newly specified representation.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight: cohort-based allocation is a sensible population regime. But for the proponent’s anchor, the paper’s own continuous-market framework and budget search leave little room for a worthwhile new mirror.
