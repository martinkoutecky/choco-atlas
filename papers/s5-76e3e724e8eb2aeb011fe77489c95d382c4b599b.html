The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s rule.

The closest named result is the unnumbered Appendix THEOREM, proved in this paper:

If \(W\) is an \(n\times n\) stochastic matrix and \(W^k\) converges to \(W^\infty\), then
\[ > \lim_{\alpha\to 1^-}(1-\alpha)(I-\alpha W)^{-1}=W^\infty . > \]

This is a structural convergence theorem, not a result asserting membership in P, NP-hardness, FPT-ness, or any other computational classification. Equations (4)–(10) are model equations and derivations, not named computational results. The complexity claims or algorithms mentioned in the references are not results of this paper. Thus there is no formally eligible anchor. I would not present the paper as a compliant ChoCo case without stating that limitation.

Nevertheless, its population model admits a credible mirror. The best candidate is Continuous Abel-Consensus Evaluation, corresponding to the Appendix THEOREM.

Take a large organization with many repeated social roles: for example, a school district with tens of thousands of students, or a workplace with many employees in the same department, grade, or position. A type \(t\) includes the complete information relevant to the model: baseline opinion, exogenous covariates, and the type’s influence affinities toward other types. There are \(\tau\) such types, with \(n\gg\tau\) individuals. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\).

Let \(h_{tu}\ge 0\) be the influence affinity from type \(t\) to type \(u\), and define the population-dependent stochastic kernel

\[ P^\mu_{tu} = \frac{h_{tu}\mu_u} {\sum_v h_{tv}\mu_v}, \]

assuming every denominator is positive. This says that a member of type \(t\) distributes influence across the available mass of other types according to the same affinity profile. Let \(b_t\) be the type’s initial or exogenously induced opinion. The type-level dynamics are

\[ y^{(r+1)}=\alpha P^\mu y^{(r)}+\beta b . \]

The continuous problem is:

Given rational \(T,\mu,h,b\), and a rational threshold \(q\), promised that \((P^\mu)^k\) converges, compute the limiting opinion vector
\[ > y^\infty=(P^\mu)^\infty b > \]
and decide whether the population-average settled opinion
\[ > \sum_t\mu_t y^\infty_t > \]
is at least \(q\).

Equivalently, in the Abel form used by the paper, compute

\[ \lim_{\alpha\to1^-} (1-\alpha)(I-\alpha P^\mu)^{-1}b . \]

This is a genuine population continuization: individuals are replaced by a distribution over repeated types, while opinions remain ordinary scalar opinions, time remains discrete, and the influence process remains the object being studied. Rational masses give an exact high-multiplicity interpretation by replacing each type with \(N\mu_t\) identical individuals for a common denominator \(N\).

The authors would probably recognize this as a continuous analogue of their model, especially their normalized \(W\), peer-effects, and consensus cases. It is not a collision with the paper itself: their \(W\) is an individual-level \(n\times n\) matrix, and they never formulate a distribution over population types or study the complexity of computing from such a distribution. Mean-field and opinion-dynamics work is relevant analytic background, but not a prior computational treatment of this question.

The expected classification is Class A. Once the population has been compressed into \(\tau\) types, the equilibrium or Abel limit can be obtained by rational linear algebra on a \(\tau\times\tau\) matrix, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The interesting computational programme would then be to ask whether this tractability survives richer tasks: minimum-cost type-level interventions that move the average opinion past \(q\), robustness against a bounded mass of opinion changes, or optimization of the influence kernel subject to network constraints.

The weakest point is decisive: this is a new computational problem built from the paper’s equations, not a continuization of a computational theorem proved by the paper. Moreover, if one takes the paper’s arbitrary individual-level \(W\) literally, preserving every influence neighborhood as part of a type may require \(\tau\) nearly as large as \(n\), destroying the high-multiplicity gain. The positive case therefore depends on a substantive but plausible block-homogeneous regime in which many agents genuinely share influence roles. That makes the mirror defensible as a research question, but not enough to overcome the absence of a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that Friedkin and Johnsen provide no qualifying computational result to mirror. The Appendix THEOREM is an Abelian limit theorem:

\[ \lim_{\alpha\to 1^-}(1-\alpha)(I-\alpha W)^{-1}=W^\infty \]

under the hypothesis \(W^k\to W^\infty\). It is a matrix-analysis identity, not an assertion about an algorithm, complexity class, approximation scheme, or computational problem. Equations (4)–(10) likewise derive models; they do not classify how any input is computed. The programme’s rule makes this fatal: a new computational task based on an analytic theorem is not a continuization of a computational result proved by the paper.

The proposed Continuous Abel-Consensus Evaluation therefore cannot repair the gap. It adds a finite type space, a homogeneous affinity kernel \(h\), the population-dependent normalization

\[ P^\mu_{tu} = \frac{h_{tu}\mu_u}{\sum_v h_{tv}\mu_v}, \]

and a new aggregate-threshold objective. Those may define a sensible high-multiplicity research problem, but none is present in the theorem. Asking for intervention, robustness, or influence-design optimization would move still further away: those could be worthwhile ChoCo problems, but they would be new problems inspired by the paper, not mirrors of its named result.

There is also a substantive modelling weakness. The paper permits an arbitrary individual-level \(W\), whose rows can encode completely idiosyncratic social positions. If influence structure is part of an agent’s complete type, then a generic instance has \(\tau\) essentially equal to \(n\), so the high-multiplicity compression disappears. The proponent’s construction works only after imposing block-homogeneous or mean-field structure. That is a plausible restriction—so it is not a proof that no continuous model exists—but it is an additional model whose quotient matrix is simply a finite \(\tau\times\tau\) social-influence system.

Thus the strongest negative verdict is: this paper has no worthwhile *ChoCo mirror*, because its contribution is analytic rather than computational and the proposed computation is newly attached from outside. I would not claim that no respectable continuous-population study could ever be built from its ideas; the block-homogeneous model is genuinely defensible. But if “mirror” means what the programme says it means—an anchored computational question from the paper—this candidate fails decisively.
