The continuous mirror question

Given finite complete assignment types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), explicit finite sets \(B_t\) of integral bundles with rankings \(\succ_t\), and rational linear upper bounds on per-capita resource use, choose a feasible mass allocation \(z\) with \(z_{t,b}\geq 0\) and \(\sum_{b\in B_t}z_{t,b}=\mu_t\). Uniform within-type randomization gives type \(t\) the bundle lottery \(p_t(b)=z_{t,b}/\mu_t\). Require that no feasible lottery over mass allocations gives every positive-mass type a first-order-stochastically weakly preferred bundle lottery and at least one type a strictly preferred one.

The model it lives in

A type records the complete preferences, eligibility, constraints, and equality class relevant to assignment; \(\mu_t\) is its population mass. Each realization assigns mass to integral bundles subject to per-capita upper bounds, and uniform within-type randomization gives all members of a type the same lottery. Minimize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in B_t} r_t(b)z_{t,b}\), where \(r_t(b)\) is the bundle’s rank for type \(t\); an optimal feasible mass allocation is ordinally efficient.

The objection that survived

With explicit bundle lists and linear resource constraints, minimizing total rank already yields an ETE–OE assignment by a routine LP, so the mirror may add little complexity beyond that formulation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the general-upper-bounds ETE–OE construction in Theorem 2; it leaves the paper’s other efficiency results, strategy-proofness analysis, and broader discussion of equality outside the question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which compact descriptions of bundle rankings and feasibility preserve this population mirror while making its computation more than an explicit finite-variable LP?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is an ETE–OE construction for a high-multiplicity assignment market, but there is a qualification about the required anchors: the paper has no numbered result that directly classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its computational-efficiency claims appear in the surrounding prose. The closest numbered anchor is Theorem 2, which the author proves here.

Theorem 2 says that if \(y\) is produced by serial dictatorship using a priority list in which each group of equals appears consecutively, and feasibility satisfies the general upper-bounds constraint, then the ETE reassignment \(\epsilon(\sigma_y)\) is ordinally efficient. The paper’s proposed method is to choose such a priority list, run serial dictatorship, and apply ETE reassignment; the paper describes this construction as computationally efficient. So Theorem 2 is a structural algorithm anchor, though not itself a numbered complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large course-allocation market: thousands of students, but far fewer complete preference and eligibility profiles. A type \(t\) would specify a student’s ranking over integral course bundles, eligibility and resource constraints, and policy-relevant equality class. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort with that full profile. This is credible only where submitted preferences and constraints actually repeat; with highly individualized rankings, the support could approach the population size.

Here is a precise continuous question, Mass Serial Dictatorship for ETE–OE. The input is a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); an explicit finite set \(B_t\) of integral bundles and a ranking \(\succ_t\) for each type; and rational, downward-closed linear upper-bound constraints on the per-capita use of resources. An assignment in one realization is a mass vector \(z\), where \(z_{t,b}\geq0\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(b\), \(\sum_{b\in B_t}z_{t,b}=\mu_t\), and all resource bounds hold. Thus fractional mass is allowed, but individual bundles remain indivisible.

The output is a finite lottery over feasible mass vectors, together with a within-type random rotation: in each realization, divide each type’s mass among its bundles according to \(z\), then rotate that division uniformly through the type’s cohort. Every individual of type \(t\) consequently receives the same bundle lottery \(p_t\), with \(p_t(b)=\mathbb{E}[z_{t,b}]/\mu_t\). Require that no other feasible lottery gives every positive-mass type a first-order-stochastically-better bundle lottery and gives at least one type a strictly better one. That is the continuous ETE–OE problem. It asks for a finite, verifiable representation of an assignment, not merely for a favorable fractional outcome.

The expected answer is Class A for this explicit linear-constraint version: process types as consecutive blocks, allocate each type’s mass in preference order as far as the remaining upper bounds allow, then apply the within-type rotation. The mass-allocation steps can be expressed as linear programs. The paper’s theorem makes the proposed extension plausible, but a prover would still need to establish ordinal efficiency against all feasible lotteries in this continuum model; Theorem 2 does not establish that result.

The main weakness is that the paper’s serial-dictatorship argument is stated for named individuals, and the continuous extension needs its own proof. The paper’s complete preference and constraint types may also proliferate until \(\tau\) is no smaller than the population. And since this paper presents no named hardness result, this mirror would test whether its efficient construction extends to a mass model; it would not show that continuization dissolves discrete hardness. A natural follow-up is whether rank-minimizing ETE assignments admit a comparable type-mass LP under general upper bounds.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about research value, not whether a continuous version can be defined. Theorem 2 is the closest anchor, but it proves an efficiency property; the polynomial-time construction appears in the surrounding discussion rather than as a complexity result. That weakens the computational hook, though it does not erase it: the paper does give a constructive method, and Theorem 2 certifies its output.

For the proponent’s explicit mass model, however, the central question is already a finite LP. Let \(z_{t,b}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(b\), with \(\sum_b z_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and the stated resource constraints. Minimize \(\sum_{t,b} r_t(b)z_{t,b}\), where \(r_t(b)\) is the bundle’s rank for type \(t\). The resulting type-level lotteries satisfy ETE by construction; a within-type rotation can implement them. If another feasible allocation first-order stochastically improves every positive-mass type and strictly improves one, its expected rank sum is lower, contradicting optimality. So this LP gives an ETE, rank-minimizing assignment—and hence an ETE–OE assignment—without needing the continuous analogue of the serial-dictatorship proof.

That also answers the stronger rank-minimizing version of the mirror. With explicit bundle lists, ranks, and linear resource constraints, both versions are routine LP formulations. To make the computational question harder, one would need a compact description of bundles, preferences, or feasibility that creates a nontrivial pricing problem. The paper’s stated computational assumptions do not supply that question; its complexity could come from the added representation choices rather than from continuizing the population.

This is a real reason to doubt that the mirror warrants a dedicated complexity study. But it does not defeat the anchor on admissibility: repeated course-allocation or school-choice profiles are plausible, and the resulting tractable mass problem is a legitimate high-multiplicity question. Under ChoCo’s standard, an easy Class A answer can still matter. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile scenario survives; the negative case is weak precisely at that point.
