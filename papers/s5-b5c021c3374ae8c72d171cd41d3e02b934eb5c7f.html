What the mirror covers

No result of the paper has a computational mirror: Theorems 1 and 2 are axiomatic characterizations, while the remaining named results concern topology and homology.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive point is that large electorates often do have a natural high-multiplicity regime: take strict rankings as types, let \(\mu_t\) be the share holding ranking \(t\), and consider elections with many voters but relatively small support \(\tau\). A mass transfer \(x_{t,t'}\) could represent voters changing reports. That makes the population description plausible for computational voting questions.

But this paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Theorem 1 states that, for \(n\ge3\), a social choice function is surjective and strategy-proof if and only if it is dictatorial. Theorem 2 states that a social choice function is monotonic and unanimous if and only if it is dictatorial. The paper proves the latter and derives the former; neither is a complexity result. The remaining named results concern topology and homology. So there is no qualifying anchor, and consequently no continuous prover question per anchor that I can honestly give.

There is also a substantive obstacle to turning the natural electorate regime into a mirror of these theorems. The theorems concern deviations by a particular agent, and dictatorship depends on that agent’s identity. A distribution over ranking types records population shares, not which person holds each ranking. In an atomless population, one person’s report change has zero mass and leaves any rule depending only on \(\mu\) unchanged. Modeling a positive mass changing reports would instead ask about coordinated group manipulation; retaining named individuals would require carrying their identities into the types, potentially making \(\tau\) as large as the population.

Thus the paper suggests a plausible high-multiplicity setting for other computational voting problems, but it does not establish a good continuous mirror of its own results. Its topological treatment of social choice functions is a mathematical study of an impossibility theorem, not computational complexity analysis. The weakest point in the positive case is that the central property—individual strategy-proofness—loses its force when the society is represented only by atomless type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 (Gibbard–Satterthwaite) and Theorem 2 (Muller–Satterthwaite) are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. The other named results concern topology and homology. None gives a complexity bound or an algorithmic problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart. That is the decisive gap for ChoCo: the paper’s main contribution is a new proof of an impossibility theorem, not a computational question about societies.

The most tempting replacement is to take a large electorate, represent ranking types by their population shares, and ask how much mass must change reports to alter the outcome. That is a coherent continuous voting problem, but it asks about coalition manipulation or bribery. The paper’s theorems characterize immunity to a *single agent’s* deviation; they do not study a rule family, an optimization objective, or the complexity of computing the least mass to move. Making that a computational project would add the central question rather than mirror one the paper answers.

The identity issue remains even under the best population model. For a rule \(f:\Delta(T)\to A\) that depends only on the distribution of rankings, an individual in an atomless population has measure zero, so changing their report leaves the input to \(f\) unchanged. Unilateral strategy-proofness then becomes vacuous. Let a positive mass deviate and the question becomes coalition strategy-proofness. Preserve the theorem’s named dictator instead, and the rule must distinguish individuals who have the same ranking; putting their identities into the types removes the high-multiplicity compression, while discarding identity makes dictatorship impossible to express from the aggregate alone.

One could ask a new axiomatic question about mass-based monotonicity or coalition manipulation in a continuous society. But the former is outside the programme’s computational scope, and the latter needs a new computational problem not supplied by this paper. So the negative case is strongest against a mirror of this paper: its electorate setting is plausible, but none of its results provides a computational anchor, and the natural attempt to create one changes the strategic question.
