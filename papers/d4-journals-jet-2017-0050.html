The case FOR (proponent)

I find no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Its Propositions 1–9 characterize ex ante or Bayesian stable matching outcomes, establish existence, or derive efficiency properties. Proposition 10 proves an incentive-compatibility claim for a mechanism. These are substantive results, but none states a computational problem’s complexity or gives an algorithmic result with a complexity bound. The deferred-acceptance algorithm appears as background, citing Gale and Shapley; it is not a computational result proved here.

The closest structural foothold is Proposition 6, proved in this paper. With increasing utilities, anonymous preferences, and all matches individually rational, it says: (i) a matching appears in the set of Bayesian stable outcomes for some worker-type vector exactly when it is maximal and matches the highest-type firms; and (ii) for each such matching there are unique thresholds, ordered with firm types, such that Bayesian stability is characterized by the matched workers’ types meeting those thresholds, with unmatched workers’ types at least the minimum. This is a characterization of stable outcomes, not an algorithm or a complexity claim.

A plausible *new* mirror would concern a large internship market: many applicants and positions, grouped into a small number of complete types with identical preferences and utility schedules. The decision variable would be a mass assignment from worker types to firm types; the task would be to find a feasible assignment with no Bayesian blocking pair under the paper’s information and no-return assumptions. That is a credible continuous-population question, but the paper does not establish a named computational result to anchor it, and its recursive Bayesian blocking analysis would need a careful aggregate formulation.

So the positive case is that the paper’s matching model has a plausible high-multiplicity regime and a structural characterization worth investigating. Its weakest point is decisive under your anchor rule: the paper reports no named computational result, so there is no honest result-anchored continuous mirror to present.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is categorical: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–9 characterize stability and efficiency; Proposition 10 establishes incentive compatibility. The deferred-acceptance algorithm is background, not an algorithmic result proved here. In particular, Proposition 6 characterizes which matchings can be Bayesian stable and gives type thresholds under anonymous preferences. It is substantial, but it says nothing about computational complexity.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror would take a large internship market with many workers and firms grouped into complete types, represent assignments by a mass coupling \(\pi\), and ask for a Bayesian-stable assignment. That is a coherent possible research question. But it requires choosing what to compute: one stable assignment, a stable set, or a test for membership. Proposition 6 provides no selection objective or computational target to carry over. Adding one would create a new algorithmic problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its result.

So the case against is strong under the programme’s result-anchoring criterion, but weak as a universal claim about the subject. High-multiplicity matching may be sensible, and the paper’s internship setting could support it. The material here does not justify saying that no worthwhile continuous computational matching project exists; it supports the narrower conclusion that this paper supplies no computational result for such a mirror to extend.
