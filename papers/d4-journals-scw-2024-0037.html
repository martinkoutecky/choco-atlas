The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying anchor-based case: the paper has no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Its closest candidates are Theorem 1, proved in the paper, which characterizes the Talmud rule by axioms, and Theorem 2, also proved here, which characterizes Young’s rules for fixed populations of more than two claimants. Both are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results.

There is a plausible follow-on population model. In a large claims pool with many claimants but few standardized claim levels, a type could be a claimant’s claim \(c_t\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of claimants of that type. Given per-capita endowment \(e\), the allocation is \(a_t\), with \(0\le a_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=e\). One could ask for the awards prescribed by the Talmud or a Young rule. That is a concrete high-multiplicity allocation problem, but the paper supplies no complexity anchor for it, and its solution is defined by a rule rather than an optimization objective.

The weak point is decisive: the paper studies axioms for dividing an already divisible resource among a finite population. A mass version could be a sensible computational extension, but claiming it mirrors a computational result in this paper would overstate the evidence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent concedes the decisive point: this paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 characterizes the Talmud rule through axioms; Theorem 2 characterizes Young’s rules through axioms. Neither asks for an algorithm, an optimization, or a complexity classification. So neither supplies an anchor for a continuous computational mirror.

The proposed claims distribution is nevertheless coherent. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of claimants with claim \(c_t\), \(e\) the endowment per claimant, and \(a_t\) the award per claimant of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t a_t=e\). But computing the Talmud awards in this model reduces to finding a scalar threshold in a piecewise-linear balance equation. With finite support, sorting the claim breakpoints and solving that equation is straightforward. For Young’s rules, the paper allows an arbitrary continuous function \(f\), so a computational version first needs an encoding of \(f\). Once one is supplied, finding the awards amounts to solving a monotone scalar balance equation; any difficulty may lie in the representation of \(f\), rather than in the continuous population.

A more ambitious task—such as designing a rule subject to axioms and an optimization objective—could be worthwhile, but it would add a computational problem the paper does not pose. The population model passes the sense-making test; the paper itself does not provide a computational result to mirror. That is a strong negative case against a mirror of this paper, though it cannot establish that no future computational project on claims allocation would be worthwhile.
