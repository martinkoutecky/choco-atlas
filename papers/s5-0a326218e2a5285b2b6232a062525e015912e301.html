The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational anchor under your rule. Its numbered material consists of definitions; Table 27 summarizes which voting rules exhibit which paradoxes, without making complexity claims. The closest lead is the introduction’s statement that computing a Dodgson winner is NP-complete, attributed to Bartholdi et al. [7] (1989). That claim is unnumbered and cited from elsewhere, so it cannot serve as a required numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition. There are therefore zero problems to provide *per eligible anchor*.

The strongest conditional positive lead is a continuous Dodgson winner problem. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking, and let \(\mu_t\) be its fraction of the electorate. For candidate \(c\), define \(D_\mu(c)\) as the infimum of the mass-weighted Kendall distance \(\sum_{t,t'} d_K(t,t')x_{t,t'}\), over transfers \(x_{t,t'}\ge 0\) that produce a society where \(c\) defeats every opponent by a strict majority. The problem is to return a candidate minimizing \(D_\mu(c)\), with a specified tie rule. Its instance is the candidate set and the finite support of rankings with rational masses; transfers may end at any complete ranking. A plausible regime is a national election with millions of voters but relatively few distinct ballots. The paper itself describes elections at that scale and groups identical rankings, sometimes as percentages. Its definition of Dodgson’s rule—fewest pairwise swaps needed to make a candidate the Condorcet winner—gives authors a clear connection to this mass-transfer version.

I would leave this problem’s complexity open. The discrete NP-completeness claim does not establish hardness for the continuous version, since fractional transfers can change the optimum. The \(m!\) possible destination rankings also leave a substantial pricing question. Tractability and continuum-specific hardness are both live possibilities; discrete hardness would transfer only with an embedding argument.

The weakness is decisive for the requested case: this Dodgson formulation is a plausible follow-up to an aside, not a numbered result of this paper. The paper’s main contribution is a qualitative classification of paradox vulnerability, which is already phrased over preference profiles and belongs to the axiomatic side of social choice. That makes a continuous population description natural, but does not supply the required computational result to anchor a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is that it offers no computational result to continuize. Its main contribution is a taxonomy of paradoxes and a qualitative classification of which voting rules exhibit them. Its numbered statements are definitions; it gives no numbered complexity theorem, algorithmic problem, or approximation result. Replacing voter counts with proportions would describe the same profile properties on a simplex, but that is an axiomatic or geometric reformulation, not the computational-complexity programme ChoCo is meant to pursue.

The proposed Dodgson question is the closest lead. A natural version defines \(D_\mu(c)\) as the infimum of the mass-weighted Kendall cost of transfers that make \(c\) a strict Condorcet winner, then selects the candidate with minimum \(D_\mu(c)\). A large electorate with relatively few ballot types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting; grouping identical rankings is exactly the point of the model. But that question comes from the paper’s introductory citation about discrete Dodgson complexity, not from a result the paper establishes. It is a follow-up to an aside, not a mirror of the paper’s contribution. The shared programme also records continuous Dodgson as an earlier solved example. That changes what a prover would be asked to do; under the stated prior-art rule, it does not by itself make the question unworthy.

A stronger attempt might ask for the least mass perturbation that produces a given paradox, or characterize the regions of the profile simplex where a paradox occurs. Such questions could be worthwhile in their own right. But the paper defines no distance, perturbation budget, or computational task of that kind. Adding one supplies a new objective rather than continuizing a result in the paper. Its paradox classifications could also be restated over continuous profiles, but that remains a profile-geometry question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

So the case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror is strong: no eligible anchor survives, and the one computational lead is not a paper result. The universal claim that no scenario inspired by the paper could yield worthwhile research is harder to defend. A mass-based robustness project is plausible; I can only say that it would be new work motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
