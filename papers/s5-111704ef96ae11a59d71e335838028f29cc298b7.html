The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set of applicant types \(T\), project capacities \(c_p\), regional quota rates \(q_r\), type preferences, and weak project priorities over types, choose assignment masses \(y_{t,p}\ge0\), boundary rationing, and project cutoffs \(d\) such that \(\sum_p y_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{t,p}\le c_p\), and \(\sum_{r}\sum_{p\in r}\sum_t y_{t,p}\le q_r\) as appropriate, with the assignment cutoff-induced, fair, and minimal under cutoff decreases; maximize \(\sum_{t,p}y_{t,p}\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity \(\mathrm{REG}_\infty\) matching model in which types encode applicant preferences and all project-relevant scores, \(y\) assigns population mass, capacities and quotas scale per capita, cutoffs allow partial boundary allocation, and the objective is maximum matched mass subject to fairness and cutoff minimality.

The objection that survived

The proponent permits boundary mass to split but does not specify whether minimality is invariant across admissible rationing choices, so a canonical or quantified boundary convention remains necessary.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6's maximum-size cutoﬀ/weak-stable REG problem and leaves the other stability, algorithmic, and egalitarian-funding results untreated; Theorem 2 is not relied upon.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(|T|=\tau\), does Maximum-Mass Cutoﬀ-Stable \(\mathrm{REG}_\infty\) reduce to a polynomial-time flow or linear program?
  • When \(\tau\) grows, which preference or priority structures preserve the discrete hardness or inapproximability bound?
  • What boundary-rationing convention makes cutoff minimality canonical and independent of arbitrary allocations within a tied type?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think this paper admits a credible continuous mirror, with Theorem 6 as the strongest anchor.

The natural regime is a large centralized internship or hiring market: \(N\) applicants, \(N\gg \tau\), but only \(\tau\) distinct applicant profiles. A type records everything the model uses: the applicant’s preference list over projects, eligibility, and priority score at every project. Projects are recurring project classes, with capacities and supervisor budgets scaling per capita. Thus \(N\mu_t\) applicants have type \(t\), project \(p\) has capacity \(Nc_p\), and supervisor \(s\) has budget \(Nq_s\). This is plausible for a national internship programme or large employer consortium using standardized applications and recurring project templates.

The continuous variables are not fractional internships for an individual. They are aggregate masses: \(y_{t,p}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to project \(p\), and \(x_{s,p}\) is supervisor funding per unit population. Feasibility is

\[ \sum_{p}y_{t,p}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t y_{t,p}\le c_p, \]

\[ \sum_{s\in S_p}x_{s,p}=\sum_t y_{t,p},\qquad \sum_{p\in P_s}x_{s,p}\le q_s. \]

For REG, supervisors are regions, projects in a region share a quota, and the regions partition the projects. Each infinitesimal applicant still receives one integral project; only the aggregate mass \(y\) is divisible.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: “Finding a maximum size cutoﬀ/weakly stable matching is NP-hard for REG, even if every hospital has one seat and each region has at most two hospitals.” The proof reduces from MAX-SMTI and preserves matching size; the paper also observes that the \(21/19\) inapproximability bound transfers.

The corresponding problem is Maximum-Mass Cutoff-Stable REG\(_\infty\). Its input is a rational type distribution \(\mu\), project and region capacity rates, applicant-type preferences, and project priorities over types. A solution is a feasible mass assignment \(y\), funding allocation \(x\), and project cutoff vector \(d\).

Cutoffs are interpreted in the natural high-multiplicity way. Project \(p\) admits all mass from types strictly above \(d_p\), rejects types strictly below \(d_p\), and may split the boundary type. Each type sends its mass to its most-preferred project admitting it, with any rejected boundary mass continuing down its preference list. The assignment must also be fair: if \(y_{t,q}>0\), \(p\succ_t q\), and \(y_{u,p}>0\), then \(u\) must have priority at least as high as \(t\) at \(p\). Finally, the cutoff vector must be minimal: lowering any positive cutoff by one priority level must make the induced mass assignment infeasible. The objective is

\[ \max \sum_{t,p}y_{t,p}. \]

This is recognizably the paper’s maximum-size cutoff-stable problem, not a welfare problem substituted for it. The main change is that a large number of interchangeable applicants can be split across projects. I would expect the fixed-\(\tau\), per-capita version to be Class A: the two-project regional gadgets in the paper rely on choosing which individual receives an integral seat, whereas mass can be divided between the alternatives. Theorem 1’s max-flow feasibility test and Theorems 3–4’s cutoff-decreasing algorithm provide an obvious starting point for a polynomial algorithm. The unresolved issue is whether maximizing over all minimal cutoff vectors can itself be reduced to one flow or LP, rather than requiring an exponential search over cutoff patterns.

If \(\tau\) is allowed to grow with the instance and the preference-incidence structure carries the hardness, a Class B result may remain possible. That is a useful boundary question rather than a defect: it separates hardness caused by individual seat choices from hardness carried by the project/type structure.

A second, weaker anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here: “Checking the existence of a strongly stable matching is NP-complete even for REG.” The construction reduces from Restricted MAX-SMTI and works even when every supervisor has capacity one and supervises at most two projects.

The matching analogue is Strong-Stability Existence REG\(_\infty\). It uses the same input and feasible mass assignments. A mass assignment is fair as above. For strong non-wastefulness, take any type \(t\) with positive mass currently assigned to \(q\), or unmatched, and any preferred project \(p\succ_t q\). Either \(p\) is at capacity, or there must be no sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\) for which moving \(\varepsilon\) mass of type \(t\) from \(q\) to \(p\) yields a feasible assignment, possibly after reallocating supervisor funding. The question is whether such a strongly stable \(y\) exists.

I would tentatively expect this fixed-type version also to be Class A, though less confidently. The paper’s basic nonexistence example disappears once the two applicants can be split across the two projects: the continuum can assign positive mass to both while exhausting the shared regional capacity. A flow or fractional stable-matching characterization may therefore replace the discrete choice gadget. A continuum-specific Class C result remains plausible if the “every small transfer is infeasible” condition creates a genuinely nonconvex minimality problem.

The authors should recognize these as their problems. The applicant preferences, project priorities, project capacities, regional or supervisor budget constraints, fairness, strong stability, cutoff stability, and maximum-cardinality objective are all retained. The paper already uses network flow for feasibility and cutoff descent for stability. The new object is specifically the applicant population: \(\mu\) and \(y\), not merely the already-divisible funding variables. Clearing rational denominators gives a finite clone interpretation, subject to the boundary tie-breaking convention.

The weakest point is that the paper’s definitions are stated for named applicants with strict project priorities and one-applicant blocking pairs. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, so literal blocking becomes vacuous. The mirror therefore needs type-level priority ties and positive-mass deviations. That is a genuine extension, especially for Theorem 2. If the referee insists on preserving strict identity-level priorities, the number of types may rise to \(N\), destroying the intended high-multiplicity regime. I would consequently treat Theorem 6 as the lead case and Theorem 2 as supporting evidence, not rely on both equally.

This covers the paper’s two principal hardness results. I am not claiming that its egalitarian funding theorem is itself a population-continuization result: that part already concerns divisible supervisor budgets, whereas the proposed mirror makes the applicant society continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors depend on applicants being identifiable atoms, whereas the proposed mirror makes them exchangeable mass. That creates a choice: preserve the paper’s stability notions and lose high multiplicity, or obtain a genuine mass model by changing the notions being anchored.

Theorem 6 is the better anchor, but its hardness construction is not a high-multiplicity construction. The reduction from MAX-SMTI uses each applicant’s individual preference list as part of the encoded instance, together with project-specific comparisons between named applicants. In the paper’s model, those comparisons are strict. If two applicants are genuinely one type, then their preferences and every project priority relevant to the problem must coincide. Any identity-based tie-breaking that distinguishes them is additional type information. Preserving the reduction therefore requires essentially one type per applicant, so \(\tau\) grows with the population and \(\mu\) is merely a normalized encoding of the original finite instance.

The proposed rescue—standardized applicant profiles and recurring project classes—does not preserve that model. It replaces strict applicant priorities by priorities over types, hence by ties within each type. Once a boundary type is partially assigned to a project, the paper’s fairness condition, which quantifies over every named applicant assigned there, no longer has its original meaning. One must choose a new rule: allow tied agents to be split, impose an internal priority order, or specify a rationing mechanism. The first produces anonymous fractional stability; the second reintroduces latent identities; the third makes cutoff stability depend on an extra allocation rule. These are all legitimate research problems, but they are not merely the paper’s cutoff-stability problem with counts replaced by masses.

The difficulty is particularly sharp for minimal cutoffs. In the finite model, a cutoff vector induces a unique matching because each applicant either reaches a threshold or does not. With mass, a cutoff generally does not determine the allocation of a boundary type. Whether lowering the cutoff is feasible depends on how that boundary mass was rationed and on which mass is moved. Thus minimality is no longer a property of the cutoff vector alone. The proponent’s \(\mathrm{REG}_\infty\) formulation has silently added a new type-level stability and rationing theory. Its eventual complexity may be interesting, but Theorem 6 no longer anchors it in the strong sense claimed.

Theorem 2 fares worse. Strong stability is defined through a blocking pair \((a,p)\) and the feasibility of replacing \(a\)’s current assignment. An individual has zero mass in the continuum. Replacing the deviation by moving some \(\varepsilon>0\) mass changes a pairwise deviation into a collective, type-level deviation; replacing it by a tangent-direction condition changes it again. If a type is assigned to several projects, there is not even one current project \(M(a)\) for that type. If splitting is forbidden, the positive masses are indivisible cohorts and the population has not been continuized. If splitting is allowed, the two-agent nonexistence gadget is no longer an instance of the paper’s strong-stability concept.

The same dichotomy defeats the most sympathetic application story. Scaling capacities and budgets per capita is acceptable in itself, but a large internship programme with repeated applicant profiles necessarily has type-level priority ties. Preserving strict project rankings requires adding scores, tie-break positions, or other latent identifiers to the type. If those identifiers are sufficiently fine to reproduce the paper’s blocking pairs, the number of types tracks the number of applicants. If they are omitted, the resulting problem is an anonymous fractional matching model with a newly defined stability notion.

This is therefore the best case against both anchors: Theorem 6’s hardness is tied to applicant-specific preference and priority structure, while Theorem 2’s existence notion is tied even more directly to named-agent deviations. A continuous population cannot retain both that structure and genuine exchangeable types.

The negative case is not airtight. If the programme explicitly accepts weak type priorities, fractional boundary rationing, and a newly defined mass version of cutoff stability, then the proponent has identified a plausible and potentially worthwhile problem—especially for Theorem 6. I cannot honestly claim that no such scenario exists. The defensible negative conclusion is narrower: the paper does not supply a clean continuous mirror of its two headline computational results without replacing the identity-sensitive matching model by a different anonymous fractional one.
