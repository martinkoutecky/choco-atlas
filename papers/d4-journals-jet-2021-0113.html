The continuous mirror question

Given finite worker and firm type sets with rational masses, explicit contract menus, strict rankings, and initial partnership cohorts with matched mass on both sides, find a feasible matching flow and final commitment masses such that the resulting state has no blocking positive-mass chain or cycle and no implementable measurable coalition can weakly improve for all its members while strictly improving a positive mass. Types record all relevant preferences, initial contracts, partner cohorts, and commitment status; each unit of mass receives at most one contract.

The model it lives in

A finite-type, atomless matching market with rational type masses and explicit contracts. The decision variables are contract flows \(x_{ij\alpha}\), single-agent masses, and final committed masses; the task is to construct a feasible witness, with no welfare-maximization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2, including Theorem 1's stable-matching stage as an ingredient; it does not cover the paper's other structural results or its unstudied incentive-compatibility questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a mass-based Hybrid Procedure find an exact witness in time polynomial in the number of types, contracts, and input bit length?
  • Can such a continuous witness be rounded to a discrete matching while preserving or approximating stability and strict-core membership?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that the matching \(\Omega\) generated by the authors’ Hybrid Procedure is a strict core matching, and that the matching state \((\Omega,C(\Omega))\) is strongly stable with \(C(\Omega)\subseteq C_0\). In other words, the procedure constructs a feasible matching that cannot be improved upon by an implementable coalition, has no blocking chain or cycle under the final commitments, and leaves no more agents committed than were committed initially. Theorem 1 supplies the stable matching used in the procedure’s first stage, but Theorem 2 is the stronger anchor.

The paper does not state a named complexity-class result such as membership in P. Its computational contribution is a constructive existence theorem: the authors give a finite Hybrid Procedure and prove that it produces the stated outcome. I would not describe Theorem 2 as a polynomial-time theorem. My case is that its constructive question has a credible continuous-population version.

Call that problem Senior Commitment Matching\(_\infty\). An instance has finite worker and firm type sets, rational masses, and a finite explicit contract menu. A type records everything the model uses: its side of the market, strict ranking of contracts and the single option, initial contract and partner class, and initial commitment status. Split types further by initial partner class and contract, so each initial partnership cohort has equal mass on its worker and firm sides. Entrants and initially single agents are allowed. Each worker and firm has unit capacity; masses describe how many agents of each type there are.

The output is a nonnegative mass \(x_{ij\alpha}\) assigned to each worker type \(i\), firm type \(j\), and mutually available contract \(\alpha\), together with mass assigned to single status and a final committed mass for each type. The matching masses must exhaust every type’s supply without exceeding either side’s capacity. The output must be feasible in the paper’s sense: each assigned contract is acceptable or relatively acceptable as required by that agent’s initial status. Final commitment mass must be drawn from initially committed mass.

The question is to construct such an output for which the resulting state is strongly stable and its matching is in the strict core. In the continuous definitions, strong stability rules out any positive mass of agents who can strictly improve through a chain or cycle that respects current commitments. Strict-core membership rules out any implementable measurable coalition that can rematch its members so everyone is weakly better off and positive mass is strictly better off. Implementability carries over the paper’s initial consent rule: a coalition that includes committed mass must include the corresponding mass of its initial partner. Requiring positive mass to improve is the atomless version of the paper’s “at least one agent” condition. The objective is to find a witness, not to maximize welfare or the number freed; Theorem 2 makes no such maximization claim.

A plausible regime is a large, standardized internal placement market—for example, a public service with many one-position roles, established employees, and entrants. Workers might share preference rankings by credential and job class; positions might share rankings by qualification class; contract menus, tenure rules, and initial assignment categories might be standardized. There could be hundreds of thousands of agents but only dozens or hundreds of complete types. Fractional \(x_{ij\alpha}\) then means that some mass of one cohort takes each available contract. It does not give any individual a fractional job. This retains the authors’ one-to-one matching, contracts, initial commitments, and stability concepts, so I think they would recognize it as their problem at high multiplicity.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A) when the type and contract lists are explicit. The paper’s procedure already has an aggregate shape: workers propose, firms retain preferred proposals subject to commitment, and the TTC stage removes cycles. With rational masses, an analogue could process cohorts and transfer bottleneck masses rather than individual agents; the natural target is a running time polynomial in the number of types, contract entries, and input bit length. That complexity claim is a question for the mirror, not a result proved by this paper. A useful follow-up is whether an aggregate Hybrid Procedure has a polynomial event bound, and whether its exact output can be rounded to a discrete matching while preserving or approximating the two stability properties.

This case covers Theorem 2’s constructive matching result, including Theorem 1’s stable-matching stage as an ingredient. It does not cover the paper’s unstudied incentive-compatibility questions, and it does not continuize outcomes, time, or axioms. The weakest point is the regime: many labor markets have highly individualized preferences and initial partnerships, which would make the number of complete types large. The proposed standardized placement setting is plausible, but the paper itself does not establish that it has small support. The case survives that limitation because high multiplicity is a property of this particular market regime, and the mirror preserves the paper’s core matching and commitment problem rather than simplifying it into a different one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 2 offers less computational substance to continuize than its headline suggests. It proves existence and gives a finite procedure, but it does not bound that procedure’s running time or optimize an objective. Theorem 1 is the stable-matching stage of that construction, not a separate complexity result. So the proposed mirror is a new mass version of a constructive existence result, rather than a high-multiplicity version of a computational problem the paper has analyzed.

There is also a representation problem at the heart of that construction. The paper’s contracts and commitment constraints are attached to particular worker–firm pairs. A type-level model must preserve the initial partner relation, since it determines which agents can form an implementable coalition and who can consent to dissolve a match. In a market with individualized contracts or preferences, retaining that information may make each initial pair a distinct type, so support grows with the population and the hoped-for compression disappears.

The proponent has a real answer to this objection: in a standardized placement market, agents could share preferences, contract terms, and partner classes, with many interchangeable initial pairs in each cohort. Then aggregate matching flows could preserve the consent structure and be realized as individual assignments. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, and it defeats any claim that partner identity makes a continuous mirror impossible. A mass version of the deferred-acceptance and trading-cycle stages also seems plausible. It might yield an efficient algorithm, even if the paper’s theorem says nothing about its running time.

What remains against the mirror is therefore a question of value, not coherence. In the symmetric regime, the likely contribution is to aggregate a finite matching procedure over cohorts; outside it, the complete types may be too numerous to help. That makes this a less compelling fit for a programme centered on complexity landscapes and continuous-optimization methods. But it does not establish that the mirror is unworthy: an algorithm polynomial in the number of types could matter when cohort sizes are encoded compactly.

I cannot honestly defeat this anchor across all scenarios. The negative case is weak at its central point: the paper’s initial-pair structure can be represented by cohort masses in a plausible standardized market, and the resulting computational question is recognizable as the authors’ matching problem. The best objection is that its likely payoff is narrower than the proponent claims, not that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
