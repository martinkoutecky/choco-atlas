The continuous mirror question

Given project atoms \(P\), a DNNF integrity constraint \(\Gamma\), a finite support \(\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^P\) of approval-ballot types with rational masses \(\mu_B\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a set of literals \(L\), determine whether some satisfying allocation \(A\subseteq P\) maximizing \(K_\mu(A)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_B\bigl(|A\cap B|+|(P\setminus A)\cap(P\setminus B)|\bigr)\) agrees with every literal in \(L\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite support of approval types \(B\subseteq P\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_B\). Allocations remain indivisible subsets \(A\subseteq P\); \(\Gamma\) constrains feasible allocations, and the objective is expected Kemeny agreement with the society.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Kemeny outcome queries under DNNF constraints with a rational distribution over approval ballots. It leaves the Slater and leximax variants, quota-feasibility hardness, and the paper’s axiomatic results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For the paper’s quota and dependency embeddings, characterize when a polynomial-size DNNF can be constructed from binary-encoded PB inputs, including the role of category overlap and pathwidth.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, fairly narrow mirror here. The paper’s citizens are described by approval ballots, so a continuous voter type can simply be a complete ballot \(B\subseteq P\). Let \(\mu_B\) be the fraction of the population with ballot \(B\), with rational masses summing to \(1\). A plausible regime is citywide participatory budgeting with hundreds of thousands of residents but far fewer distinct ballots, perhaps because residents choose from the same shortlist and many submit the same approval set. The projects remain indivisible: continuization applies to the society, not the funded outcomes. Project categories used for quotas are a separate part of the model from voter types.

My lead is Proposition 6, proved in this paper. It states that, given a PB instance and a project type system, deciding whether there is a budget-feasible allocation satisfying the quotas is NP-complete for either the cardinality-type or cost-type aggregator, with NP-hardness already for one resource. The paper proves this by reduction from SET SPLITTING: make one unit-cost project for each element, one project type for each set, and impose lower and upper quotas \(1\) and \(|U|-1\) on each type \(U\). An allocation satisfying those quotas is exactly a split in which neither side contains any set in full.

Here is a continuous outcome problem that puts that result inside the paper’s social-choice setting. Call it Continuous Kemeny PB with Quotas. An instance contains projects \(P\), resources and budgets, project costs, overlapping project categories \(\mathcal U\) with cardinality quotas, a rational approval distribution \(\mu\), and a query \(L\) consisting of desired positive or negative project literals. An allocation \(A\subseteq P\) is feasible if it satisfies the resource limits and every category quota. Its continuous Kemeny score is

\[ K_\mu(A)=\sum_{B\subseteq P}\mu_B\bigl(|A\cap B|+|(P\setminus A)\cap(P\setminus B)|\bigr). \]

The question is whether some feasible allocation of maximum \(K_\mu\) agrees with every literal in \(L\); a solution can return such an allocation. For the Proposition 6 reduction, take \(\mu_{\varnothing}=\mu_P=1/2\). Every project then has approval mass \(1/2\), so every allocation has the same Kemeny score. With \(L=\varnothing\), a maximum-score feasible allocation exists exactly when the quota instance is feasible. Thus Proposition 6 gives NP-hardness of this continuous outcome problem. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the reduction’s combinatorics live in the projects and overlapping quota categories, and the two-point population distribution is itself a finite profile with two voters. The hardness does not depend on population multiplicity.

A second, useful anchor is Corollary 2, also proved in the paper as a consequence of Theorem 1. It says that when the integrity constraint \(\Gamma\) is represented as a DNNF circuit, the problem \(\mathrm{OUTCOME}(F)\) is polynomial-time solvable for \(F\) equal to Kemeny, Slater, or leximax. Here \(\mathrm{OUTCOME}(F)\) takes \(\Gamma\), a judgment profile, and a set \(L\) of literals, and asks whether some \(F\)-optimal admissible judgment extends \(L\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Kemeny Outcome under DNNF Constraints: take a DNNF constraint \(\Gamma\) over project atoms, a rational approval distribution \(\mu\), and a literal query \(L\); ask whether a Kemeny-optimal satisfying allocation extends \(L\). I expect this to be tractable (Class A) in the DNNF size, number of projects, support size \(\tau\), and total bit length of the masses. For each project \(p\), the positive-literal weight is \(\sum_{B:p\in B}\mu_B\), and the negative-literal weight is its complement. DNNF evaluation can maximize the sum of these exact rational weights, both with and without the query literals, and compare the two optima. Corollary 2 proves the finite-profile version; this weighted, binary-rational version is the proposed mirror, not a result claimed by the paper. Its tractability is conditional on the constraint’s DNNF representation; compiling PB constraints into such a circuit may itself be expensive.

The bridge is exact for ordinary profiles: if \(n_B\) citizens submit ballot \(B\), then \(\mu_B=n_B/n\), and multiplying the continuous score by \(n\) recovers the finite Kemeny score. The continuous input can represent very large repeated electorates without listing every voter. The authors are unusually likely to recognize this as their problem: their rules already aggregate approval judgments, Kemeny already sums agreement across citizens, and the paper explicitly studies PB constraints on projects. This mirror covers Kemeny winner determination with quota constraints and with compiled feasible sets; it does not try to continuize the paper’s axiomatic analysis.

My weakest point is that Proposition 6 is a feasibility result about project constraints, not a hardness result caused by the voter population. In the reduction above, the population score is deliberately neutral. So it establishes a hardness floor for continuous Kemeny outcome determination, rather than showing that continuizing the electorate creates a new source of hardness. That limitation is also why I would keep the tractable DNNF question alongside the hardness question: together they describe a meaningful boundary in the paper’s computational setting. Further questions include whether quota hardness persists for restricted ballot distributions or simpler category overlap, and which parameters of the quota structure permit efficient continuous outcome computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Proposition 6 is a poor anchor for population continuization, while Corollary 2 yields a much narrower and more routine mirror than the proponent suggests.

Proposition 6 concerns whether any allocation satisfies the project quotas. Its reduction contains no voter profile. The proposed Kemeny wrapper makes this omission explicit: when half the mass approves nothing and half approves everything, every project has approval mass \(1/2\), so every allocation gets the same score. The continuous society contributes no information to the question; the quota feasibility problem does all the work. That is a real weakness in this anchor, beyond the fact that its hardness comes from project constraints: the asserted result is unchanged if the population is removed. One could ask about weighted Kemeny optimization with non-neutral approval masses and quotas, but that is a new question, not a population version of Proposition 6’s hardness result. The paper gives no result showing that population weights create or illuminate its computational difficulty.

Corollary 2 is harder to dismiss. A citywide population with repeated approval ballots is plausible, and rational masses define a coherent Kemeny objective. But that objective sees only each project’s approval share. Writing \(a_p=\sum_{B:p\in B}\mu_B\), its score is

\[ K_\mu(A)=\sum_{p\in A}a_p+\sum_{p\notin A}(1-a_p). \]

So ballot correlations and the rest of the distribution vanish; the whole society reduces to \(m\) rational coefficients. On a DNNF constraint, the proposed algorithm is the same max-plus model-counting computation as in the paper, with those coefficients supplied as rational weights. The continuous input can encode a large repeated electorate compactly, but the extension introduces no new population-level structure or optimization method. And the circuit condition matters: the paper’s own embeddings can be pseudo-polynomial or parameterized, so the weighted result does not by itself make the underlying PB instance easy to compile.

A stronger version would ask for direct weighted Kemeny optimization over quota-constrained allocations, perhaps with parameters for category overlap and budget structure. That is coherent, but its population component still enters only through the \(m\) approval shares; any further difficulty lies in optimizing over project allocations. A campaigning or mass-transfer problem could make ballot types and their distribution matter more, but the paper supplies neither ballot-change costs nor such an intervention problem. It would add a new computational setting rather than mirror one of this paper’s results.

This negative case remains limited. It undermines Proposition 6 as evidence for continuization and makes the Corollary 2 mirror look like a small weighted extension, not a new algorithmic frontier. But the weighted DNNF problem is well-posed, population-based, and close enough to the paper’s Kemeny outcome problem that the authors could reasonably recognize it. Under the programme’s stated criteria, I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror; Corollary 2 survives the strongest objection, though narrowly.
