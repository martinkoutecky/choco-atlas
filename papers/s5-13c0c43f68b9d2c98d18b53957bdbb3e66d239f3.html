The continuous mirror question

For each \(N\), take \(N\) identical agents and a symmetric transferable-utility partition function assigning a coalition \(B\) worth \(N W(|B|/N,M_N)\), where \(M_N=\sum_{B}(|B|/N)\delta_{|B|/N}\), \(W\) is a fixed-degree rational polynomial in coalition mass and a fixed number of moments of \(M_N\), and at most \(K\) coalitions form. Under the specified type-blind randomized proposer and respondent protocol, take the \(\delta\uparrow1\) limits of the stationary subgame-perfect equilibria with acceptable proposals at every stage, then let \(N\to\infty\). Does the resulting coalition-mass distribution and per-agent payoff converge; if so, what are the limits, and if not, what are the subsequential limits?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is narrow: the paper has no theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time, or parameterized tractable. Its closest computational anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. For symmetric partition functions, under the stated positivity condition on coalition worths, there is a \(\delta^*<1\) such that for every \(\delta>\delta^*\), any equilibrium making an acceptable proposal with positive probability at every stage forms, after departed structure \(n\), a coalition of size \(t(n)\). The rule \(t(n)\) selects the largest size maximizing \(v(t,c(n.t))/t\), where \(c\) is the paper’s recursively computed completion. The proposer’s payoff is \(v(t(n),c(n))/(1+\delta[t(n)-1])\), and the resulting numerical coalition structure is \(n^*=c(\phi)\). This is a finite algorithmic characterization of equilibrium outcomes, not a complexity bound.

My lead mirror is Mass-Symmetric Endogenous Coalition Prediction. A plausible regime is a large industry of otherwise interchangeable firms: same technology, costs, bargaining rules, and access to binding agreements. The population is \(\mu_{t_0}=1\) for one firm type, with \(N\) firms in a finite approximation and \(N\gg\tau=1\). A coalition’s mass \(\alpha\) is its share of the industry.

An instance gives a rational discount factor, a fixed upper bound \(K\) on the number of formed coalitions, and a symmetric transferable-utility function \(W(\alpha,M)\). Here \(M\) is the agent-weighted distribution of terminal coalition masses, and \(W\) is the coalition’s aggregate worth divided by \(N\). Give \(W\) by a fixed-degree rational polynomial in \(\alpha\) and a fixed number of moments of \(M\). In the \(N\)-firm game, a coalition of size \(s\) in partition \(\pi\) has worth \(N W(s/N,M_N)\), where \(M_N=\sum_{B\in\pi}(|B|/N)\delta_{|B|/N}\). Use a type-blind randomized proposer and respondent order so firms remain one type.

The question is: compute the limiting coalition-mass distribution and limiting per-firm payoff of the stationary subgame-perfect equilibria with acceptable proposals at every stage, as \(N\) grows and \(\delta\) approaches one. A solution is the limiting distribution \(M\) and its associated payoff, or, if the limit is not uniquely determined, two distinct limiting equilibrium outcomes. The mass analogue suggested by Theorem 3.1 chooses a coalition mass \(\alpha\) to maximize the continuation coalition’s average worth \(W(\alpha,M_{\mathrm{completion}})/\alpha\), with the remaining mass completed recursively. The decision is still about bargaining over coalition formation with partition-dependent worths; coalition mass is the action variable.

For this bounded-\(K\), fixed-degree version, I would expect Class A: the recursion has finitely many stages, each selecting from a one-dimensional mass interval. Whether the finite equilibrium predictions converge to that recursion is a substantive part of the problem. Allowing \(K\) or the number of relevant moments to grow could change the classification. Follow-up questions include whether the limit is unique, which curvature conditions guarantee convergence, and when vanishing-mass coalitions still affect payoffs.

The soft spot is that Theorem 3.1 is about finite, symmetric games and a restricted class of equilibria; it says nothing about computational complexity or convergence as the population grows. The tempting Cournot anchor, Theorem 3.6, has an additional limit problem: its number of singleton firms grows on the order of \(\sqrt n\), while their population share vanishes. A mass description may lose precisely the coalition-count information that result uses. So I see a credible high-multiplicity research question here, but the paper does not already establish a continuous equilibrium theory.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would vote against a continuous mirror, chiefly because the paper does not state a computational result in the programme’s sense. Theorem 3.1 gives a finite recursion for predicting equilibrium coalition sizes from a symmetric partition function. It does not define an encoded input, analyze running time or complexity, or ask for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm. Its input is effectively the worths across the relevant partitions. Turning it into a computational problem requires choosing a new representation for those worths; the resulting complexity would be driven by that choice.

The strongest proposed repair is sensible as a new model: take \(N\) otherwise identical firms, scale coalition worths as \(N W(\alpha,M_N)\), and ask for limits of equilibrium outcomes. But those choices are not supplied by Theorem 3.1. Its recursion depends on the full finite continuation structure and maximizes over integer coalition sizes, with a largest-size tie-break. A continuum version needs a sequence of partition functions, a normalization, and conditions ensuring that the finite maximizers converge. The proposed fixed-degree \(W\) and bounded number \(K\) of coalitions provide one such package of assumptions; they also select a narrower model, excluding cases where the number of coalitions grows or small coalitions matter. Relax those restrictions and the mass objective \(W(\alpha,M)/\alpha\) may be sensitive to the boundary at \(\alpha=0\), so additional structure is needed there too. None of this makes the question impossible, but it makes the computational problem an analyst-designed extension rather than a continuation of a computational problem in the paper.

There is a deeper modeling change as well. In the finite game, an individual proposer invites a coalition, every invited member can reject, and a rejector takes over the next proposal. A positive-mass block in an atomless society has no evident counterpart to that proposer-and-veto process: a single agent has zero mass, while a “mass proposer” who can assemble a block is a new strategic actor. One can define rules for mass proposals and acceptance, but the equilibrium being computed then depends on those rules. The paper’s discrete bargaining mechanism does not select them.

Theorem 3.6 makes the loss especially concrete. Its Cournot outcome has \(L\) singleton firms, with \(L\) growing on the order of \(\sqrt n\), and one large cartel. In the agent-weighted mass distribution, the singleton mass is \(L/n\to0\), so the limit is the same as for a full cartel: \(M_n\Rightarrow\delta_1\). The result’s distinctive growing fringe disappears from that limit, and under the paper’s normalization the per-firm payoffs vanish as well. Recovering the fringe would require tracking a second scale, such as \(L/\sqrt n\); that preserves coalition-count information the population-mass limit discards.

The weakness in this negative case is real: a restricted model with only macroscopic coalitions and a specified mass-bargaining protocol could be a worthwhile study. But that would establish a new computational problem, not mirror a complexity result of this paper. The named equilibrium characterizations are valuable; they do not, by themselves, provide a convincing computational anchor for ChoCo.
