What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive seed is the paper’s one-to-many result in the residency-assignment setting, but it does not meet the required anchor rule. Theorem 3.5, proved here, says that if every lying woman is weakly better-off, then every woman is weakly better-off and every man has gained only worse matches. Theorem 2.2, also proved here, gives the monogamous version: if no lying woman is worse-off, then no woman is worse-off and no man is better-off. These are results about Gale–Shapley’s outcomes, not results that classify a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized-tractable.

A plausible continuous setting would be a large annual residency market: applicant types encode complete preference rankings, hospital types encode rankings and per-capita capacity, and mass represents cohort share. A lie could be a redistribution of applicant mass across reported rankings. One could ask whether the paper’s welfare implication survives for deferred acceptance on these mass distributions. That is a recognizable analogue of the authors’ question, and matching cohorts make a sensible high-multiplicity scenario.

But it is a new analogue of a welfare theorem, not a continuous mirror of a named complexity result. Theorem 1.3 is cited from Gale and Shapley; it says the algorithm terminates with a stable matching and that no stable matching is better for any proposer. It also supplies no complexity classification. I therefore cannot honestly provide a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem *per anchor* under your instructions. The soft spot is decisive: the paper has strong algorithmic and strategic results, but no named computational-complexity result for the continuization programme to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed residency-market model is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: applicants can be grouped by complete preferences, and liars can be represented by splitting and moving mass between report types. That makes a population version of the paper’s question coherent. It does not, however, make the question a computational mirror.

Theorem 2.2 and Theorem 3.5 are conditional welfare statements about the outcomes of two deferred-acceptance runs. They give no complexity classification or search problem. Their direct continuous counterparts would ask whether the same welfare implications hold for mass matchings—a structural or analytic question, outside ChoCo’s computational brief. Turning them into an optimization problem, such as finding a beneficial mass misreport, adds a task the theorems do not solve. It may be a worthwhile project, but it is an adjacent manipulation problem, not a mirror of these results.

Theorem 1.3 does not supply a qualifying anchor either: it is cited from Gale and Shapley, and establishes stability and proposer optimality, not the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous deferred-acceptance model could prompt algorithmic questions, but those would again be new questions rather than mirrors of a named complexity result in this paper.

The negative case is therefore strong on the programme’s stated criterion, but weaker as the universal claim that no related continuous project could ever be worthwhile. The paper’s own open questions about coordinated lies and uncertain preferences could motivate new computational work. They do not rescue a continuous mirror of this paper’s named results: making them computational would require adding the central problem, not continuizing one already there.
