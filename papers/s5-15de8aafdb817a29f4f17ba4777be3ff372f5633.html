A type would specify an agent’s role and all other characteristics relevant to coalition worth and allocation; mass would be the fraction of agents of that type. One could represent a component by the masses of its types and ask for the payoff per unit of mass under a continuum extension of an allocation rule. Proposition 3.1 characterizes an outside-option claim rule; Propositions 4.1–4.5 establish axiomatic properties, uniqueness results, and an identity relating allocation rules. The proposed large-organization example is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: agents with the same complete role and game-relevant attributes could be represented by types and masses. I would not reject it on the ground that continuous coalition games are impossible. A stronger version could impose type-symmetric worths, define coalition worths over type masses, and seek a measure-based counterpart to the Shapley-derived values. But that would introduce a new game domain and new value definitions; this paper supplies neither a computational problem nor a result about one for that domain.

One could then ask how hard it is to compute those values from a chosen representation of the worth function. That may be worthwhile research, but its difficulty would depend on the new representation and assumptions, not on any result in this paper. Treating it as a mirror would turn “continuize a computational result” into “invent a computational problem adjacent to an axiomatic paper.” Axioms about outside-option transfers are not computational questions merely because their values could someday be computed.

So the negative case is strong for the paper-as-mirror judgment: none of the proponent’s possibilities survives as a qualifying mirror, because there is no computational anchor. The universal claim that no continuous cooperative-game computation in this area could be worthwhile is weaker and not established; the role-type setting might motivate one. It would be a new project, though, rather than this paper’s continuous mirror.
