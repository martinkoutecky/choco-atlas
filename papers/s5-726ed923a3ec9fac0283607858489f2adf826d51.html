The continuous mirror question

Given firms \(i\in[N]\), categories \(m\in[M]\), and worker types \(s\in[\tau]\) with rational masses \(\nu_s\) summing to one, where each type has category \(g(s)\), a strict ranking \(\pi_s\) of firms, and an unknown firm-specific reward law shared by all its mass, can a polynomial-time policy choose a stable mass allocation each round and achieve sublinear Bayesian regret? Each allocation \(x_{i,s,t}\ge 0\) must satisfy \(\sum_i x_{i,s,t}\le\nu_s\), per-capita category minima \(\sum_{s:g(s)=m}x_{i,s,t}\ge\underline q_i^m\), and per-capita total capacities \(\sum_s x_{i,s,t}\le\overline Q_i\). Stability means no positive amount of a type prefers another firm that can accept it using spare capacity or by replacing less-preferred assigned mass without violating its category minima. Assigning mass \(x_{i,s,t}\) yields a Poisson number of independent reward observations with mean \(\lambda x_{i,s,t}\); regret is measured against the firm-optimal stable mass allocation under the true reward means.

The model it lives in

A bandit matching market with a finite support of complete worker types, rational mass per type, per-capita quotas, shared unknown reward laws within each type, and Poisson feedback proportional to assigned mass. The policy chooses quota-feasible stable flows and learns against the true-mean firm-optimal stable flow.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers MMTS's stable matching and Bayesian regret question after pooling workers by complete type; it leaves Theorem 5.3's incentive-compatibility claim, named-worker rankings, and the finite-instance experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do feasible instances always admit a stable mass allocation under the stated pairwise blocking rule, and can one be computed in time polynomial in \(N,M,\tau,L\)?
  • Can a policy achieve sublinear Bayesian regret in \(T\) with runtime and regret bounds in \(N,M,\tau,L,\lambda\), and how do these bounds compare with the discrete guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real, but it needs a qualification: the paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, W[1]-hard, or FPT. Its closest named computational anchors are algorithmic results, proved in this paper. The unnumbered Appendix B gives the MMTS matching cost as \(O(N\sum_m K_m)\). So this paper can motivate a continuous algorithmic question, but it supplies no named hardness result whose difficulty might disappear in the continuum.

The best direct anchor is Theorem 5.1. It says that, given both sides’ preferences and \(M\) worker types, the double-matching procedure returns a firm-optimal stable matching at every round \(t\in[T]\). This result is proved here, using deferred acceptance in the two matching stages.

A plausible regime is a large hiring platform with a moderate number of firms and many applicants in recurring, standardized cohorts. A worker type \(s\) would record the quota category \(g(s)\), the worker’s ranking \(\pi_s\) of firms, and the firm-specific reward distributions used by the problem. Everyone in a type shares those relevant properties; the platform receives the type’s mass \(\nu_s\), with \(\tau\) supported types and \(\tau\ll\) the number of workers. Thus the type is a complete description for this matching problem, not merely a broad label such as “software developer.”

Call the resulting question Type-Mass Double Matching. Its input is firms \(i\in[N]\), categories \([M]\), a finite support of worker types \(s\in[\tau]\) with rational masses \(\nu_s\), known strict worker rankings \(\pi_s\), firm rankings over types, and quotas \(q_i^m,Q_i\). The decision variable is a mass flow \(x_{i,s}\geq 0\), subject to

\[ \sum_i x_{i,s}\leq \nu_s,\qquad \sum_{s:g(s)=m}x_{i,s}\geq q_i^m,\qquad \sum_s x_{i,s}\leq Q_i. \]

The question is to produce a feasible flow that is stable under the paper’s two-stage quota and preference convention, lifted to mass: no positive amount of a type can move to a firm that the type prefers when that firm can accept it by replacing less-preferred mass or using capacity without violating its quotas. A solution is the flow and its stability certificate. This is my lead mirror of Theorem 5.1. I expect it to be tractable for finite \(\tau\), through a weighted deferred-acceptance procedure or a finite flow formulation. The main open questions are whether the lower quotas always permit such a stable flow and whether the firm-optimal result survives fractional mass splitting.

A second, more ambitious anchor is Theorem 5.2, also proved here. With \(K_{\max}=\max_m K_m\) and \(K=\sum_m K_m\), it states that, when all firms follow MMTS, with probability \(1-1/(QT)\) their Bayesian expected regret satisfies

\[ R(T)\leq 8Q\log(QT)\sqrt{K_{\max}T}+\frac{NK}{Q}. \]

That theorem concerns the paper’s online learning problem, not just a matching encoding.

Its mirror is Stable Type-Bandit Mass Matching. The instance extends Type-Mass Double Matching with a horizon \(T\), priors on the unknown firm–type reward means, and a finite-rate feedback model: assigning mass \(x_{i,s,t}\) yields a Poisson number of observations with mean \(\lambda x_{i,s,t}\), each with the type–firm reward distribution. Each round’s action is a feasible, stable mass flow under the sampled rankings. Regret is the expected total reward gap against the firm-optimal stable flow under the true means. The problem is to give a polynomial-time policy that returns a stable flow each round and establish its regret rate in \(N,M,\tau,L,Q,T,\lambda\), with a matching lower bound if possible. The sampling model is an explicit co-modification: it preserves finite-rate learning when mass is continuous.

I expect this second problem also to be tractable when the type support is small: the learned parameters are per firm–type pair, and the matching step can work over \(\tau\) types. It could become hard if the quota and blocking rules make stable choices non-substitutable. Further questions include whether the discrete regret guarantee converges to the mass version, and how the answer changes between finite-rate feedback and aggregate observations whose noise falls with assigned mass.

These are recognizably the paper’s problems: matching firms and workers under type quotas, stability, unknown firm preferences, and learning over time. But the mirror requires an honest co-modification. In the paper, workers within a quota category can have distinct firm rankings and reward means. Grouping workers into complete types introduces ties among copies, while the authors assume strict rankings and list indifferent preferences as future work. A referee could therefore accept the mirror as a natural high-multiplicity regime, but would need the tie and mass-stability rules stated explicitly.

The strongest point is that a large cohort of standardized candidates really can have a small number of complete profiles relative to its size. The weakest point is that the paper’s theorems do not establish complexity results for the mass-flow problems above, and the learning mirror needs a specified finite-rate feedback model. The paper’s cited continuum work on meeting rates and social welfare does not appear to be a collision: it does not formulate these quota-constrained stable matching and learning questions. The scope here is the stability and learning results in Theorems 5.1 and 5.2; I would not stretch the case to cover Theorem 5.3’s incentive-compatibility claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.1 is the lead anchor, and its stability claim does not support the proposed mass-stability question under the usual blocking-pair interpretation. Take one worker category, firms \(A,B\), and workers \(x,y,z\). Let \(q_A=q_B=1\), \(Q_A=1\), \(Q_B=2\). Let \(B\) rank \(y\succ x\succ z\), and let \(x\) prefer \(B\) to \(A\). A first-stage deferred-acceptance run can match \(A\) to \(x\) and \(B\) to \(y\): \(B\) prefers \(y\) to \(x\). In the second stage, \(B\) takes \(z\). The final assignment has a blocking pair: \(x\) prefers \(B\), and \(B\) prefers \(x\) to its assigned worker \(z\). The block persists when each worker is replaced by a positive-mass cohort and quotas scale with population. The proposed mass-stability condition—allowing replacement of worse mass or use of spare capacity—therefore does not lift the result the paper proves.

One could define “stable” to mean that first-stage matches are irrevocable. That yields a certificate for this particular procedure, but it makes stability depend on the algorithm’s sequence of commitments rather than on the final allocation and agents’ preferences. A more defensible replacement is global stability under lower and upper quotas. That is a sensible matching question, but it changes the object Theorem 5.1 analyzes; complementary quotas also make the relevant bundle preferences and blocking rule central. The paper’s theorem supplies no result about the existence or computation of those globally stable mass assignments.

Theorem 5.2 has a related gap. Its \(M\) worker types are quota categories, not complete high-multiplicity types: each named worker \(j\) has its own firm–worker reward means \(\mu_{i,j}\), posterior, and worker ranking \(\pi_j\). In the paper’s continuous-mean simulations, those reward profiles are almost surely distinct. Treating them as complete types gives support size close to the number of workers. To obtain small support, one must posit cohorts with shared unknown reward laws and preferences, introducing ties and shared learning across workers. That is a plausible extension, and the authors list indifferences and side information as future directions. But the proposed Poisson feedback rate is an additional modeling choice: feedback from every assigned person scales with population, while finite-rate sampled feedback requires a separate observation mechanism. The resulting regret theorem would answer a new typed-bandit question, not continuize the theorem’s per-worker learning model.

There is also a scope mismatch. The paper has no numbered worst-case complexity classification. Theorem 5.1 proves a stability property of a deferred-acceptance construction; Theorem 5.2 proves a Bayesian regret bound. Appendix B gives a polynomial running-time bound for the finite procedure. A typed mass version could be studied, but its main question would concern stochastic learning and a newly specified stability notion. It does not expose the kind of computational decision problem or optimization bottleneck that motivates ChoCo.

So the two proposed anchors do not establish a worthwhile mirror as stated, and their most natural repairs require substantial new modeling. The broader claim that *no* scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is harder to defend honestly: a population of observable, repeated worker profiles with shared reward laws is coherent, and a global quota-stability problem could be interesting in its own right. That possibility is the main weakness in the negative case; it would be a new, author-recognizable extension rather than a result carried over from this paper.
