The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and approval types \(t\) with approval sets \(A_t\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), find an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) such that, for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every cohort of submasses \(0\leq\nu_t\leq\mu_t\), if \(\sum_t\nu_t\geq\ell/k\) and \(\left|\bigcap_{t:\nu_t>0} A_t\right|\geq\ell\), then \(\sum_{t:\,|A_t\cap W|\geq\ell}\nu_t>0\). What is the complexity of finding such a committee as a function of \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete approval-ballot types, with rational mass \(\mu_t\) per type; the decision variable is an integral size-\(k\) committee \(W\). EJR requires every cohort with mass at least \(\ell/k\) whose types share at least \(\ell\) approved candidates to include positive mass of voters represented by at least \(\ell\) elected candidates.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s MaxSwapPAV result. Its two proved-here parts are Proposition 1, which says MaxSwapPAV returns a committee satisfying EJR, and Proposition 2, which says it runs in \(O(n^2mk^4\ln k)\) time. Together they establish a polynomial-time algorithm for finding an EJR committee. The paper has no numbered hardness result to anchor on; the NP-hardness of finding a PAV winner mentioned in the introduction is cited prior work on a different problem.

I would mirror the committee-search problem directly. Call it Continuous EJR Committee Search. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a committee size \(k\), and a finite list of approval types \(T\), each with an approval set \(A_t\subseteq C\) and binary-rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The decision variable is an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\); there is no score to maximize, only an EJR condition to satisfy.

Define a cohort by submasses \(0\le\nu_t\le\mu_t\). For every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every such cohort, require that
\[ \sum_t\nu_t\ge\frac{\ell}{k} \quad\text{and}\quad \left|\bigcap_{t:\nu_t>0}A_t\right|\ge\ell \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{t:\,|A_t\cap W|\ge\ell}\nu_t>0. \]
In words: any sufficiently large cohort whose members share at least \(\ell\) approved candidates must contain positive mass of voters who get at least \(\ell\) elected candidates. A solution is a committee satisfying this for every \(\ell\) and every cohort.

This is a population continuization: the types are complete approval ballots, and mass is the fraction of society casting each ballot. The committee stays discrete, since the paper’s decision is which \(k\) candidates to select. A plausible regime is a large association or citywide election over a small candidate slate, where many voters choose from a relatively small menu of approval patterns: \(N\gg\tau\). The paper’s group entitlements already scale with electorate size, and its PAV score is additive over voters, so replacing voter counts by type masses is natural.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is unusually direct here. For a finite profile with \(n_t\) ballots of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). A discrete EJR violation gives a violating cohort of masses; conversely, a violating continuous cohort can be expanded to include every voter of each type in its support, preserving both the common approvals and underrepresentation. Thus this formulation agrees with ordinary EJR on embedded finite profiles. It treats the existential witness in EJR as positive mass in the continuous cohort, rather than as a massless named individual.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. MaxSwapPAV’s harmonic PAV score can be written as \(\sum_t\mu_t H(|A_t\cap W|)\), and its potential is bounded by \(H(k)\). A weighted version of the paper’s progress argument looks plausible: if a cohesive underrepresented cohort overlaps the current committee’s approvals, that overlap can be aggregated across at most \(k\) candidates. A smaller \(k\)-dependent swap threshold—for example, one on the order of \(1/k^5\)—should then give inverse-polynomial progress without relying on a single voter’s contribution. Enumerating swaps and evaluating their scores over the \(\tau\) types would give a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\). That weighted progress bound is an extension to prove; it is not established by the paper’s propositions.

The mirror covers the EJR search result and its swap-based method, not the paper’s discussion of PAV-winner optimization or the other representation axioms. Further questions include the precise support-and-bit-complexity bound for the weighted algorithm, the complexity of checking a proposed committee, and how stable EJR is under small changes in type masses.

My weakest point is the translation of “there exists a voter” into “positive mass of the cohort.” A literal point-existence condition has little meaning for a continuum, while the positive-mass version makes representation an aggregate property. The finite-profile correspondence and the fact that ballot type is the complete information EJR uses make this a defensible choice, but it is still the point an author or referee could challenge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can make one real objection, but I can’t honestly make it defeat the anchor. Propositions 1 and 2 are a computational result: MaxSwapPAV finds an EJR committee in polynomial time. The natural mirror is EJR committee search over a rational distribution of approval types, with the committee still integral. That is a plausible large-election regime, and the mirror is recognizable as the authors’ problem. ([Aziz and Huang](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10415))

The strongest negative point is that the proposed continuum adds little new structure to the EJR condition itself. For a fixed committee \(W\), a violation is equivalent to some \(\ell\) and some candidate set \(D\) of size \(\ell\) having
\[ \sum_{t:\,D\subseteq A_t,\ |A_t\cap W|<\ell}\mu_t\ \geq\ \frac{\ell}{k}. \]
Any violating cohort can be expanded to the full masses of its underrepresented types, so the arbitrary submasses disappear. That makes this a weighted, support-based combinatorial problem rather than a new continuous geometry. It may therefore be a modest extension, not a major new model.

The proponent’s proposed \(1/k^5\) progress threshold is not established: the paper’s unit-voter gain does not carry over unchanged to rational masses. But that gap appears repairable. With total mass normalized to \(1\), a violating cohort has mass at least \(1/k\). If the least PAV marginal is near \(1/k\), the marginal-sum bound forces a substantial part of that cohort to approve a current winner; among at most \(\tau\) types, one such type has mass \(\Omega(1/(k\tau))\). Its swap contribution gives an inverse-polynomial gain, for example a threshold on the order of \(1/(\tau k^4)\). Since weighted PAV is bounded by \(H(k)\), this suggests a polynomial number of swaps in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the rational input length. So the specific algorithmic claim needs proof, but the better mirror looks tractable rather than ill-posed.

My best case against is thus about likely low novelty: the cohort formulation collapses to weighted type masses, and the paper’s potential argument seems to extend with a support-size bound. That does not show the mirror is not worthwhile; it could still formalize the high-multiplicity, bit-complexity result. I cannot support the universal negative claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror.
