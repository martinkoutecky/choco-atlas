The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), indexed team templates \(\mathcal E\), integer requirements \(a_{te}\ge 0\), and strict preferences \(\succ_t\) over each type's incident templates, with at most \(3\) templates per type, find a stable allocation \(x_e\ge 0\). Under the promise that one exists, require \(\sum_{e\in\mathcal E}a_{te}x_e\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and for each template \(e\), some \(t\) with \(a_{te}>0\) must satisfy \(\sum_{f\in\mathcal E(t):\,f=e\text{ or }f\succ_t e}a_{tf}x_f=\mu_t\). What is the computational complexity of finding such an allocation?

The model it lives in

A population of interchangeable types with rational masses, each ranking at most three indexed team recipes; \(a_{te}\) records how much type \(t\) each recipe consumes, and \(x_e\) is the mass of teams formed with recipe \(e\). The allocation obeys type-mass capacities and the paper's stability condition at the type level.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's exact stability problem at degree \(3\), and therefore the degree-\(4\) hardness it implies. It leaves Theorem 3's degree-\(2\) algorithm and Theorem 4's approximate-stability result open in this type-mass model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational type-mass instance of degree at most \(3\) admit a stable allocation, and is finding one in PPAD?
  • Does the degree-\(2\) polynomial-time algorithm extend to nonuniform rational type masses?
  • Can degree-\(3\) hardness be shown when the support size \(\tau\) is fixed or small?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here, but it is a population-level extension of the paper’s matching problem, not a claim that its fractional edge variables already continuize the society. I would anchor on Theorem 2, which is proved in this paper. It says that, given a hypergraph with strict preferences over each vertex’s incident edges and maximum degree exactly \(3\), finding a stable fractional matching is PPAD-complete. A solution assigns \(x(e)\ge 0\) to each edge, with \(\sum_{e\ni v}x(e)\le 1\) for every vertex, and for every edge \(e\), some vertex \(v\in e\) satisfies
\[ x(e)+\sum_{\substack{f\ni v\\ f\succ_v e}}x(f)=1. \]
The proof combines the cited unrestricted PPAD-completeness result, Theorem 1, with the paper’s own degree-reduction Lemma 5.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large workforce forming project teams from standardized role and preference cohorts. For example, \(N=100{,}000\) people might fall into a few dozen types, each with the same preferences over feasible team compositions. A team recipe could require two people of one type and one of another. This is plausible when preferences concern a team’s role composition rather than the identities of its members. Here mass means the fraction of the workforce in each type, and the decision is how much team mass to form under each recipe.

My lead mirror is Type-Mass Stable Hypergraph Matching, degree at most \(3\). An instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a finite indexed list of feasible team templates \(\mathcal E\). A template \(e\) uses \(a_{te}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\) members of type \(t\); indexed templates allow distinct options with the same composition. Each type has a strict preference order over the templates in which it participates, and at most three such templates. The problem is to find a stable allocation, with the usual existence promise if needed: nonnegative template masses \(x_e\) such that
\[ \sum_{e\in\mathcal E} a_{te}x_e\le \mu_t \qquad(t\in T), \]
and, for every \(e\), some participating type \(t\) satisfies
\[ \sum_{\substack{f\in\mathcal E(t)\\ f=e\ \text{or}\ f\succ_t e}} a_{tf}x_f=\mu_t. \]
The allocation is spread uniformly within each type: \(a_{te}x_e/\mu_t\) is the per-person share of type \(t\) assigned to template \(e\). Thus stability says that every feasible team recipe has a type whose members are fully accounted for by that recipe and recipes they prefer to it. There is no scalar objective; the requested solution is the stable mass vector \(x\).

This mirrors the paper’s underlying stability question, with population mass replacing unit vertex capacity. The capacity and type-level stability equations are necessary co-modifications: templates consume cohort mass, and their stability condition is per capita. The population, rather than the outcome variables, is what becomes continuous.

The paper’s hardness transfers to this mirror. Given a finite instance with \(N=|V|\), group vertices with identical incident-edge menus and preference orders into types. Set \(\mu_t=|V_t|/N\), and let each original edge become a distinct template, with \(a_{te}=|e\cap V_t|\). From a stable matching \(x\), set \(x'_e=x(e)/N\). For each type, the capacity constraint becomes the original vertex constraint multiplied by \(|V_t|/N\); a witnessing vertex for an edge gives the type-level stability equality multiplied by the same factor. The reverse scaling recovers a solution to the original instance. This is a polynomial reduction, so the mirror is PPAD-hard. I expect hardness to transfer; PPAD-completeness of the weighted version would additionally require establishing membership for rational type capacities.

My weakest point is that the hardness reduction does not show hardness when the number of types is small relative to the population: its support can grow with the original instance. So it establishes hardness for the general continuous problem, not for the particularly attractive regime \(N\gg\tau\). Nor does the paper itself prove the weighted-capacity existence and membership results needed to make the full mirror total without a promise. Those are concrete follow-up questions: does the degree-\(2\) polynomial algorithm of Theorem 3 extend to nonuniform type masses? Is the degree-\(3\) problem still hard for bounded support \(\tau\)? Does the relevant Scarf existence argument extend to rational type capacities? Theorem 2 is the result this mirror covers; the paper’s fractional assignments are background, not the continuization itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a named computational result: stable fractional hypergraph matching remains PPAD-complete at maximum degree \(3\). So the case against cannot rest on there being no computational theorem to mirror, or on the paper already making its population continuous. Fractional edge assignments alone do not make that argument.

The strongest skeptical point is that the paper’s edges are specific coalitions of named vertices, and each vertex ranks its exact incident edges. A type-level mirror with team templates assumes that agents’ preferences depend on a recipe, such as role composition, rather than on who their teammates are. If preferences depend on identities or context, the complete types may proliferate with the population. The proponent’s grouping argument also does not establish hardness for a small number of types: grouping vertices with exactly the same incident-edge menu can leave the hard instances with essentially one type per vertex.

That concern does not defeat the better mirror. Cohorts with common preferences over team recipes are a plausible setting, and mass stability has a natural interpretation: a recipe is blocked at positive scale when every participating cohort has some mass available for it. For unit-demand recipes, a finite instance with \(n\) vertices maps to type masses \(\mu_t=1/n\); dividing each original edge assignment by \(n\) gives the corresponding mass allocation and stability equalities. Each type can represent \(k\) interchangeable people, for arbitrarily large \(k\), while the recipe allocation scales by \(k\). This gives a high-multiplicity interpretation, though the number of types still grows with the hard instance.

The remaining skeptical point is narrower: Theorem 2 does not establish hardness for a fixed or very small support, the regime that may be most compelling in applications. But that limits what the paper’s result says about such a regime; it does not make the continuous question unworthy. I cannot make a strong universal case against this mirror. The best honest case is that its value depends on whether researchers find recipe-based preferences and type-level stability compelling, and the paper itself does not settle that modeling question.
