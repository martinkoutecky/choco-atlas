The continuous mirror question

Given items \(M\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many complete XOS buyer types in ordered arrival cohorts, and rational per-capita capacities \(q_j\), consider infinitesimal cohorts whose buyers choose utility-maximizing integral bundles at static anonymous item prices under fixed tie-breaking. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\) subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\), and \(x_{t,S}\ge0\). Compute prices \(p\ge0\) whose integrated sequential welfare is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/2\), or determine that none exist.

The model it lives in

A sequential posted-pricing market with finite-support masses of complete valuation types, ordered cohorts, and per-capita item supplies. Buyers still receive integral bundles; mass allocations define the offline welfare benchmark, and the decision is to find static anonymous prices meeting the \(2\)-competitive target.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the XOS guarantee in Lemma 4 and the MPH-\(k\) guarantee in Lemma 6; it leaves the single-item and earlier MPH-\(k\) bounds, the general dual framework, and the demand-oracle computability question aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(2\)-competitive guarantee persist for the fluid XOS market with per-capita supplies?
  • Can qualifying prices be computed efficiently from the type support and valuation representation?
  • What convergence rate or finite-population error bound connects replicated finite markets to the fluid model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a fluid limit of a repeated market, not a claim that the paper already studies a continuous population. In the paper, \(n\) buyers are individually indexed, their valuations are random, and there is one copy of each item. A continuous mirror would replace that finite population with masses of buyers and scale item inventory with population size so the market remains scarce.

A plausible regime is a large advertising or procurement market. There may be millions of buyers but only \(\tau\) recurring buyer templates: each type has the same complete valuation function and arrives in the same cohort. There are \(m\) item categories, each with \(Nq_j\) interchangeable units for \(N\) buyers, where \(q_j\) is the per-capita supply. This scaling is essential: with fixed inventory and an atomless population, only a vanishing fraction of buyers could receive anything. It is also a co-modification that preserves the paper’s market question. It does not make prices or costs individual-independent by fiat; buyers grouped into a type are identical in the respects the model uses.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. An instance gives item categories \(M\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and their ordered arrival cohorts. A type includes a cohort and a complete valuation function \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}\); a buyer of infinitesimal mass still receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\). Given per-capita capacities \(q_j\), the offline benchmark is \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\) for every item, with \(x_{t,S}\ge0\). The variables describe fractions of a type assigned to bundles; each infinitesimal buyer still gets an ordinary bundle.

For a static anonymous price vector \(p\ge0\), each arriving type chooses a utility-maximizing bundle among the items still in stock, with the empty bundle available and a fixed tie-breaking rule. Let \(W(p)\) be the resulting welfare integrated over the arriving mass. The continuous price-design problem is: given the instance and target \(\alpha\), find such a price vector with \(W(p)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha\), or determine that none exists. The intended high-multiplicity bridge is to take \(N\) buyers, \(N\mu_t\) buyers of each type, and \(Nq_j\) units of each item, then study normalized welfare as \(N\) grows. Convergence and finite-\(N\) error bounds are questions to prove, not assumptions.

My lead is Lemma 4, proved in this paper. Its full claim is that for every XOS valuation \(v_i\), every bundle \(S\), and every dual-feasible distribution \(\rho\) over removed-item sets \(R\), \(\mathbb E_{R\sim\rho}[v_i(S\setminus R)]\ge \tfrac12 v_i(S)\). Dual feasibility here bounds each item’s probability of removal by \(\tfrac12\). Via the paper’s Lemma 2, this gives static anonymous item prices with competitive ratio \(2\) against expected offline welfare. The continuous question is the fluid XOS Static Pricing problem above with target \(\alpha=2\). I think this is the strongest anchor for the mirror: the paper’s proof reduces XOS value to additive supporting functions, a structure that seems particularly compatible with splitting a large population into masses. The expected destination is Class A, through a continuous optimization formulation and a controlled pricing or separation problem. Further questions are whether the ratio survives the replicated-supply limit, how to compute qualifying prices in time depending on \(m,\tau,L\), and what finite-\(N\) approximation error follows.

A broader companion mirror is anchored by Lemma 6, also proved in this paper. For every \(k\ge2\), every MPH-\(k\) valuation \(v_i\), every \(S\), and every dual-feasible \(\rho\), it proves \(\mathbb E_{R\sim\rho}[v_i(S\setminus R)]\ge \tfrac{1}{\alpha_k\beta_k}v_i(S)\), where \(\alpha_k=2k+2\sqrt{k(k-1)}-1\), \(\beta_k=\sqrt{k/(k-1)}-1\), and dual feasibility bounds each item’s removal probability by \(\beta_k/(1+\beta_k)\). Through Lemma 2, this yields static anonymous prices with competitive ratio \(\alpha_k\) for MPH-\(k\) valuations. The corresponding fluid MPH-\(k\) Static Pricing problem asks for a price vector meeting \(W(p)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha_k\) on the same kind of per-capita-supply instance. Its expected destination is also Class A, though less confidently: the paper’s proof uses bounded hyperedge size and a union bound, but the fluid online process with capacities needs its own analysis. Useful follow-ups are whether that bound persists for every fixed \(k\), what changes when \(k\) is part of the input, and whether the price search introduces continuum-specific hardness.

These anchors are approximation guarantees for a pricing mechanism, not named P/NP or parameterized complexity classifications. The mirror covers these two guarantees only; it does not recast every result in the paper. The paper’s distributions \(D_i\) describe uncertainty for a finite list of buyers. In the proposed limit, the population masses describe a society of valuation types; with many independent draws from finite-support distributions, empirical type shares can approach those masses. That is an inference motivating the mirror, not an identity established by the paper.

The weakest point is that the paper has one copy of each item, whereas the mirror requires inventory to scale with the population. Its guarantee also concerns expectation over independent valuations, while the fluid instance has deterministic masses. Those changes could lead the authors to call this a related multi-unit market problem rather than their problem’s high-multiplicity version. I think the case still holds because it keeps their central object intact: sequential buyers choose utility-maximizing bundles at static anonymous item prices, and the mechanism is judged against the welfare-maximizing allocation. The price guarantees are therefore useful anchors for asking whether that same problem has a meaningful and tractable population limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Lemmas 4 and 6 do not locate a computational difficulty in population size. Lemma 4 is a pointwise inequality for one valuation and one bundle under a distribution over removed item sets. Lemma 6 has the same form, with fixed \(k\) controlling the probability that a hyperedge is removed. The buyer population enters later, when these inequalities are summed or averaged. Replacing a sum over buyers with an integral over types preserves the argument, but introduces no new problem for it to solve.

A better mirror than a literal atomless version is the proposed fluid market: finite-support valuation types, ordered cohorts, and item supplies scaled with population. That is coherent, and scaling supply avoids the degeneration that would occur with a fixed stock and an atomless population. But to get a computational question from the lemmas, one must ask for an algorithm that outputs prices. The paper’s fixed-ratio existence question is already answered by the lemmas; the paper also says its constructive proof steps recover the prices from earlier work. Its remaining computability question concerns solving the price LP with demand-oracle access. The LP’s price variables and the relevant subset or demand calculations depend on the item and valuation structure, not on the number of copies. A continuum population does not by itself remove that bottleneck.

For Lemma 4, the fluid version would integrate the same XOS survival inequality over type masses. If instead one models each copy as an item, the removal argument still concerns whether individual items survive; it does not exploit population mass. Either route might be useful for studying a large multi-unit market, but neither makes the lemma’s computational content population-sensitive.

The same applies to Lemma 6. The proof’s substantive bound is on the chance that all items in a hyperedge of size at most \(k\) survive. Integrating over types leaves that bound unchanged. With fractional category capacities, the original event that a whole item is removed no longer captures what a buyer can obtain; one must model how residual quantities affect integral bundles as a cohort consumes inventory. That could be a worthwhile new market problem, but it would need its own analysis and would not be a computational mirror of Lemma 6.

So the case against is that both anchors support, at most, a fluid multi-unit extension, while the paper’s explicit computational question remains about prices and demand oracles in the item space. The paper has named competitive-ratio results, but no complexity classification whose population dependence continuization could chart. This is a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational aim, not a claim that the fluid model is incoherent.

That case is limited. Repeated buyers with finite-support valuations and supply proportional to population are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the two guarantees may carry over or motivate useful finite-size bounds. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror. The strongest conclusion I can defend is that these lemmas do not themselves reveal a population-driven computational problem; the proponent’s XOS mirror remains plausible if the authors see the scaled-supply pricing problem as their problem.
