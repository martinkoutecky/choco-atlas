The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive scenario is a large consumer market in which many buyers share the same valuation, prior, price, and signal accuracy. A type records those characteristics and the buyer’s private signal; mass is the share of the market with each signal type. The planner’s decision variable is the share \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) moved to the early cohort, and the objective is aggregate expected consumer welfare. This mirrors the paper’s social-planner question, rather than merely making its outcome fractional. Sgroi explicitly studies choosing early movers to manage the tradeoff between their lost opportunity to learn and the information they provide to later buyers. [Sgroi’s accepted manuscript](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/50142/1/WRAP_Sgroi_Paper9.pdf)

A candidate continuous problem would take the paper’s rational signal and payoff parameters as input and ask for an \(\alpha\) maximizing
\[ W_\infty(\alpha)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{W_N(\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor)}{N}, \]
where \(W_N(M)\) is the expected aggregate welfare in Sgroi’s finite sequential model when \(M\) additional agents are forced to move early. Its scope is the planner’s welfare problem only. If the resulting limit has a tractable explicit form, I would expect this one-parameter version to be Class A; extending it to several buyer types would raise further optimization questions.

But this is not an anchor-compliant case under your rule. The nearest named result in the supplied text is Proposition 1: the welfare-maximizing number \(M\) of additional guinea pigs lies strictly between \(0\) and \(N-1\). It is proved in the paper, but it gives a structural fact about the economic optimum, not a complexity classification or an algorithmic result. The paper derives and optimizes welfare and sales expressions, but it names no result establishing hardness, tractability, or a computational formulation of the kind required for an anchor. So the proposed limit problem is a plausible research direction, not a mirror of a qualifying named result.

The main weakness is substantive too: with \(\alpha N\) early buyers, the law of large numbers can make their aggregate actions reveal the state almost perfectly for every fixed \(\alpha>0\). That can erase the finite-sample cascade tradeoff Proposition 1 concerns, and the limit at \(\alpha=0\) may behave differently from limits with \(\alpha>0\). A large-market scenario is credible, but I cannot confidently claim that this limiting optimization preserves the paper’s problem closely enough to carry the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 1 says the welfare maximizing number of guinea pigs is interior in the finite model; the other results derive welfare and sales behavior. None establishes an algorithmic upper or lower bound. So the proposed market mirror has no qualifying computational result to anchor to: optimizing the early cohort’s mass would be a new problem, not a continuous version of a complexity result in this paper.

The market scenario itself is credible. Buyers sharing valuations, priors, prices, and signal accuracy are a sensible high multiplicity population; their private signals can be represented as types. The deeper problem is what happens to the paper’s information tradeoff in that limit. For any fixed positive early share, there are infinitely many early actions. When their action rates differ across states, their aggregate frequencies reveal the state exactly. Late buyers then face no cascade uncertainty. Increasing the early share only exposes more buyers to decisions made without observational learning; as the share approaches zero, that cost disappears, while the exact-information effect remains for every positive share. At zero, the finite initial sequence and its cascade risk return. Thus the proposed normalized objective can be discontinuous at zero and need not have a meaningful maximizing share. The finite model’s balance between guinea-pig losses and better information for later buyers has collapsed.

One could preserve uncertainty by limiting how many early actions each buyer observes, or by adding a sampling network, correlated signals, or a time-dependent intervention policy. But those additions supply the nontrivial structure the continuum erased. They may support worthwhile research, yet their computational questions would concern the new observation or control model; they are not mirrors of Proposition 1. Expanding the planner’s choice to many buyer types also creates a new optimization problem, while the paper’s own model gives a scalar choice and an explicit welfare analysis.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo computational anchor. The negative case is weaker as a universal claim: the proponent has identified a sensible high multiplicity market setting, and a continuous economic model could be useful. What fails is the claim that this paper supplies a worthwhile computational result to continuize.
