The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Proposition 1, proved here, says that if the best cooperative payoff exceeds the best deceptive payoff by more than the effort cost \(\gamma\), no heuristic strategy is optimal. Proposition 2, also proved here, says that when workers’ prior \(Pr(x)\) equals the public answer distribution \(R(x)\), the Peer Truth Serum makes truthful reporting a Nash equilibrium; its proof uses the surrounding self-predicting and effort-margin conditions. Neither proposition classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The paper’s numbered Results 1–3 are empirical findings, not computational-complexity results.

The best plausible follow-on is a *continuum Peer Truth Serum equilibrium-verification problem*. Imagine a large cohort assigned the same HIT or exchangeable copies of it. A worker type specifies everything relevant to incentives: their signal accuracy and beliefs, effort cost, and preferences. The input is a finite answer set, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over worker types, each type’s signal and payoff data, the public distribution \(R\), and the serum’s payment scale. The question is whether “exert effort and report truthfully” is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium when each worker is matched with a peer drawn from the cohort’s mass distribution. A solution is a yes/no answer, with a type and profitable deviation if the answer is no. A plausible regime is a platform running many copies of one task under common instructions and priming, with a large worker cohort but a modest number of skill and effort classes. This is recognizably about the paper’s incentive mechanism, and Proposition 2 is the closest result to anchor it.

For an explicitly listed finite type set and rational inputs, I would expect equilibrium verification to reduce to checking finitely many incentive inequalities, so likely to be tractable. That is an extrapolation, not a result in the paper. The weakness is substantial: the proposed question extends a game-theoretic equilibrium result, rather than a computational result, and mainly asks for an analytic incentive check. It is closer to continuum game theory than to ChoCo’s target of classifying the complexity of computational interventions. So this is a plausible research direction, but it does not supply an admissible positive mirror under the stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 is a payoff argument ruling out heuristic strategies under a margin condition; Proposition 2 proves truthful reporting is a Nash equilibrium under specified belief conditions. Results 1–3 are empirical comparisons. None asks whether an intervention can be computed efficiently, or states a complexity result. A continuous population cannot turn those into computational results just by changing the workers’ representation.

The proposed cohort is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: many workers can perform repeated, similar tasks, and types can capture their relevant costs and signal behavior. But the proposed equilibrium-verification problem adds little computation. With answer set \(A\), type masses \(\mu_t\), and type-specific answer probabilities \(p_t(a)\), the peer’s answer distribution is \(q(a)=\sum_t \mu_t p_t(a)\). Expected payoffs for each type, signal, and report are finite sums over \(A\); verifying truthfulness means checking the resulting incentive inequalities. For the Peer Truth Serum, Proposition 2 already gives a sufficient condition for those inequalities. The continuous cohort supplies mixture weights, but creates no meaningful algorithmic bottleneck or complexity question in the paper’s sense.

A stronger adjacent project would optimize bonuses over worker types—for example, choose a payment rule that induces truthful effort under a budget. With finite rational types and answers, a basic version has finitely many incentive constraints and can often be written as a linear program. That could be useful mechanism-design work, but it asks how to design a mechanism the paper does not compute; it is not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results. Making it robust to uncertain beliefs or equilibrium selection could make it harder, but would move still further from the paper’s stated propositions.

So the negative case is strong for the mirror question, though not for the broader claim that no worthwhile computational project can grow out of this paper. The cohort model is plausible; what is missing is a computational result in the paper for that model to mirror.
