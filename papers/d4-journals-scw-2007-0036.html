What the mirror covers

The proposed construction would batch Theorem 5 across case types; it does not cover Theorems 10, 12, or 13, or the infinite-alternative results in Theorem 15 and Corollary 16.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most charitable continuous setting would be a large arbitration service handling many repeated kinds of bilateral cases: \(\tau\) distinct case types, each occurring many times. A type would have to include the entire bargaining problem \(t=(S_t,d_t,L_{1t},L_{2t})\); \(\mu_t\) would be its share of cases. A typewise rule would assign each case a nonempty set of alternatives, and one could ask whether a distribution over case types admits a unique rule satisfying lifted versions of the paper’s axioms.

The closest anchor for that construction is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: the Unanimity Compromise is the unique regular rule that is Pareto optimal, monotonic, and preference replacement invariant. Applied separately to every case type, it supplies the proposed rule. But this is only a batching of the original axiomatic result. The “population” is a mass of bargaining cases, not a population of agents; the theorem has no mass-transfer decision, objective, or computational claim. If the two bargainers themselves are replaced by populations with differing preferences, the paper supplies no way to turn those distributions into the two linear orders its model requires. The lifted question is therefore unlikely to be recognized by the authors as a continuous computational version of Theorem 5.

There are no named computational results in this paper of the kind required for a proper anchor. Theorem 5 and Theorems 10, 12, and 13 are axiomatic characterizations, not results about complexity or algorithms. The closest impossibility result, Theorem 15, says that for a neutral rule on countable problems, if a problem is invariant under a permutation and the rule selects an alternative moved by that permutation, then the rule’s selected set is infinite. Corollary 16 concludes that no finite rule on two-agent countable problems is neutral. Both concern the paper’s extension of the alternative set to countable size while keeping two bargainers; neither is a computational result or a population continuization.

So the case-type construction is the strongest positive foothold I can defend, but it does not yield a qualifying continuous problem per anchor. The weakest point is also decisive: the paper studies axiomatic bargaining between two agents, and its most tempting infinite-domain result continuizes the outcome space rather than the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive obstacle is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 5, 10, 12, and 13 characterize bargaining rules by axioms; Theorem 15 and Corollary 16 are impossibility results about neutrality and finite-valued rules. The model is two bargainers with complete rankings over alternatives. It asks which rule satisfies specified properties, not how hard it is to compute an intervention, optimum, or robustness quantity. A continuous-population complexity result would need a new computational problem before it could mirror anything here.

The strongest version of the proposed Theorem 5 mirror is a large arbitration service with many repeated cases. Give each case type its full problem \((S,d,L_1,L_2)\) and a mass. But applying the characterization separately to each type just applies UC separately; averaging its outcomes adds no interaction between cases. The mass counts cases, not agents in a shared social decision, so this is not the high-multiplicity regime the programme studies. To create interaction—say, by imposing a shared budget across cases—would introduce a new allocation problem absent from the paper. Replacing each bargainer with a population of people is a stronger population-based proposal, but then the model needs a way to turn each population’s rankings into the two linear orders used by the theorems. Any such aggregation rule changes the decision problem and the axioms, rather than continuizing the paper’s result.

Theorem 15 and Corollary 16 do not supply a second foothold. Their infinite object is the set of alternatives: a permutation moves an alternative through an infinite orbit, forcing a neutral rule that selects one moved alternative to select infinitely many. A distribution over bargaining cases would leave that argument intact within each case, without creating a computational question about a population. Putting populations on the two sides while retaining the infinite alternatives would require a new account of their preferences and would still rely on the outcome-space phenomenon that the programme excludes as its goal.

So the case-type construction is a coherent way to study a distribution of bargaining instances, but it does not make this paper a worthwhile continuous computational mirror. The negative case is strongest on the missing computational anchor; it is weaker only in the unavoidable sense that one could invent a new, coupled bargaining-service problem. That would be a different research question, not a continuization of a result in this paper.
