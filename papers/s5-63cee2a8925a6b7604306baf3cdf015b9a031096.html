The continuous mirror question

Given \(p=2\), rational \(\varepsilon>0\), and a finite support \(T\) of step-density probability-measure types \(\nu_t\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), find at most \(\tau=|T|\) cuts and assign the resulting intervals to two possibly disconnected pieces \(A_1,A_2\) so that \(|\nu_t(A_1)-\nu_t(A_2)|\leq\varepsilon\) for every \(t\in T\).

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\in T\) carries a step-density probability measure \(\nu_t\) and population mass \(\mu_t\). Choose at most \(\tau\) cut locations and assign each resulting interval to one of two pieces, subject to \(|\nu_t(A_1)-\nu_t(A_2)|\leq\varepsilon\) for all \(t\). There is no weighted objective: the \(\mu_t\) describe population shares but do not affect feasibility.

The objection that survived

The paper already formulates the same feasibility search over the support list, so adding \(\mu_t\) supplies no new population-sensitive computational structure; this limits novelty but does not defeat the stated mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the \(k=2\) Consensus-Halving membership case of Theorem 6.4; prior work cited by the paper already establishes PPA-completeness for that search task, so no complexity question remains for this anchor. It leaves discrete necklace splitting and the polygon, BSS, and \(\mathbb{Z}_p\)-star Tucker results outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the high-multiplicity version of Consensus-Halving, with two pieces. A type is a complete resident valuation: a probability measure over a shared continuous resource, represented by its cumulative distribution function. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents of type \(t\). A plausible regime is many residents in a region, but relatively few distinct valuation profiles. The decision is to cut and assign the resource into two possibly disconnected pieces so every type values the pieces almost equally. This is a feasibility search, not an optimization problem: the target is valuation discrepancy at most \(\varepsilon\).

My anchor is Theorem 6.4, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(k\ge 2\), \(k\)-Necklace-Splitting and Consensus-\(1/k\)-Division lie in the Turing closure of \(\mathrm{PPA}\text{-}k\); when \(k=p^r\) for a prime \(p\), both problems lie in \(\mathrm{PPA}\text{-}p\). The consensus problem here takes \(t\) probability measures on \([0,1]\), represented by step-function densities, and asks for a partition into \(k\) possibly disconnected pieces, using at most \((k-1)t\) cuts, such that each measure values every piece within \(\varepsilon\) of every other. I use its \(k=2\) case as my lead.

The mirror problem is: given a fixed prime \(p=2\), rational \(\varepsilon>0\), and a finite support \(T\) of valuation types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), find a partition of \([0,1]\) into two possibly disconnected pieces \(A_1,A_2\), using at most \(\tau=|T|\) cuts, such that
\[ \left|\mu_t(A_1)-\mu_t(A_2)\right|\le\varepsilon \quad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
The output is the cuts and the assignment of each resulting interval to a piece. The masses record population shares; the universal condition preserves the paper’s requirement that every agent’s valuation be balanced. For a society of \(N\) residents, the discrete high-multiplicity instance is recovered by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). The paper’s own representation also permits the broader efficiently computable, sufficiently continuous CDF families of Theorem 6.6.

I expect hardness to transfer in the two-piece case: the paper notes prior \(\mathrm{PPA}\)-completeness for Consensus-Halving, and a finite list of measures embeds directly as a mass distribution over valuation types. Duplicating each type does not change the balancing condition; it only makes the population large relative to its support. Theorem 6.4 supplies the membership side for the paper’s computational formulation. For \(p>2\), the paper establishes \(\mathrm{PPA}\text{-}p\) membership for the corresponding consensus problem, while its hardness remains open.

The scope is narrow: this mirrors the Consensus-\(1/k\)-Division result in Theorem 6.4, not the paper’s BSS or Tucker completeness results, and not the discrete Necklace-Splitting problem by itself. I think the authors would recognize it: their problem already treats agents as valuation measures, and grouping identical measures is exactly the high-multiplicity move.

The weak point is that \(\mu\) does not affect feasibility once the positive-mass support is fixed. Only the distinct valuation types matter. So this is a sensible continuous-population version, but a degenerate one: it tests whether high multiplicity changes the search problem, not whether population proportions create new computational structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor fails as a continuous-population mirror because its type masses disappear from the problem. If \(\nu_t\) is the valuation of type \(t\), the feasible partitions are determined by
\[ \left|\nu_t(A_1)-\nu_t(A_2)\right|\leq\varepsilon \quad\text{for every }t\text{ with }\mu_t>0. \]
Any two positive weightings with the same support therefore have exactly the same solutions. Drop the weights and list the \(\tau\) distinct valuations as the agents in the paper’s Consensus-\(1/2\)-Division problem; conversely, give those types any positive rational weights. This is an instance-for-instance correspondence, not a new population-sensitive question. Repeated residents of the same type add duplicate constraints that can be removed.

That correspondence is especially direct here: the paper itself defines the computational Consensus-\(1/k\)-Division problem with a finite list of measures, the same cut bound, and the same requirement that every measure value the pieces nearly equally. Theorem 6.4 places that problem in the relevant search-class closure, and Theorem 6.6 extends the reduction to broad efficiently computable, sufficiently continuous CDF families. The collision is not that the resource being divided is continuous. It is that the proposed mass-weighted version reduces exactly to a computational problem this paper already formulates and analyzes.

A more population-sensitive version could allow a small fraction of residents to be unbalanced, or minimize mass-weighted dissatisfaction. But those choices replace the theorem’s universal fairness condition with a new objective or relaxation. They might be worthwhile research questions; the paper does not single out a canonical one, and they are not the result Theorem 6.4 establishes. The discrete necklace problem offers no clearer rescue: its thieves are fixed in number, while beads describe the resource being divided, not a high-multiplicity population.

So I would reject this anchor. I cannot honestly turn that into an airtight claim that *no* worthwhile mirror exists: a mass-aware fairness variant could be valuable. But making its masses matter requires choosing a new fairness notion; preserving the paper’s stated consensus problem makes the proposed mirror a restatement of its existing continuous computational formulation.
