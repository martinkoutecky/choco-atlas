The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Theorem 5, proved here, says that the Maximal Recursive rule satisfies very strong stochastic-dominance participation: for every finite preference profile and voter, participating weakly improves the lottery for that voter relative to abstaining, and improves it strictly whenever some lottery is strictly SD-preferred to the abstention outcome. That is an axiomatic result, not a complexity classification. The paper says MR is polynomial-time computable, and cites prior work for the \(\#\)P-completeness of computing RSD probabilities, but neither claim is a numbered result of the required kind. So there is no anchor-backed continuous problem to state.

The strongest plausible population setting is a large consultation over a fixed set of alternatives, with many voters but relatively few distinct weak preference orders. A type would be a complete weak order; its mass would be its fraction of the population. The output remains a lottery over alternatives: the population, not the outcome space, is what becomes continuous.

The soft spot is that Theorem 5 concerns one voter abstaining. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, so a direct translation is vacuous. A cohort-abstention or infinitesimal-mass version could be studied for a mass-weighted MR rule, but it changes the participation question, and Theorem 5 does not establish that version. I would treat it as a possible follow-on, not as a mirror of a qualifying result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying computational anchor. That is the strongest objection to a paper-specific mirror: Theorems 1–3 relate axioms, Theorems 4–5 establish participation properties of rules, and Theorems 6–8 give axiomatic counterexamples. The paper’s polynomial-time claim for MR and its \(\#\)P-completeness claim for RSD are background facts attributed to other work, not computational results established here. Even the concluding open question asks whether an SDS with certain axioms exists; it does not ask for an algorithm or complexity classification.

The best candidate is Theorem 5, for MR’s very strong SD-participation. A large population with repeated weak preference orders is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and a weighted MR rule on a distribution over those orders is a plausible model. The obstacle is the theorem’s unit of deviation: one voter abstains. In a nonatomic society that person has zero mass, so the direct comparison leaves the society unchanged. Letting a positive-mass cohort abstain avoids that degeneracy, but asks whether a group with a shared preference can improve its lottery by withdrawing together. That is a coherent, potentially useful question; it is also a different strategic property from the one Theorem 5 proves. A computational task such as finding the smallest cohort mass that gains under withdrawal would be a new research direction, rather than a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The same scale problem affects the other participation results. The ESR and serial-dictatorship counterexamples concern a particular finite voter’s removal; the relations between SD and DL participation concern axioms quantified over individual voters in finite profiles. A continuous cohort version could be defined for these too, but it would create new mass-withdrawal questions rather than continuize a computational result from the paper. The paper’s randomized outcomes already being lotteries does not supply the missing population-level computational contribution.

This negative case is strongest on anchoring, not on whether a continuous population model is sensible. A cohort-abstention study could merit attention on its own. The paper gives no computational result to mirror, though, and the most natural continuous reformulation changes individual participation into group withdrawal.
