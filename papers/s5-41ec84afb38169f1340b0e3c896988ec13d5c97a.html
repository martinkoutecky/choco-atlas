The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a resident-mass version of \(c\)-Balanced Star Districting. The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: given a graph and its two population weights, a polynomial-time algorithm returns a \(c\)-balanced star districting with approximation ratio \(O(\sqrt n)\) on general graphs, \(O(\log n)\) on planar graphs, \(O(h^2\log n)\) on \(H\)-minor-free graphs when \(|H|=h\), and \(O(1)\) on trees and outerplanar graphs. It is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result. Section 3 also gives hardness claims, but the supplied text shows those restatable theorem statements without printed numbers, so I cannot use them as numbered anchors under your rule.

A plausible regime is a densely populated urban area divided into coarse map cells. There may be hundreds of thousands of residents but only \(n\) cells and at most \(2n\) relevant types: residents’ cell and demographic group. Residents sharing a type have the same location and group characteristics. At the finer household or street level, a cell can be divided among adjacent service districts; the graph records which cells touch. That makes the population large relative to the number of types while retaining the paper’s geography, two-group balance, star shape, and goal of maximizing covered population.

Call the problem Continuous-Mass Star Districting. An instance is a finite graph \(G=(V,E)\), a fixed \(c\ge2\), and binary-rational masses \(\mu_{v,i}\ge0\) for each cell \(v\) and group \(i\in\{1,2\}\), normalized so that \(\sum_{v,i}\mu_{v,i}=1\). For each source cell \(v\), group \(i\), and possible district center \(u\in N[v]\), choose \(x_{v,i,u}\ge0\), the mass assigned from that source type to the star centered at \(u\). Require
\[ \sum_{u\in N[v]}x_{v,i,u}\le\mu_{v,i}. \]
Let \(P_i(u)=\sum_{v\in N[u]}x_{v,i,u}\). Each nonempty district must satisfy \(P_1(u)\le(c-1)P_2(u)\) and \(P_2(u)\le(c-1)P_1(u)\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{v,i,u}x_{v,i,u}\); a solution is the allocation and its resulting star districts. Each resident is assigned to at most one district, though the mass of a type can be divided among districts.

For this star version, the continuous question is in Class A: these variables, supply constraints, and balance constraints form a linear program with \(O(|V|+|E|)\) variables and constraints, so an exact optimum is computable in polynomial time in the rational input size. Theorem 1 anchors the same underlying objective and district shape, and identifies the graph classes over which its finite-block guarantees were established. My scope is only its star-districting result; I am not claiming mirrors for arbitrary connected districts or the complete-graph and tree FPTAS results.

The weak point is territorial semantics. The paper requires vertex-disjoint districts made of whole blocks. My scenario allows a coarse cell to be cut into disjoint household-scale pieces, so districts may use different portions of the same graph vertex. That is plausible for service districts drawn below the map’s resolution, but authors who require each input block to remain indivisible may reject it as changing their problem. The finer subdivision is the co-modification that makes resident mass divisible while preserving person-level exclusivity and geographic adjacency.

Further work would need to formalize how those within-cell cuts preserve contiguity, and relate the mass optimum to the paper’s whole-block optimum as household granularity changes. Adding district-size constraints, more demographic groups, or arbitrary connected supports would also test whether the LP tractability survives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor, but it exposes a basic mismatch between population mass and the units this problem packs. Its \(n\) objects are geographic blocks: each chosen district contains whole vertices, and no vertex can belong to two districts. Residents appear only as weights on those blocks. If the continuous mirror keeps that territorial rule, replacing normalized rational masses \(\mu_{v,i}\) by integer weights \(D\mu_{v,i}\), for a common denominator \(D\), preserves every balance decision and multiplies every objective value by \(D\). The feasible districtings and their optima are unchanged. That is a change of units for an already weighted graph, not a new population-level computational question.

The proposed LP avoids that collapse by letting residents from the same cell and group go to different star centers. That is coherent as an assignment of people, but it changes the packing rule: two districts can now use the same graph vertex, provided they use different residents there. The paper’s vertex-disjoint districts have become overlapping graph supports. The input graph records that cells touch; it does not say whether the assigned fractions can be cut into disjoint, connected pieces of land. So the LP is well-defined as a resident-assignment problem, but its feasibility no longer certifies the territorial districts the paper defines.

The strongest repair is to give each coarse cell internal geometry and population density, then require its residents to be divided into spatial pieces whose boundaries form disjoint, connected districts. That would make mass splitting territorially meaningful. But it needs geometry and within-cell population data absent from the paper’s graph model; its districts and feasibility conditions would be defined by spatial cuts, not by vertex-disjoint stars. It may be a worthwhile continuous districting problem, but it is not a population mirror of the result Theorem 1 proves.

That leaves the negative case with a real limit. Large populations living in a fixed set of blocks are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a continuous spatial version could interest these authors. The case against is that preserving the paper’s actual objects makes mass normalization redundant, while making mass operational requires replacing its block packing model with a spatial partitioning model. I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would see the latter as their problem; the universal negative is weakest there.
