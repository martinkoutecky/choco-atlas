The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited by the paper itself: it contains no eligible anchor. There is no printed Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting an algorithmic or complexity result. Equations (2)–(9) define measures and indices, while the main claims are empirical. The Shapley and Banzhaf complexity facts, if any, belong to the cited literature, not to this paper. Thus I cannot honestly present a Class A/B/C verdict as a theorem-backed mirror of this paper.

The closest anchor-like object is the paper’s named Beta2 power index, defined in equation (7). For a finite weighted voting game, it integrates over a common approval probability \(p\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\), summing the probabilities of coalitions in which voter \(i\) is pivotal. The paper estimates \(\alpha=3.00\) and \(\beta=1.17\), evaluates the index by Monte Carlo simulation, and reports that Beta2 gives the best predictive performance in Table 2, with squared error \(0.627\) and perplexity \(76.6\). This is a computational object, but not a numbered theorem or complexity result.

If that anchor restriction were relaxed, the most defensible continuous mirror would be a mass-level version of their potential-power question.

Consider a large delegative organization whose members fall into finitely many complete types \(T\). A type records the member’s normalized voting weight, delegation scope, current or latent approval behaviour, and any cost of changing that behaviour. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of members of type \(t\), \(w_t\) its voting weight, and \(r_t\in[0,1]\) the current fraction voting yes. Let \(q\) be the quorum, \(W=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\) the total voting weight, and \(Y=\sum_t\mu_t w_t r_t\) the current yes-weight.

The problem, which I would call Continuous Delegation-Mass Flip, is:

Given \((T,\mu,w,r,q)\) and non-negative type costs \(\kappa_t\), find masses \(x_t^+\) and \(x_t^-\) to move from no to yes and from yes to no, minimizing \( \sum_t\kappa_t(x_t^++x_t^-) \), subject to \(0\le x_t^+\le\mu_t(1-r_t)\), \(0\le x_t^-\le\mu_t r_t\), and, when the proposal currently loses, \(Y+\sum_t w_t(x_t^+-x_t^-)\ge qW\). The output is the minimum intervention and the optimal mass transfer. Restricting the intervention to one type gives that type’s potential power; applying it to the observed \(r_t\) gives an analogue of exercised power.

This is recognisably the paper’s question: it retains delegation-induced voting weights, a quorum, potential ability to change an outcome, and observed versus hypothetical behaviour. The continuous modification is that power belongs to a type-mass or cohort, not to one named user. The resulting problem is a linear program and therefore appears Class A for fixed finite \(T\). A stochastic companion could draw the common approval parameter \(p\) from the paper’s Beta distribution and study expected flip cost or expected type-level power.

The relevant regime would be a large party, platform, or membership organization with millions of participants but relatively few recurring delegation-and-behaviour classes: for example, members with the same issue scope, delegation weight, approval profile, and intervention cost. Then \(N\) is large while \(\tau=|T|\) remains modest. The paper’s individual user identities are replaced only where the individuals are genuinely interchangeable in the model.

The weakest point is substantial. The paper’s central object is the power of an individual super-voter, whereas an individual has zero mass in a genuine continuum. Replacing that object with the power of an entire type bloc is a necessary co-modification, but it changes the unit of analysis. Moreover, the delegation network itself contains named, path-dependent users; representing it only through type weights discards some of the paper’s social mechanism. A referee might therefore accept this as a continuous analogue of the paper’s potential-power analysis, but not as an exact continuum limit of its individual Beta2 index.

So the positive case is: the paper contains a plausible mass-level voting-power problem with a clean LP mirror, likely tractable in the continuum. The negative qualification is decisive for grading: because the paper has no named computational result, this cannot satisfy the programme’s formal anchor requirement, and it does not establish a theorem-backed continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong at the programme’s gate: this paper contains no eligible computational result. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, algorithmic claim, or complexity classification. Equations (2)–(9) define empirical statistics and probabilistic indices; the claim that Beta2 predicts potential power best is a dataset-dependent experimental finding. The complexity of Shapley or Banzhaf indices, if relevant, belongs to the cited literature, not to this paper. Thus there is no theorem-backed result here whose continuous version could be a ChoCo anchor.

The proposed Beta2 anchor does not repair that problem. Beta2 is fundamentally a finite-coalition, individual-pivotality model. Its central event is that a particular voter is a swing voter in a coalition. In a genuine continuum, with per-capita voting weights normalized so total weight remains finite, one individual contributes \(O(1/N)\). Except at knife-edge margins, their probability of changing the outcome tends to zero. The object the paper measures—whether a named super-voter can reverse a vote—therefore disappears.

One can preserve a nonzero object only by changing it into the power of a positive-mass bloc. But then the finite coalition sum in equation (7) has been replaced by a deterministic threshold calculation. Conditional on the paper’s common approval parameter \(p\), aggregate voting converges to its law-of-large-numbers value; Beta2 becomes an integral such as
\[ \int_{\{p:\,\text{the bloc is pivotal at }p\}} \operatorname{Beta}(p\mid\alpha,\beta)\,dp. \]
That may be a sensible population statistic, but it is no longer individual voting power, no longer the paper’s super-voter question, and no longer a computational phenomenon caused by continuization.

The proposed “Continuous Delegation-Mass Flip” changes the problem even more substantially. The paper never asks for a minimum-cost intervention, and it introduces no costs \(\kappa_t\), mutable voting behaviour, or mass transfers. Equation (2) merely tests whether a fixed voter’s existing weight is sufficient to alter a fixed outcome; equation (3) compares the actual outcome with a counterfactual removal. An LP that buys enough members of a type to cross a quorum is a new bribery or control problem. It might be a worthwhile ChoCo problem in its own right, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The better possible repair—continuizing the delegation network itself—does not solve the identity problem. Delegation is to named users, with scope, transitive chains, revocation, and cycle resolution. Preserving those features requires a network object whose types encode delegation targets and neighbourhoods. If the target remains a named super-voter, the model retains an essential atom and is not a purely continuous population. If delegations are instead aggregated between types or roles, there is no individual super-voter left: the model has become a flow or bloc-delegation system with a new notion of power.

A continuum could certainly support descriptive statistics for delegation weights, approval rates, or network kernels. Those would extend the paper’s empirical analysis, but not its computational content. The strongest honest conclusion is therefore negative: the paper offers an interesting empirical study of individually identified, path-dependent delegation behaviour, but no named computational result to mirror, and its only plausible mass-level reformulation either destroys individual pivotality or invents a different intervention problem.
