What the mirror covers

No results qualify for a ChoCo computational mirror; the candidate-stability and unanimity classifications are axiomatic.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is very limited. The paper has a natural population mirror: take a large electorate whose members share a relatively small set \(T\) of rankings, and represent it by a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). A voting correspondence would map each candidate agenda and \(\mu\) to a set of winners; candidate stability would ask whether any candidate prefers withdrawing to the outcome when they remain. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for the voters.

But there are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper: it does not state a named result about an algorithmic problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its results classify candidate stability and unanimity under assumptions about candidates’ preferences over winner sets—for example, the abstract reports impossibility and dictatorship results under expected utility and Bayesian updating, and different conclusions for uniform priors and other preference domains. Those are axiomatic results, not complexity results. [The paper](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/1523/1/WRAP_Rodriguez_Alvarez_twerp666.pdf), [publication abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/sochwe/v27y2006i3p545-570.html)

So I cannot name a valid anchor or give the required per-anchor computational question and A/B/C expectation. The natural candidate-stability question above would ask for an axiomatic classification, not the computational complexity of a problem. That is the central weakness, and it prevents this paper from supporting the requested positive case within ChoCo’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case supplies no computational anchor. That is decisive for a ChoCo mirror: this paper studies candidate entry and withdrawal, then proves axiomatic impossibility and characterization results about unanimous, candidate-stable voting correspondences under different preferences over winner sets. It does not analyze the complexity of an algorithmic problem. [The Warwick manuscript](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/1523/1/WRAP_Rodriguez_Alvarez_twerp666.pdf), [paper abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/sochwe/v27y2006i3p545-570.html)

The best population mirror is still easy to state: represent a large electorate by a distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types, and ask whether the paper’s candidate-stability classifications hold for that society. That is a coherent continuous-population question. But its answer would be another axiomatic classification, not a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to chart.

One could try to make it computational by asking whether a given correspondence is candidate-stable, or whether a stable unanimous correspondence exists. The paper supplies neither a computational problem nor an encoding for correspondences. A profile-by-profile stability check would be a newly posed task; a universal check would be deciding an axiom over a chosen rule representation. Either requires choices absent from the paper, and neither mirrors one of its results. The population can sensibly be high-multiplicity here; that does not bridge the gap between the paper’s contribution and ChoCo’s scope.

So the negative case is strong on scope, not on modelling: a continuous voter society is plausible, and a continuous axiomatic extension might be worthwhile in another programme. I see no worthwhile ChoCo computational mirror of this paper.
