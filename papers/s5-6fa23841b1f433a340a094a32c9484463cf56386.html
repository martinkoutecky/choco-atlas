The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule. The paper contains no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, parameterized-hard, or similar.

The closest candidate is Theorem 3.8, proved in the paper: in one-to-one, men-proposing Deferred Acceptance, truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for every man, holding everyone else’s preferences fixed. That is a strategic property, not a computational-complexity result. Theorem 3.3 (the algorithm finishes with everyone matched), Theorem 3.4 (its matching is stable), and Theorem 3.5 (the matching is man-optimal) likewise establish properties of the algorithm, not a complexity classification. In the school-choice discussion, the school-manipulation result is presented through Example 4.1 and sufficient conditions, not a numbered complexity theorem.

There is a plausible regime for a *new* continuous question: a large annual student cohort with relatively few distinct preference and priority types, represented by rational masses, and school capacities scaled as fractions of the cohort. But the paper’s individual student strategy-proofness result does not transfer cleanly: an infinitesimal student cannot change a mass allocation, so individual truthfulness becomes vacuous. Studying whether a positive-mass group of one type can benefit by coordinating on a false ranking would be more substantive, but it changes the strategic question from the one Theorem 3.8 proves.

So the paper suggests a possible continuous population model, but it supplies no qualifying anchor for the requested prover questions. Calling that a case *for* a continuous mirror would overstate the evidence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is a missing computational anchor. The paper proves properties of Deferred Acceptance: completeness, stability, student strategy-proofness, and an example of school manipulation. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem or give an algorithm for finding a manipulation. Theorem 3.8, the closest candidate, says an individual student cannot benefit by misreporting. That is a strategic property, not a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror.

The best continuous version of Theorem 3.8 exposes the mismatch. In an atomless population, one student’s report has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate assignment, so individual truthfulness is vacuous. Letting a positive-mass group coordinate on a false ranking gives a substantive question, but changes the result into one about coalition manipulation. That could be a useful ChoCo problem in its own right; the paper neither states nor analyzes its computational complexity.

School manipulation is the strongest rescue. With a large student cohort, finitely many preference and priority types, and capacities scaled with the cohort, a school’s altered report could change assignments for positive mass. That is a sensible continuous model. The paper’s contribution here, though, is Example 4.1 and proposed sufficient conditions, not a complexity result. Asking how hard it is to find a beneficial school report would be a new problem prompted by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result it establishes.

I would not argue that school choice lacks a meaningful continuum regime. Existing work explicitly models a continuum of applicants and finitely many schools, and studies Deferred Acceptance and stable assignments in that setting. This is evidence that the regime is sensible, not a collision with ChoCo: the cited work is analytic rather than a computational complexity treatment. [Grigoryan, “A continuum model as a limit of large finite matching markets”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.3982/TE5478).

So the negative case is strong for the narrower claim that this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to mirror. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can arise from it is weaker: the school-manipulation direction could merit separate study, but calling it this paper’s computational mirror would overstate what the paper proves.
