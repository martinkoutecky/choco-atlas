The continuous mirror question

Given a finite online stream of job cohorts with rational masses and types \(t=(r_t,d_t,v_t)\), revealed when they arrive, and processor capacity scaled to \(\kappa\) processors per unit population, find a causal preemptive fluid scheduling policy with a competitive guarantee against the clairvoyant fluid optimum for the population-normalized weighted completion objective \(\sum_t v_t\int C\,d\nu_t(C)\), where \(\nu_t\) is the completion-time mass distribution for cohort \(t\). Determine whether a type-aggregated policy can achieve a \(2\)-competitive bound and its running time in the support size \(\tau\), bit length \(L\), and other instance parameters.

The model it lives in

A fluid online scheduling model over finitely many job types: each unit of cohort mass represents an ordinary job requiring \(d_t\) units of nonparallel processing and incurring weight \(v_t\) at completion; cohorts arrive at \(r_t\), and aggregate service is bounded by \(\kappa\). The scheduler sees only arrivals so far, while the offline benchmark sees the full stream.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the thesis’s online preemptive scheduling problem in Chapter 5. It turns the population into the continuous object while keeping the central decision—when and where jobs run—intact.

A convincing regime is a large cloud or cluster serving batch jobs from recurring templates. A type records a job’s release time \(r\), runtime \(d\), and waiting-cost weight \(v\). Jobs of the same type are identical in every respect the scheduling model uses; the support \(\tau\) might be a few dozen or hundred templates even when there are millions of jobs. Each user submits one job, as in the paper. To keep the limit nondegenerate, processor supply scales with population: for \(N\) jobs, there are about \(\kappa N\) identical processors. That is the allowed co-modification of supply, while job mass still represents agents receiving whole jobs.

My anchor is Chapter 5, Theorem 4, proved in this thesis. It states: if every job follows its myopic best response under P-DLGM—truthfully reporting its type and choosing the node that maximizes its tentative utility—then P-DLGM is \(2\)-competitive for \(P|r_j,\mathrm{pmtn}|\sum_j v_j e_j\). In full, the mechanism’s total weighted completion time is at most twice the clairvoyant offline optimum, for jobs with release dates, preemption, and any number of identical parallel processors. The thesis also notes that this bound does not require the baseline mechanism’s restriction on when long jobs may arrive.

Here is a continuous problem that mirrors that result:

High-Multiplicity Preemptive Scheduling. An instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over job types \(t=(r_t,d_t,v_t)\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to one. The type cohorts arrive online: at time \(r_t\), mass \(\mu_t\) of jobs of that type is revealed. Formally, interpret this as the limit of populations with \(N\mu_t\) jobs of type \(t\) and processor counts \(m_N\) satisfying \(m_N/N\to\kappa>0\). Each job requires \(d_t\) units of processing, can be preempted and resumed, and contributes \(v_t C\) if it completes at time \(C\). The task is to produce a causal scheduling policy and its aggregate completion-time distribution, minimizing the population-normalized objective
\[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i C_i \]
against a clairvoyant offline benchmark. A computational version asks for a type-aggregated implementation, with running time in \(\tau\), the bit length \(L\), and the other instance parameters, rather than in the possibly enormous \(N\).

I expect the fluid counterpart of P-DLGM to be \(2\)-competitive, so this is a Class A candidate. The finite theorem supplies a strong starting point; the new work is to aggregate equivalent jobs and establish the guarantee and computation cost directly from the distributional input. Further questions include whether \(2\) is sharp in the limit, and whether a type-aggregated policy can attain the bound without expanding each mass into \(N\) individual jobs.

This mirror covers the preemptive scheduling result, not the thesis as a whole. It is plausible as the authors’ problem: it retains their job attributes, preemption, completion-time objective, and online allocation decision. The weakest point is the strategic layer. Theorem 4 relies on individual jobs’ best responses; when each agent has infinitesimal mass, a job’s effect on the allocation and payments may disappear. So I would present this as a mirror of the underlying online scheduling problem and its algorithmic guarantee, not claim that the theorem’s equilibrium result automatically survives the continuum limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Chapter 5, Theorem 4 is a genuine computational result: P-DLGM gives a \(2\)-competitive bound for preemptive online scheduling. So I cannot make the negative case by claiming there is no result to mirror. The strongest objection is that the proposed limit does not preserve the theorem’s central object.

P-DLGM is a mechanism for individual jobs. Each job chooses a node; its priority affects the queue there; and its payment compensates particular jobs it displaces. The \(2\)-competitive theorem is proved for that job-by-job process in a myopic best-response equilibrium. A fluid version has to choose what “mass of jobs” means at this level. If infinitesimal jobs take the place of individuals, an agent’s report no longer changes the aggregate schedule in the same way. If a whole cohort acts as one mass, it can affect the schedule, but it is now a coordinated bloc rather than the paper’s individual job. Either way, the equilibrium and compensation structure behind Theorem 4 needs a new model and a new theorem.

The proponent’s scaling of processor supply with job count is permitted, and recurring job templates with common release times, runtimes, and waiting costs are conceivable. The better formulation would make cohort arrivals genuinely unknown until they occur; giving the scheduler the full distribution \(\mu\), including future release times and cohort masses, risks turning the online problem into one with advance knowledge of aggregate demand. Even after that correction, however, the result would be a fluid online scheduling problem. It could be worth studying, but it would no longer be a direct continuization of the paper’s strategic mechanism. Dropping the strategic layer to retain only an aggregate schedule and a competitive ratio leaves a different contribution to establish.

That objection does not show that no worthwhile mirror exists. A distribution over job templates and a proportionally scaled resource pool can define a sensible population-level scheduling problem; the dissertation’s focus on online scheduling makes it plausible that the authors would recognize it. The negative case is therefore weak at its hardest point: it can argue that the proposed mirror does not carry Theorem 4 over intact, but it cannot rule out a worthwhile fluid scheduling mirror with a newly formulated strategic or algorithmic result.
