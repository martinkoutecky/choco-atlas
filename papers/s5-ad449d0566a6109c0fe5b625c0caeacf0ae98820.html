The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), a finite support \(R\) of ranking types of length at least two, rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), compute the paper’s unweighted consensus scores over the population. For each item \(x\) and ordered pair \(x,y\), let \(p_x=\sum_{r:x\in r}\mu_r\) and \(p_{xy}=\sum_{r:x\text{ precedes }y\text{ in }r}\mu_r\). For each type \(r\), count its listed items with \(p_x\ge\alpha\) and its listed ordered pairs with \(p_{xy}\ge\alpha\), normalize by the numbers of items and pairs in \(r\) to obtain \(\kappa_1^r(\alpha)\) and \(\kappa_2^r(\alpha)\), and return \(K_i(\alpha)=\sum_{r\in R}\mu_r\kappa_i^r(\alpha)\) for \(i\in\{1,2\}\), together with the supported pattern sets.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite distribution over complete ranking types, including each type’s ranked subset when rankings are incomplete. Aggregate mass for each item and ordered pair, threshold those masses at \(\alpha\), and return each type’s supported-pattern fractions and their mass-weighted averages.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s unweighted individual scores and their overall averages. It leaves the position and gap weighted scores, outlier detection, and experimental findings unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a continuous version of the paper’s unweighted \(q\)-support consensus calculation. It is a natural high-multiplicity model: in the paper’s Mechanical Turk experiments, roughly 800 people rank four candidates, so there are at most 24 complete ranking types. A type is one complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of participants with that ranking. This is a concrete regime where the population is large and the number of distinct types is small.

My lead question is Continuous \(q\)-Support Consensus Evaluation. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a finite support \(R\) of ranking types \(r\) (each ranking at least two candidates), rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to 1, and a rational threshold \(\alpha\in(0,1]\). For each item \(x\), let \(p_x=\sum_{r:x\in r}\mu_r\); for each ordered pair \(x,y\), let \(p_{xy}=\sum_{r:x\text{ precedes }y\text{ in }r}\mu_r\). For each type \(r\), count its listed items with \(p_x\ge\alpha\), and its listed ordered pairs with \(p_{xy}\ge\alpha\). Divide those counts by the number of items and the number of pairs in \(r\), respectively, to obtain \(\kappa_1^r(\alpha)\) and \(\kappa_2^r(\alpha)\). The task is to output
\[ K_i(\alpha)=\sum_{r\in R}\mu_r\kappa_i^r(\alpha),\qquad i\in\{1,2\}. \]
The supported item and pair sets can also be returned. When \(\mu_r\) is the fraction of an electorate with ranking \(r\), and \(\alpha=q/N\), these quantities recover the paper’s scores for that finite electorate.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It defines \(\pi(x,r)\) as the position of \(x\) in ranking \(r\), or zero if \(x\) is absent. Its function \(f(x,y)\) counts rankings containing \(x\) when \(x=y\), and rankings containing \(x\) before \(y\) when \(x\ne y\). For a particular ranking \(r_l=(r_{l_1},\ldots,r_{l_m})\), it forms a matrix with entry \(A^{r_l}[j,i]=1\) exactly when \(i\le j\) and \(f(r_{l_i},r_{l_j})\ge q\), and zero otherwise. The theorem states that the individual scores are
\[ \kappa_1^{r_l}(q)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A^{r_l})}{m}, \qquad \kappa_2^{r_l}(q)= \frac{\mathbf e^T A^{r_l}\mathbf e-\operatorname{tr}(A^{r_l})}{m(m-1)/2}. \]
The paper then uses this matrix representation in its consensus-calculation algorithm. This is a named computational characterization, not a theorem claiming a complexity class.

The continuous question should be tractable: scan the supported rankings to accumulate the mass of each item and ordered pair, then count which of each ranking’s patterns reach \(\alpha\). This takes polynomial time in the number of candidates \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), for example \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations. That is my complexity expectation for the mirror; the paper does not state this bound. The authors should recognize the model as theirs: their \(q\)-support is already a threshold on how many rankings contain a pattern, and their experiments discuss \(q/N\). Replacing counts by population mass and the unweighted average over rankings by a mass-weighted average preserves the consensus question. The paper’s finite ranking list is still genuinely distinct from an input distribution with arbitrary rational masses.

The scope here is just Theorem 1’s unweighted individual scores and their overall averages. It does not claim to mirror the weighted scores or outlier method. Further questions include threshold evaluation—whether \(K_2(\alpha)\ge\beta\)—and finding every threshold value at which the supported pattern sets change.

The weak point is that the paper supplies no named hardness result or named complexity-class theorem. Theorem 1 is a formula used by an algorithm, so this mirror gives a clear tractable computational question, but it cannot support the programme’s central test of whether a discrete hardness result dissolves in the continuum. The case is strong on model fit and the high-multiplicity regime, and modest on what it contributes to a complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is Theorem 1, and it is not a complexity theorem: it identifies the paper’s \(q\)-support counts with entries of a matrix. The paper also gives an algorithm, so there is a computational task to mirror, but it does not establish a complexity classification or a result whose tractability depends on a difficult subproblem.

The strongest continuous version is clear and sensible. Treat each complete ranking—including its ranked subset in the incomplete-ranking setting—as a type with rational mass, and replace “at least \(q\) of \(N\) rankings” by “at least an \(\alpha\) fraction of the population.” Average each type’s score by its mass. The paper’s Mechanical Turk data, with roughly 800 people ranking four candidates, is an especially convincing high-multiplicity setting: there are at most 24 complete ranking types. The authors’ use of \(q/N\) makes this a natural extension, and neither type aggregation nor loss of individual identities is a serious objection here.

The negative case is about what this mirror contributes. Its best version is a weighted form of the same descriptive statistic: accumulate mass for each item and ordered pair, threshold those totals, and count the supported patterns in each type. The position-weighted scores replace averages by mass-weighted averages; the outlier scores can be reported per type. These are all direct extensions of the paper’s definitions. The matrix in Theorem 1 organizes those counts, but the mirror has no evident optimization or search problem for continuous-optimization methods to unlock. It looks like a useful weighted-data formulation, but a small step from a frequency table to a probability distribution rather than a new complexity question in the programme’s central sense.

That is the strongest case against spending ChoCo effort on this anchor: the paper studies consensus measurement and outlier detection, not a rule, strategic intervention, or other decision problem, and its continuous formulation appears to add little computational structure. But this does not establish that the mirror is unworthy. The high-multiplicity regime is credible, the authors would likely recognize the question, and a simple or already-understood answer cannot by itself defeat a well-posed mirror. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case stick here; the proponent’s anchor survives, though its contribution to a complexity landscape may be modest.
