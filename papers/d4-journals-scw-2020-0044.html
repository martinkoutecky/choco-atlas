The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. It has numbered Results 1–5, but they report experimental findings about contributions, price suggestions, and decision quality. For example, Result 3 finds evidence that voice increases public-good contributions, especially in hierarchical organizations; Result 4a finds evidence that voting worsens individual price suggestions. Neither is a result about the complexity or algorithms for a named computational problem. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition of the kind required here.

The most sympathetic population-level analogy would be a large organization whose members fall into complete behavioral and informational types, with each type assigned a mass. One could model access to voting or discussion and individual contribution choices, then ask how governance affects average contribution or payoff. But that would turn the paper’s experimental question into a new model of institutional design; the paper does not formulate or solve that optimization problem. Its experimental groups have five members, and repeating decisions over twenty periods does not make the population high-multiplicity.

So I cannot give a continuous problem per anchor or assign it a tractability, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness expectation without inventing a computational result. Any such mirror would be a proposed follow-up inspired by the paper, not a continuization of one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for identifies no anchor to continuize. That is the central problem for any positive verdict: Results 1–5 are empirical findings about treatment effects, not complexity results about a named computational problem. The paper asks whether changing voting and voice arrangements changes contributions, price suggestions, and outcomes in a controlled experiment. It gives no algorithmic task, instance representation, or complexity claim for a continuous mirror to inherit.

The strongest proposed mirror would place a large organization into finitely many behavioral and informational types, represent each type by its population share, and ask how vote and voice affect aggregate contributions or payoffs. That is a sensible research model. To turn it into a computational problem, though, one must add an objective such as choosing the governance arrangement that maximizes welfare, or specify an equilibrium problem over strategies. The paper does neither. Its four treatments are experimentally assigned conditions for estimating causal effects; optimizing over them would be a new institutional-design problem, not a continuization of a result in this paper.

The high-multiplicity setting is plausible for some organizations, so the objection is not that mass is inherently meaningless. The difficulty is preserving the paper’s mechanisms and experimental object. Subjects make decisions over twenty periods, learn from outcomes, and sometimes communicate with specific group members while competing against another group. A type complete enough to capture those differences would need to encode behavioral traits, information, and evolving histories; a small fixed type space would risk suppressing the heterogeneity the experiment is designed to measure. One could make a tractable model by simplifying these features, but that would supply new assumptions and questions.

Result 4a is the strongest candidate for a mechanism-level mirror: voting may weaken information-processing effort because each person is unlikely to be pivotal. Yet its experimental comparison involves groups of five. In a nonatomic population, an individual ballot has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate decision, so the pivotality mechanism being studied changes fundamentally. A model could add bloc voting, sampling, or a finite deliberation network, but those additions would define a different problem. The voice result may still inspire continuous-population work, but the paper reports an empirical effect; it does not pose a computational question about how to produce or optimize it.

So no anchor from the proponent survives, because none was offered. The negative case is strongest for this paper’s fit to the programme: it contributes experimental evidence, not a computational result with a population input that can be continuized. It cannot establish that no worthwhile algorithmic model could ever be built from the topic. That broader claim is the weak point: a future paper on large organizations could formulate one, but it would be new work rather than a mirror of this paper.
