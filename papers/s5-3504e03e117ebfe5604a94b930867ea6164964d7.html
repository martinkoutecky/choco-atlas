What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 gives a finite-population mechanism nonuniqueness result; Theorem 2 gives a non-domination result; Theorems 3 and 4 give mechanism characterizations. None supplies a computational complexity result with a surviving population-continuum analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no named result establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Its main results concern mechanism properties.

The closest hook is Theorem 2, proved here: on the acceptable domain, if \(g\) is ex-post efficient, strategy-proof, and boundedly invariant, then no strategy-proof, boundedly invariant mechanism stochastically dominates \(g\). Theorem 1, also proved here, shows that with at least five agents there are ex-post efficient, symmetric, strategy-proof mechanisms that are not welfare-equivalent to RSD. These are meaningful results, but neither classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 4’s acyclicity characterization of when DA has certain properties is likewise an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible *unanchored extension*: model a large school-choice population by masses \(\mu_t\) of students with each strict ranking type \(t\), scale school capacities to per-capita supplies \(q_a\), and study type-conditional assignment probabilities \(x_{t,a}\). One could ask whether a suitably defined continuous analogue of RSD can be stochastically dominated while preserving strategy-proofness and bounded invariance. The paper’s school-choice motivation makes that setting recognizable, and capacity scaling is a natural co-modification. But defining continuum RSD, individual deviations, and ex-post efficiency would require substantial new work. The resulting question would be axiomatic, outside ChoCo’s computational focus; the paper gives no basis to classify it as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

So the strongest honest positive point is that the paper suggests a sensible population-level research direction. It does not supply the named computational anchor or a precise, anchor-backed continuous problem the requested case requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves nonuniqueness of RSD under axioms; Theorem 2 proves a nondomination claim about whole mechanisms; Theorems 3 and 4 characterize mechanisms by axioms and priority structure. None states the complexity of a search, decision, or optimization problem. A continuous version of one of these could be a worthwhile economic theorem, but it would not be the kind of computational mirror ChoCo is looking for.

That objection does not depend on denying that high multiplicity makes sense here. A large school-choice market with recurring student preference types and capacities scaled per capita is a credible regime. Fractional type-level assignment probabilities are also a natural representation. Those choices clear the model’s plausibility gate; they do not turn the paper’s conclusions into computational questions.

The best mirror of Theorem 2 would define mass allocations for those preference types and ask whether a continuous analogue of RSD can be stochastically dominated while preserving the relevant properties. But Theorem 2 compares mechanisms as mappings over the entire preference-profile domain. It does not ask what allocation to compute for a given society, nor optimize a specified cost or gain on that society. To turn it into an algorithmic problem, one would have to supply a new input representation and objective—for example, a finite family of mechanisms and a metric for improvement. That could motivate new research, but the paper does not anchor those choices.

Strategy-proofness makes the gap more fundamental than a matter of notation. In an atomless society, one person’s report changes no aggregate mass. Retaining the paper’s individual incentive notion therefore calls for a distinguished agent whose report can affect their own assignment; replacing individual reports with positive-mass deviations instead changes the incentive property to a coalition or group notion. Either version could be studied, but each yields an axiomatic extension, not a computational result already posed by the paper.

The strongest repair for Theorem 1 would scale its five special preference roles to positive-mass groups and replace its finite assignment swaps with mass reallocations. That may preserve a sensible nonuniqueness question, but its conclusion would still be the existence of a mechanism satisfying axioms. Theorem 4’s DA result similarly invites a continuous model with priority types and per-capita capacities, but its stated contribution remains an equivalence between axioms and acyclicity. Neither is a complexity classification. Checking a finite priority structure for cycles would be a new recognition task, not a computational result claimed by this paper.

So I would not argue that no plausible continuous population exists, or that the continuous questions have already been answered. The negative case is narrower: the paper’s best candidates lead to continuous mechanism-design theorems, while making them computational requires adding a problem formulation that the paper does not provide. That is a strong case against a ChoCo mirror, though not proof that the authors could not find an axiomatic continuous extension worthwhile on its own.
