The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational interaction rates \(\lambda_{tu}\ge 0\), a seed-mass budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), and \(\epsilon>0\), consider populations of size \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\). For sufficiently large \(N\), put an independent directed edge from each type-\(t\) agent to each type-\(u\) agent with probability \(\lambda_{tu}/N\). For a feasible vector \(x\), where \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\), seed \(\lfloor Nx_t\rfloor\) agents of each type and let \(F(x)\) be the limiting expected fraction reached under IC as \(N\) grows along sizes with integral type counts. Can one compute a feasible \(x\) with \(F(x)\ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\max_{y:\,0\le y_t\le\mu_t,\,\sum_t y_t\le\kappa}F(y)\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\), independent of \(N\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-type sparse directed block network: the population is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) roles, \(\lambda_{tu}/N\) specifies independent IC edge probabilities, and the decision is a seed-mass vector \(x\) under budget \(\kappa\); maximize limiting expected reached mass.

The objection that survived

The mirror replaces topology-specific selection of a few vertices with type-level allocation of positive seed mass on a generated block network, so it does not capture the paper's arbitrary-network small-seed regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It mirrors the expected-influence objective and approximation goal of Theorem 3.1 in a finite-type random-network regime; it does not transfer that theorem's graph-size runtime guarantee, the early-termination tradeoff of Theorem 4.1, or the adjacency-list lower bound of Theorem 4.5.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the expected reached fraction \(F(x)\) have a tractable characterization for arbitrary finite type-interaction matrices, including regimes with giant reachable components?
  • Can the optimal seed-mass vector be approximated in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\), and with what finite-\(N\) convergence rate?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would base the positive case on large online communities with a modest number of recurring interaction roles: for example, heavy sharers, ordinary users, and mostly passive readers. Millions of people can share each role’s contact and forwarding rates even though their realized links differ. A type records those rates to every other role, so it is a complete description for this model. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population in role \(t\); the number of people is enormous while the number \(\tau\) of roles stays fixed or modest. A campaign budget stated as “seed at most \(\kappa\) of the population” is natural in this setting.

Here is the mirror I would ask someone to solve: Type-Mass Independent-Cascade Influence Maximization. An instance gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational nonnegative interaction rates \(\lambda_{tu}\), a seeding budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), and an accuracy parameter \(\epsilon>0\). For each population size \(N\), make \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type \(t\). Each directed pair from type \(t\) to type \(u\) has an independent live edge with probability \(\lambda_{tu}/N\). Choose a seed mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) for each type, with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\); in a finite population, seed that fraction of each type, rounded to an integer. Let \(F(x)\) be the limit, as \(N\) grows, of the expected fraction of agents reached by the IC cascade. The task is to output a feasible mass vector \(x\) whose \(F(x)\) is at least \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\) times \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty=\max F(x)\). The computational question is whether this can be done with probability at least \(3/5\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\), independent of \(N\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that there is a constant \(c\) such that, for every \(\epsilon\in(0,1/2)\), setting \(R=cmk\epsilon^{-2}\log n\) makes the paper’s algorithm return a seed set \(S\) with expected influence at least \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\), with probability at least \(3/5\), in runtime \(\Theta(R)=\Theta(mk\epsilon^{-2}\log n)\). The theorem is proved here. It anchors the same underlying question—choosing initial adopters to maximize expected IC reach—and its live-edge coverage structure is what makes the mass version credible. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness result to use as a comparable anchor. Its Theorem 4.5 is a lower bound for adjacency-list query access; I would not treat that as a hardness anchor for an input that explicitly gives type masses and interaction rates.

I expect the mass problem to be Class A. At every finite \(N\), IC influence is a monotone submodular coverage objective. Grouping exchangeable agents by type suggests a continuous diminishing-returns objective over seed masses, where continuous optimization could replace the discrete greedy choice. The central algorithmic question is whether the reverse-reachability information used in Theorem 3.1 can be estimated or priced from the type-level input in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). That is the part a prover would need to establish; Theorem 3.1 itself does not prove the compressed runtime bound. Further questions include how to handle large reverse-reachable components in the limit, whether the finite-population approximation converges at a useful rate, and where efficient type-level estimation stops being possible.

The mirror covers the paper’s main approximation result for IC influence maximization. It does not claim that the paper’s precise runtime bound transfers: the paper takes an explicit arbitrary graph and a budget of \(k\) vertices, while this mirror takes a compressed interaction model and a budget expressed as population mass. That proportionate budget is a co-modification that makes sense when the population itself is the continuous object.

The weakest point is that the type model changes the network input. It replaces a fixed arbitrary topology with a sparse, type-exchangeable random network. The authors would recognize the objective and diffusion process, but could reasonably regard this as a restricted generative-model version of their problem rather than a direct limit of every graph they study. If real users’ relevant network roles are so individualized that \(\tau\) grows with \(N\), the compression disappears. The positive case stands on the plausible regime where many agents genuinely share the same interaction law.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives an approximation algorithm for choosing seeds to maximize expected influence under the independent cascade model. Its input, though, is an arbitrary, already specified network. The algorithm’s work depends on that network’s particular reachability structure: it samples reverse-reachable sets from the given graph and builds a hypergraph from them.

That makes the proposed type-mass model a substantial change of problem. In a fixed network, two vertices are interchangeable only when their positions in the network make them indistinguishable for influence. A complete type may therefore need to encode global connectivity information, not just a forwarding role. On arbitrary graphs, those types can be nearly all distinct, so grouping by type may leave \(\tau\) close to \(n\). Grouping by coarser roles and pairwise interaction rates avoids that growth, but \(\lambda_{tu}/N\) then describes a sparse random block network. It does not summarize the fixed graph from Theorem 3.1. Networks with the same type masses and average rates can still have different bottlenecks and reachability.

A graphon or neighborhood-distribution version is a possible rescue, but it faces the same tradeoff. A coarse summary can lose global structure that determines cascades; a rich enough representation to preserve arbitrary sparse-network structure may carry essentially the original network’s complexity. Finite-rank kernels return to a restricted random-network family. That family may be a sensible subject in its own right, but the theorem’s near-linear algorithm and its graph-access problem no longer anchor its computational question.

The seeding budget creates a second gap. The paper permits a fixed \(k\), including a small number of individually chosen vertices. In a population continuum, those seeds have zero mass. Their influence can still be macroscopic, but it depends on which vertices they are and how they connect to the rest of the network—information a type-mass vector omits. Setting the budget to \(\kappa N\) gives seeds positive mass, but changes the decision regime from choosing a few influential individuals to allocating a population-scale campaign. That can be a reasonable co-modification, though it does not preserve the paper’s central seed-selection regime.

So the objection is not that the continuous answer might be easy, or that a type-based influence problem cannot be posed. It is that the strongest proposed mirror needs both a new network model and a new budget regime; more faithful versions risk retaining the identity and topology the continuum was meant to compress. I would not treat Theorem 4.5’s query lower bound as a separate hardness anchor here: the proponent is right that it concerns a particular access model.

This is a substantial case against this mirror as a direct continuization of Theorem 3.1, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. A type-based sparse random network with a proportionate campaign budget is plausible, and authors might recognize it as a useful influence-maximization variant. The negative case is weakest precisely there: its force depends on whether that changed model is considered a ChoCo mirror of their result or a different problem.
