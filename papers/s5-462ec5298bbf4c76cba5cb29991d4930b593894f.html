The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a scoring vector \(s_m\), a designated candidate \(c\), and a support of \(\tau\) partial-order types \(P\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_P\ge 0\) summing to one, where each type draws uniformly from its linear extensions \(\Omega(P)\), compute exactly as a rational \(E_\mu(c)=\sum_P\mu_P\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Omega(P))}[s_m(\pi^{-1}(c))]\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over partial-order voter types; each unit of mass of type \(P\) has the paper’s uniform distribution over \(\Omega(P)\). The per-capita expected score is \(E_\mu(c)=\sum_P\mu_P\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Omega(P))}[s_m(\pi^{-1}(c))]\), and the continuous MEW maximizes this score over candidates.

What the mirror covers

Covers the expected-score questions for partial-order types under plurality, veto, and \(k\)-approval, corresponding to Theorems 7–9; it leaves separate MEW-selection complexity and the RIM and partitioned-preference tractability results unclassified.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is a continuous-mass expected-score problem for partial-order voter types. A type \(P\) is a partial order over the candidates, together with the paper’s rule that its complete rankings are drawn uniformly from its linear extensions \(\Omega(P)\). A standardized survey could produce a large electorate in which many respondents report the same partial order: the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those reports, with \(\mu_P\) the fraction of the electorate of type \(P\), and only \(\tau\) types having positive mass. The meaningful regime is \(N\gg\tau\), for example a large survey with a limited set of response patterns.

For a scoring vector \(s_m\), define the per-capita expected score of candidate \(c\) by \(E_\mu(c)=\sum_P \mu_P\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Omega(P))}[s_m(\pi^{-1}(c))]\). The continuous MEW is the set of candidates maximizing \(E_\mu(c)\). The computational question I would hand to a prover is Continuous-Mass ESC: given the candidates, a support of partial-order types, binary-rational masses summing to one, a scoring rule, and a candidate \(c\), compute \(E_\mu(c)\) exactly as a rational. For a finite profile, setting \(\mu_P\) to the fraction of voters reporting \(P\) gives its expected score divided by \(n\); the winner set is unchanged. Thus this keeps the paper’s expected-score objective and makes the population itself the distribution over types.

My lead anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper: given a partial voting profile, a distinguished candidate, and plurality, computing that candidate’s expected score is FP\(^{\#P}\)-complete. Its continuous counterpart, Plurality-Mass ESC, asks for \(E_\mu(c)=\sum_P\mu_P\,\Pr_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Omega(P))}(c\text{ is ranked first in }\pi)\). It is FP\(^{\#P}\)-complete as well: the one-type instance \(\mu_P=1\) contains the one-partial-order instances used by the paper’s hardness proof, and computing each type’s rank probability uses a \(\#P\) oracle. This anchor is strongest because the mirror is direct and its hardness already holds at \(\tau=1\).

Two further anchors are worth including because they extend the same model to other standard scoring rules. Theorem 8, also proved here, says that expected-score computation from a partial voting profile for a distinguished candidate under veto is FP\(^{\#P}\)-complete. Veto-Mass ESC asks for \(E_\mu(c)=\sum_P\mu_P(1-\Pr_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Omega(P))}(c\text{ is ranked last in }\pi))\); its hardness transfers by the same single-type restriction. Theorem 9, proved here, says that the corresponding expected-score computation under \(k\)-approval is FP\(^{\#P}\)-complete. \(k\)-Approval-Mass ESC asks for \(E_\mu(c)=\sum_P\mu_P\Pr_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Omega(P))}(c\text{ is ranked in the top }k\text{ in }\pi)\); again the paper’s hard instances embed with mass one. Each problem returns the exact expected score, not just whether the candidate wins. In all three cases I expect hardness to transfer (Class B), not continuum-specific hardness: the reductions’ combinatorics lie in the alternatives and linear extensions, and survive without population multiplicity. The results cover these expected-score questions; they do not establish a separate complexity classification for MEW selection or mirror the paper’s RIM and partitioned-preference tractability theorems.

These questions also point to natural follow-ups: can the three expected scores be approximated efficiently under a useful margin promise, and do structural parameters of the partial orders—such as the cover width used in the paper’s RIM-based solver—yield tractable regimes when \(\tau\) is large? Small support alone cannot be expected to help these exact problems, since the hardness is already present at \(\tau=1\).

The main weakness is that this mirror is close to an explicit move in the paper: Section 8 groups identical votes into a weighted voting profile. Normalizing those integer weights to rational masses does not, by itself, give a new algorithm, and the hardness here does not dissolve under continuization. That makes this a narrow case for a continuous mirror rather than a major new tractability result. Still, the paper treats voters as individually indexed inputs and does not classify exact expected-score computation by type support and mass bit length. Its weighted grouping is good evidence that the authors would recognize the type-based population as their problem; the continuous question then records a meaningful hardness boundary for that regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against spending ChoCo’s effort here is that the proposed mirror has almost no population-level computation in it. For a partial-order type \(P\), let \(r_P(c)\) be the expected score of \(c\) under a uniform draw from its linear extensions. Then the continuous score is just
\[ E_\mu(c)=\sum_P \mu_P r_P(c). \]
Compute one rank marginal per supported type, take a rational weighted sum, and compare candidates. There is no interaction between masses, and no optimization over the society. The paper’s computational core—the rank-estimation problem—remains untouched.

That applies to each proponent anchor. Plurality-Mass ESC weights each type’s probability of ranking \(c\) first; Veto-Mass ESC weights its probability of ranking \(c\) last; \(k\)-Approval-Mass ESC weights the probability that \(c\) is in the top \(k\). They are three scoring functionals of the same per-type rank distribution, not three different population problems. A stronger mirror that makes each type include both the partial order and its preference model has the same structure: calculate that type’s expected score, then average across types. The paper already groups identical votes, which is evidence for this regime, though not a collision with a continuous-population treatment.

This is a case for low priority, not for dismissing the questions. The paper has a credible large-electorate scenario with repeated response types, and exact computation from rational masses, parameterized by support size and bit length, is a well-posed high-multiplicity question. For tractable type families, the existing per-voter algorithms suggest a direct weighted extension. But the continuous step adds only aggregation and rational arithmetic; it does not expose a new population-level algorithmic structure. The hard cases already occur with one type, while the tractable cases are handled type by type.

I can’t honestly defeat all three anchors as worthwhile questions under the programme’s stated standard. Their weakness is that they are one narrow mirror with little population-specific content, not that the regime is implausible, the answer is already known, or hardness at one type disqualifies the mirror. If the reader treats any sensible continuous question as enough, this paper should pass.
