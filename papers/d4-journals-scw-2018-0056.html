What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover Theorem 4.3 and Corollary 4.4; the paper's other equilibrium characterizations and results are also analytical, and no computational result is mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. It gives necessary and sufficient geometric conditions for nonconvergent Nash equilibria when \(c<1\): the extreme occupied positions must each contain two candidates, no position can contain more than two, paired candidates’ interior half-electorates must have a common length, and the endpoint and unpaired electorates must satisfy the stated length equalities and lower bounds. Corollary 4.4, also proved here, says such equilibria exist for every \(m\ge2\) except \(m=3\), and there are infinitely many when \(m\ge6\).

Those are named equilibrium results, but they are not named computational results: the paper does not claim a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Proposition 4.1 is attributed to Cox (1987) and characterizes convergent equilibria; it is analytical too. So none qualifies as an anchor under your rule.

There is a possible computational follow-up—given rational \(m,c\), decide whether a pure-strategy equilibrium exists and construct one—but for the paper’s uniform electorate, its results already settle that question directly. More fundamentally, the paper starts with a unit mass of voters distributed continuously over \([0,1]\). A mirror that makes the population continuous would largely restate its model. My honest positive case therefore stops here: the paper suggests an easy computational reframing of its equilibrium characterization, but supplies no eligible computational anchor for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest named result is Theorem 4.3, but it characterizes Nash equilibria analytically: for a fixed best-worst rule, it gives geometric conditions on candidate positions. Corollary 4.4 establishes existence, and Proposition 4.1 characterizes convergent equilibria (building on Cox). None states a computational problem or a complexity result to mirror.

For Theorem 4.3, one could ask an algorithm to find equilibria. But with the paper’s assumptions, that asks for an algorithmic presentation of a characterization over \(m\) and \(c\), not a continuization of the society: the electorate is already a unit-mass continuum on \([0,1]\), and scores are already calculated over that population. The same applies to Corollary 4.4. Rewriting the electorate as a measure over ideal points changes the notation, not the population model.

The best rescue is a new problem: given rational masses on \(\tau\) ideal-point types, compute or decide the existence of a pure-strategy equilibrium. That is a coherent computational question, and the paper’s treatment of the uniform electorate could serve as a benchmark. But the paper’s named results do not address that input family; its complexity would come from generalizing the model to arbitrary heterogeneous distributions. That could be worthwhile research, but it is a weak basis for calling this paper’s results continuous mirrors. The negative case is strongest for the anchors actually offered; it cannot rule out every useful computational extension of spatial electoral competition.
