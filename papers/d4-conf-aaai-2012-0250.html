The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper’s *model* has a plausible population mirror, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it. The numbered results are structural: Theorem 1 proves Pareto optimality for two agents; Theorem 6 proves it for every maxsum envy-free allocation under piecewise uniform valuations; Theorem 8 gives a three-agent, piecewise-constant example where no maxsum envy-free allocation is Pareto optimal; and Theorem 9 compares welfare under maxsum envy-free and equitable allocations. None asserts a complexity classification or presents a computational algorithm. Theorem 8 uses an LP in its proof to establish the example’s properties, but it is not a named result giving a formulation and computing with it. The algorithms discussed in connection with Cohler et al. are prior work, not this paper’s results.

A plausible mirror would take types to be normalized valuation-density functions over positions in a divisible resource, such as access time to a shared server. In a large population, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with type \(t\); each type receives a per-capita fractional allocation \(y_t(x)\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t y_t(x)\leq 1\). Envy-freeness compares each type’s value for its own allocation with its value for every other type’s allocation, and the objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t\int v_t(x)y_t(x)\,dx\). One could ask whether maxsum envy-free allocations are Pareto optimal for piecewise uniform types, and whether the failure exhibited by Theorem 8 persists for piecewise-constant types. That is a concrete population extension of the paper’s questions, in a regime where many users share a small number of valuation profiles.

The weak point is decisive under your anchor requirement: those questions mirror structural claims, not named computational results. So I would not present them as per-anchor continuizations or assign them a class in the programme’s trichotomy. The paper supports the plausibility of a continuous-population *model*, but not the required positive case about continuizing one of its computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is the one the proponent concedes: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 1 concerns Pareto optimality with two agents; Theorems 6 and 8 concern Pareto optimality under restricted valuations; Theorem 9 compares welfare under envy-freeness and equitability. Theorem 11 supplies an equilibrium existence result used in a proof. These are structural claims, not complexity bounds or algorithmic results. The algorithms mentioned are prior work, and the LP in Theorem 8’s proof is evidence for an example, not a computational result of this paper.

The proposed population model is coherent. Give valuation types masses \(\mu_t\), scale cake supply with population, and compare each type’s per-capita allocation with other types’ allocations. One could then ask whether the Pareto claims or welfare inequality persist. That makes the population continuous, but leaves the questions structural. The cake was already divisible; making the *population* a distribution does not turn these theorems into computational questions. Nor does the fact that the paper’s examples use two or three agents rule out a many-agent regime: it simply does not supply a computational anchor for one.

The best computational rescue is to ask for an algorithm for maxsum envy-free-and-Pareto-optimal allocations, an open problem the paper itself raises, or to study whether its structural claims can be decided from a finite-support type description. But that is a new algorithmic problem, not a continuization of a result established here. With finitely encoded piecewise-constant valuations, maxsum envy-free allocation already has a direct finite optimization formulation; the paper’s discussion of its algorithmic background points to earlier work. The Pareto constraint may create a worthwhile separate challenge, but the article does not solve or classify it.

There is a further wrinkle for the Pareto anchors: ordinary finite-agent Pareto optimality does not transfer literally to an atomless population, since improving one zero-mass individual need not affect aggregate feasibility. A type-level or positive-mass notion of improvement would be a sensible repair, so this does not defeat the proposed structural questions. It does underline that those questions need a new efficiency definition before they can even be compared with Theorems 1, 6, and 8.

So the negative case is strong for the task ChoCo sets: the proponent’s mirrors are plausible population continuizations, but none mirrors a computational result of this paper. It is not an airtight claim that no adjacent algorithmic problem could be worthwhile; the paper’s open problem is the natural place to look. That would be a new project built from the paper’s setting, rather than a computational mirror of its results.
