The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of house classes \(H\), a finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a type description \((e_t,\succ_t)\) giving each type's endowed class and strict incomplete preferences, let \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\). Given a feasible rational allocation \(y_{t,r}\) with \(\sum_r y_{t,r}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{t,h}=q_h\), decide whether there is no mass transfer \(z_{t,r,h}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_h z_{t,r,h}\leq y_{t,r}\), \(z_{t,r,h}=0\) unless \(h=r\) or \(h\succ_t r\), and \(\sum_{t,r}z_{t,r,h}=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\sum_{r,a}z_{t,r,a}\) for every \(h\), with \(\sum_{t,r,h:\,h\succ_t r}z_{t,r,h}>0\).

The model it lives in

Types record both the preference relation over house classes and the endowed class. The distribution \(\mu\) describes population mass; \(y_{t,r}\) describes the mass of type \(t\) receiving whole units of class \(r\), and \(z\) describes which mass joins a blocking coalition and where it goes. The conservation constraints preserve the coalition's endowment of every class, and positive strictly improving mass is exactly the blocking condition.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers membership in a mass core under the paper's blocking relation and offers a companion strong-core test. It leaves the TTC-over-completions characterization itself, the mechanism-design results in Theorems 1 and 2, and the efficiency and housing-allocation extensions without mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a mass analogue of Proposition 1 relate the mass core to an appropriate completion-and-TTC construction?
  • For finite high-multiplicity markets with fixed type support, what convergence or additive approximation relates the discrete core to the mass core?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a continuous version of the paper’s core, anchored by Proposition 1. It is proved in this paper, in Appendix A, adapting techniques from earlier work; it is not just a cited result. For every profile of incomplete, transitive, strict preferences, Proposition 1 says that the paper’s sequential completion procedure generates exactly the set of completions, and that
\[ \bigcap_{\hat{\succ}\in SC(\succ)} TTC(\hat{\succ}) \subseteq CS(\succ) \subseteq C(\succ) = \bigcup_{\hat{\succ}\in SC(\succ)} TTC(\hat{\succ}). \]
Here \(TTC(\hat{\succ})\) is the allocation produced by Top Trading Cycles under a completion. So the core consists exactly of the TTC outcomes over all completions; the strong core lies between their intersection and the core. This is a constructive characterization of the paper’s central stability object. The paper does not give a named polynomial-time, NP-hardness, or other complexity-class result, and Proposition 1 itself gives no polynomial runtime bound.

A plausible mirror is Mass-Core Membership for Indecisive Housing Markets. Consider a regional housing exchange with many incumbent households and repeated housing categories—for example, thousands of households and dozens of apartment classes. A type \(t\) records both a household’s incomplete, transitive, strict preference relation over the classes and the class \(e_t\) of the unit it owns. Two households share a type only if they agree on both. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of households of type \(t\), with support size \(\tau\); the supply of class \(h\) is \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\).

An allocation is a mass assignment \(y_{t,h}\): the fraction of type \(t\) receiving a whole unit of class \(h\). It must satisfy \(\sum_h y_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{t,h}=q_h\). The fractions describe how many households receive each class; they do not give any household a fractional apartment.

The decision problem is: given \(\mu\) and a feasible rational allocation \(y\), is \(y\) in the mass core? A mass coalition can be described by variables \(z_{t,r,h}\), the mass of type \(t\) currently receiving class \(r\) who join the coalition and receive class \(h\). Require \(\sum_h z_{t,r,h}\le y_{t,r}\); permit positive \(z_{t,r,h}\) only when \(h=r\) or \(h\succ_t r\); and, for every class \(h\), require
\[ \sum_{t,r} z_{t,r,h} = \sum_{t:e_t=h}\sum_{r,a}z_{t,r,a}. \]
The left side is the coalition’s allocation of class \(h\); the right side is the stock of that class owned by its members. Maximize \(\sum_{t,r,h:\,h\succ_t r}z_{t,r,h}\). The allocation is in the mass core exactly when this linear program’s optimum is zero. A positive optimum gives a blocking mass transfer; zero means none exists. This is a direct aggregation of the paper’s blocking condition, including the restriction that core-blocking members can compare their old and new houses.

I expect this membership question to be tractable: the program has \(O(\tau k^2)\) variables for \(k\) house classes, and rational linear programming gives a polynomial-time test in \(k,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The same construction gives a companion strong-core membership question by also permitting incomparable assignments, \(h\otimes_t r\).

The scenario is credible for these authors because it retains their setting’s key ingredients: endowed households, non-monetary exchange, incomplete preferences, and coalitional blocking. Grouping interchangeable units into repeated classes is a co-modification needed to describe a large population, while \(y\) records fractions of households receiving indivisible units. The type definition also makes the high-multiplicity assumption explicit instead of treating household differences as noise.

The weakest point is that this LP mirror does not follow as a complexity result from Proposition 1. Proposition 1 characterizes the entire finite core through completions and TTC; the proposed problem tests membership in a continuous core, and the finite proposition does not establish its tractability. The completion characterization may require enumerating many completions, and computing the full mass-core correspondence could be harder than checking one candidate allocation. That leaves worthwhile follow-up questions: whether a mass analogue of Proposition 1 holds, how to compute the full correspondence, and whether finite high-multiplicity markets converge to the mass-core answers. I would limit this case to the paper’s core characterization, not claim a continuous mirror for its mechanism-design results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that Proposition 1 is not a complexity result. It characterizes the finite core through completions and TTC, but gives no complexity classification or efficient algorithm. The proposed LP therefore does not continue the proposition’s computational content: it defines a new mass-blocking problem and tests that definition directly. Its polynomial size follows from listing the type-to-house flows, rather than from the paper’s completion characterization or a difficult pricing problem. That makes it a modest ChoCo result, not an evident research programme priority.

There is a related modelling cost. The mirror works best when many households own genuinely interchangeable units and share the same preference ordering over a small set of housing classes. That is a plausible regime, but it abstracts away the location and unit-specific features that often make housing exchange difficult. Richer types could make the support large, though that is a limitation of some instance regimes, not a reason to reject the model outright.

Those objections do not defeat the anchor. The proposed LP appears to encode mass blocking correctly: its stock constraints preserve the coalition members’ endowments, and positive objective is equivalent to positive mass strictly improving. Under the programme’s accepted co-modification for repeated indivisible units, the model is coherent. A stronger question—whether a mass analogue of the completion/TTC characterization holds—also remains meaningful. I therefore can’t honestly sustain the universal negative claim: the best case against is that this is a direct, probably easy mirror whose value may be limited, not that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
