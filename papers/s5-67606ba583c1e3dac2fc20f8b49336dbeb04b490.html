The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(H\), rational capacities \(q_h\) with \(\sum_{h\in H}q_h=1\), a finite complete type set \(T\), rational mass distribution \(\mu\), public priority orders over types, and strict school rankings for each type, define weighted continuum TTC by transferring maximal feasible residual mass around cycles to obtain \(x_{t,h}\). Determine, as a function of \(m=|H|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and encoding length \(L\), the minimum worst-case certificate length \(\log_2|\mathcal C|\) allowing every type \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\) to recover \(x_{t,\cdot}/\mu_t\) from its own ranking and a certificate \(C\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity school-choice market with finite complete types \(T\), mass \(\mu\), per-capita capacities \(q_h\), and public priorities over types; weighted TTC produces mass allocation \(x_{t,h}\), and a blackboard certificate lets each positive-mass type recover its conditional allocation from its own ranking and the certificate.

The objection that survived

The paper's decoder is explicitly addressed to a named applicant with a private report, whereas the proposed \(D_t\) returns a type-level conditional allocation; equivalence requires an explicit exchangeability, tie-breaking, and information model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the TTC representation result and the DA verification result, including their type-level certificate and local-checking analogues. It leaves the one-applicant outcome-effect and options-effect results largely untouched because a zero-mass individual must be replaced by a positive-mass cohort.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can weighted TTC be computed and its type-level allocation represented in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) for rational data?
  • What is the exact certificate complexity when \(\tau\ll m^2\), and when can the \(m^2\)-bit gadget be represented by repeated types rather than distinct roles?
  • Does a weighted DA analogue of the improvement-graph certificate verify the extremal allocation with complexity polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity school-choice mirror of the paper’s representation and verification problems. I would lead with Theorem 5.5, and use Theorem 6.5 as a second, algorithmically cleaner anchor. I would not claim that this paper supplies an NP-hardness or polynomial-time anchor: its named results concern communication and structural complexity, not ordinary running-time classes. That is the principal limitation of the case.

The regime is a large school district with \(N\) applicants, \(m\) schools, and \(N\gg m\). A type \(t\) completely specifies an applicant’s school ranking and all priority-relevant attributes, such as distance category, sibling status, or lottery rank. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(t\). School capacities are population shares \(q_h\), rather than fixed integer capacities. Every individual applicant still receives one integral school assignment; only the aggregate allocation \(x_{t,h}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(h\), is fractional.

This is not merely fractionalising the outcome space. The population itself is the continuous object, while school supplies scale with population. It is also exactly the regime the authors already motivate in Section 5: many more applicants than institutions, with public priority information. A plausible instance is a large annual cohort of students whose preferences and priority attributes fall into a relatively small number of recurring profiles.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.5, proved in this paper, although its upper bound uses the prior result [LL21, Theorem 1]. It states that the concurrent representation complexity of TTC is \(\widetilde{\Theta}(\min\{|A|,|I|^2\})\). Here a blackboard certificate must allow every applicant, using the certificate, her own preferences, and the publicly known priorities, to determine her match. The upper bound is \(\widetilde O(|A|)\) by writing down the matching and \(\widetilde O(|I|^2)\) through the cutoff representation of [LL21]. The paper proves the matching lower bound: with \(k^2\) top applicants \(d^T_{i,j}\), left and right applicants \(d^L_{i,j},d^R_{i,j}\), and \(k\) top, left, and right institutions, the preferences of the \(d^T_{i,j}\) encode arbitrary bits \(b_{i,j}\in\{L,R\}\). The TTC outcome forces the corresponding \(d^L_{j,i}\) or \(d^R_{j,i}\) to receive the top institution, so every certificate must reveal all \(k^2\) bits. Thus the lower bound is \(\Omega(|I|^2)\), and padding or ignoring surplus applicants gives the stated \(\widetilde{\Theta}(\min\{|A|,|I|^2\})\) bound.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

Continuous TTC Type-Representation. Given a finite school set \(H\), capacities \(q_h\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), public priority orders over types, and strict school preferences for every type, compute the continuum-TTC allocation \(x\). A continuum-TTC run operates on residual masses: each type points to its favourite remaining school, each school points to its highest-priority remaining type, and a directed cycle transfers the largest feasible common mass around that cycle. The output is the aggregate allocation \(x_{t,h}\), while each unit of mass receives one school.

More structurally, fix \(T\), \(H\), and the priorities. A type-local representation protocol consists of a finite codebook \(\mathcal C\) and decoders \(D_t\) such that, for every rational population distribution \(\mu\), some certificate \(C\in\mathcal C\) lets type \(t\) recover its conditional allocation \(x_{t,\cdot}/\mu_t\) from its own preference order and \(C\). Given a bound \(B\), the question is whether such a protocol exists with \(\log_2|\mathcal C|\le B\), and, if so, to produce it.

I expect the aggregate allocation problem to be tractable for fixed \(|T|=\tau\): it is a finite mass-flow process, or equivalently a structured transportation problem, with complexity polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. The interesting question is the dependence of the smallest certificate on \(m\) and \(\tau\). When \(\tau\) is small, the paper’s \(\Omega(m^2)\) gadget cannot be reproduced, because its \(m^2\) independent bits are carried by \(m^2\) distinct role types. When \(\tau=\Theta(m^2)\), however, the same construction can be populated by arbitrarily large cohorts of each role type, so the lower bound becomes a type-dimensional lower bound rather than a population-size lower bound. This makes the problem a plausible Class A/Class B boundary: population multiplicity may collapse the dependence on \(N\), while the agenda and type complexity can still preserve the TTC obstruction.

The second anchor is Theorem 6.5, proved in the paper. It states that the verification complexity of both applicant-proposing and institution-proposing deferred acceptance is \(\widetilde{\Theta}(|A|)\). The lower bound comes from Proposition 6.2, which shows \(\Omega(|A|)\) bits are necessary even with only \(O(1)\) institutions, by reducing verification to counting \(k\) private bits. The upper bound uses Lemma 6.7: a proposed matching equals IPDA exactly when it is stable and the institution-side improvement graph is acyclic; the applicant-side dual characterizes APDA. Lemma 6.8 shows that applicants can locally verify the graph using the proposed matching, the public priorities, and their own preferences. The certificate therefore contains the matching and improvement graph and has \(\widetilde O(|A|)\) bits.

The corresponding continuous question is:

Continuum DA Outcome Verification. Given a finite type distribution \(\mu\), school capacities \(q_h\), public priority orders over types, type preferences, and a proposed mass allocation \(\widehat x\), decide whether \(\widehat x\) is exactly the applicant-proposing or institution-proposing DA allocation. If it is, output a type-level certificate consisting of school cutoffs, rejected-mass information, or a compressed improvement graph. Every positive-mass type must be able to verify its own local condition, while aggregate capacity and mass-conservation constraints verify the population-level feasibility.

This is a natural Class A candidate. The individual lower bound \(\Omega(|A|)\) should become a bound in \(\tau\), \(m\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\), because identical applicants no longer require separate checks. A DA mass algorithm can sort the finite types by priority, compute cutoff types and partial boundary masses, and verify stability and the extremal DA outcome in polynomial time. The core research questions are the exact certificate complexity in \((m,\tau,L)\), whether the improvement-graph proof survives partial type masses without extra information, and how rational finite-population instances converge to the continuum certificate.

I would not claim that Theorems 3.8 or 4.3 automatically transfer. Their constructions concern how one named applicant changes an entire matching or another applicant’s menu. In an atomless society, one individual has zero mass, so the honest analogue must replace the named applicant by a positive-mass cohort. That is a meaningful co-modification, but it needs its own theorem.

The weakest point is therefore clear: the paper has no standard NP-hardness, P-membership, FPT, or approximation theorem. Moreover, the TTC mirror requires a carefully defined mass-TTC semantics and its strongest lower bound only survives when the number of distinct types grows with the number of institutions. The positive case nevertheless survives on the DA side: the authors explicitly study large applicant populations, many-to-one capacities, public priorities, cutoff representations, and verification. Replacing named applicants by a finite distribution of complete applicant types is therefore a genuine high-multiplicity version of their matching problem, not an unrelated fractional matching exercise.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that every named complexity measure in this paper is identity-sensitive. The paper is not measuring the complexity of an allocation \(x\); it measures the information needed for a named applicant to recover her own match or to check a certificate using her own private report.

That defeats the proposed reading of Theorem 5.5. Its representation protocol requires a decoder \(D_d(P_d,C)\) that returns the particular applicant \(d\)'s match. In a continuum society, two members of one type have identical inputs to such a decoder. Yet a feasible allocation can split their mass. For example, if one type fills a unit mass, ranks \(h_1\succ h_2\), and the two schools jointly have capacities \(1/2\) and \(1/2\), then \(x_{t,h_1}=x_{t,h_2}=1/2\). No type-only certificate can tell one member which school she personally receives.

The proposed replacement, \(x_{t,\cdot}/\mu_t\), is therefore not a representation of each applicant’s match. It is a conditional aggregate allocation. That may be a sensible new matching problem, but it discards precisely the object whose communication complexity Theorem 5.5 studies. To recover individual assignments, one must add an individual quantile, lottery rank, or tie-breaking coordinate \(u\). Then the effective type is \((t,u)\), and the model has reintroduced a continuum of identities rather than a finite high-multiplicity type space. If one instead interprets \(x_{t,\cdot}/\mu_t\) as a lottery, the question has become one about randomized or fractional outcomes.

The lower-bound gadget does not repair this defect. The \(k^2\) independent bits in Theorem 5.5 are carried by \(k^2\) separately addressable roles \(d^T_{i,j}\), and the other agents are distinguished by their priority positions. Replicating each role \(M\) times gives a high-multiplicity population, but it does not make the certificate problem more continuous: the certificate still records the same \(k^2\) type-level distinctions, independently of \(M\). If the roles are merged into fewer types, the gadget loses the very priority and preference distinctions that make the lower bound work. Thus the only faithful version is a type-indexed communication problem whose difficulty is governed by the number of distinct roles, not by population continuization.

The same problem is sharper for Theorem 6.5. Proposition 6.2 obtains its \(\Omega(|A|)\) bound from \(k\) named applicants holding independent private bits. In a distributional model, either the population distribution \(\mu\) is public, in which case the verifier can compute the aggregate DA outcome directly, or the bits are retained as privately held information by \(k\) distinguishable priority types. In the latter case, the lower bound is a bound in the number of types, not in population mass. Duplicating every type produces no additional verification burden: all copies perform the same check.

The improvement-graph proof has the same fate. Its vertices are individual applicants and its acyclicity condition characterizes the exact extremal stable matching. A type-level version would replace those vertices by weighted types, boundary masses, and aggregate flow constraints. That could be defined, but it is a new certificate for a weighted stable-allocation problem. It is not a continuization of the paper’s applicant-by-applicant verification task. Moreover, once \(\mu\) and the type data are centrally available, checking the weighted DA outcome is an ordinary finite computation; the paper’s distributed blackboard problem has disappeared.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer would be easy, nor an objection based on existing high-multiplicity work. The issue is that the proposed mirrors change the information being represented and verified. The aggregate versions may be worthwhile new problems, but neither Theorem 5.5 nor Theorem 6.5 supplies a faithful computational mirror of the paper’s actual results.

The negative case is nevertheless not airtight. If the programme explicitly accepts “type-level conditional allocation” as the intended replacement for an individual matching, and accepts a weighted TTC/DA semantics with type priorities and fractional boundary assignments, then Theorem 5.5 yields a defensible high-multiplicity research question. I cannot honestly claim that no such question could be valuable. What can be defended is the narrower conclusion that the proponent has not shown a continuous mirror of these named results; they have proposed a new aggregate matching-and-certificate theory inspired by them.
