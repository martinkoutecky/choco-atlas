The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population version of the paper’s subject, but I cannot give it a qualifying anchor. The paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: a rule satisfies strong efficiency, unanimity-basedness, and strategy-proofness if and only if it is a weak-veto rule. Proposition 2, also proved here, says that when \(m=3\), every K-weak-veto rule is K-strategy-proof; Proposition 3 says strong efficiency and K-efficiency are equivalent when \(m=3\); Corollary 1 combines those properties for K-weak-veto rules. These are axiomatic results, not complexity classifications. The introduction’s mention of LP simulations for small cases does not supply a named computational result either.

The most credible continuous setting would be a large membership organization with many members but relatively few distinct complete rankings over its alternatives. A type is a ranking \(R\); \(\mu_R\) is the fraction of members holding it; and \(U(\mu)=\bigcap_{\mu_R>0}R\) records their unanimous comparisons. Given the paper’s exogenous priority order \(o\) over social rankings, the weak-veto outcome is the highest-priority ranking \(R\) satisfying \(R\supseteq U(\mu)\). That is a recognizable population version of the paper’s rule.

But it is only a candidate computational question, not a result the paper establishes. Its outcome depends on which types have positive mass, not on their masses. Reweighting the same support leaves the outcome unchanged. And a nonatomic individual has no effect on the population, so the paper’s individual strategy-proofness question does not carry over directly. One could ask how to compute the weak-veto outcome from a support list and a specified representation of \(o\), but the paper does not study that problem, and its complexity would depend on how \(o\) is encoded.

So the positive case is limited: the population model is natural for preference aggregation, and the weak-veto rule has a direct definition over it. There is no honest way to supply one continuous problem per qualifying anchor, or to identify a lead anchor, because there are none.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes weak-veto rules by axioms; Proposition 2 establishes K-strategy-proofness for a subclass when \(m=3\); Proposition 3 equates two efficiency axioms in that case; and Corollary 1 combines those properties. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Extending these statements to distributions over rankings would ask new axiomatic questions, outside ChoCo’s scope. So the proponent’s named results do not supply a qualifying anchor.

A large membership organization with repeated ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. For its distribution \(\mu\), the direct extension picks the highest-priority ranking \(R\) in \(o\) with \(R\supseteq U(\mu)\). But this rule sees only which types have positive mass: changing their proportions without changing the support changes nothing. With full support, \(U(\mu)\) contains only the reflexive comparisons, so the rule returns the first ranking in \(o\). And adding an arbitrarily small positive mass to a previously absent type can change the unanimous relation and potentially the outcome. The resulting rule is a discontinuous function of the population shares, with no positive mass threshold for robustness.

That also defeats the more promising computational framings. Computing the direct extension with an explicitly listed \(o\) is just a scan of the list, checking each ranking against \(U(\mu)\); the masses do not create the algorithmic problem. If \(o\) is given compactly, the difficulty lies in optimizing over rankings under that encoding, not in continuizing the population. Likewise, the infimum mass transfer needed to change the support can be zero, so a mass-based robustness question is trivial unless one adds a threshold. A threshold version may be worth studying, but it changes the unanimity condition and is a new computational model, not a mirror of Theorem 1.

Individual strategy-proofness does not rescue the K-based results: in a nonatomic society, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot affect the support, making individual strategy-proofness vacuous. A positive-mass group’s deviation is a different, coalition-level question. This makes Proposition 2 and Corollary 1 poor candidates for continuous computational anchors as well; Proposition 3 remains an axiomatic equivalence.

So the case against a ChoCo mirror is strong: the paper’s results are axiomatic, and the direct population extension either ignores mass or requires new structure to become computationally interesting. It is not a proof that no worthwhile project could grow from these rules, especially if one deliberately designs a mass-sensitive variant. That would be follow-on work rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
