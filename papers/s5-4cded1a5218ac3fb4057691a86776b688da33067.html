What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Proposition 3's RR1 and RA1 non-manipulability claim when a Condorcet preference winner exists; it leaves the other systems, domains, and results outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered result in this paper that classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or parameterized-hard. So, under a strict reading of the requested anchor rule, there is no qualifying complexity anchor. The closest result worth mirroring is Proposition 3, proved in this paper in Appendix B. It is a game-theoretic non-manipulability result, not a complexity theorem.

Proposition 3 says that for any number \(m\) of candidates, if the true electorate profile has a Condorcet preference winner \(A\), then RR1 and RA1 are non-manipulable even when preference cycles exist among the other candidates. Under RR1, voters rank candidates sincerely on the basic ranking and put \(A\) first on the electorate ranking; the order below \(A\) may be arbitrary. Under RA1, voters rank sincerely and use Rule 1 for approvals: they approve \(A\) and everyone they prefer to \(A\), unless \(A\) is last, in which case they approve every candidate except \(A\). If all other preference groups follow these base strategies, no one group can change the winner from \(A\) to a candidate that group prefers to \(A\), whatever ballot strategy it adopts.

A direct continuous mirror is RR1/RA1 Mass Manipulation. Its instance is a candidate set \(C\), a support \(T\) of complete strict preference rankings, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a selected type \(t^\dagger\in T\), and a rule \(R\in\{\mathrm{RR1},\mathrm{RA1}\}\). Require that the profile has a Condorcet preference winner \(A\), and use the paper’s no-tie conventions. A type is a ranking; its mass is the fraction of society with that ranking. All types other than \(t^\dagger\) vote by the base strategy above. The decision variable is an allocation \(z_b\) of the selected type’s mass across legal ballots \(b\), with \(\sum_b z_b=\mu_{t^\dagger}\); allowing a split across ballots represents any part of the type deviating while the rest stays with the base ballot. Replace every voter count in the paper’s aggregation procedure by the corresponding mass-weighted sum. The question is whether some such allocation makes the winner \(w\) satisfy \(w\succ_{t^\dagger} A\). A solution is the ballot-mass allocation and the resulting preferred winner; otherwise the answer is NO.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large membership organization, electorate, or constituency with many people but relatively few distinct complete rankings. For example, millions of members could fall into dozens of stable preference blocs, with polling or public discussion giving them the electorate information the paper assumes. The premise is less convincing for a small committee, and the paper itself says these more complex systems fit sophisticated or limited electorates better than large public elections. Still, the paper’s own unit of strategic agency is already the preference group \(G\), so replacing headcounts by proportions is a natural extension. The mirror keeps the society continuous while leaving candidates, ballots, and the winner discrete.

On Proposition 3’s promised domain, I expect the continuous question to be tractable in the strongest but least surprising sense: the answer is always NO. The proof’s majority arguments and induction over candidate sets appear to use mass inequalities rather than integer voter counts, so the same result should carry over when group sizes are rational masses. This covers Proposition 3’s RR1 and RA1 result only; it does not extend the paper’s claims to profiles without a Condorcet preference winner or to its other four systems.

The weakness is substantial: this is not a new complexity landscape. Proposition 3 is not a runtime result, and every rational mass profile can be represented by a discrete electorate after scaling by a common denominator. The continuous formulation is therefore a sensible mirror of the paper’s strategic question, but its expected answer largely restates the paper’s theorem. There is no anchored hardness result here whose discrete hardness might dissolve in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the sole anchor, Proposition 3, is that it supplies no computational problem for continuization to classify. It proves a strategic property of RR1 and RA1 under specified base strategies: no preference group can profitably change the winner when a Condorcet preference winner exists. It asks for no algorithm, optimization, or complexity bound. A “mass manipulation” problem can be defined around it, but that adds a computational task to a result that did not pose one.

The direct mirror is coherent. Rankings are natural types, and the paper already treats each preference group as a strategic unit and allows part or all of a group to deviate. Replacing group counts by rational masses fits that model. The proof also appears to carry over: its key steps use majority inequalities and induction on the number of candidates. Scaling rational masses to integer counts does not undermine this as a high-multiplicity setting.

That fit is also the mirror’s weakness as a ChoCo project. On Proposition 3’s domain, asking whether a mass allocation lets a group manipulate simply packages the theorem’s existing strategic claim. Asking for a harder problem—such as the complexity of finding manipulations outside that domain—would require specifying behavior where the paper supplies no base strategy or result. Adding perturbation costs or an incentive-margin objective would create another research question, rather than strengthen this anchor.

So the negative case is narrow and not decisive: Proposition 3 is a plausible continuous-population mirror, and the paper’s group-based model supports it. The case against rests on the absence of a computational result to mirror and the lack of an evident algorithmic question attached to this particular theorem. That weakens the claim that this paper offers a worthwhile *computational* continuization anchor; it does not show that the mirror is ill-posed.
