The continuous mirror question

Given weight types \(T\), positive integer weights \(w_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a population scale \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral, and a quota fraction \(\rho\in(0,1)\), form the weighted majority game with \(N\mu_t\) players of type \(t\) and quota \(Q=\lceil \rho N\bar w\rceil\), where \(\bar w=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\). What randomized algorithm, operating on this type-mass input, estimates each type’s total Shapley–Shubik power \(\Phi_t\) so that \(\frac12\sum_t|\hat\Phi_t-\Phi_t|<\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and what is its running time in the input bit length, \(|T|\), \(N\), \(1/\varepsilon\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\)?

The model it lives in

A weighted majority society represented by weight types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), and per-player weights \(w_t\), together with a population scale \(N\) and quota fraction \(\rho\). The target is the vector of total Shapley–Shubik power held by each type in the corresponding finite game; the computational task is randomized additive approximation from the type-mass representation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: mirror the paper’s Monte Carlo approximation of Shapley–Shubik power, not the broader question of exact power computation. The paper’s numbered result I would anchor on is Theorem 5, proved here. There is no numbered P/NP/FPT result in the paper; its named computational results are sampling guarantees.

Theorem 5 assumes players are ordered by nonincreasing weight. It says: (1) for player \(i\), \(M\ge(\ln 2+\ln(1/\delta))/(2\varepsilon^2 i^2)\) samples suffice for \(\Pr[|\varphi_i^{A2}-\varphi_i|<\varepsilon]\ge1-\delta\); (2) if \(M\ge(\ln 2+\ln(1/\delta))/(2\varepsilon^2)\), then the probability that this error bound holds for every player is at least \(1-2\sum_{i=1}^n(\delta/2)^{i^2}\); and (3) if \(M\ge(|N^*|\ln 2+\ln(1/\delta))/(2\varepsilon^2)\), then the total variation error \(\frac12\sum_i|\varphi_i^{A2}-\varphi_i|\) is below \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\). Here \(N^*=\{i<n:\varphi_i>\varphi_{i+1}\}\cup\{n\}\), so \(|N^*|\) counts distinct power levels.

A plausible regime is a large federation of chapters or local bodies whose voting rights come in a small number of statutory weight classes: perhaps \(N=100{,}000\) bodies but only \(\tau=5\) weights. A type is a body’s voting weight \(w_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the federation. Bodies in a type are interchangeable for everything this game uses. The society is the distribution \(\mu\), while the quota remains a fraction \(\rho\) of total voting weight. This is a high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every voting body is large.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover:

High-Multiplicity Type-Power Approximation. The input is a finite set of weight types \(T\), positive integer weights \(w_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a population scale \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral for every type, a quota fraction \(\rho\), and error parameters \(\varepsilon,\delta\). Let \(\bar w=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\), and form the corresponding weighted game with \(N\mu_t\) players of type \(t\) and quota \(Q=\lceil \rho N\bar w\rceil\). The target is the type-level power vector \(\Phi\), where \(\Phi_t\) is the sum of the Shapley–Shubik indices of all players of type \(t\). Output an estimate \(\hat\Phi\in\Delta(T)\) such that \(\frac12\sum_t|\hat\Phi_t-\Phi_t|<\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

This target preserves the paper’s underlying power question: \(\Phi_t\) is the share of total pivotal power held by the weight class, and it can be estimated by grouping the paper’s per-player A2 estimates. Equal-weight players have equal true indices and equal A2 estimates, so grouping preserves total variation error. There are at most \(\tau\) distinct power levels, which makes Theorem 5’s total-variation sample bound depend on type support rather than the number of players. That is the lead mirror; the natural prediction is Class A for randomized additive approximation. It also asks a sharper computational question than the theorem settles: can the estimator be run from the compressed mass input in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length, \(1/\varepsilon\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\)?

The regime is plausible for these authors: their paper already groups repeated weights in its EU Council and US examples, and their target is exactly power under weighted majority rules. There is also analytic large-game prior art: Shapiro and Shapley study weighted majority games as vote blocks are split among many small players and derive a limiting expression. That makes the continuous setting recognizable, but does not settle the compressed-input sampling question posed here. ([Shapiro and Shapley, “Values of Large Games, I: A Limit Theorem”](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.3.1.1))

The soft spot is real: A2’s sample count may be independent of \(N\), but each sample still takes \(O(N)\) time. Theorem 5 therefore supports the sample-complexity side of this mirror, not a fast algorithm on binary-encoded type masses. And if “continuous” is required to mean an atomless limit with no finite clone interpretation, this mirror needs another step. The positive case survives because high multiplicity is the intended bridge: the population is supplied as type masses, and the computational question is whether the paper’s approximation can exploit that compressed description.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives sampling guarantees, and repeated statutory weight classes are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I would not object that the types are artificial or that the theorem is “only” about approximation.

The trouble is what survives when the population actually becomes continuous. Shapley–Shubik power is defined by the identity of the player who crosses the quota in a permutation. In an atomless population, a single player has zero mass and cannot be pivotal; the meaningful limit is the aggregate power of a type. For fixed finite weight types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), weights \(w_t\), and an interior quota \(Q_N=\lceil \rho N\bar w\rceil\), where \(\bar w=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\) and \(0<\rho<1\), the large-population limit is
\[ \Phi_t\longrightarrow \frac{\mu_t w_t}{\bar w}. \]
That is a deterministic weight share. Individual indices shrink as \(N\) grows, so fixed additive-error guarantees for them become vacuous; the nontrivial type-level target has a simple limiting answer. Continuum and asymptotic values for weighted majority games are mathematically established, but I do not count that analytic literature as a computational collision. [Shapiro and Shapley](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.3.1.1) study vote fragmentation limits, and [Neyman](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.13.4.556) proves existence of asymptotic values for weighted majority games.

The proponent’s finite-\(N\) formulation keeps exactly the feature that disappears in the limit: it constructs \(N\) players and asks for their finite-game power. Algorithm A2 also still costs \(\mathcal{O}(N)\) per sample, so its support-sensitive sample count is not a compressed-input running-time bound. Accuracy fine enough to resolve deviations from \(\mu_t w_t/\bar w\) could motivate useful work, but that would compute finite-population corrections in a compressed discrete game. Boundary quotas or macroscopic blocs could also preserve richer behavior, at the cost of changing the proposed fixed-interior, all-types-growing regime.

That is the strongest negative case, and its limit is real: a fast algorithm for finite-\(N\) type power may be worthwhile. I do not see a strong reason to treat that possibility as a continuous mirror of Theorem 5; the continuum target itself has lost the paper’s sampling problem.
