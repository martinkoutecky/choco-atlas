The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), an integer \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(b\), decide whether there are a committee \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=k\) and allocations \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=1/k\) for each \(c\in S\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}\operatorname{rank}_t(c)\le b\). Classify this problem, including its complexity when parameterized by \(p=\lceil b\rceil\), with input size measured using \(|C|\), \(|T|\), and the rational bit length.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings. Choose a size-\(k\) committee and allocate each type's mass among its members so every representative receives exactly \(1/k\) of the population; minimize average rank cost.

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers Theorem 2's Monroe-MW hardness result. It leaves Theorem 1's Chamberlin–Courant result and the paper's other discussion of representation rules unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of continuous Monroe budgeted assignment parameterized by support size, committee size, and rational input bit length?
  • What parameter correspondence, if any, relates the discrete total-cost bound \(\beta\) to average cost on a normalized society beyond the paper's reduction?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would anchor the positive case on Theorem 2 and the Monroe rule. The theorem, proved in Section 5 of this paper by a parameterized reduction from \(\Clique\), says that Monroe-MW is W[1]-hard with respect to \(\beta\), the minimum *total* sum of voters’ assigned ranks. In threshold form, the question is whether there is a valid size-\(k\) Monroe assignment with total misrepresentation at most a given bound. Thus, unless FPT \(=\) W[1], there is no algorithm running in \(f(\beta)\cdot |I|^{O(1)}\). This is a result proved here, not a cited hardness theorem.

A natural continuous mirror is Continuous Monroe Budgeted Assignment. An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C\), an integer \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings of \(C\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(b\). It asks whether there is a committee \(S\subseteq C\), \(|S|=k\), and an allocation \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) such that
\[ \sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=\frac1k\quad(c\in S), \]
with \(x_{t,c}=0\) for \(c\notin S\), and
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}\,\operatorname{rank}_t(c)\le b. \]
The solution is the committee and the allocation of each type’s mass to its representatives. The objective is average rank per person. The population is continuous; candidates and rankings remain discrete.

The regime is a large electorate choosing a fixed-size delegation under an equal-representation rule: for example, a large association or regional body where many members share one of a relatively small number of ballot types, and each representative serves exactly \(1/k\) of the membership. A type is a complete ranking, since that is precisely what determines the misrepresentation cost here. This is a credible high-multiplicity setting: the number of people can be far larger than the number of observed ranking types. Monroe’s quota already speaks in shares of the electorate, so the mass formulation is close to the rule’s own meaning. I would expect the authors to recognize this as their problem with voters represented by divisible population mass.

The reason I expect hardness to transfer is unusually concrete. Fix a committee \(S\). The continuous allocation problem is a transportation LP. If \(D\) is a common denominator for the masses, scale the variables by \(kD\): each type then supplies \(kD\mu_t\) integer units, and each selected candidate demands \(D\) units. The transportation constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so there is an optimal allocation on this grid. In other words, the continuous optimum equals the discrete Monroe optimum for an appropriately replicated electorate. The theorem’s reduction gives an especially direct embedding: its construction has \(N=Lk\) voters, with \(L=1+h(h-1)\), so its empirical masses and Monroe quotas scale back to integer voter counts and quotas. Fractional assignment does not break that reduction.

There is a normalization wrinkle worth stating precisely. The paper’s parameter is total cost \(\beta\), whereas the continuous objective is average cost \(b\). On the theorem’s construction, \(b=\beta/N\). Its bound is \(\beta=h+2h\binom h2+L\binom h2=O(h^4)\), while its padding gives \(N=Lk>\beta\). Therefore \(b<1\). For the continuous threshold problem parameterized by the natural integer \(p=\lceil b\rceil\), the reduction has \(p=1\); it consequently gives NP-hardness even at that parameter value. That is a derived consequence for this normalized problem, not the paper’s stated W[1]-hardness theorem or a claim that \(\beta\) and \(b\) are the same parameter.

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s Monroe-MW hardness result. It does not try to continuize the paper’s separate Chamberlin–Courant result, approximation claims, or other representation rules. It suggests further questions about algorithms parameterized by support size \(\tau\), committee size \(k\), and rational input bit length, and about how the average-cost parameter behaves for arbitrary rational societies.

My weakest point is that the hardness argument preserves the discrete committee-selection combinatorics; it does not show that allowing divisible mass creates a new source of hardness. Also, the paper’s theorem is parameterized by total voter cost, while the mirror uses per-capita cost. The scaling above makes the connection exact for the reduction, but any broader parameterized classification should keep that distinction explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the parameterized claim, not to the continuous Monroe problem itself. The paper’s \(\beta\) is a total cost over \(N\) voters, while a normalized society \(\mu\) has no population size \(N\). Replicating every voter \(q\) times leaves \(\mu\) and its average cost unchanged, but multiplies the discrete total cost by \(q\). So \(\beta\) has no canonical counterpart in the continuous input. The natural continuous budget is average rank \(b=\beta/N\); in this reduction, \(b<1\). Parameterizing by \(\lceil b\rceil\) makes the reduction a hardness result at parameter \(1\), which is not the paper’s W[1]-hardness result with respect to \(\beta\). Recovering \(\beta\) requires a population scale that the continuous society deliberately forgets.

That objection does not defeat the proposed budgeted-assignment mirror. The threshold problem is still well-posed, and the theorem’s construction has \(N=Lk\), so its empirical distribution gives a valid continuous instance with exact Monroe quotas. For any fixed committee, the allocation is a transportation LP; scaling by a common denominator gives an integral optimum. Thus the continuous optimum matches that of a replicated discrete election. This limits what is new—the mirror studies a compressed high-multiplicity version of Monroe, rather than a new fractional allocation effect—but the programme explicitly treats that bridge as valuable. It is not a reason to dismiss the computational question.

The scenario also survives: a large association choosing an equal-representation delegation can have many members with the same complete ranking. Mass records their shares, and the assignment depends on type-level ranks rather than personal identities. The paper has named computational theorems, no identity-dependent objective, and no existing continuous treatment identified here. The high-multiplicity interpretation is support for the mirror, not a collision.

So the negative case is weak. It can reject a casual claim that the paper’s \(\beta\)-parameterized theorem transfers unchanged. It cannot honestly rule out the better continuous budgeted question, and the paper’s reduction gives that question a direct computational anchor.
