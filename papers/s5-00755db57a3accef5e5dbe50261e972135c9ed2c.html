The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper states no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 5, proved here, says that if risk spectrum \(\phi\) first-order stochastically dominates \(\psi\), then \(C_\phi\supseteq C_\psi\). Corollary 11 derives several inclusions among familiar cores. Those are mathematical results about core inclusion, not classifications or algorithms for computing a core. Proposition 10 represents existing core concepts as special cases. The discussion mentions computational difficulty, but proves no complexity result. None qualifies as an anchor under your criterion.

There is still a plausible continuous-population problem suggested by the paper. Consider a large mutual insurance or reinsurance pool whose members fall into \(\tau\) actuarially complete types: members of a type have identical exposures, resources, and relevant terms. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A coalition is described by its type masses \(x\in[0,\mu]\), and its payoff in state \(s\) is \(v_s(x)\). Given state probabilities \(p_s\) and a common spectral risk function \(\phi\), let \(q_x\) be the quantile function of those state payoffs and define \(W_\phi(x)=\int_0^1\phi(p)q_x(p)\,dp\).

One possible target is Risk-Core Feasibility\(_\infty\). Its input is rational type masses, state probabilities, a risk spectrum, a designated status-quo state \(s_0\), and explicitly encoded state-payoff functions \(v_s\). The question is whether there is a per-member allocation \(a_t\) for each type such that
\[ \sum_t\mu_t a_t=v_{s_0}(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t x_t a_t\ge W_\phi(x)\ \text{for every }x\in[0,\mu]. \]
A solution is such an allocation, or a report that none exists. This directly extends the paper’s risk-adjusted blocking condition: the society is continuous, and every fractional composition of the population can deviate.

The regime is many pool members but few distinct actuarial types. The authors could plausibly recognize this as a high-multiplicity version of their core problem: it retains uncertain coalition payoffs, risk-adjusted deviations, and stable allocations. It covers the characteristic-function-form risk-based core; it does not attempt to continuize the paper’s full recursive treatment of residual partitions. For concave, piecewise-linear payoff functions and a fixed finite set of states, I would investigate a tractable convex-optimization or separation formulation. That is a conjecture about this proposed problem, not a result of the paper. With variable state sets or general partition externalities, hardness remains open. Further questions include the variable-state complexity and how solutions relate to finite pools with large but finite membership.

The weakest point is that a finite game’s values on named coalitions do not uniquely specify values for fractional type masses. The mirror needs an explicit continuous payoff model, and the paper itself supplies no complexity anchor for it. That makes this a credible research question, but not a positive case anchored in one of the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against has one decisive point: this paper states no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 5 and Theorem 6 compare cores; Corollary 11 gives inclusion relations; Proposition 10 represents existing core concepts in the risk-based framework. None classifies the complexity of computing a core or gives an algorithm. Section 4 mentions computational difficulty and possible faster approximation, but defines no computational problem or complexity claim. So there is no result here for a continuous-complexity mirror to anchor to.

The proposed insurance-pool problem is the strongest follow-up suggested by the paper. A large pool with many members of a few complete actuarial types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and stability against fractional coalitions is a coherent question. But it needs a payoff law \(v_s(x)\) for every coalition-mass vector \(x\). The paper supplies only values for coalitions of a finite player set. Those values do not determine what happens at fractional masses. One can make the bridge more natural by specifying an actuarial model directly, but then the model and its payoff structure are additional assumptions; they are not a continuization of a result this paper established. Restricting the payoffs to concave, piecewise-linear functions may make a computational problem precise, but that structure is also doing substantive work.

That leaves a limit to the negative case: the continuous insurance problem could be worth studying in its own right, and the population regime is credible. I cannot honestly rule out every useful model inspired by this paper. But the proponent did not identify a surviving anchor in the paper’s named results. Their proposal is a plausible new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
