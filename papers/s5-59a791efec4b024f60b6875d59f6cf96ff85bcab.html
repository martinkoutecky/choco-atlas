The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods and \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), complete rankings \(\pi_t\), demands \(r_t>0\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a feasible baseline allocation \(a_{tj}\), decide whether there is a feasible allocation \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=r_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), such that every type weakly lexicographically prefers \(x_t\) to \(a_t\) and at least one type strictly prefers it. By averaging allocations within each type, this decision is equivalent to asking whether a positive mass of individuals can be weakly improved, with a strict improvement for positive mass.

The model it lives in

The society has \(\tau\) complete preference types with masses \(\mu_t\); each type shares its ranking and demand. The input includes per-capita supplies and a feasible baseline allocation for each type. The decision variables are the per-type allocation vectors \(x_t\); the question is whether a feasible allocation weakly lexicographically dominates the baseline for every type and strictly improves at least one type.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 15’s Pareto-efficiency verification for singleton goods and non-Leontief lexicographic preferences. It does not cover the paper’s strategy-proofness, group strategy-proofness, Leontief, or other mechanism results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is High-Multiplicity Lexicographic Pareto-Efficiency Verification. The paper’s goods are already divisible; the continuization here is of the population. Think of a citywide school-placement lottery with many applicants, a modest number of distinct preference rankings, and school capacities that scale with the cohort. A type \(t\) is a complete ranking \(\pi_t\) and a per-person demand \(r_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of that type. Each person receives a unit lottery share across schools, and \(q_j\) is school \(j\)’s capacity per applicant. The number of applicants may be large while the support size \(\tau\) remains moderate.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance gives \(m\) goods, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational demands \(r_t>0\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a feasible type-level allocation \(a_{tj}\). Feasibility means \(\sum_j a_{tj}=r_t\) for every type and \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{tj}\le q_j\) for every good. Decide whether \(a\) is Pareto efficient: is there a feasible allocation of bundles to the unit-mass society under which almost every person weakly lexicographically prefers their bundle to \(a_t\), with a strict improvement for a positive mass of people? A NO instance can be witnessed by a feasible dominating allocation.

The anchor is Corollary 15, proved in this paper. It states that there is a polynomial-time algorithm for checking whether a given allocation is Pareto efficient. I use its singleton-good, non-Leontief case: finite agents have lexicographic rankings over goods, specified demands, and a supplied feasible allocation. This is the paper’s named polynomial-time result most directly about an underlying computational problem; its main mechanism-design theorems do not give complexity classifications of that kind.

There is a useful dictionary to the continuous version. If agents of a type receive different bundles in a proposed improvement, average those bundles across the type. Feasibility and per-person demand are preserved, and the average remains weakly lexicographically better: with finitely many goods, among the positively many strict improvements there is a first ranked coordinate at which a positive mass gains. Thus testing type-level alternatives suffices. Equivalently, treat each type as a representative finite agent with demand \(\mu_t r_t\) and allocation \((\mu_t a_{tj})_j\). Positive scaling preserves its lexicographic ranking. Applying the paper’s verifier to these \(\tau\) weighted representatives gives the expected Class A result, polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\).

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ underlying efficiency-verification question: it keeps their goods, demands, rankings, and lexicographic Pareto criterion, while replacing a long list of agents with a distribution over complete agent types. It also makes the population scaling explicit. The scope is Corollary 15 alone; I am not claiming a mirror of the paper’s strategy-proofness results. A natural follow-up is whether a synchronized-greedy mechanism for such type masses retains strategy-proofness, and what capacity conditions it would require.

The soft spot is that Corollary 15 is peripheral to the paper’s main contribution, and its polynomial algorithm already makes this mirror look like a weighted compression of a finite problem rather than a new hardness landscape. Still, that compression is exactly the high-multiplicity population regime the programme asks about. The paper offers no comparable named hardness result to anchor a question about whether discrete hardness dissolves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it appears to add almost no computational work. Corollary 15’s continuous question is whether a type-level allocation is Pareto efficient for a unit-mass society with rational type masses. For each type \(t\), make one finite representative with demand \(\widehat r_t=\mu_t r_t\) and allocation \(\widehat a_{tj}=\mu_t a_{tj}\), leaving supplies \(q_j\) unchanged. Scaling preserves lexicographic comparisons, so Corollary 15 applies directly.

Allowing people of one type to receive different bundles in a proposed improvement does not seem to make the mirror richer. Averaging their bundles preserves feasibility. With finitely many ranked goods, if a positive mass strictly improves, there is a first ranked coordinate at which a positive mass gains; the type average is therefore weakly better, and strictly better in that coordinate. So type-level alternatives suffice. The verifier reduces to the paper’s finite verifier on \(\tau\) representatives, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

That is a coherent mirror, but its continuous formulation supplies no evident new computational structure: the population enters as weights, and the result transfers by a direct representative construction. A more ambitious version might allow unequal baseline allocations within a type, but then those allocations must be represented in the instance and may enlarge the support. Restricting to equal allocations keeps the support small, as in the proposed school-lottery scenario, while preserving the direct reduction. Either way, the case for spending programme effort here rests mainly on making the high-multiplicity dictionary explicit.

The paper’s main theorems suggest a stronger alternative: study synchronized greedy allocation for type masses. Individual strategy-proofness in an atomless population is vacuous, since one person has zero mass. A positive-mass coalition question avoids that degeneracy, but it resembles Theorem 7’s existing coalition guarantee, with mass replacing the total demand of a finite coalition. That may be a useful reformulation, but it does not reveal a clear new computational problem.

So this is an argument against prioritizing the mirror, not a sound claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A symmetric, high-volume placement lottery is a plausible population regime, and the proposed verifier is well posed. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case: Corollary 15 survives the basic test for a continuous mirror. The strongest objection is that it looks like a low-yield application of an existing finite result, and that objection may not be enough for the programme’s standard.
