What the mirror covers

The attempted analogue covers the pool-optimality result for the efficiency-gap campaign problem; it does not cover generating or searching all feasible district plans.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity electorate for the paper’s pool-restricted, efficiency-gap version of votemandering. Imagine a state election with millions of voters aggregated by precinct or census block. A type is a location, party affiliation, and any campaign-relevant attribute such as turnout eligibility or responsiveness. Voters in a type share the information the model uses; their mass is their fraction of the electorate. Thousands of geographic types can still represent millions of people.

My lead anchor is Theorem \(\ref{thm: polytime}\). The supplied text leaves its resolved numeral unexpanded, so I cite its printed reference label rather than guess a number. Theorem \(\ref{thm: polytime}\) is proved in this paper. It states that, given a pool \(\mathcal P\) of \(\poolsize\) feasible, not necessarily fair, plans for \(n\) districts, a plan in \(\mathcal P\) maximizing the votemandering bonus can be found in time polynomial in \(\poolsize\) and \(n\). Its proof reduces the fairness step for each candidate plan to a sequence of linear programs, with at most \(n\) updates.

The continuous problem I would hand a prover is Pool-Restricted Continuous EG Votemandering. Its instance contains a geographic adjacency graph with cells \(K\); an initial plan \(D_0\); a pool \(\mathcal P\) of feasible plans, each assigning those cells to \(n\) districts; rational masses \(\mu_{k,A}\) and \(\mu_{k,B}\) of eligible supporters in each cell, normalized so their total is \(1\); a baseline turnout \(\alpha\); a fixed, known opponent campaign allocation \(q^B_k\); Party \(A\)’s campaign budget \(\beta_A\); and an EG threshold \(\delta\). A campaign decision is a vector of added turnout masses \(x_k\), with \(0\le x_k\le(1-\alpha)\mu_{k,A}\) and \(\sum_k x_k\le\beta_A\). Thus the campaigned profile has masses \((\alpha\mu_{k,A}+x_k,\alpha\mu_{k,B}+q^B_k)\), while the target-map election uses the original profile \((\mu_{k,A},\mu_{k,B})\).

The question is to choose \(D\in\mathcal P\) and \(x\) maximizing Party \(A\)’s total district wins on \(D_0\) under the campaigned profile plus its wins on \(D\) under the original profile, subject to the paper’s EG condition \(EG(D,\text{campaigned profile})\le\delta\). A solution is the maximizing pair \((D,x)\). The winner and tie convention is fixed as part of the instance. This keeps the paper’s central interaction intact: campaign mass changes the election and the apparent fairness of a proposed plan, while the later election evaluates that plan against the original support profile.

I expect this pool-restricted version to be Class A. For a fixed plan, the continuous campaign allocations and the wasted-vote accounting are linear once district win/lose statuses are fixed. The paper’s proof already exploits that structure by solving linear programs over overlapping pieces of the initial and target plans. With rational masses and budgets, the corresponding continuous instance should admit the same style of polynomial algorithm, with running time polynomial in \(|\mathcal P|\), \(|K|\), and the input bit length. The mass variables are exactly what the electorate interpretation calls for; the map and seat outcomes remain those of the paper.

The regime is plausible for statewide elections: voter counts greatly exceed the number of precinct-party types, while campaigns and election data are already handled in aggregated geographic units. If turnout response or campaign price differs within a precinct, those attributes can refine the types. The authors would likely recognize this as their problem: it retains their campaigning decision, EG constraint, two-round objective, and candidate-plan pool. The mirror covers the pool-optimality result in Theorem \(\ref{thm: polytime}\), not global search over all redistricting plans. The paper states that the broader MIP is difficult, but the supplied text contains no named computational hardness theorem to anchor.

This proposal also raises useful next questions: does the LP argument extend when \(\mathcal P\) is replaced by all feasible plans, and how does the continuous optimum relate to a finite election with \(N\) voters and campaign decisions in increments of \(1/N\)? In particular, one could seek conditions under which rounding the continuous campaign allocation preserves the EG bound and seat counts.

The weakest point is that the paper already aggregates voters by geographic unit and allows fractional campaign allocations. Replacing voter counts with normalized masses may therefore be close to a rescaling of its existing formulation. Also, the proposed tractability result deliberately keeps the plan pool as input; it does not resolve the spatial combinatorics of generating good maps. That limits the novelty and scope of the mirror, but it does not erase the central question of how the result behaves when the electorate itself is modeled as mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem \(\ref{thm: polytime}\), and it is a genuine computational result. The objection is that its proposed continuous mirror appears already built into the model the theorem analyzes.

The paper represents voters by party affiliation and geographic unit, using aggregate vote counts \(v_{init,k}^A,v_{init,k}^B\). Its campaign allocations \(b_k^A\) are fractional, and the proof reduces them to capacities on overlaps between the initial and target plans. Within the pool, the algorithm uses those aggregate capacities, district margins and fairness totals; it never needs individual voter identities or indivisible campaign contacts.

Normalize all counts and budgets by the total electorate \(P\): set \(\mu_{k,p}=v_{init,k}^p/P\) and scale every campaign allocation by \(P\). District winners are unchanged, the EG ratio is unchanged, and the LP feasible region and objective are just rescaled. Conversely, any rational mass instance can be scaled to integer voter counts. So the proposed mass formulation and the theorem’s formulation have the same computational content. The theorem’s LP argument already operates on campaign mass; expressing the electorate as normalized fractions would be a change of units, not a new continuization result.

Adding type attributes such as turnout responsiveness or campaign cost could give a more detailed model. Those attributes would refine the aggregate inputs and could motivate an extension of the existing LP. A spatial continuum of voters could also be studied, but it would replace the finite geographic units and plan pool with a problem about continuous map geometry. That is a separate modeling move; high multiplicity in the population does not require it.

The negative case is strongest for this anchor: the proposed mirror collides with the paper’s existing aggregate, fractional formulation. I cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous spatial-redistricting project, but that would not establish a distinct population-continuization mirror of Theorem \(\ref{thm: polytime}\).
