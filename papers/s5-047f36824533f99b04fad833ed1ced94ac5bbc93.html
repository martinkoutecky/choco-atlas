What the mirror covers

The candidate extension concerns the equivalence-class question behind Proposition 4; it does not carry over the paper’s finite class counts, ILP enumeration, or geometric maps as continuous computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I find no named computational-complexity result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. The exact class counts are reported in Table 2, and equation (9) gives an ILP characterization, but the paper does not state a numbered result about the complexity of a problem or an algorithm’s running time.

Proposition 4 is the closest candidate, and it is proved here. It says that for any family of strictly decreasing scoring vectors \(s^m\), each of the \(m-1\) weight vectors \((\alpha_j^m,\beta^m,0,\ldots,0)\), where \(\alpha_j^m=s_1^m-s_{j+1}^m\) and \(\beta^m=s_1^m-s_2^m\), identifies a different weighted-committee equivalence class. Thus the number of classes grows without bound with \(m\). That is a structural result about finite weight vectors, not a complexity classification, so presenting it as the required kind of anchor would stretch your criterion.

A plausible continuous research question would model a large shareholder or delegate population by a distribution over ranking and per-capita voting-weight types, then ask whether two weighting schemes induce the same scoring-rule winner for every admissible society distribution. But the paper has no qualifying named computational result to mirror, so I cannot honestly attach that question to an anchor or predict its complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the closest named anchor, but it does not establish a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror. It proves a structural lower bound: for strictly decreasing scoring vectors, at least \(m-1\) weight vectors belong to different equivalence classes. It gives no algorithm or running-time classification. The paper’s class counts and ILP in Section 4 are computational work, but they do not supply a numbered complexity result for the proposed mirror to continue.

The proponent’s best repair is a large shareholder or delegate population, with complete types recording both ranking and per-capita voting weight. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; dismissing it because weights become type-level would misunderstand the programme. But it also shows why this paper is a poor mirror candidate. For each ranking \(\pi\), all that matters to scoring is the aggregate weighted mass
\[ z_\pi=\int_{\text{agents with ranking }\pi} q\,d\mu, \]
where \(q\) is per-capita voting weight. The distribution of weights within a ranking type has no further effect. A winner query is therefore just a finite weighted-score calculation. Asking whether two weighting schemes produce the same winner for every society reduces to comparing the induced weighted decision maps—the paper’s own equivalence question, expressed through aggregate masses.

That reduction is especially awkward as a claim of new continuization: the paper already normalizes weight vectors to the unit simplex and studies their geometric equivalence regions. For the strongest version of this proposed mirror, population masses provide coordinates for a finite weighted profile; they do not create a new computational object with its own complexity landscape. One could add optimization over population changes, but that would introduce a problem the paper does not study rather than mirror one of its results.

So the negative case is strongest against this paper as a source of ChoCo mirrors: it offers no qualifying complexity anchor, and its most natural high-multiplicity reformulation collapses back to weighted-profile geometry. The case is weaker against the broader universal claim. Large shareholder populations are plausible, and a continuous optimization problem built around them could be worthwhile; I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario. The point is that this paper does not, on its own, give a named computational result that makes that new question a mirror of its contribution.
