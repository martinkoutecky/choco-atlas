The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), positive rational singleton-worth rates \(a_t\), and rational grand worth per capita \(g\), compute an efficient type-level payoff \(x\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=g\), that maximizes \(\min_{0<q\le\mu}\frac{\sum_{t\in T}q_t x_t}{\sum_{t\in T}q_t a_t}\). Here \(q\) ranges over the masses of all nonzero measurable coalitions. The question is whether this continuous population allocation has the same optimizer as the paper’s optimistic satisfaction problem.

The model it lives in

An atomless society of total mass \(1\) is partitioned into payoff-equivalent types \(t\in T\), with mass \(\mu_t\) and singleton-worth rate \(a_t>0\). Each type receives a per-person payoff \(x_t\), subject to per-capita efficiency \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=g\). A coalition is specified by its submass vector \(q\), and its optimistic satisfaction is \(\frac{\sum_t q_t x_t}{\sum_t q_t a_t}\).

The objection that survived

The mirror uses only type masses, singleton-worth rates, and grand worth, so it adds no pricing problem or meaningful complexity bottleneck; the proponent acknowledges that it is simple but does not address this limit on its computational reach.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. The paper states no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result: its theorems characterize allocation values. So there is no hardness anchor here, and no basis for claiming that continuization dissolves a known discrete hardness result. The best positive case rests on two named results that explicitly determine the solution to an optimization problem; the algorithms below are consequences of their formulas, not complexity claims made by the authors.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in the paper. For every TU-game with positive singleton worths, it says that the optimistic optimal-satisfaction allocation gives every player the same ratio of payoff to singleton worth, and equals the proportional division value:
\[ \phi^o_i(v)=\frac{v(\{i\})}{\sum_{k\in N}v(\{k\})}\,v(N). \]
The theorem’s underlying problem is to choose an efficient payoff vector that lexicographically maximizes the coalitions’ optimistic satisfaction ratios.

A direct continuous version is the Optimistic Type-Mass Allocation problem. Its input is a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), positive rational singleton-worth rates \(a_t\), and a rational grand-coalition worth \(g\) per unit of population. Choose a per-person payoff \(x_t\) for each type, subject to efficiency,
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=g, \]
to maximize the least satisfaction among all nonzero coalition masses \(q\), where \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\) and
\[ \sigma_o(q,x)=\frac{\sum_t q_t x_t}{\sum_t q_t a_t}. \]
The worst coalition satisfaction is \(\min_t x_t/a_t\): every coalition’s ratio is a weighted average of the type ratios, and a coalition consisting only of one type attains that type’s ratio. Efficiency fixes the weighted average of those ratios, so maximizing their minimum uniquely equalizes them. The solution is
\[ x_t=\frac{a_t g}{\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s a_s}. \]
This is also the unique result of the paper’s lexicographic objective in this model: its first, max-min stage already determines the allocation.

This is a genuine population mirror. For a finite replication with \(n\mu_t\) players of type \(t\), singleton worth \(a_t\), and grand worth \(ng\), Theorem 3 gives each type-\(t\) player exactly the \(x_t\) above. A plausible regime is a large cooperative enterprise or mutual-benefit scheme with many members in a modest number of standardized roles, each role sharing the same payoff-relevant characteristics. Here \(n\) can be large while \(\tau=|T|\) stays small. With binary-rational input, the displayed formula is computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. It is a class-A result, though a simple one.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 7, also established in the paper, with its proof omitted as analogous to the proof of Theorem 3. For games with positive marginal contributions, it says that the pessimistic optimal-satisfaction allocation gives every player the same ratio of payoff to grand-coalition marginal contribution, and equals the proportional allocation of non-separable contribution value:
\[ \phi^p_i(v)=\frac{\widehat v(\{i\})}{\widehat v(N)}\,v(N), \qquad \widehat v(\{i\})=v(N)-v(N\setminus\{i\}). \]

Its continuous counterpart, Pessimistic Type-Mass Allocation, takes the same type masses \(\mu_t\), a positive marginal-contribution rate \(b_t\) for each type, and grand worth \(g\). It asks for an efficient per-person allocation \(y\) maximizing
\[ \inf_{0<q\le\mu}\frac{\sum_t q_t y_t}{\sum_t q_t b_t}, \qquad \sum_t\mu_t y_t=g. \]
The optimum uniquely equalizes \(y_t/b_t\), giving
\[ y_t=\frac{b_t g}{\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s b_s}. \]
In a finite replication where every type-\(t\) member has marginal contribution \(b_t\) and the grand worth is \(ng\), Theorem 7 yields exactly this per-person payoff. A plausible setting is a large cooperative production network with many contributors in a small number of standardized roles and the same full-coalition marginal contribution within each role. This question also has a direct polynomial-time formula.

The article’s proportional values make these type-mass questions recognizable as the authors’ own allocation problems, rather than as continuity of the outcome space or of an axiom system. The proposed mirrors cover the satisfaction-optimization results of Section 3, especially Theorems 3 and 7. They do not extend the associated-consistency and dual-axiom results in Section 4; those are axiomatic questions, outside the programme’s computational scope.

The weakest point is also central: the paper is about axiomatization, and both anchors already give explicit formulas. The continuous questions are well-posed and faithful to those value computations, but they do not expose a difficult complexity landscape, and they do not show that population continuization makes a previously hard problem tractable. If “anchor” is restricted strictly to a named complexity-class result, this paper has no qualifying anchor; the case above is the strongest positive value-computation reading available.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Its theorems characterize allocation values; they do not study an algorithmic problem’s complexity, running time, or parameter dependence. Theorems 3 and 7 do identify leximin optima, so they deserve the strongest reading. But the proposed mirrors must supply a computational question that the paper itself never develops.

For Theorem 3, the proponent’s mass model is mathematically coherent, and a large group of members in standardized roles is plausible. With type masses \(\mu_t\), singleton-worth rates \(a_t\), and grand worth per capita \(g\), efficiency fixes the weighted average of \(x_t/a_t\). Maximizing the least coalition satisfaction makes all those ratios equal, yielding \(x_t=a_tg/\sum_s\mu_sa_s\). I would not reject this mirror on multiplicity or on the passage to continuous mass.

Its weakness for ChoCo is that the proposed computation factors entirely through those summary statistics. The paper’s theorem already gives the value for arbitrary finite player sets, including players with different singleton worths. The continuous version translates the same formula to type masses; it does not expose a pricing problem, a high-multiplicity obstacle, or an algorithmic question whose formulation is missing from the paper. Giving coalition worths a richer type-dependent structure does not help this anchor: Theorem 3’s objective and value ignore that structure. A computational problem that uses it would concern a different solution concept or a different objective.

Theorem 7 has a further modelling cost. Literally, an individual in an atomless population has measure zero, so removing that individual changes no aggregate worth; the paper’s leave-one-out marginal contribution vanishes. A reasonable repair is to use a type’s marginal contribution *rate*, derived from a finite-replication limit or a derivative of aggregate worth. That can make a continuous PANSC analogue well-defined, and I would not treat the repair itself as a fatal mismatch. But after the repair, the allocation again depends only on the type rates, the masses, and grand worth:
\[ y_t=\frac{b_tg}{\sum_s\mu_sb_s}. \]
The rest of the coalition-worth function drops out. If computing those rates is hard, that difficulty comes from a separately chosen representation of the worth function; it is not a computational question raised or analyzed by this paper.

So both anchors survive as possible continuous allocation formulas. The negative case is about fit and contribution: neither is a computational result, and neither proposed mirror opens the complexity landscape that motivates ChoCo. If “worthwhile” includes any coherent continuous restatement of an allocation value, the case against is weak: the Theorem 3 mirror in particular is sensible. If it means a useful computational mirror of this paper’s results, these two anchors do not make it.
