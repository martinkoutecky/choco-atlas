The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, cited from Gibbard (1977), says that with at least three alternatives, a strategy-proof, ex-post Pareto-optimal decision scheme is a random dictatorship. Theorem 2, proved here, adds anonymity and weak strategy-proofness under i.i.d. beliefs and concludes that the scheme is the anonymous random dictatorship. Theorem 3, also proved here, gives the corresponding conclusion for extended anonymous decision schemes under Poisson electorate models. These are characterization theorems, not results about computational complexity or algorithms.

The paper does offer support for the *plausibility* of a population-based model: it studies uncertainty generated by distributions over voter preferences, and its Poisson model assigns independent counts to preference types. But that does not provide a named computational result to mirror. Under the requested anchor rule, I therefore can’t make a paper-anchored positive case for a continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no anchor, so there is no continuous question here to defend. The paper’s Theorems 2 and 3 characterize which decision schemes satisfy axioms; Theorem 1 is cited from Gibbard. None states a complexity bound or an algorithmic result.

The deeper obstacle is that the paper gives no computational representation of a decision scheme. It treats \(d\) and \(D\) as arbitrary functions over profiles, including countably many profiles in the Poisson model. There is no encoded input, size measure, or specified operation whose complexity could be studied. Replacing count vectors with population masses would change the domain of those functions, but would not supply the missing computational problem. A continuum of types is plausible here; it does not by itself make the paper’s question computational.

One could introduce a representation—say, a circuit for a rule—and ask whether it admits a profitable manipulation at a rational mass distribution. That may be worthwhile research, but the choice of rule language would define the problem and its complexity. It would be a new computational project inspired by this paper, not a mirror of a result it proves. The Poisson model is relevant evidence for population uncertainty, but its voters remain discrete and its result remains axiomatic; neither point changes that conclusion.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as written: it offers no paper-anchored computational result for ChoCo to continuize. The limit of the claim is that a researcher could build a new, explicitly represented computational problem around the same setting. This material alone does not show that such adjacent work could never be worthwhile.
