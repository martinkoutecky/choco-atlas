The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case with a qualifying anchor: this paper has no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification.

The closest results are analytical. For example, Theorem 2 (proved here) says that when both agents are risk-averse, the Nash bargaining solution gives each utility \(1/2\), regardless of changes in risk aversion. Corollary 1 (proved here) says that when both are risk-lovers, a player’s Nash-solution utility increases as the opponent becomes more risk-loving. These are comparative-statics claims, not complexity results. Theorem 1 is a representation theorem attributed to Yaari, also not a computational result.

The paper’s continuum is over lotteries and outcomes; its society remains two bargaining agents. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong here: the paper contains no named result about computational complexity. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic representation theorem. Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 establish comparative statics for a two-person Nash bargaining problem. The strategic results likewise concern equilibrium existence and outcomes. None specifies a computational problem, an input encoding, or an algorithmic or hardness claim for a continuous population to mirror.

The best population version of Theorem 2 would have many independent bargaining pairs, with risk-preference types drawn from distributions. Each pair would still have two bargainers; with finite supports, one could evaluate the at most \(\tau_1\tau_2\) type-pair cases and aggregate their outcomes. The theorem supplies no population-level decision problem beyond that aggregation. Treating a whole mass as one bargaining party would require defining its collective preferences and bargaining objective. Those choices could be interesting, but the resulting theorem would concern a new model rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 2.

Corollary 1 has the same problem: it compares one opponent becoming more risk-loving. A distribution over risk types supplies no single opponent or specified aggregate comparison. Pairing agents independently again yields pairwise applications of the result; making the pairing or coalition formation consequential adds a new mechanism. Theorem 1 offers no better anchor: its continuity is over lotteries, while its preference relation belongs to an individual. Making that preference theorem computational would continuize the outcome or representation, not the population.

One can imagine building a computational bargaining problem around a continuum of people, but this paper does not identify the collective choice, constraints, or algorithmic question that would make it the paper’s mirror. The case is strongest for that paper-centered judgment; it cannot rule out every worthwhile model someone might build from its ideas.
