The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of complete strict rankings over seat categories \(A\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_a\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{a\in A}q_a\ge 1\), and nonnegative rational category values \(w_a\), choose \(x_{ta}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{a\in A}x_{ta}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{ta}\le q_a\). For each type \(t\), let \(a^t_1,\ldots,a^t_m\) list its categories from best to worst and define \(F_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{t,a^t_j}\). Call feasible \(y\) a Pareto improvement on \(x\) if \(F_{t,k}(y)\ge F_{t,k}(x)\) for every \(t,k\), with strict inequality for some \(t,k\). Find a feasible \(x\) that admits no such improvement and maximizes \(W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in A}w_a x_{ta}\) among all such assignments.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic assignment market with ranking types \(t\), mass \(\mu_t\), per-capita category supplies \(q_a\), and category values \(w_a\); the decision variables \(x_{ta}\) allocate whole seats to portions of each type, and the objective is maximum welfare among assignments Pareto-efficient under type-level rank-prefix comparisons.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2’s reduction uses individually tailored preferences, so it does not establish hardness when the continuous instance has a small support of recurring ranking types.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s constrained-welfare allocation problem under complete rankings and object-based values; it leaves the paper’s other complexity results, its agent-specific distance-weight case study, and its incentive results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the stated problem in the number of categories \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does the type-level Pareto condition admit a useful price or linear-program characterization?
  • Which continuous assignments can be rounded to discrete allocations while preserving welfare and Pareto efficiency?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s central optimization problem, ConstrainedWelfareMax, in a large assignment market. A plausible regime is a large school or university system: many thousands of students, a much smaller set of schools or programs, and a relatively small support of recurring complete preference rankings. A type is one complete ranking; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students with ranking \(t\). Seats scale with the population: \(q_a\) is the number of seats of category \(a\) per person. The planner’s value \(w_a\) for assigning someone to category \(a\) is the same for every student, as in the paper’s object-based weights. Such values could encode a policy goal such as maintaining enrollment across programs. Every student still receives one whole seat; the continuous variables record what fraction of each type goes to each category.

Lead mirror: Continuous Constrained-WelfareMax. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of ranking types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(A\) of seat categories with rational per-capita supplies \(q_a\) satisfying \(\sum_a q_a\ge 1\), a complete strict ranking \(R_t\) of \(A\) for each type, and nonnegative rational values \(w_a\). A feasible assignment is a matrix \(x\) with \(x_{ta}\ge 0\), \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{ta}\le q_a\). Its welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,a}w_a x_{ta}\).

To define Pareto efficiency for this mass assignment, let \(a^t_1,\ldots,a^t_m\) be type \(t\)’s ranking from best to worst, and set \(F_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^k x_{t,a^t_j}\). A feasible \(y\) Pareto-improves \(x\) if \(F_{t,k}(y)\ge F_{t,k}(x)\) for every \(t,k\), with strict inequality for at least one pair. These prefix inequalities say that the mass of each type receiving one of its top \(k\) choices never decreases. For a continuum of indistinguishable agents within each type, they capture whether assignments can be coupled so that no individual is worse off and a positive mass is better off. The problem is to return a feasible \(x\) that is Pareto-efficient and has greatest \(W(x)\) among all Pareto-efficient assignments.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: “CONSTRAINEDWELFAREMAX is NP-hard even under object-based weights and complete preferences.” In full, finding a highest-welfare Pareto-efficient allocation is NP-hard even when every agent strictly ranks every object and the welfare weight of an object is identical for all agents. The paper proves this by reduction from the NP-hard problem of finding a minimum-size Pareto-efficient allocation, completing preferences and adding objects whose weights make the welfare objective encode the number of agents left unassigned in the source problem. That is a direct anchor for the mirror’s underlying allocation problem, rather than for its integer-program encoding.

The match to the paper is unusually close: both versions allocate objects or seats, respect ordinal preferences through Pareto efficiency, and maximize the planner’s sum of object-based values. The required co-modification—scaling seat supplies with population—is natural for a large school or program system. The paper itself treats multiple seats as copies of objects, so grouping equivalent seats into categories preserves its allocation story. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s restricted hardness result; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s other results or its full agent-specific distance-weight case.

My initial guess is Class B, hardness transfers, because the reduction’s apparent source of difficulty is the preference and object structure, not merely a small population being copied many times. But that is a hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 2. The continuous assignment can split a type across categories, and that may defeat the finite reduction’s choices. The key research question is whether its object-and-preference combinatorics can still encode hardness when mass is divisible. If not, the resulting problem might instead admit a polyhedral or price-based algorithm.

The discrete bridge should be part of the investigation: counts \(n_t\) and seat counts \(s_a\) give \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(q_a=s_a/n\), and any discrete allocation yields a feasible mass assignment. The reverse direction is subtler because a continuous assignment can split a type’s mass. Further questions are whether the mirror is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), whether Pareto efficiency has a useful price or LP characterization in this setting, and which rational continuous solutions can be rounded to discrete allocations while preserving welfare and efficiency.

The weakest point is the gap between the paper’s indivisible agents and the mirror’s divisible mass within a type. Also, complete rankings and object-based values narrow the application regime; the paper’s Estonian case study uses agent-specific distance weights and incomplete preference data. I still regard the proposed school or university regime as credible for the stated restrictions, but those limitations are why I present a precise, bounded mirror of Theorem 2 rather than a claim about the whole paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves NP-hardness for ConstrainedWelfareMax under complete preferences and object-based weights. So the objection cannot be that the paper lacks a computational problem to mirror.

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that Theorem 2’s hardness construction is built from individually tailored gadgets. Its reduction adds \(|N|\) distinct objects and uses particular agents’ rankings to encode a minimum-size Pareto-efficient allocation. A population model with a fixed set of recurring types cannot carry that construction over directly: preserving the gadget agents’ distinct rankings makes the support grow with the population, while merging them would change their type-level preferences. The paper’s most concrete application has the same pressure in another form: student-specific distances are part of the welfare weights, so treating those weights as part of a complete type can leave little repetition.

The proposed restriction to shared object values and recurring rankings avoids that support problem, but it narrows the connection to the paper’s practical cases. It also needs care with supply. If per-capita seat supply sums to exactly \(1\), every seat category is filled, so welfare is fixed at \(\sum_a q_a w_a\). The continuous objective then loses the trade-off that makes Theorem 2 interesting; this echoes the paper’s balanced, complete, object-based case in Theorem 4. Persistent excess capacity avoids that collapse, and excess seats are plausible in school allocation, but it is an additional feature the mirror needs in order to retain a nontrivial objective.

That is a case for treating this as a narrow, independently motivated mirror, not as a continuation of Theorem 2’s hardness result. It does not defeat the better version the proponent offers: recurring ranking cohorts, common values by school or program, and persistent excess capacity define a coherent continuous allocation problem. Fractional type assignments have a natural interpretation as assigning whole seats to positive-measure groups of indistinguishable agents. I cannot honestly rule that scenario out or argue that it is already solved. The negative case is therefore weak on the user’s universal standard: it identifies a mismatch between the paper’s reduction and the high-multiplicity regime, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists.
