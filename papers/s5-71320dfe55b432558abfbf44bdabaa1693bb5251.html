The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over provider types \(\theta=(p_{\theta j},c_{\theta j},b_\theta)_{j\in J}\), a dependency DAG \(D\), and a rational workload rate \(\lambda\) of workflow copies per provider, determine the exact, approximation, or parameterized complexity of finding a maximum-expected-welfare assignment of task occurrences to provider mass, where each unit of mass receives an integral bundle of size at most \(b_\theta\), and computing the limiting per-provider externality payment from the paper’s rule.

The model it lives in

A mass configuration model over provider types \(\theta\) with distribution \(\mu\), finite per-provider workload capacity \(b_\theta\), and \(\lambda\) copies of a dependent workflow per provider; allocate integral task bundles to maximize expected welfare and derive per-provider externality payments.

The objection that survived

The proposed supply of each provider type is nonbinding under the paper’s unlimited task bundles, so the mass model needs a finite per-provider capacity assumption.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the welfare-maximizing assignment and externality-payment computation underlying Theorem 6 for dependent tasks; it does not transfer Theorem 6’s equilibrium guarantees or cover the independent-task mechanism and the impossibility results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does pricing over integral provider bundles for dependent workflows admit an efficient algorithm in \(\tau\), \(|D|\), and the input bit length, or does the DAG structure retain hardness?
  • Can the finite-agent externality payment be given a well-defined per-provider limit while preserving a meaningful individual truth-telling condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: a large pool of cloud or network service providers, with many providers sharing the same complete cost and reliability vector \(\theta=(p_j,c_j)_{j\in J}\) across a fixed set of service classes. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(n\) large and support size \(\tau\) small. To avoid making the paper’s finite set of tasks negligible, task demand would need to scale with the provider pool. A mass \(x_{\theta j}\) of type \(\theta\) could be assigned to task class \(j\), with each provider still receiving an integral bundle and \(x_{\theta j}\) representing the fraction assigned. Expected welfare per capita would be
\[ \sum_{j\in J} v_j\sum_\theta p_{\theta j}x_{\theta j} -\sum_{\theta,j}c_{\theta j}x_{\theta j}. \]
That aggregate allocation problem looks tractable as a linear optimization problem.

But it does not give this paper a qualifying anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says the Single Task Mechanism satisfies individual rationality, incentive compatibility, center rationality, social efficiency, and no free riders. Its allocation selects an agent maximizing \(p_iV-c_i\). That is a mechanism-design guarantee, not a stated complexity result. Proposition 2 and Theorems 3, 5, and 6 likewise concern mechanism properties or impossibility: for example, Theorem 3 rules out a mechanism satisfying IC, IR, CR, and SE for combinatorial valuations when \(n,t\ge2\). None classifies a computational problem. The paper itself says computation of its allocation and payment rules presents non-trivial algorithmic problems and leaves that work for the future.

The continuous allocation LP above is therefore only an unanchored candidate, not a problem I can honestly present as the continuous form of a named computational result in this paper. More seriously, it captures the welfare-maximization objective while leaving the paper’s central strategic guarantees unresolved. A prover-ready extension could ask whether a direct mechanism on a finite-support type distribution can assign mass to task classes and pay providers so that truthful reporting is optimal for each provider, truthful allocation maximizes expected welfare, and IR and center rationality hold. But the paper neither formulates nor solves that continuum mechanism problem, and the finite-agent theorems do not establish what happens to its incentive constraints there.

So the positive case is that this domain has a credible population regime and a natural mass-allocation model. The weakest point is decisive for the requested anchoring standard: the paper contains no named computational result to mirror, and its mechanism guarantees do not by themselves supply one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The single-task LP is too weak to carry the case: with a fixed number of tasks it does not capture population scale, and its allocation rule is just a maximum over types. The better anchor is Theorem 6, whose mechanism for dependent tasks chooses a welfare-maximizing assignment. The paper’s discussion explicitly identifies computing its allocation and payments as a nontrivial algorithmic problem. That gives a computational object tied to a named result, even though the paper does not classify its complexity.

A continuous question is to take providers with complete reliability-and-cost types \(\theta=(p_{\theta j},c_{\theta j})_{j\in J}\), represented by a finite-support distribution \(\mu\), and replicate a fixed task-dependency DAG in proportion to the provider population. Providers still receive integral task bundles; \(\mu_\theta\) records how much supply of each type is available. For the equilibrium mechanism of Theorem 6, ask for the exact, approximation, or parameterized complexity of computing a welfare-maximizing assignment and its induced per-provider payments, in terms of support size, DAG size, and input bit length. One natural formulation uses columns for integral assignments of a workflow and asks whether the resulting configuration LP has an efficient pricing algorithm.

This keeps the paper’s strategic guarantee and its dependency structure, while making the population the continuous object. The additive, independent-task version may be easy; the dependent-task case is where the algorithmic question becomes substantive. So “there is no numbered complexity theorem” is literally true, but it does not rule out a worthwhile mirror: the paper names the mechanism, leaves its computation open, and directly points toward the proposed problem. The caveat is that this is a computational continuation of Theorem 6, not a continuous counterpart of a complexity classification the paper already proved.
