The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Propositions 1–5 are numbered results, but they characterize equilibria and report expected payoffs and honesty rates; none classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithmic result of the kind your anchor rule requires. The experimental Results 1–4 do not supply one either.

The closest result is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. For the finitely repeated competition treatment, it gives a subgame-perfect equilibrium in which players form reciprocal pairs, report one strong and one weak preference per pair until the endgame, and follow a specified breakup strategy after a deviation. The equilibrium yields expected payoff \(13/8\) per period and honesty rates \(H_w=H_s=3/4\). That is an equilibrium characterization, not a computational-complexity result.

A plausible extension would take a large population of agents with shared types given by their complete vectors of project priorities, represent those types by masses \(\mu_\theta\), and study how partner links and reports form in a continuum version of the competition treatment. It fits the paper’s strategic subject, but the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor that question. Its 40 periods and linked projects do not themselves make the population continuous. Under the requested standard, there are no anchor-specific continuous problems to hand to a prover, so I cannot make a grounded case for this paper as a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only candidate is Proposition 3, so that is the anchor to test. It characterizes an equilibrium of a finite repeated game in groups of four: players choose partners, and reciprocal pairs can link two projects by reporting one strong and one weak preference. It gives strategies, expected payoffs, and honesty rates. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result. Under the requested standard, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The strongest population version of Proposition 3 still splits in two. Keep groups of four, with a continuum of groups, and the population is just a distribution over copies of the same finite game. Averaging their outcomes does not create a new computational problem. Enlarge the groups so partner choice becomes a population-level matching problem, and the model changes: its central object is now a dynamic matching network, with public histories driving partner selection and punishment. A mass distribution over current high and low priorities cannot capture that process. One could augment it with distributions over histories and links, but then the proposed research question is an algorithmic problem for a new continuum dynamic game, not a mirror of Proposition 3.

A broader attempt to use complete priority vectors as types has the same issue. Priorities are drawn independently for each project and period; they are not persistent preference types that explain the partner relationships. For independent random pairs, a continuum over those draws merely averages the same pairwise game. For endogenous partner choice, the history and matching state does the substantive work. Making the projects or periods continuous would change another axis of the model, while pooling the exogenous report budgets across people would define a different mechanism. The paper’s natural multiplicity is in projects and repeated interactions, not in a large society of agents.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo complexity mirror: neither Proposition 3 nor the paper’s other results supplies a computational anchor, and the obvious population extensions either replicate finite games or introduce a new dynamic matching problem. I cannot establish the literal claim that no worthwhile continuum game could ever be built from these ideas; that broader claim is weaker. Such a game may be worthwhile in its own right, but the paper does not ground it as a continuous mirror.
