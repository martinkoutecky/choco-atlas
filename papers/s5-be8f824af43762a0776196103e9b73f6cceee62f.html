The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(\theta\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), rational additive valuations \(v_\theta(j)\), positive rational entitlements \(w_\theta\), and item classes \(j\in J\) with rational per-capita supplies \(s_j\), find an explicitly listed, polynomial-size rational assignment \(\pi_{\theta,B}\) of mass to integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\). Require \(\sum_B\pi_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,B}\pi_{\theta,B}B_j=s_j\), and that every pair of positive-mass bundle groups \((\theta,B),(\phi,D)\) satisfy WEF1T as evaluated by type \(\theta\): either \(v_\theta(B)/w_\theta\ge v_\theta(D)/w_\phi\), or transferring one item from either bundle to the other makes that inequality hold. Is such an assignment computable in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A continuum of complete agent types carrying mass, with each unit of mass receiving an integral bundle. The assignment variables \(\pi_{\theta,B}\) distribute mass over bundles, while item-class supplies scale per capita; there is no welfare objective.

The objection that survived

The WEF1T constraints are disjunctive across bundle configurations, and neither Theorem 1 nor the goods/chores split supplies a type-compressed procedure for handling them; the proponent acknowledges the missing compression but gives no way to address those constraints.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's WEF1T construction for mixed manna. It leaves aside Theorem 2's ordinal impossibility result, the examples in Section 3.3, and Theorem 14's two-agent WEF1 plus fractional Pareto-optimality algorithm.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a feasible WEF1T assignment be computed in polynomial time in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length?
  • Can every feasible rational instance be represented by a polynomial-size rational support?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that for any number of agents, if \(A(G)\) is a WEF1 allocation of the goods and neutral items and \(A(C)\) is a WEF1 allocation of the chores, then combining them agent by agent gives a WEF1T allocation of the mixed manna instance. The theorem supplies the key construction; using the polynomial-time WEF1 procedures cited in §3.1, the paper concludes that WEF1T mixed-manna allocation is computable in polynomial time. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a hardness mirror, so this is the strongest relevant result.

I would call the mirror Continuous WEF1T for Mixed Manna, and expect it to be tractable. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, each with an additive valuation \(v_\theta(j)\) for each item class \(j\) and entitlement \(w_\theta>0\). The type masses \(\mu_\theta\) are binary rationals summing to \(1\). Each item class consists of copies with the same valuation vector across agent types, and has rational supply \(s_j\) per unit of population. The decision variable is \(\pi_{\theta,B}\): the mass of type-\(\theta\) agents assigned the integral bundle \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\). It must satisfy \(\sum_B\pi_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,B}\pi_{\theta,B}B_j=s_j\). The requested solution is a finite-support rational \(\pi\); there is no welfare objective in the anchored result.

For every two positive-mass bundle groups \((\theta,B)\) and \((\phi,D)\), require WEF1T using the paper’s weighted, one-item-transfer notion: as evaluated by type \(\theta\), either \(v_\theta(B)/w_\theta\ge v_\theta(D)/w_\phi\), or moving one item from \(B\) to \(D\), or one item from \(D\) to \(B\), makes that inequality hold. Thus the mass variables describe how many agents receive each bundle; every agent’s bundle remains integral. A natural complexity question is whether such a solution can be found in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length.

A plausible regime is a large university system allocating recurring teaching and service duties. It may have thousands of faculty but only a modest number of complete valuation-and-entitlement profiles, with many sections or duties in each item class. As the institution grows, the supply of those duties grows per capita. This keeps the paper’s additive valuations, unequal entitlements, mixed goods and chores, and individual item transfers intact. The paper’s Table 1 also points to work on weighted fair allocation with few chore types, supporting the plausibility of low-type regimes in this area.

I would classify the mirror as Class A, as a conjecture rather than a consequence of Theorem 1. The theorem’s goods/chores split suggests a route: solve the two sides in a way that respects the type masses, then combine them. It would take new high-multiplicity procedures to do this without expanding the population into a named-agent list. Further questions include whether every rational instance has a finite-support solution, whether its support can be bounded polynomially, and how far WEF1T can be combined with Pareto efficiency in this regime.

The weak point is that Theorem 1’s construction relies on finite-agent WEF1 allocations, and the paper does not show that those procedures compress to polynomial time in the number of types. The per-capita item supply is also a co-modification of the model. I think authors could recognize the mirror because it preserves individual integral bundles and their weighted fairness comparisons while making repeated agents and duties explicit; whether the resulting type-level construction is efficient remains the open part. This case covers Theorem 1’s WEF1T result only. Theorem 14’s \(n=2\) restriction and Theorem 2’s ordinal impossibility do not provide comparably good population-complexity anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 1 offers little structure for a continuous algorithm. Its proof combines separate WEF1 allocations for goods and chores; it does not exploit the number of agents in a way that obviously compresses by type. In the proposed mirror, bundles remain integral and WEF1T is checked between every pair of bundle groups. The population continuum therefore leaves the one-item combinatorics intact: it replaces named agents with a distribution over integral bundle configurations. Those pairwise conditions are disjunctive, and the theorem gives no method for handling them in time polynomial in the number of types and the input bit length.

A finite expansion does guarantee feasibility. For rational masses and per-capita supplies, clear denominators to obtain a finite instance and apply Theorem 1. But the resulting number of agents can be exponential in the binary input length, so this does not give an efficient compressed algorithm. Keeping a fixed item stock as the population grows would dilute the resources; the stronger mirror avoids that by scaling item supplies per capita. That is a reasonable co-modification, though it makes the computational question about compressing both repeated agents and repeated item classes.

Those points make the tractability conjecture weak, but they do not defeat the mirror. The proposed model preserves the paper’s integral bundles and weighted fairness comparisons, and a large institution with repeated faculty profiles and recurring duties is a plausible regime. The per-capita supply adjustment is allowed by the programme, while existing work on few agent types supports the regime rather than colliding with it. I can argue that this may be a specialized high-multiplicity algorithm problem whose continuous formulation does not smooth the fairness constraints. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; the case against is weak, and the mirror survives.
