The continuous mirror question

Given a finite family \(H\) of cell templates \(h\), each a finite hypergraph \(G_h\) of girth at least \(4\) with monotone valuations, and rational cell masses \(\alpha_h\), compute a complete allocation \(X^h\) for each positive-mass template such that \(X^h\) is EFX for its local agents. Copying \(X^h\) to almost every cell of type \(h\) must yield an aggregate allocation rule.

The model it lives in

An atomless population of repeated cells, each with its own copy of a finite template's goods and agents. Agent types are the template roles \((h,i)\), with mass \(\mu_{h,i}=\alpha_h/\sum_{k\in H}\alpha_k|V_k|\); local goods are canonically relabeled across cells and valued across cells at zero. The decision is a complete integral allocation for each template, with feasibility defined by EFX within each cell.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's polynomial construction for hypergraphs and Theorem 2's pseudo-polynomial construction for qualifying multi-hypergraphs, applied template by template. It leaves the reduction in Lemma 4.1 and the general EFX existence problem untouched; the cited hardness of EFX orientations concerns a different problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • For qualifying multi-hypergraph templates, can Theorem 2's pseudo-polynomial dependence be replaced by a bit-polynomial bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a continuum of repeated, independent local fair-division markets. A cell might be a small department allocating shared equipment: its agents are vertices, its indivisible goods are hyperedges, and only agents at an edge’s endpoints value that good. Many cells recur, with the same finite set of role types and the same valuation profiles up to renaming the cell’s goods. The role types are complete descriptions in this model; cell names have no preference-relevant meaning.

Formally, let \(H\) be a finite set of cell templates, with cell mass \(\alpha_h\) for each \(h\in H\). Template \(h\) has agents \(V_h\), goods \(E_h\), a hypergraph \(G_h\), and normalized monotone valuations \(v_{h,i}\). The atomless population consists of cells of these templates. Its type \(t=(h,i)\) has mass \(\mu_{h,i}=\alpha_h/\sum_{k\in H}\alpha_k|V_k|\). Goods belong to their own cell; agents value goods in other cells at zero.

Call the resulting problem Continuum Cellwise EFX. Given the templates and their rational masses, find, for each \(h\), a distribution over complete allocations of that cell, supported entirely on EFX allocations. The atomless mass of cells lets us implement each distribution by partitioning those cells accordingly. Each person still receives an integral bundle of indivisible goods. EFX within cells is the paper’s exact fairness condition; between cells, agents value one another’s goods at zero. The objective is feasibility, and the solution is a finite allocation rule for each template.

My lead is Theorem 1 (the result labeled \(\texttt{thm:girth4}\), proved here). It says that hypergraph instances of girth at least \(4\), with heterogeneous normalized monotone valuations, always have an EFX allocation, constructible in polynomial time in the number of agents and goods. The continuous question is: if every cell template is such a hypergraph, can we construct the allocation distributions? I expect Class A for this finite-support cell model: solve each template once using the theorem, then repeat that allocation across its cells. This is a narrow but faithful high-multiplicity question, with population mass recording how much of the society occupies each role.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 2 (labeled \(\texttt{thm:girth4\_multi}\), proved here). It says that a multi-hypergraph whose underlying simple hypergraph has girth at least \(4\) admits an EFX allocation, constructible in pseudo-polynomial time, if some vertex has incident-edge multiplicities at most \(|e|-2\) for every incident edge \(e\). Its mirror asks the same allocation-rule question when every cell has that multi-hypergraph structure and a qualifying vertex. I tentatively expect tractability for fixed finite-support templates, since each cell template can be solved once. The theorem only gives a pseudo-polynomial bound, though, so it does not establish polynomial time for binary-encoded valuation magnitudes. A useful next question is whether the continuous representation permits a bit-polynomial algorithm.

The authors should recognize these as their problem: the local agents, indivisible goods, valuations, incidence restrictions, and EFX comparisons are unchanged. Only the population regime changes: many copies of a finite set of local roles replace a finite list of individually named agents. This is also why the construction is a limited case, not a proposed mirror of every hypergraph instance.

The weak point is that independence between cells makes the mirror unusually separable. If a referee treats cell-specific good identities as preference-relevant, agents in different cells may count as different types and the support would grow with the population. The mirror depends on the natural resource-allocation convention that local good names can be canonically relabeled. Also, these anchors are existence-and-algorithm results, not hardness results: the paper has no named hardness theorem for EFX allocation itself. Its cited NP-completeness result concerns EFX orientations, a different problem. So this case supports a tractable continuous analogue of the paper’s two constructive results; it does not show that discrete EFX hardness dissolves, or resolve the general EFX existence problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors are genuine computational results, so I would not argue that the paper lacks something to mirror. The problem is that the proposed continuum cell model makes cells, rather than agents, the units of mass. EFX is still checked among the finite agents inside each cell.

That grouping is necessary to preserve the paper’s question. EFX compares one agent’s bundle with another’s, and the hypergraph specifies which agents share each good. The masses \(\mu_{h,i}\) alone do not say which agent roles occur together around the same goods. To recover that information, the model must retain each whole cell template—with its agents, goods, incidence structure, and valuations—as a unit. Then Theorem 1 applies separately to each template, and its allocation can simply be repeated in every copy. The cell masses do no computational work.

A distribution over EFX allocations for a template does not change this. The theorem already guarantees an EFX allocation, so a deterministic choice suffices; between cells, comparisons are vacuous because agents value other cells’ goods at zero. The result is a batch of finite instances, not an EFX problem whose society has become continuous. A global quota or aggregate objective could couple the cells, but that would add a new question absent from the theorem.

The same issue defeats the proposed mirror of Theorem 2. Each qualifying multi-hypergraph remains a finite indivisible-goods instance. The theorem can be applied to it and its allocation repeated across cells. Continuous cell mass neither alters the multiplicities within a cell nor supplies a population-level computational task. Asking whether masses permit a better bit-polynomial algorithm would require specifying what those masses change in the problem; as stated, they change nothing.

A more literal continuum of agents does not preserve the paper’s object. With finitely many indivisible goods, assigning them to individual members of an atomless population affects zero mass, while assigning average bundles to type masses can give fractional bundles and erase the individual EFX comparisons. Scaling goods with the population could avoid that mismatch, but preserving EFX would then require specifying which agents share each good and comparing agents within those groups—the cell construction again.

This is a substantial negative case against the proposed mirrors: they preserve the theorem by keeping its finite allocation problems intact, while the continuous masses are incidental. It is not an airtight claim that no conceivable continuum model could be worthwhile. A richer scaling limit might be possible, but the paper supplies no canonical one, and defining it would require a new model of how goods, incidence, and EFX comparisons scale.
