The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant positive case with a named computational anchor: the paper contains no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is tractable, hard, or complete.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that for every finite \(n\ge2\), every subgame-perfect equilibrium of PA&C gives each player her marginal contribution, and the mechanism subgame-perfect implements exactly the efficient alternatives: every equilibrium selects an efficient alternative, and each efficient alternative is selected by some equilibrium. Theorem 3, also proved here, says that under a uniformly random order, every subgame-perfect equilibrium’s expected payoffs equal the Shapley value of the associated TU game. These are equilibrium characterizations, not complexity results.

A population setting is imaginable: a large stakeholder body could have a distribution \(\mu\) over utility types, with each type describing a complete utility vector over the alternatives. But the paper’s sequential mechanism gives strategic roles and rejection rights to individual players. Treating each type’s mass as a bargaining player would change that structure, and the paper offers no result that validates the change. So I don’t see a continuous computational problem here that can honestly be presented as a mirror of one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s opening point is the strongest negative case: the paper states no computational complexity result. Theorem 1 characterizes subgame-perfect equilibrium payoffs and outcomes; Theorem 3 identifies expected payoffs with the Shapley value. Neither asks whether a computational problem is tractable, hard, or approximable. Their continuous versions might be interesting equilibrium or limit theorems, but they would not mirror the computational results this programme is designed to study.

The best attempt to rescue Theorem 3 is a large bargaining population with a distribution over utility types, and a continuous version of randomized PA&C whose aggregate payoffs approach a Shapley allocation. That population is conceivable. But replacing repeated types by masses changes the game: individuals take turns, hold distinct positions, and pay or receive transfers through those positions. Grouping them into a type mass loses that structure; putting order position into the type restores it only by making position part of the population description. Either way, the resulting continuum game is a new model, not a validated high-multiplicity version of the paper’s mechanism.

One could then ask how to compute its limiting Shapley allocation. That is a plausible adjacent question, but it is not one the paper poses or answers. The same applies to Proposition 8’s balanced-VCG/ENSC identity: it relates payoff formulas, without establishing a computational problem. A new algorithmic question built on either result might be worthwhile, but it cannot supply the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated criterion: no named computational result survives because none is present. It cannot prove that no useful continuous bargaining or Shapley computation project could ever be devised; that would overstate the evidence. It can say that such a project would be a new research direction, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
