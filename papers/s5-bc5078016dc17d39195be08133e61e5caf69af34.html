Why no mirror

A cohort-reversal condition over a distribution of rankings is a coherent extension of half-way monotonicity. But Theorems 4 and 6 establish impossibilities about voting-rule axioms, not computational results; using SAT to prove them does not change what they assert. The proposed mirrors therefore fail the computational gate.

What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort condition parallels Theorems 4 and 6, including their set-valued variants; it leaves Proposition 3, Theorem 7, and Corollary 10 without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that complete rankings already form a natural type space, so this paper has a plausible population-level extension. But it does not supply a named computational-complexity result of the kind ChoCo requires. Its named results are SAT-derived existence and impossibility theorems about voting axioms; SAT is the proof method, not a complexity classification of an underlying input-output problem.

A plausible extension would take a society to be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings of \(A\). For each pair \(a,b\), \(a\) is a Condorcet winner when it has mass greater than \(1/2\) against \(b\). If \(t\) is a ranking, let \(t^{\mathrm{rev}}\) be its reverse, and define \(R_{t,\epsilon}\mu=\mu-\epsilon\delta_t+\epsilon\delta_{t^{\mathrm{rev}}}\). A continuous resolute rule would be a function \(F\) from such societies to alternatives. It would select any strict Condorcet winner, and satisfy cohort reversal monotonicity: \(F(\mu)\succeq_t F(R_{t,\epsilon}\mu)\) for every \(t\) and \(0\le\epsilon\le\mu_t\).

The regime is plausible: a large membership referendum or citywide consultation could have many participants but comparatively few distinct rankings. A type is a complete ranking; its mass is the share of participants with that ranking; the action is to reverse the reports of some of that share. The problem would be to decide whether such a rule exists, with a rule itself as a positive solution. That is a coherent population-level axiom question, but it asks for existence of a function over an entire simplex. The paper supplies no succinct computational instance or algorithmic objective for it.

The closest named anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for \(m\ge4\) alternatives and every odd \(n\ge15\), no resolute Condorcet extension satisfies half-way monotonicity. This gives the continuous extension an immediate negative answer. Restrict any proposed \(F\) to the distributions induced by 15-voter profiles, with each voter contributing mass \(1/15\). Reversing one vote of type \(t\) is exactly \(R_{t,1/15}\), so the restriction would contradict Theorem 4. This is a direct transfer of the impossibility, not a new tractability or hardness result.

A second nearby result is Theorem 6, also proved here: for \(m\ge4\), odd \(n\ge15\) or even \(n\ge24\), no set-valued Condorcet extension satisfies either pessimistic or optimistic half-way monotonicity. Its continuous counterpart would return a nonempty set of alternatives, be the singleton strict Condorcet winner when one exists, and impose the corresponding condition on the best or worst member of the set after a mass reversal. Restricting to the finite profiles covered by the theorem again transfers the impossibility directly.

The weakest point is also the decisive one for ChoCo: reversing positive mass is a bloc deviation, whereas the paper studies one voter reversing a report. An individual has zero mass in the continuum, so individual manipulation disappears unless the model adds a positive-mass cohort action. That addition is sensible, but it changes the question into an axiomatic study of group deviations. Theorem 4 is the lead for that limited extension; Theorem 6 supports its set-valued variant. These are the paper’s best candidates, but they do not establish a computational mirror in ChoCo’s sense.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the kind of result the paper proves. Theorem 4 and Theorem 6 assert that certain classes of voting rules cannot satisfy specified axioms. The SAT solver is the proof method; the paper does not classify the complexity of an input-output problem. That distinction matters for ChoCo: a continuous population can make the axioms more natural to state, but it does not turn an existence or impossibility theorem about rules into a computational result.

The proposed population model itself is coherent. Let rankings be types, let \(\mu\) give their population shares, and consider reversing a cohort of mass \(\epsilon\):
\[ \mu'=\mu-\epsilon\delta_t+\epsilon\delta_{t^{\mathrm{rev}}}. \]
A rule could select any strict Condorcet winner and require that type \(t\) weakly prefer its outcome before the cohort reversal. This fits large consultations with repeated rankings. And restricting such a rule to societies induced by finite profiles, with \(\epsilon=1/n\), recovers the individual reversal studied in Theorem 4. Theorem 6 transfers similarly to optimistic and pessimistic set-valued rules.

That transfer makes the continuous axiom question well posed, but it does not supply a computational mirror. Its central question remains whether a rule exists that satisfies the axioms; the embedded finite profiles already force the negative answer. This is not an objection that the answer is dull or known. It is that the result ChoCo would need to continuize—a complexity classification of an algorithmic problem—is absent.

The best attempt to get one is to ask for an algorithm that, given a rule and a society, finds a profitable cohort reversal. But that requires a representation of the rule over the whole simplex and a specification of what counts as an input and a deviation. The paper supplies neither: its positive rules are lookup tables on finite profile spaces, and its impossibility proofs establish no complexity bound for finding a manipulation. A complexity result for a chosen representation of continuous rules could be worthwhile, but it would be a new problem, not a mirror of these theorems.

The same issue applies to any stronger anchor from the paper: the forced outcome in Theorem 7 and the disjunctive manipulation statement in Corollary 10 are also claims about rules and axioms, not complexity results. So I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no plausible continuous mirror exists. The cohort-reversal model is plausible. I can defend the narrower, decisive negative for ChoCo’s stated scope: this paper offers a sensible axiomatic continuization, but no named computational result to mirror.
