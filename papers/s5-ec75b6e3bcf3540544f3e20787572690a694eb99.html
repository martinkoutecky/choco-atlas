The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), a compatibility graph \(H\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), per-type load profiles, and an encoded \(m+a\) value formula extending the paper’s energy objective to type-mass vectors, determine the supremum of \(\sum_{x\in\mathcal P}v(x)\) to additive error \(\epsilon\). Here \(\mathcal P\) is any finite collection of nonzero vectors \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{T}\) whose positive-support types induce a connected subgraph of \(H\), and \(\sum_{x\in\mathcal P}x=\mu\); return a feasible collection and a certified upper bound with gap at most \(\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A type records a household load profile and every eligibility role used by the objective or graph. The population has mass \(\mu_t\) at each type, and its network is the block blow-up of \(H\): members within a type and across linked types are mutually adjacent. A coalition is a real type-mass vector with connected support; a solution partitions the total mass into finitely many such vectors and maximizes their summed energy-purchasing value, including the mass-scaled management term.

The objection that survived

The \(-|C|^\gamma\) management term can make the continuous supremum unattained as mass is split into ever-smaller coalitions; the proponent’s additive-error formulation does not settle the output-size or certificate complexity, and the discrete merge-tree bound does not transfer automatically.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the energy-purchasing coalition objective and poses a continuous anytime-certificate question for block-structured eligibility graphs. It leaves the discrete edge-contraction theorem, sparse individualized networks, other benchmark functions, parallel speedups, and empirical results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the supremum be approximated with a certified upper bound in time polynomial in the type count, input bit length, and a specified function of \(1/\epsilon\)?
  • Can a valid continuous subtree bound account for arbitrary splitting of type mass, and when is the energy objective’s supremum attained?
  • Does discrete GCCF hardness transfer when each type’s mass may be split among multiple coalitions?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a continuous, high-multiplicity version of the paper’s energy-purchasing GCCF problem. It covers the paper’s optimization and bounding result, not every application or the empirical speedups.

Imagine a large energy cooperative with many households but a modest catalog of load profiles and participation roles. A type records everything the objective and eligibility rules use: its consumption profile, prices or costs, and its role in the communication network. Let \(T\) be these types, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of households of type \(t\), and let \(H\) connect types whose members can form a coalition. To make the graph model exact, take the corresponding individual network to be a blow-up of \(H\): members within a type are mutually connected, and linked types have complete cross-links. Then \(N\) may be in the hundreds of thousands while \(\tau=|T|\) is in the tens or hundreds. This is a particular instance regime, not a claim that the paper’s 2,732 measured households already have few types.

The computational anchor is the theorem in Section 5 cited internally as prop:4. The supplied text prints its heading as “Theorem” without a numeral, so I cannot quote a printed theorem number. It is proved here: the body gives a proof sketch and says the full proof is in the online appendix. Its content is that, for an \(m+a\) value \(V=V^++V^-\), a current search-tree state \(CS_i\) has the upper bound
\[ M(CS_i)=V^-(CS_i)+V^+(\overline{CS_i}) \ge \max\{V(CS_j):CS_j\in ST(CS_i)\}, \]
where \(\overline{CS_i}\) is formed by removing red edges from the state’s two-coloured graph and contracting the remaining green edges. CFSS computes with this bound to prune the search and to give anytime guarantees. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a theorem that the problem is in P. The paper’s introduction attributes GCCF’s NP-completeness to Voice et al.; that is prior work, not a hardness result proved here.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass-GCCF-\(m+a\). An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), a compatibility graph \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and explicit rational formulas for a coalition value \(v(x)=v^+(x)+v^-(x)\), where \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\) is a coalition’s type-mass vector. The formulas satisfy the paper’s \(m+a\) promise: whenever two disjoint portions of society can be combined into a feasible coalition, \(v^+\) is superadditive and \(v^-\) is subadditive. A nonzero vector \(x\) is a feasible coalition when its positive-mass types induce a connected subgraph of \(H\). A solution is a finite list \(x^1,\ldots,x^K\) of feasible coalition vectors with \(\sum_jx^j=\mu\); its value is \(\sum_jv(x^j)\). The task is to compute the optimum to additive error \(\epsilon\), returning a feasible list and an upper bound within \(\epsilon\) of its value. For the energy-purchasing instance, \(v(x)\) is the negative procurement bill for the aggregated load profile, plus the negative management cost as a function of coalition mass. This is the mass analogue of the paper’s objective of maximizing total coalition value, or equivalently minimizing those costs.

I would expect the unrestricted problem to retain hardness from the compatibility graph: the combinatorics can live in \(\tau\) and \(H\), even when each type’s mass is divisible. That makes hardness transfer a plausible outcome for suitable subclasses, but the cited NP-completeness result does not prove it for this mass formulation. A discrete reduction must survive the option to split type mass. The continuous question also invites a Class A line of work: determine when a pricing problem over connected type supports is tractable, and whether that gives a column-generation or other exact algorithm for structured tariffs and graphs. The theorem’s bound is useful motivation for such work; its discrete proof does not itself establish the corresponding continuous bound.

The authors should recognize this as their coalition-structure-generation problem: the decision remains how to partition a society into graph-feasible coalitions to maximize the sum of their characteristic values. The mass objective even follows their energy application closely. The weakest point is the network co-modification. Their benchmark uses individualized household profiles and an individualized graph; the paper explicitly says that its number of agent types can equal the number of agents. The mirror needs repeated profile-and-role classes and a type-level graph that faithfully represents coalition eligibility. That is a substantive, narrower regime. It weakens the claim that the mirror covers their empirical dataset, but it does not erase the underlying GCCF question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the mismatch between the paper’s graph structure and a finite type space. In GCCF, a type has to preserve an agent’s eligibility links as well as its contribution to coalition value. For the sparse social and scale-free networks studied here, those links generally distinguish agents, so a type that preserves them can have size \(\tau\) close to \(n\). Grouping households by load profile instead loses information needed to decide whether a coalition is connected. The proposed blow-up graph restores type-level feasibility by making same-type and linked-type pairs mutually adjacent, but that is a dense block network in place of the paper’s sparse, individually wired networks. Sparse graph limits also lose connectivity: edges occupy vanishing population mass, and a few agents that bridge large groups can disappear in the limit.

There is a more specific problem for the paper’s bound theorem and its energy benchmark. The theorem’s state is a finite red-green edge-contraction state; its bound relies on descendants being formed by merging current coalitions. With a divisible population, mass can also be split. For the stated management term \(\kappa(C)=-|C|^\gamma\), a natural mass scaling gives \(-N^\gamma x^\gamma\) for a coalition of mass \(x\). Splitting unit mass into \(K\) equal coalitions makes the total management term \(-N^\gamma K^{1-\gamma}\), which approaches zero as \(K\) grows. If procurement value is linear in mass, it stays constant under those splits, so the finite-list problem can have a supremum without an attained optimum. The current \(V^-\) term also ceases to bound descendants when splitting can improve it. A setup fee or minimum coalition mass could address this, but either changes the model’s economics or restores a granularity assumption.

That weakens this particular anchor, but it does not eliminate the computational relevance of the paper. The theorem is a genuine algorithmic result, even though it is not a complexity classification, and coalition structure generation over a finite type graph with mass vectors is recognizable as a GCCF problem. A cooperative with repeated load profiles and block-structured eligibility links is a plausible instance regime. The programme explicitly permits such co-modifications when they are part of the authors’ problem.

So the negative case can argue that the paper’s demonstrated sparse-network regime has no natural, faithful finite-type continuum, and that its central bound does not transfer directly to divisible coalitions. I cannot honestly extend that to the required universal claim: the proposed type-graph energy problem survives as a worthwhile mirror under a narrower, structured network regime. The case against is therefore weak at precisely the point the comparison is meant to test.
