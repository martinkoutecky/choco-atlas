The continuous mirror question

Given a finite multigraph \(G\) with a supplied \(t\)-coloring, chromatic number \(t\), and girth at least \(2t-1\), with monotone cancellable valuations on its vertices, consider a unit mass of identical copies of \(G\). Does there exist a measurable allocation assigning each copy's goods whole to their endpoints such that the allocation is EFX within almost every copy?

The model it lives in

A unit mass of copies of one finite graph template \(G\), each containing one agent per vertex and one indivisible good per edge. Copies share the same valuations under canonical relabeling; goods are allocated integrally within each copy, and the decision is a measurable choice of an EFX allocation for almost every copy. There is no aggregate objective.

The objection that survived

The stated almost-every-pair condition does not test fairness within a copy, and the corrected copywise question factors into independent finite instances, so population mass adds no shared computation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 12 for repeated copies of a \(t\)-chromatic multigraph with girth at least \(2t-1\) and cancellable valuations; it does not cover the tree result for general monotone valuations or the paper's other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mirror cover a finite support of distinct graph templates with rational masses?
  • Can a type-level configuration LP represent broader populations of interacting local networks?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a narrow, role-based mirror of Theorem 12. Proved in this paper, it says that given a \(t\)-coloring of a \(t\)-chromatic multigraph of girth at least \(2t-1\), with monotone cancellable valuations, Algorithm 3 computes an EFX allocation in polynomial time. The graph conditions, valuation assumptions, and coloring are part of the result; I would keep them in the mirror rather than weaken the problem.

Call the mirror High-Multiplicity EFX on Repeated Graph Templates. Its instance is a finite multigraph template \(G=(V,E)\) satisfying those graph conditions, with one monotone cancellable valuation for each vertex role. The society consists of a continuum of copies of \(G\), indexed by \(x\in[0,1]\). Copy \(x\) has agents \((x,v)\) and indivisible goods \((x,e)\); each good can be allocated to either endpoint, and only its endpoints value it. Role \(v\) has mass \(1/|V|\), and each edge role has supply \(1/|V|\) per unit of total agent mass. Valuations are specified on local edge bundles and are identical across copies under the canonical relabeling of each copy’s goods.

The question is: Does there exist a measurable allocation that gives every good wholly to one endpoint and is EFX for almost every pair of agents? A solution is such an allocation, equivalently a probability distribution over integral allocations of one template, with positive weight only on EFX allocations. Individuals still receive integral bundles; only the aggregate allocation across copies is represented by mass. Agents in different copies value each other’s goods at zero, so global EFX reduces to EFX within each copy.

This regime can describe many repeated local networks—say, service districts with the same finite pattern of roles, shared resources, and valuations. With \(N\) copies, there are \(N|V|\) agents but at most \(|V|\) role types. The number of agents can therefore grow while the type support stays fixed. The authors should recognize the mirror: goods remain tied to graph endpoints, the fairness test remains exact EFX, and the algorithm’s graph structure is retained. Applying Theorem 12 to the template gives an integral EFX allocation \(P\); assigning \(P\) to every copy is a measurable solution. I expect this restricted mirror to be tractable, by the theorem’s algorithm.

I use no hardness anchor: the paper’s numbered computational result that fits this model is Theorem 12. The paper’s discussion of NP-hard EFX orientations cites other work and concerns a different decision problem, so it does not anchor this mirror.

The main weakness is that the continuum mostly repeats the template. The theorem already solves each copy, so the mirror shows high multiplicity is a sensible regime but does not demonstrate that continuity dissolves hardness. There is also a genuine type-definition risk: if agents are considered to have different preferences whenever their goods have different global item names, then copy-specific roles are not identical types. I think canonical relabeling is defensible here because copy names do not affect values or choices, but a referee could reject it under a literal global-item interpretation.

That vulnerability suggests the next questions: can the same result cover a finite support of different template types with rational masses, and can a type-level configuration LP solve that broader problem? A further step would be a continuum of locally finite high-girth networks not decomposable into repeated finite templates. Whether the proof extends there, or encounters a continuum-specific obstruction, remains open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 12 is a genuine computational result, so “there is nothing here to mirror” is not a defensible objection. The problem with this anchor is that its proposed continuum makes the copies independent, and its stated fairness condition does not actually test fairness inside a copy.

Under the usual product measure on pairs of agents, the set of pairs from the same copy lies on the diagonal \(x=y\), which has measure zero. Agents in different copies value each other’s goods at zero, while valuations are nonnegative. So any allocation is EFX for almost every pair, even if every copy receives an allocation that is not EFX. The claimed equivalence with a distribution supported only on EFX allocations is therefore false. Requiring instead that almost every copy be EFX repairs the condition, but then the question is a family of finite EFX questions, one per copy.

That repair still leaves no shared population-level computation: apply Theorem 12 to the template and repeat its allocation in every copy. Varying the template across copies gives the same factorization, with each template handled separately. The continuous mass records how many copies of each finite instance there are; it does not enter the fairness constraints or create a new computational object. That makes this a plausible repeated-district setting, but a weak continuization of the paper’s problem.

A stronger mirror would need goods or envy relations to connect different copies. Then the continuum must retain those connections as extra structure: type masses alone do not say which agents share a good. If goods become divisible mass, EFX’s test of removing any one good needs a new interpretation; if goods remain indivisible and agents remain individually incident to them, the graph of those incidences remains central to the model. This is a real modeling obstacle for turning this paper’s local graph result into a population-level computational problem.

The negative case is strongest against this proposed anchor, not universal. A continuous model with cross-copy interactions and a finite way to represent its graph structure could be worthwhile, but this paper does not supply one. I would not claim that no such mirror can exist; I would say the repeated-template construction does not establish it.
