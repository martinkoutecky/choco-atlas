The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of applicant and position states with rational masses, preferences, priors, and learning histories, together with a feasible mass matching and consistent beliefs, does there exist a finite sequence of positive-mass transfers between mutually blocking states that updates participating agents’ beliefs by Bayes’ rule and ends at a stable mass matching?

The model it lives in

A two-sided market represented by a distribution over applicant and position states, with matchings as mass flows and learning histories included in the states. A transfer rematches equal masses of mutually blocking states and updates the beliefs of participating mass.

The objection that survived

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying computational case from this paper: it contains no named result that classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally hard.

The nearest lead is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that for any matching problem under uncertainty and any self-consistent initial outcome, there exists a stable outcome consistent with that initial outcome. Its proof constructs a path by satisfying blocking pairs and updating beliefs. Corollary 1 then says stable and consistent outcomes exist for every such problem. These are existence results, not complexity results: the paper gives no running-time bound, and its termination argument relies on finite sets of named agents. Theorems 2–4 likewise establish relationships between stable outcomes under uncertainty and stable matchings under complete information; none supplies a computational anchor.

The best *candidate* mirror would be a “mass paths to stability with learning” problem. Imagine a large annual market of applicants and one-place positions. An agent’s type would include their market side, hidden category, full preference ranking over opposite-side categories, and prior over those categories. The instance would give rational mass for each type, with many agents but relatively few complete types. A matching would be a feasible flow \(x_{ij}\) of mass between applicant type \(i\) and position type \(j\), with unmatched mass allowed. Starting from a consistent mass matching and beliefs, ask whether there is a finite sequence of reallocations in which positive mass of mutually blocking type-states rematches, old partners are released, and the participating mass updates beliefs by Bayes’ rule, ending at a stable mass matching. A solution would be the sequence of reallocations and belief updates, not just the terminal matching.

That is a plausible population version of Theorem 1’s path question, but it is not an anchor the requested standard permits. It also exposes the main modeling risk: the paper defines blocking using pairs of named people and tracks what each person learns along the path. A mass-flow model must decide whether a blocking pair means any individual pair, or a positive mass of possible pairs. The latter makes mass reallocations meaningful, but changes the blocking condition; preserving the former may require tracking identity-specific histories and defeat the small-type representation.

So my positive case is limited: the paper’s constructive path-to-stability result suggests a natural high-multiplicity research question in cohort matching. I would tentatively expect a restricted finite-type, with-replacement version to be more tractable, while leaving the unrestricted learning-and-history version open. But that is a proposed direction, not a complexity conclusion supported by a named result in this paper. The weakest point is decisive for this assignment: there is no eligible computational anchor, so I cannot honestly present this as a qualifying continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive weakness is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish existence; Theorem 1’s proof constructs a finite path, but states no complexity bound for finding one. Theorems 2–4 prove relationships between stable outcomes in two models. None defines a computational problem with an input, output, and complexity measure.

Theorem 1 is the strongest possible anchor for a mirror. A generous version would take a large, repeated applicant-and-position market with finitely many preference and hidden-category types, common priors, and type-level matching flows. One could ask for an algorithm that finds a path of mass reallocations and Bayesian updates to a stable mass matching. That is a plausible population setting. But it makes a new computational question from an existence theorem: “find a path” is guaranteed on the paper’s finite instances, and studying its complexity—or adding an objective such as minimizing path length—is not a computational claim the paper makes.

More seriously, the mass version does not preserve the paper’s learning process without extra structure. Its beliefs concern named people, and agents’ histories differ as they learn the types of particular partners. Two initially identical agents can therefore become distinguishable for future blocking. Preserving that information requires tracking belief and history states, which can make support grow with the population. Collapsing agents back into preference categories loses the identity-specific knowledge that drives the path. In an atomless society, a single pairwise match changes zero mass; requiring a positive mass of mutually hopeful pairs to move at once gives blocking a new meaning. With replacement, learning about one partner says nothing about other named people; without replacement, the information gained from one match relies on a finite population and vanishes for any particular other person in the continuum limit.

Theorems 2–4 do not supply another computational anchor. They relate matchings over named individuals; Theorem 4’s homomorphism counts same-type matches, but its result is still an existence correspondence under an alignment condition. Replacing those counts with masses may yield an interesting equilibrium correspondence, but it does not turn the paper’s result into a complexity statement.

A finite-type mass-flow model of learning and stability could be worth studying on its own. That is the honest limit of the negative case: I cannot rule out every useful model someone might build. But the strongest proposed version requires new blocking and learning rules, while the paper itself offers no computational result to mirror. By the programme’s stated standard, this paper does not furnish a qualifying continuous computational mirror.
