The case FOR (proponent)

There are no numbered Theorems, Lemmas, Corollaries, or Propositions in the paper that establish a computational complexity result. So it provides no eligible named anchor, and I cannot honestly give an anchor-backed continuous problem as requested.

The closest computational task is the bundle selection rule in §2.3.1: form candidate bundles \(D\subseteq C\) of size at most \(k\), then choose one with maximum Choquet score. That is a problem definition, not a numbered complexity result. The reported experiments in §§3.1–3.2 are evidence about recommendations and elicitation, but they are not complexity anchors either.

The strongest exploratory mirror I see is Continuous-Population Choquet Group Bundle Recommendation. Fix a reference product and its candidate set \(C\). Each shopper type \(t\) has a complete Choquet capacity \(\nu_t\) over the paper’s bundle criteria; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the customer population, given as a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For each feasible bundle \(B\subseteq C\), \(|B|\le k\), let \(x(B)\in[0,1]^q\) be its criterion vector. The task is to return a feasible bundle maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t C_{\nu_t}(x(B)). \]
The bundle itself remains an ordinary, discrete set of products; the continuous object is the population whose preferences are being aggregated.

A plausible regime is a large food subscription service or marketplace promoting one shared basket to many customers viewing the same regional product set. There might be hundreds of thousands of customers but only a small support of stable preference types, such as the paper’s net-zero, healthy, or local-business personas. A type must include every preference and context detail that affects its utility or feasible bundles; customers with different capacities or candidate sets belong to different types. In that regime, “mass” is the share of shoppers represented by each type.

This is recognizably related to the paper’s bundle-utility model and its choice of a highest-scoring bundle, though it changes the setting from a personalized recommendation to a group recommendation. For fixed \(k\) and \(q\), the direct approach enumerates \(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{|C|}{j}\) bundles and evaluates each type’s score, so it is tractable by enumeration in that regime. The paper does not establish this complexity claim, and it supplies no hardness anchor from which to ask whether hardness dissolves in the continuum. The candidate covers bundle selection and its Choquet objective, not the paper’s preference-elicitation results or the experiments as a whole.

The weakest point is that the paper recommends bundles to individual users; choosing one basket for a population is an extension, not the problem it studies. The paper’s simulated personas make a small-support population scenario plausible, but they do not demonstrate that real customers fall into such a small set of shared types. Thus there is a reasonable positive mirror to explore, but no named computational result in this paper to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. It defines bundle selection and describes an elicitation procedure, then reports experiments; it proves no theorem, lemma, or corollary about complexity or algorithms. The proposed anchor—the highest-scoring bundle in §2.3.1—is a problem definition, not such a result. That leaves no result-backed continuous mirror here.

The proposed group version is the strongest direct extension, but it exposes why the population adds little to this paper’s computational problem. If recommendations remain personalized, each type \(t\) gets its own bundle maximizing \(C_{\nu_t}(x(B))\). The masses \(\mu_t\) do not change those choices; they only weight aggregate reporting. There is no joint decision for the population to compute.

If the platform instead chooses one shared bundle, the proposed objective reduces exactly to a single Choquet recommendation. Define \(\bar{\nu}(A)=\sum_t\mu_t\nu_t(A)\). Because the Choquet integral is linear in its capacity,

\[ \sum_t\mu_t C_{\nu_t}(x(B))=C_{\bar{\nu}}(x(B)). \]

So the distribution of shoppers matters only through one average capacity. The resulting task is the paper’s ordinary bundle-selection task with a pooled preference model, not a new population-level computational problem. A common basket for a subscription service is plausible, but it changes the paper’s personalized objective; adding varying candidate pools or fairness requirements would require further modeling choices absent from the paper.

The elicitation results do not supply a stronger anchor. They concern asking one user queries to learn that user’s capacity. Asking questions across a population under a shared query budget could be an interesting new problem, but it requires a new objective for allocating queries across types. The paper neither formulates nor analyzes that problem.

So the negative case is strongest against a direct, result-anchored mirror: there is no computational result to continue, and the natural population objectives either separate by type or pool into one capacity. It is weaker against the broader claim that the authors might recognize a newly designed group recommendation or population query-allocation problem as worthwhile work. That would be an extension built around the paper’s methods, rather than a continuous mirror of a result it established.
