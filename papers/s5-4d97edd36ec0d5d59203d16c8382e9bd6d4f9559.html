The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\), rational \(\eta>0\), a rational unit target \(v^\star\in S^{d-1}\), a finite list of rational unit opinion types \(u_i\in S^{d-1}\) with binary-encoded rational masses \(\mu_i\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\), does there exist a common infinite intervention sequence \(v^{(t)}\in S^{d-1}\) such that types of total mass at least \(q\) converge to \(v^\star\) under the paper's update rule? Equivalently, does some \(a\in S^{d-1}\) satisfy \(\sum_{i:\langle u_i,a\rangle>0}\mu_i\ge q\); determine the complexity in the input bit length and \(d,\tau\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution over complete initial opinion types \(u_i\), each with mass \(\mu_i\). A campaign applies one common sequence of interventions, each type follows the paper's normalized update rule, and the objective is to maximize the total mass converging to \(v^\star\). The optimum equals the maximum mass in an open hemisphere.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the asymptotic persuasion objective and the hemisphere equivalence of Theorem 4.1, with Theorem 4.2's hardness applying to equal-mass instances. It leaves the random-intervention, short-term, and dueling-influencer results aside; the weighted small-\(\tau\) complexity and a weighted extension of Theorem 4.3's stability guarantee remain open.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the exact and approximation complexities of weighted densest hemisphere when the support size \(\tau\) is small?
  • Does Theorem 4.3's stable-hemisphere algorithm extend to rational weighted supports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s one-influencer asymptotic persuasion problem in Section 4. The opinion vectors and interventions already live in a continuous geometric space, but the society is still a finite list of \(n\) agents. The mirror makes the *population* continuous while keeping the paper’s dynamics and campaign objective intact.

A plausible regime is a very large audience represented by a much smaller number \(\tau\) of stable attitudinal segments. A type is a segment’s complete initial opinion vector \(u_i\in S^{d-1}\); the paper’s persuasion strength \(\eta\) is shared by all types. Its mass \(\mu_i\) is that segment’s fraction of the audience, with \(\mu_i\geq 0\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Thus the society is \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\delta_{u_i}\), with rational masses encoded in binary. A campaign broadcasts the same sequence of interventions \(v^{(1)},v^{(2)},\ldots\) to everyone. Each type follows the paper’s update rule, and the campaigner wants to maximize the mass whose opinions converge to its target \(v^\star\).

Mass Asymptotic Persuasion. Given \(d,\eta,v^\star\), the supported types and their masses, and a threshold \(q\), does there exist a sequence of interventions such that types of total mass at least \(q\) converge to \(v^\star\)? A solution can be certified by a direction \(a\) whose open hemisphere contains mass at least \(q\): \(H_a=\{u:\langle u,a\rangle>0\}\). Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, supplies the bridge: for a finite society, a set of agents can be made to converge to the target exactly when their initial opinions fit in an open hemisphere. The same argument applies to the selected atoms of a finite-support mass distribution. Repeatedly applying \(a\) clusters those types; subsequent interventions move the cluster toward \(v^\star\). Conversely, the paper’s cone and antipodal arguments rule out capturing a group that does not fit in a hemisphere.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It states that unless P=NP, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), no polynomial-time algorithm can distinguish densest-hemisphere instances \(D=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\}\) where some open hemisphere contains more than \(1-\varepsilon\) of the points from instances where every hemisphere contains fewer than \(1/2+\varepsilon\). The theorem also says that, even when promised a hemisphere of density greater than \(1-\varepsilon\), no polynomial-time algorithm can always return one of density greater than \(1/2+\varepsilon\). The paper derives this from the cited maximum-agreement-halfspace hardness of Guruswami and Raghavendra, using a reduction it spells out from that problem to densest hemisphere.

In the mass problem, the objective is exactly \(\max_a\sum_i\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[\langle u_i,a\rangle>0]\). The hard instances of Theorem 4.2 are included by setting \(\mu_i=1/n\), so the theorem gives the same approximation barrier for this continuous-population formulation. The expected classification is hardness transfers (Class B): the finite-agent instances embed directly as equal-mass populations, and the hemisphere combinatorics remain. The continuous question is the authors’ problem in a natural high-multiplicity regime: it still asks which common intervention strategy persuades the largest share of the population in the long run. It does not replace their campaign objective with a one-step proxy.

The paper’s geometry of individual opinions does not itself make this a prior continuous-population model. The society in the paper is still given as an explicit list of agents; the mirror replaces that list with masses on opinion types. Nor does this mirror claim that the geometric update rule or continuum-agent dynamics are new. Its scope is narrower: it asks the computational question of optimal asymptotic persuasion over a population distribution.

The scope is deliberately limited to Section 4’s one-influencer asymptotic objective and Theorem 4.2’s hardness result. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s random-intervention or dueling-influencer analyses. Further questions include whether weighted rational masses admit useful algorithms parameterized by \(\tau\) or \(d\), and how stability or margin assumptions change the approximation picture. The paper’s cited Theorem 4.3 on stable hemispheres suggests one such direction, though the weighted extension would need its own analysis.

The weakest point is that Theorem 4.2’s hard instances do not establish hardness when the number of types is small relative to the population: the reduction uses a number of distinct opinions that grows with the instance. So the anchor supports a genuine continuous-population problem and a hardness-transfer result, but it does not show that continuization is hard in the most compressed, low-\(\tau\) regime. Exact type equality is also a modeling commitment; a segment must share the same modeled opinion vector for this mirror to apply. The case remains credible because the mirror preserves the paper’s core persuasion question, while making its high-multiplicity regime explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 4.2 does not establish hardness in a genuinely compressed high-multiplicity regime. Its instances can have \(\tau=n\), so replacing each voter by an equal-mass type may save no input at all. And if initial opinions are sampled from an atomless distribution, exact repeated opinion vectors occur with probability zero. Thus the theorem does not show that the hard cases persist when a very large population has relatively few opinion types.

That objection limits the result, but it does not defeat the mirror. A population divided into stable, identical attitudinal segments is a plausible regime, and in the paper’s model equal initial opinions make agents indistinguishable for every purpose the dynamics uses. Theorem 4.1 then gives the mass version directly: the maximum persuadable mass is the maximum mass in an open hemisphere. Setting \(\mu_i=1/n\) recovers the paper’s original problem exactly, so Theorem 4.2’s hardness carries over. Requiring approximate or coarsened opinions would raise a separate robustness question; it does not invalidate the exact type model.

I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case here. The paper supplies a named computational result, the mirror keeps its persuasion objective, and the population masses form a sensible high-multiplicity instance regime. The low-\(\tau\) gap is a real weakness in the proponent’s case, but under the stated standard, hardness transferring from equal-mass instances is still a worthwhile mirror.
