What the mirror covers

No named computational result is covered; the proposed repeat-user policy model would extend the paper's suggestion and accuracy themes, while leaving its individual-user studies and heuristic characterization without a continuized computational theorem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case for a continuous mirror is a population of repeat users of a product-search tool. A type would have to capture everything relevant to the interaction: the user’s initial preferences, true ranking of the available items, and response to each possible suggestion. Mass \(\mu_t\) would be the share of users of type \(t\). The system’s decision would be a suggestion policy mapping interaction histories to displayed examples; the objective could be to maximize the mass of users who select their true best item, subject to a bound on average interaction effort. Large customer bases with a modest number of recurring preference and behavior profiles make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime. It preserves the paper’s interest in suggestions, preference construction, accuracy, and effort.

But that is a proposed research problem, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The closest named result is Proposition 1, proved here: an option dominated under preference set \(R\) becomes Pareto-optimal after adding a preference \(r_i\) if and only if it is strictly better under \(r_i\) than every option that currently dominates it. This supports the paper’s suggestion heuristic; it does not establish that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The accuracy and interaction-time figures in Tables 1 and 2 are experimental findings, not numbered computational results.

So there are no qualifying anchors, and no honest way to supply “one continuous problem per anchor” under the requested standard. The proposed policy-optimization problem is the strongest positive lead I see, but its type-level response model would need to be supplied and its optimization studied; the paper does not do that. My weakest point is also the decisive one: the paper evaluates individual search interactions, and a population objective over users may be a sensible extension without being the continuous form of any named computational result here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Its Proposition 1 gives a condition under which one option becomes Pareto-optimal for an individual preference model. It does not classify the complexity of finding suggestions, optimizing an interaction, or solving another computational problem. The suggestion formulas define a heuristic score; the passages on sorting and branch-and-bound report no new complexity result. The paper’s main findings are user-study comparisons of decision accuracy and effort. Those may be important findings, but they are not computational results in ChoCo’s sense.

The strongest candidate mirror would model repeat users as a distribution over complete behavioral types. Each type would include a user’s initial preferences, true ranking, and responses to possible interaction histories. A platform could then choose an adaptive suggestion policy to maximize the share of users who find their best item, subject to average interaction effort. With recurring profiles, this is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. It avoids the mistaken objection that users are too individual to group.

But that policy problem adds a new platform-level objective and decision variable. The paper compares fixed search approaches through experiments; it does not study how to compute an optimal population-level policy. One could study that adjacent problem, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. Lifting Proposition 1 to a distribution over user types would likewise produce an aggregate version of its condition, not a complexity question the paper poses.

So the negative case is strong on the narrower point: there is no qualifying anchor here. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could be built from the paper’s subject. The repeat-user policy problem looks plausible and may be worth studying on its own; the paper simply does not supply the computational result for it to mirror.
