The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational problem to continuize. Theorems 1 and 2 derive utility representations; Theorems 3 and 4 characterize efficient lotteries; Theorems 5 and 6 axiomatize generalized utilitarian and Nash choice functions. None specifies a computational input, an algorithm, or a complexity claim. The proponent’s observation that the paper contains no named computational result therefore survives: there is no result here whose complexity landscape the continuous-population programme can chart.

The best candidate is a high-multiplicity bargaining problem. Group agents with identical utility functions into types, represent them by rational masses, and ask for a lottery maximizing aggregate utility, such as \(\sum_t \mu_t u_t(\ell)\), or a normalized Nash objective such as \(\exp(\sum_t \mu_t \log u_t(\ell))\). That is a sensible question, and the paper’s characterizations could help motivate it. But it adds an encoded outcome space, an optimization task, and a population aggregation rule that the paper never studies. The theorems say which rules satisfy axioms; they do not ask how hard those rules are to compute. So this may be a worthwhile adjacent project, but it is not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The paper’s use of lotteries does not supply the missing population model: it makes outcomes probabilistic, while the agents remain finite. That is not a reason to dismiss the paper because its outcomes are already continuous; it simply does not establish a continuous society.

This negative case is strong on the absence of an anchor, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror could exist. The high-multiplicity bargaining extension is plausible. What the paper supports is the narrower conclusion that such work would be a new computational question inspired by its axioms, rather than a continuous mirror of its results.
