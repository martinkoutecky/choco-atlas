What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-profile model covers forming \(P\) from student types and computing \(\pi\); it does not provide a continuous counterpart to a named computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Lemma 1 derives a survey-sample estimate; Theorems 1–5 concern conditional unbiasedness, uniqueness, stationary-distribution invariance, and comparative statics (including the result named “Strategyproofness”). None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper gives algorithms for estimating \(P\), iterating toward \(\pi\), and simulating uncertainty, but no named theorem about their computational complexity. Theorem 3 is the nearest possibility: it says that, when \(P\) is irreducible and aperiodic, the solutions to \(\pi=\pi P\) and \(\pi=\pi P^m\) coincide. That is a mathematical identity, not a complexity result. Theorem 5’s “strategyproofness” is a derivative claim, not a computational one.

The strongest plausible population mirror is still easy to describe. In a large annual applicant cohort, let a type \(t\) specify the colleges \(A_t\) that admitted those students and the college \(j_t\in A_t\) they choose; let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s population share. Define
\[ P_{ij}= \frac{\sum_{t:\,i\in A_t,\ j_t=j}\mu_t} {\sum_{t:\,i\in A_t}\mu_t}. \]
Given rational masses and an irreducible \(P\), ask for the normalized authority vector \(\pi\) satisfying \(\pi=\pi P\), and the resulting college ranking. Millions of students sharing a smaller set of admissions-and-choice profiles is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors would recognize the ranking task. The expected answer is tractable by computing a stationary distribution.

But this is an unanchored mirror, not one supported by a qualifying named result. It also largely formalizes the paper’s existing probability matrix and stationary-equilibrium calculation, rather than exposing a new computational question. The case is weakest where the real applicant population has many distinct admission and choice profiles, so the number of types may not be small. I would not claim more: by the paper’s stated anchor rule, there are no continuous problems to supply per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with the anchor rule: there is no named computational result here to continuize. Theorem 1 is a statistical unbiasedness claim; Theorems 2 and 3 are identities about the stationary distribution; Theorems 4 and 5 are comparative statics. The estimation, power iteration, and simulation procedures are algorithms, but the paper makes no theorem-level claim about their computational complexity. In particular, Theorem 5’s “strategyproofness” is a derivative result, not a computational strategyproofness result.

The proposed type model is sensible: a type could record the set of colleges that admitted a student and the college they chose. But once those masses are aggregated into \(P\), the ranking depends only on the \(n\times n\) matrix, through \(\pi=\pi P\). The support size \(\tau\) affects the work of forming \(P\); it does not create a new complexity question in the ranking calculation. That calculation is already the paper’s stationary-distribution procedure. The proposed mirror therefore formalizes the population behind the input, but it is not a counterpart to a computational theorem in the paper.

The strongest rescue would be a population-level intervention problem: for example, find the least mass of students who would need to change college choice to raise a target college’s authority. The paper does discuss strategies to improve ranking scores, so its authors might recognize that question. But it would require specifying which type transfers are feasible and how they change the rows of \(P\); the paper supplies derivatives with respect to matrix entries, not a model of costed changes among student types. A single student’s effect vanishes in an atomless society. Allowing a positive-mass coalition avoids that, but makes the question a new collective-intervention model rather than a mirror of a result the paper proves.

That is the negative case, and its limit is real: the paper’s large applicant cohorts make a continuous population plausible, and the authors could recognize a coalition-intervention extension as relevant. I would reject the proponent’s stationary-ranking proposal as an unanchored, population-independent computation, but I cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror could be built. The case is strongest as a reason this paper lacks a qualifying anchor, not as proof of the universal claim.
