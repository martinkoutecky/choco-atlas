The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\), a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a resolution \(N\) such that \(N\mu_t\) is integral, and each type's ballot probabilities \(p_{t,j}\) and inspection cost \(c_t\), choose adaptively which uninspected ballot unit to inspect. Each unit of type \(t\) independently votes for \(j\) with probability \(p_{t,j}\), and inspection costs \(c_t/N\). Minimize expected total cost until the strict absolute-majority winner, or the absence of one, is certified.

The model it lives in

The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over complete ballot types \(t=(p_{t,1},\ldots,p_{t,d},c_t)\), with \(N\mu_t\) integral ballot units of each type. The decision variable is an adaptive inspection policy over the observed ballot history; its objective is expected total per-unit inspection cost until exact absolute-majority certification.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the adaptive absolute-majority guarantee of Theorem 1, the relative-majority guarantee of Theorem 3, and the two-round absolute-majority guarantee of Theorem 4. It leaves Theorem 2's three-round result, exact optimality, and the paper's open hardness and adaptivity-gap questions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a constant-factor policy be computed in time polynomial in \(d\), \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(\log N\), without expanding all \(N\) ballot units?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity ballot-audit society. A type \(t\) records a voter’s full signature for this problem: the ballot probabilities \(p_{t,1},\ldots,p_{t,d}\) and the cost \(c_t\) of inspecting that ballot. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of voters of type \(t\). This fits a large referendum or election audit in which many records share the same forecast and retrieval cost, so the number of voters \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\).

To retain the paper’s independent ballot uncertainty, give the continuous type distribution a finite resolution \(N\), with \(N\mu_t\) integral. There are \(N\mu_t\) ballot units of type \(t\), each of mass \(1/N\); its ballot is drawn independently according to \(p_t\). An auditor adaptively inspects one uninspected unit, paying \(c_t/N\), and stops when every possible completion of the unseen ballots has the same election result. The objective is minimum expected total inspection cost. Dividing every original cost by \(N\) leaves approximation ratios unchanged. The type histogram \(\mu\) is the population description; \(N\) retains the observation resolution.

My lead is AM-Certification: given \(d,\tau,\mu,N\), the type probability vectors and positive inspection costs, find an adaptive policy minimizing expected cost until it certifies either the strict absolute-majority winner or that there is no such winner. A policy is a rule mapping inspection history to the next type to inspect or to a certified result.

Its anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: Algorithm 1 is an adaptive \(4\)-approximation for evaluating the absolute-majority function, meaning its expected inspection cost is at most four times that of an optimal adaptive strategy. This is a strong match for a mass-based audit: individual ballots remain integral and uncertain, while the electorate is summarized by fractions of voters in indistinguishable inspection types. The theorem’s algorithm applies to the expanded \(N\)-ballot instance, so it gives a polynomial-time approximation in the explicit population size \(N\). I expect this finite-resolution version to be tractable for constant-factor approximation.

A second, distinct mirror is RM-Certification: with the same type-distribution input and inspection model, minimize expected cost to certify the unique relative-majority winner, or certify that there is a tie. Its anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: Algorithm RMF-S is an adaptive \(8\)-approximation for evaluating the relative-majority function. It first inspects low-cost ballots until at most two candidates can still win, then uses a threshold-evaluation method and, if needed, an optimal strategy for checking the remaining candidate. The predicted direction is again tractability for constant-factor approximation. The result is especially relevant to audits where the question is whether one candidate leads every other candidate, rather than whether anyone exceeds half the electorate.

A further policy constraint gives Two-Round AM-Certification: solve AM-Certification with a policy that uses at most two rounds, each round having a predetermined inspection order selected from the information available at its start. Its anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here: there exists a \(10\)-approximation for absolute-majority evaluation with two rounds of adaptivity. This asks whether limited-round policies retain useful guarantees when voters are represented by type masses. It is a separate practical question, though it shares the absolute-majority outcome rule with Theorem 1.

These mirrors cover the paper’s absolute-majority and relative-majority approximation results. The paper supplies no named hardness result: its conclusion says that even NP-hardness is open. So this case is about extending algorithmic guarantees to a high-multiplicity regime, not about showing that discrete hardness dissolves. A natural next question is whether the policies can be computed from the compressed type histogram in time polynomial in \(d,\tau,L,\log N\), rather than by expanding \(N\) ballots. The stated theorems alone establish no such compressed running time, and they do not settle the complexity of finding an exact optimal policy.

The weak point is the resolution parameter. In a literal atomless population with independent ballots at every infinitesimal point, aggregate vote shares become deterministic, and inspection uncertainty can disappear. Keeping \(N\) makes the mirror a continuous type histogram with finite ballot resolution, rather than a fully atomless stochastic evaluation model. That is the price of preserving the paper’s central question—adaptive inspection of random individual votes. If a mirror must remove \(N\) entirely, this paper is a much weaker fit; if high multiplicity means a large population described by a small distribution of complete voter types, the fit is convincing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has exactly the kind of named computational results this programme wants: Theorems 1, 3, and 4 give approximation algorithms for explicit stochastic evaluation problems. So the negative case cannot rest on the absence of a result to mirror. Its strongest point is that the paper’s uncertainty lives in individually inspectable random ballots, and that uncertainty does not survive an atomless population limit.

Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\) and ballot probabilities \(p_{t,j}\). In a measurable atomless model with independent ballot outcomes, the mass of type \(t\) voting for candidate \(j\) is \(\mu_t p_{t,j}\) almost surely. Thus the aggregate share for \(j\), \(\sum_t \mu_t p_{t,j}\), is known from the input. The absolute-majority and relative-majority outcomes are known before any inspection. Inspecting an infinitesimal voter has zero mass and reveals nothing about the aggregate; inspecting a positive mass of voters reveals the deterministic proportions. The exact-certification objective that Theorems 1 and 3 approximate has therefore collapsed. Theorem 4’s two rounds collapse with it: a predetermined order over mass has no informative sequence of ballot revelations.

The finite-resolution proposal is the strongest repair. It is also a sensible high-multiplicity audit: retain \(N\) individually inspectable ballot units, group them by complete type, and perhaps charge \(c_t/N\) per inspection. But \(N\) is essential to the stochastic problem, not merely a convenient resolution. It determines the majority threshold, the distribution of outcomes, and which partial observations certify a result. Two instances with the same \(\mu\), \(p\), and \(c\), but different \(N\), are different evaluation problems. Scaling costs by \(1/N\) changes their units, not their discrete query structure. Theorem 1, Theorem 3, and Theorem 4 then apply to the expanded finite instance; the population itself has not become atomless.

The natural alternatives do not preserve the paper’s problem. If an auditor inspects a positive mass at once, independent outcomes average to their known proportions. If aggregate uncertainty is retained through a common random shock to type shares, ballots are no longer independent as in the paper; the task becomes inference about a latent environment. If the auditor may stop with a small probability of error, that changes exact certification into approximate evaluation. Each could motivate research, but none is a continuous version of the named results without changing their central stochastic model.

This is a real case against an atomless continuous mirror of the exact-certification problem, and it defeats the literal continuum versions of all three anchors. Its limit is equally real: the finite-\(N\), type-compressed audit remains a plausible and potentially worthwhile high-multiplicity problem, and the programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as evidence in favor. If that finite-resolution hybrid counts as a continuous mirror, I cannot honestly claim to have defeated every anchor. The negative case is strongest on the boundary between a genuinely continuous population and a discrete audit represented by type masses; it is weak against the latter.
