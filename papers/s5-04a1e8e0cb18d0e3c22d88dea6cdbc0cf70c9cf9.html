What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers approximate probability intervals through a weighted-median aggregation rule; it leaves the exact-judgment characterizations and the applications to classifications, error-bound measurements, and conditional judgments untouched. The paper establishes no computational theorem for the proposed mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result establishing that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Its theorems characterize aggregation rules by incentive and axiomatic properties. The strongest positive lead is Theorem 4.1, but it is only an algorithmic near-miss, not a complexity result.

Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, considers approximate judgments represented by order intervals over a lattice. For an odd number \(n\ge3\) of agents, on a rich domain of single-peaked preferences over the interval judgments, it assumes an anonymous, bi-idempotent aggregation rule that is strategy-proof. It proves that these statements are equivalent: the interval space is a bounded lattice; it is a bounded distributive lattice; and the rule is the majority rule
\[ f(x^N)=\bigvee_{S\in W_{\mathrm{maj}}}\ \bigwedge_{i\in S}x_i, \qquad W_{\mathrm{maj}}=\{S\subseteq N: |S|\ge \lceil(n+1)/2\rceil\}. \]
The proof uses the paper’s Claim 1 and its exact-judgment characterization in Theorem 3.1(iii), alongside cited technical results. This is a characterization of a rule, not a runtime theorem.

A concrete continuous question suggested by it is Mass-Majority Approximate-Probability Aggregation. An instance gives a finite support of approximate probability reports \(J_t=[a_t,b_t]\subseteq[0,1]\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The underlying population consists of many experts grouped by complete preference and elicitation types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of each type. Given their reports, compute an aggregate interval. Use the lower weighted median of the \(a_t\)'s and the lower weighted median of the \(b_t\)'s as its endpoints, resolving exact half-mass ties by choosing the lower median. The paired endpoints form a valid interval because \(a_t\le b_t\) for each report. There is no optimization objective in this rule-based problem: the solution is the aggregate judgment.

A plausible regime is a large forecasting or risk-assessment service with many analysts but a relatively small number of recurring roles, elicitation protocols, and preference types. Millions of analysts could then contribute mass over a support of hundreds or thousands of types. Approximate probability intervals are already one of the paper’s central examples, so the authors would likely recognize this as a mass-based version of their aggregation problem. The continuization is of the expert population; the interval remains the paper’s judgment format.

For this interval case, the proposed computation is tractable: sort lower and upper endpoints separately and accumulate their rational masses. That takes polynomial time in the support size and input bit length. The predicted outcome is Class A, though this is a result to prove for the mirror, not a conclusion of Theorem 4.1. Further questions include extending the computation to succinctly represented distributive lattices and defining a non-vacuous incentive notion for positive-mass groups.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 4.1 does not classify computational complexity, and the proposed rule is a natural extension of its formula rather than a result the authors establish. Also, in a nonatomic population, individual strategy-proofness is vacuous because one person has zero mass; testing manipulation by a whole type would change the incentive question to group strategy-proofness. So this is a plausible continuous aggregation problem anchored in the paper’s subject, but it is not a strong, strict ChoCo case under the requirement for named computational-complexity anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with a mismatch in what the paper proves. Theorem 4.1 is an axiomatic characterization: under its assumptions, the interval judgment space is a bounded distributive lattice exactly when the specified rule is majority. Theorem 3.1 likewise characterizes aggregation rules. Neither states a computational problem or establishes its complexity. The paper gives no input encoding, runtime bound, or hardness result for the proposed mirror to continuize.

The best version of the proponent’s idea is coherent: represent a large expert population by rational masses over complete types, including preferences, and aggregate interval reports by mass majority. Many experts sharing recurring roles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But that formulation extends the paper. Its theorems concern a finite, odd number of agents and individual strategy-proofness; they say nothing about incentives for positive-mass groups or the computation of a mass manipulation. Asking for the minimum mass needed to change an outcome would add a new objective and cost model. Those could be useful research questions, but they are not computational results from this paper.

So the anchor fails as a direct ChoCo mirror: it turns an axiomatic result into a computational question that the paper does not ask or answer. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that *no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario*. Population mass is sensible here, and an independently motivated computational study of mass-based expert aggregation could be worthwhile. The paper alone does not establish that broader case against it.
