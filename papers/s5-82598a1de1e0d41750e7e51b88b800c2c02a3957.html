The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. The closest anchor is an unnumbered claim in Section 2: estimating Mallows maximum-likelihood parameters is NP-hard, and its maximum-likelihood reference ranking is the Kemeny social ranking. The paper cites Young (1988) for this connection; it does not prove the claim there. So this is not a compliant numbered anchor, but it is the strongest honest basis for a positive case.

A plausible mirror is Continuous-Mass Kemeny Ranking. An instance consists of alternatives \(A\) and a finite support of complete rankings \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_\tau\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_i\) summing to one. The question is to find a ranking \(\pi\) minimizing

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,d_K(\pi,\sigma_i), \]

where \(d_K\) is the number of pairwise disagreements. A solution is a minimizing ranking. This is the mass-weighted Kemeny reference-order problem underlying the paper’s Mallows MLE claim.

The natural regime is a large population repeatedly ranking the same slate of alternatives—for example, users ranking a shared catalogue—with many people giving identical complete rankings. The type is a ranking, and its mass is the fraction of the population holding that type. Then the number of people \(n\) can greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\), while \(m\) and \(\tau\) remain explicit complexity parameters. That seems recognizable to the authors: the paper already frames rankings as population preferences and studies the Mallows model’s reference ranking.

I would expect hardness to transfer (Class B): a finite Kemeny profile maps directly to rational masses by assigning each ranking its fraction of voters. The mirror therefore asks whether the paper’s Mallows-reference-ranking hardness remains for a population supplied by type masses. It also invites questions about algorithms parameterized by \(m\) or \(\tau\), and about approximation under binary-encoded masses.

This covers only the Mallows reference-ranking result. It does not mirror the paper’s empirical comparisons of Normal RUM, Plackett–Luce, and Mallows, or its Normal RUM parameter estimation. The weakness is substantial: the paper already models rankings as draws from a population distribution, and its finite-sample likelihood can be written using ranking frequencies. The mirror may therefore be a high-multiplicity restatement of an existing computational problem, rather than a new consequence of continuization. With no qualifying numbered anchor, this is a modest positive case, not a clean one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about computational complexity. Its only complexity anchor is an unnumbered piece of background in Section 2: Mallows maximum-likelihood estimation is NP-hard, and its reference ranking is a Kemeny ranking. The paper neither proves that result nor studies its complexity. Its own contribution is empirical model fitting and interpretation, not a complexity result. That leaves no named computational result of this paper for the proposed mirror to extend.

Even granting that background claim as an anchor, the proposed mass-weighted Kemeny problem is the strongest possible mirror—and it is hard to distinguish from the problem already described. For rational masses \(\mu_i\), take a common denominator \(D\) and set \(n_i=D\mu_i\). Then

\[ \sum_i \mu_i d_K(\pi,\sigma_i) =\frac{1}{D}\sum_i n_i d_K(\pi,\sigma_i). \]

The minimizers are exactly the Kemeny rankings of the ballot multiset with \(n_i\) copies of each ranking. The paper’s Mallows MLE already computes that ranking from observed ballots; aggregating them by type gives the same objective. A compressed input may invite a useful high-multiplicity analysis, but that is a new encoding or parameterization of the background problem, not a computational result established by this paper.

I would not argue that the population regime is implausible: many users ranking a shared set can sensibly be grouped by complete ranking type. Nor does mass make the Kemeny objective degenerate or erase relevant identity. The negative case is therefore narrow. The proponent has a coherent question, and the paper’s distributional framing makes it a natural one; the case against is that this question is not anchored in a computational result of the paper, and its strongest candidate is already the frequency-weighted form of the MLE problem the paper cites.
