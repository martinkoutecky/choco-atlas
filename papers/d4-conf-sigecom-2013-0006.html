The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_{g,v}\) over finitely many cohort-value types, a monotone service-cost oracle \(C\) on served-mass vectors, and a black-box baseline allocation rule \(\mathcal A\) for the aggregate population, can one compute in time polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\), support size \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and oracle runtime a BIC, individually rational mechanism that recovers expected cost and has expected social cost at most \(O(\log h)\,\mathbb E[SC(\mathcal A)]+\epsilon\)?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population has rational type masses \(\mu_{g,v}\), where \(g\) identifies a cohort and \(v\) is a private value from a finite support. A mechanism assigns each type a service probability \(q_g(v)\) and expected payment \(p_g(v)\), with BIC and individual rationality; its objective is expected unserved value plus \(C(x)\), where \(x\) is the served-mass vector. The question is whether the paper’s black-box threshold reduction yields cost recovery and an \(O(\log h)\) guarantee with complexity polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 4 uses finite, named-agent inputs; it does not prove that a cohort-invariant aggregate allocation oracle and mass-based cost function admit the proposed polynomial-time compression in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s BIC black-box cost-recovery reduction and its value-range guarantee. It does not cover Theorem 6’s incentive lower bound or the ex-post truthful conversions in Theorems 7–8 and Corollary 2.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the threshold reduction be implemented over finite type masses with an aggregate allocation oracle and polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and \(L\)?
  • What oracle access to individual reports and aggregate reports suffices to define the interim allocation curves and preserve BIC in the atomless model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational mirror is Theorem 4, proved in the paper. It says that, given \(\epsilon>0\), black-box access to an arbitrary algorithm \(\mathcal A\) and the known independent value distributions, one can construct a BIC mechanism that recovers cost in expectation and has expected social cost at most \(O(\min\{\log h,\log n\})\,\mathbb E[SC(\mathcal A)]+\epsilon\). Its running time is polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\), \(n\), and the running time of \(\mathcal A\). The paper does not give a conventional NP-hardness theorem; its computational anchor is this polynomial-time mechanism transformation.

For its continuous mirror, take a large population divided into a small number of cohorts \(g\). Members of each cohort share the same public service-cost and valuation-distribution parameters. Their private values come from a known finite support \(V_g\subseteq[1,h]\). The society is the rational mass distribution \(\mu_{g,v}\) over complete types \((g,v)\), with support size \(\tau\). Think of a large district seeking a shared service: people in each cohort have the same connection conditions, while the provider’s cost depends on how much of each cohort it serves. This makes service by mass natural. The individual values remain private; the mass distribution describes the population’s type law.

The instance for the continuous problem consists of that mass distribution, a monotone cost function \(C\) on served-mass vectors, and a black-box baseline allocation rule \(\mathcal A\), including access to its allocation and cost on queried profiles. A solution is a measurable randomized direct mechanism: for each cohort and reported value it specifies a service probability \(q_g(v)\) and an expected payment \(p_g(v)\). It must satisfy BIC and individual rationality, recover expected service cost, and achieve expected social cost at most \(O(\log h)\,SC(\mathcal A)+\epsilon\). The \(O(\log n)\) branch has no finite population-size parameter in this model; the value-range branch is the relevant one. The question I would hand to a prover is whether this guarantee can be achieved with complexity polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and the oracle running time, where \(L\) is the input bit length.

I expect this problem to be tractable, in the sense of the programme’s Class A. The paper’s threshold construction is a plausible route: truncate the interim allocation curves at successive value thresholds, compute the corresponding BIC payments, and use the first threshold whose expected payments cover expected cost. In the finite-support version these operations become sums over types. This is still the paper’s problem: choosing whom to serve, charging them, meeting BIC, recovering service cost, and controlling social cost. The mass-dependent cost is a co-modification for a homogeneous shared-service regime, not a change of objective.

A second, especially credible anchor is Theorem 6, also proved here. In its public-excludable-good instance, \(n\) agents have values \(v_i=a_i/(4n)\), where the \(a_i\) are independent, equal-revenue draws: zero with probability \(1/h\), and otherwise distributed with density \(1/a^2\) on \([1,h]\). Serving any nonempty set costs \(1\). The theorem says every BIC mechanism that recovers cost in expectation has expected social cost at least \(\Omega(\log h-\sqrt{h/n})\).

Here is a finite-support continuous-population version of that lower-bound question. Let \(h=2^k\), and let a unit-mass population have types \(a=0,1,2,\ldots,2^{k-1}\), with \(\mu(0)=2^{-k}\) and \(\mu(2^j)=2^{-(j+1)}\) for \(0\le j<k\). Give type \(a\) value \(a/4\) in aggregate-welfare units: this is the original value scaled by the population size, with values and payments scaled together so incentives are preserved. A shared good costs \(1\) whenever positive mass is served, and nothing otherwise. The problem is to find a BIC, individually rational allocation and payment rule minimizing expected social cost subject to expected cost recovery.

The equal-revenue law caps expected BIC revenue at \(1/4\) per unit of population mass. Cost recovery therefore implies that positive mass is served with probability at most \(1/4\). The total value of the population is \(k/8\), so on the event of no service—whose probability is at least \(3/4\)—the entire \(k/8\) is lost. Thus expected social cost is at least \(3k/32=\Omega(\log h)\). For \(k\ge8\), serving everyone has social cost \(1\), which is optimal without the incentive and cost-recovery requirements. This is the Class B-style outcome: the logarithmic obstruction survives at high multiplicity. It is an incentive and approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness claim.

Theorem 4 is my lead computational anchor; Theorem 6 makes the population regime unusually convincing. In both, the number of agents can be enormous relative to the number of distinct types, and the public-good case is already about a shared service with a cost determined by aggregate participation. The paper’s use of random value distributions is not itself the continuization: the mirror makes the population’s type masses the object being modeled.

The soft spot is the atomless-agent limit. An individual has zero effect on aggregate service mass, so the mirror must preserve incentives through each agent’s allocation and payment schedule, rather than through their impact on the population profile. More concretely, Theorem 4’s polynomial dependence on \(n\) does not establish a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau\) and \(L\); that compression is part of the proposed research question. Theorem 6 is less exposed to this objection: its i.i.d. large-population setting and pooled fixed cost already motivate the limit, and the lower-bound argument becomes cleaner when aggregate type masses are fixed. This case covers the paper’s BIC expected-cost-recovery results, not its ex-post truthful and no-bossiness results or arbitrary costs that depend on named individuals.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 4 is that its polynomial time claim depends on a finite, named-agent oracle. Its input is an algorithm on \(n\)-coordinate profiles, distributions \(F_i\), and a monotone cost function \(C(S)\) over subsets of identities. Compressing agents into \(\tau\) cohorts preserves that theorem only when the costs and algorithm respect the cohort structure. Otherwise, either identities must be included in the types, leaving no compression, or the continuous model needs a new oracle whose input is a mass profile. That is a real modeling gap in the proposed mirror. But the best repair—cohort-invariant costs and an algorithm defined on aggregate reports—is coherent, and the threshold argument appears to transfer. The gap weakens the claim that the paper’s generic reduction itself yields a \(\tau\)-based algorithm; it does not rule out a worthwhile mirror.

Theorem 6 is harder to defeat. One could object that its proposed mirror treats exact type masses as though they were realized outcomes of independent draws. A cleaner version takes the finite-support equal-revenue law as the population’s type distribution and gives each agent a private value drawn from that law. In the nonatomic limit, aggregate masses are fixed while an individual’s report still affects their own allocation. For the public good, the integrated BIC revenue bound remains \(1/4\); cost recovery therefore bounds the probability of serving positive mass by \(1/4\). The expected value left unserved is then \(\Omega(\log h)\), while serving everyone has social cost \(1\) for sufficiently large \(h\). This is a meaningful continuous-population approximation lower bound, even though it is not an NP-hardness result.

So I cannot honestly defeat every anchor. Theorem 4’s universal black-box claim does not carry over without a new aggregate-oracle model, but Theorem 6 survives a more careful formulation. The negative case is therefore weak: the strongest objection identifies a limitation in the first mirror, while the second appears to be a credible continuous question.
