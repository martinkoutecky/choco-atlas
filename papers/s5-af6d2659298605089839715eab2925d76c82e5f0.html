The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-member costs \(c_t(x_t)=\langle\theta_t,\phi_t(x_t)\rangle\), compact convex action sets, and a rational per-capita feasibility polytope such as \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=q\), let one organized aggregator per type be a strategic VCG player with total cost \(\mu_t c_t(x_t)\). Each aggregator answers \(K\) allocation-pair queries with Bradley–Terry probabilities based on its total net utility. Determine whether a D-optimal query and learned-cost VCG mechanism can give high-probability bounds, as functions of \(K,d,\tau,\mu\), on each cohort's gain from arbitrary reports, truthful utility under a zero allocation, and the per-capita welfare gap.

The model it lives in

Types specify complete cost, feature, and feedback profiles; \(\mu_t\) is each cohort's population share, \(x_t\) its per-member allocation, and \(J_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t c_t(x_t)\) the per-capita social cost. Each type's aggregator submits cohort feedback and receives the VCG externality transfer for the total cost \(\mu_t c_t(x_t)\).

The objection that survived

The paper's guarantees concern individual agents, while the proposed mirror makes each type a coordinated strategic cohort; moreover, aggregate Bradley–Terry feedback may change the dependence of the claimed μ_tε_K bounds on cohort mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror targets Theorems 1 and 2's one-shot and online guarantees; it leaves the auxiliary lemmas and simulations as evidence rather than separate mirrors, and it does not supply runtime bounds for arbitrary compact feasible sets.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do the incentive and welfare bounds depend on \(K,d,\tau\), and the type masses \(\mu_t\) when feedback reflects each cohort's total utility?
  • Can query design, cost estimation, allocation, and VCG transfers be computed in polynomial time for rational quadratic costs and rational-polytope feasibility?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named theorem about membership in P, NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, or running time. Its strongest anchors are algorithmic performance guarantees: the paper proves that its learning and VCG mechanism has particular incentive, welfare, and regret bounds. So there is a positive continuous mirror to argue for, but it would extend those guarantees; it would not mirror a complexity classification already established by the paper.

A plausible regime is local demand response with many households but relatively few complete cost and flexibility types. Let a type \(t\) specify the household’s cost parameters, features, feasible actions, and preference-feedback model. Suppose type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and the support has size \(\tau\). Millions of households might then be represented by tens or hundreds of types. A type can be a cohort represented by an aggregator, whose members share the same relevant characteristics and whose reports and payments are managed collectively.

For a concrete model, let \(x_t\) be the flexibility assigned to each household of type \(t\), and let its per-household cost be \(c_t(x_t)=\langle\theta_t^*,\phi_t(x_t)\rangle\), with the paper’s bounded linear-parameter assumptions. The planner minimizes per-capita cost
\[ J_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t c_t(x_t) \]
subject, for example, to type-specific action bounds and a per-capita supply requirement \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=q\). Query responses are sampled from households within each type. A type cohort’s cost and VCG transfer are mass-weighted; the cohort can distribute its transfer among its members. Assume each cohort can opt out while the other cohorts still satisfy the feasibility constraints, as needed for the individual-rationality guarantee.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under the paper’s bounded linear-cost and feature assumptions, and with \(K>d(d+1)/2\) preference queries per agent, the one-shot learned-cost VCG mechanism has, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), all three properties: an agent’s utility gain from any strategic feedback is at most \(\epsilon_K(\delta)\); under the zero-allocation assumption, a truthful agent’s utility is at least \(-\epsilon_K(\delta)\); and when all agents are truthful, the social-cost gap is at most \(N\epsilon_K(\delta/N)\). The paper gives \(\epsilon_K(\delta)\in\tilde{\mathcal O}(d\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/K})\).

The corresponding lead question is Type-Mass Preference-VCG, One-Shot. An instance consists of the finite supported type set, binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), compact type action sets, rational feature and cost bounds, a feasible set such as the supply-constrained set above, a query budget \(K\), and a sampling oracle returning Bradley–Terry comparisons from each type. The decision variable is the type-level allocation \(x\); the mechanism first chooses D-optimal comparisons, estimates each \(\theta_t^*\) from the responses, then minimizes the estimated mass-weighted cost and computes VCG transfers for the type cohorts. The problem is to construct that mechanism and prove its guarantees against arbitrary cohort-level feedback deviations. A solution would provide the query rule, allocation and transfer procedure, and a proof that—with probability at least \(1-\delta\)—each cohort’s total utility gain from deviation is at most \(\mu_t\epsilon_K(\delta/\tau)\), its total truthful utility is at least \(-\mu_t\epsilon_K(\delta/\tau)\), and the per-capita welfare gap is at most \(\epsilon_K(\delta/\tau)\). Equivalently, the per-member incentive and individual-rationality errors are bounded by \(\epsilon_K\), while the per-capita welfare error no longer grows with the number of households.

I would expect a Class A result for an explicit convex demand-response model, such as the paper’s quadratic discomfort costs and a rational polytope of supply constraints: the allocation step is convex optimization in roughly \(\tau d\) variables, while learning and experimental design operate in dimension \(d\) for each type. But that expectation depends on the feasible-set and design-oracle representations. The paper assumes a compact feasible set and does not give a general running-time bound, so its theorem alone does not establish tractability for arbitrary compact sets. The further computational questions are the bit complexity of design and allocation, and whether the result survives when those oracle assumptions are removed.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. For fixed cost parameters over time and \(K>d(d+1)/2\) queries per agent, the paper’s multi-round mechanism, after \(T\) rounds, bounds the cumulative gain from deviating and the negative truthful utility by \(\tilde{\mathcal O}(dT^{2/3}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)})\) per agent. With all agents truthful, welfare regret is at most \(\tilde{\mathcal O}(NdT^{2/3}\sqrt{\log(N/\delta)})\). Its continuous counterpart is Type-Mass Preference-VCG, Online: use the same fixed type distribution and cost parameters over a horizon \(H\); in each stage, sample \(K\) comparisons per type, update its cost estimate, and run weighted VCG for exploitation. The task is to establish cumulative cohort incentive and individual-rationality errors of \(\mu_t\tilde{\mathcal O}(dH^{2/3}\sqrt{\log(\tau/\delta)})\), and per-capita welfare regret of \(\tilde{\mathcal O}(dH^{2/3}\sqrt{\log(\tau/\delta)})\). This too looks like Class A for the explicit convex model, conditional on efficient optimization and design routines. The paper’s repeated electricity-market setting makes this a genuine companion question rather than a different use of continuity.

The population is the continuous object in both questions: masses replace voter or household counts, and supply is expressed per capita. The paper’s continuous allocation space is not the reason to call these mirrors continuous. Nor do I claim the paper’s theorem is already a collision: its population remains finite, and the proposed questions ask what happens when the population itself is represented by a type distribution.

The weakest point is the strategic unit. In the paper, each individual is strategic and receives VCG utility. In the proposed mirror, the natural positive-mass type cohort acts through an aggregator and its members share the cohort’s objective. That is plausible for organized demand-response groups, but it is an extension: in a nonatomic population, an individual’s effect on the allocation can vanish, making individual VCG incentives degenerate. The honest follow-up is to determine whether the cohort guarantee has a meaningful finite-population implementation and how its incentive bounds change as cohort sizes grow. The mirror covers the paper’s one-shot and online preference-learning VCG guarantees, not every assumption-free individual-strategy interpretation of them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that neither theorem is a computational-complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to classify. Theorem 1 bounds statistical and incentive error after \(K\) preference queries; Theorem 2 bounds regret over \(T\) rounds. The paper gives no running-time or bit-complexity result for computing the design, allocation, or payments, and its general model allows an arbitrary compact feasible set. A type-mass version could extend its statistical guarantees, but that would be a new mechanism-learning question, not a continuous mirror of a complexity result in this paper.

There is also a mismatch in the proposed Theorem 1 mirror. The paper’s strategic unit is an individual: agent \(i\) supplies \(K\) reports, has her own cost, and receives a VCG payment defined by the effect of excluding her. The proposed bound \(\mu_t\epsilon_K\) instead treats every member of type \(t\) as one strategic bloc with one pooled report and aggregate utility. Identical costs make people the same *type*; they do not make them a coalition that coordinates its reports or pools its payments. If individuals remain separate strategic actors in a nonatomic population, a single person has zero mass and cannot affect the allocation or its aggregate objective. The resulting VCG incentive question becomes degenerate. Giving each type a positive-mass VCG role restores a nontrivial externality, but changes who acts strategically. A finite-population approximation or a representative aggregator could make that a sensible new model; neither is the theorem’s individual-agent guarantee.

Theorem 2 inherits that problem and adds a repeated-interaction choice. If households answer separately over time, one household’s reports have vanishing influence on the type estimate and allocation in the continuum. If a type-level aggregator supplies the repeated reports, the aggregator is again the strategic player, and the theorem’s bound is for that new player. The paper’s online regret rate remains a natural object to study under that model, but the continuous-population formulation does not itself supply a computational question: the guarantees concern query counts and learning error, while design and optimization still depend on how the feasible set is represented.

The negative case has a real limit. A mass-weighted one-shot learning and VCG theorem for demand-response cohorts is coherent, and the paper’s quadratic example with a rational polytope gives it a plausible setting. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario makes such a study worthwhile. My case is narrower: these are credible extensions of statistical mechanism-design guarantees, but the paper offers no named complexity result for ChoCo to mirror, and the proponent’s cohort bounds rely on changing the strategic unit from individual households to coordinated blocs.
