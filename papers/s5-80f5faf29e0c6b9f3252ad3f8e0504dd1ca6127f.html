What the mirror covers

The proposed selector question follows Theorem 17's refinement-existence theme and could extend to Theorems 18–21; it leaves the group-theoretic criteria and the multiwinner and reversal-specific characterizations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a specified representation class of \(C_\infty\), when does a \(W\)-equivariant resolute selector exist over rational ranking distributions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: the paper’s *tie-breaking question* has a natural population version, even though the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

The plausible regime is a large public consultation in which many respondents submit ballots from a relatively small set of rankings over \(n\) alternatives. A type is a ranking, possibly paired with a decision-power class if the application distinguishes classes of respondents; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the population, and the support size \(\tau\) can be much smaller than the number of respondents. For a decisive correspondence \(C_\infty\) defined on such distributions, the analogue of a resolute refinement is a selector \(f\) with \(f(\mu)\in C_\infty(\mu)\). If \(W\le S_n\) describes which alternatives are interchangeable, require \(f(\psi\cdot\mu)=\psi f(\mu)\) for every \(\psi\in W\). The instance would specify \(n\), \(W\), and a representation of \(C_\infty\); a solution would be such a selector over the distribution domain. This is recognizable as the paper’s refinement question, rather than a mirror of fractional outcomes or noisy profiles.

The closest result is Theorem 17, proved in this paper. It states that for a decisive, \(V\)-anonymous, \(W\)-neutral social preference correspondence \(C\), a \(V\)-anonymous and \(W\)-neutral resolute refinement exists if and only if \(\gcd(\gamma(V),|W|)=1\). That is a strong existence characterization, but it is not a qualifying computational anchor under the requested standard: it does not assert that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The other numbered main results likewise characterize existence or count refinements; none supplies a computational-complexity result. So there are no anchors on which I can honestly base the required per-anchor computational questions.

The continuous selector question also exposes why the analogy is limited. With full neutrality, a uniform distribution over rankings is unchanged by every alternative permutation. A resolute neutral selector would then have to choose a ranking unchanged by every permutation, which is impossible when \(n\ge2\). This gives a simple continuum-specific symmetry obstruction for the unrestricted domain; restricting the domain or weakening \(W\) could change the question. The paper’s authors would likely recognize the selector problem as a continuous version of their existence question, but its answer is about axiomatic symmetry, not computational tractability or a hardness landscape.

That is the weakest point and the decisive one for this programme: even in a credible high-multiplicity scenario, the mirror continues the paper’s axiomatic existence analysis. It does not provide a named computational result whose discrete hardness or algorithmic status can be studied under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest candidate is Theorem 17, but it is an existence characterization, not a computational result. It says when a decisive correspondence has a resolute refinement preserving two symmetries; it gives no complexity claim about an algorithmic problem. The same is true of the paper’s other main results: they characterize existence, or count refinements. So there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The best continuous version is still easy to state. Let a society be a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, define a correspondence \(C_\infty(\mu)\), and ask for a selector \(f\) with \(f(\mu)\in C_\infty(\mu)\) that respects alternative relabelings. A large consultation with repeated ballot types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors could recognize this as their refinement question. That makes it a sensible axiomatic extension. It does not turn the paper’s result into a computational question.

One could instead ask for an algorithm that, on input \(\mu\), returns a ranking in \(C_\infty(\mu)\). If \(C_\infty(\mu)\) is explicitly given, this is just choosing an element from a finite set. If it is implicit and hard to compute, the complexity comes from evaluating that correspondence—for example, from finding a Kemeny ranking—not from Theorem 17’s existence characterization. Or one could make the entire correspondence and distribution domain part of the input and ask whether a globally equivariant selector exists. But the paper gives no finite representation for that global object; that would be a new problem about axiomatic selection, not a computational mirror of a result it proves.

The symmetry obstruction at a uniform distribution makes the boundary especially clear. If the distribution is fixed by every alternative permutation, a neutral resolute selector would need to return a ranking fixed by every permutation, which is impossible for \(n\ge2\). Restricting the domain or weakening neutrality may restore existence, just as the paper’s conditions do in finite settings. Either way, the answer concerns which axioms a selector can satisfy, not its computational complexity.

This is not a case where continuization is incoherent: a measure over rankings is natural, and the refinement question survives. The negative case is narrower but firm for ChoCo’s purpose: even the strongest plausible mirror continues an axiomatic existence programme, while any computational task one adds either becomes trivial when the correspondence is explicit or imports a different optimization problem when it is not. No anchor raised here supports a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror of this paper.
