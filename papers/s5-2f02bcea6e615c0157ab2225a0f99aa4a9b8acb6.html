The continuous mirror question

Given rational criterion profiles \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^m\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\), rational labeled submasses \(\lambda_{t,h}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_h\lambda_{t,h}\le\mu_t\), rational utility knots, ordered categories, and fixed rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), find the piecewise-linear marginal utilities and category thresholds. For each labeled profile-category mass, impose the paper’s category-fit constraints with nonnegative inconsistency slacks; first maximize \(\alpha\epsilon-(1-\alpha)r^{-1}\sum_{t,h}\lambda_{t,h}(\delta^+_{t,h}+\delta^-_{t,h})\), where \(r=\sum_{t,h}\lambda_{t,h}>0\), then, among optimal fits, minimize the total absolute change between adjacent utility slopes. Return the threshold-rule category for each profile and the resulting category mass shares.

The model it lives in

Represent each applicant by its complete criterion profile, with the labeled submass by category retained to capture inconsistent assignment examples. The decision variables are the marginal utilities, thresholds, margin, and slack variables; the two-stage objective is the mass-weighted max-margin fit followed by minimum total adjacent-slope change. Apply the learned threshold rule to each profile’s mass to obtain category shares.

The objection that survived

The applicants are cases judged by one decision maker, so their mass records repeated data rather than a population whose preferences drive a collective computation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the max-margin fit, the curvature-minimizing tie-break, and aggregate category shares for repeated profiles; it leaves finite-pool query selection and the paper’s experimental comparisons aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions does aggregating identical profile-and-label cases preserve the finite-instance optimum and predicted category shares exactly?
  • Can query selection over profile types account for their masses while retaining the paper’s active-learning objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s final sorting-model construction, not its whole incremental elicitation procedure. A plausible regime is a large pool of loan applicants or firms rated under one common multi-criteria policy. If the criteria are rounded or bucketed, many cases can share the same complete criterion profile: the number \(n\) of cases may be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles is moderate. A profile’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the pool. Each case still receives one ordered category; the mass assigned to a category is an aggregate share.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It defines \(\gamma_{j\ell}\) as the absolute change between the slopes of adjacent linear segments of criterion \(j\)’s marginal utility function. It shows that the paper’s complexity-control model, which minimizes the sum of these absolute slope changes subject to the constraints of model (M-1) and equality to (M-1)’s optimal objective value \(J^*\), can be written as an LP: replace each absolute value by a variable \(\gamma_{j\ell}\), impose \(\gamma_{j\ell}\ge d_{j\ell}\) and \(\gamma_{j\ell}\ge-d_{j\ell}\), and retain the \(J^*\) equality and the (M-1) constraints. Those constraints encode category assignments with slack, piecewise-linear utilities, threshold gaps, utility bounds, and the permitted margin range. This is a formulation result, not a theorem stated in complexity-class terms; the polynomial-time expectation below follows from standard rational LP optimization.

I would pose the continuous problem as Mass-MCS-MinCurvature. An instance has \(m\) criteria, \(q\) ordered categories, rational piecewise-linear knot locations, and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) criterion profiles \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^m\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. It also gives reference-label masses \(\lambda_{t,h}\ge0\): the fraction of the population with profile \(t\) already assigned by the decision maker to category \(h\). Require \(\sum_h\lambda_{t,h}\le\mu_t\), and let \(r=\sum_{t,h}\lambda_{t,h}>0\). Allowing several positive \(\lambda_{t,h}\) for one profile preserves the paper’s ability to model inconsistent assignment examples.

For each labeled profile-category pair, introduce nonnegative slacks \(\delta^+_{t,h},\delta^-_{t,h}\), and let \(U_t=\sum_j u_j(x_{tj})\). Use the paper’s category-fit constraints: for \(h=1\), \(U_t\le b_1-\epsilon+\delta^-_{t,1}\); for \(h=q\), \(U_t\ge b_{q-1}-\delta^+_{t,q}\); and for \(1<h<q\), \(b_{h-1}-\delta^+_{t,h}\le U_t\le b_h-\epsilon+\delta^-_{t,h}\). The functions \(u_j\) are piecewise linear over the given knots, with the paper’s utility, threshold, and margin constraints. First maximize
\[ \alpha\epsilon-(1-\alpha)\frac{1}{r}\sum_{t,h}\lambda_{t,h} (\delta^+_{t,h}+\delta^-_{t,h}), \]
for fixed rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Then, among models attaining that optimum, minimize the total absolute change between adjacent slopes. The solution is the optimal marginal utilities and thresholds, their slack certificate, and the resulting category mass shares for the pool. Each profile is still assigned by the paper’s threshold rule; only the population and aggregate inconsistency measure use mass.

This is a recognizable extension of the authors’ problem: it retains their non-monotonic additive value functions, ordered thresholds, tolerance for inconsistent assignment examples, and curvature-minimizing tie-break among best-fitting models. Mass-weighting the reference examples and reporting category shares are the natural aggregate versions when the cases occur in repeated types. The continuous object is the population of cases, not a fractional category for each case.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: the support-based formulation has size polynomial in \(m\), \(q\), the number of utility knots, \(\tau\), and the rational input length. The weighted max-margin stage is an LP, and Theorem 1 supplies the LP formulation for the curvature stage. The result does not establish a new complexity separation: fitting the finite model is already optimization-based, and the main gain here would be scaling with distinct profiles rather than with every case. Follow-up questions include whether mass-weighted fitting agrees with fitting the expanded finite population, and how to select queries over profile types while accounting for their masses.

The soft spot is that the paper classifies alternatives; it does not study a society of preference-bearing voters, and its active questioning models one decision maker. A referee who reads “population” narrowly as voters may reject this as outside the programme’s core. The case survives best when the alternatives really are a large population of applicants or firms, and the claimed scope is expressly limited to the final sorting-model construction—not to the decision maker’s entire elicitation process.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only plausible anchor, but it is a formulation result: it converts the absolute slope-change terms in (M-2) into an LP using epigraph variables. The paper gives no complexity classification for that problem. Its main computational setting is fitting a sorting rule from examples supplied interactively by one decision maker, then applying that rule to the remaining alternatives.

The proposed mass version is mathematically coherent. With repeated criterion profiles and label masses \(\lambda_{t,h}\), it gives a weighted version of the same LP. But that makes the mass distribution a dataset of cases being classified. The criterion profiles describe alternatives; they are not preferences held by a population whose collective choice is being computed. The applicants or firms do not supply judgments in this model. One decision maker does. Counting cases by profile may compress the data, but it does not make the society continuous in ChoCo’s sense.

The stronger applicant-cohort scenario does not close that gap. If the goal is to learn one decision maker’s common rating policy from many repeated applicant profiles, the mass LP asks how to weight repeated training examples and report category shares. The model still has no population-dependent choice or optimization: unlabeled applicants affect only the post-hoc distribution of predicted categories. Giving the applicants preferences and aggregating them would create a different problem, with a different source of judgments and a different objective. The paper’s active-learning procedure over a finite pool could also be extended to profile types, but that would be population-based query selection in a classification task, not a continuous mirror of a social-choice computation.

So the mirror has a sensible high-multiplicity data interpretation, and I would not call that interpretation inherently bad. The negative case rests on fit with the programme: Theorem 1 does not establish the computational result the programme is charting, and its mass extension is about repeated classification records rather than a continuous society of preference-bearing agents. That case against is strongest at the scope boundary and weak as a universal claim; a programme that includes high-multiplicity population classification could reasonably pursue it.
