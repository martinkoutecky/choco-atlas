The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a compliant computational anchor for this paper: it has no numbered result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Theorem 3.5 says every EFX allocation under nonnegative additive valuations is \((10/17)\)-MMS; Theorem 3.7 gives the same guarantee for PMMS. Theorem 4.2 gives allocations that are both PMMS and EFX\(_0\), with ratios tending to \(10/17\), and Corollary 4.3 states that both constants are exact. These are strong named results, but they are structural guarantee and tightness results, not computational complexity results. Theorem 5.2’s fixed-\(n\) EFX/price-of-anarchy equivalence is also an identity, not a complexity classification.

There is a plausible population-continuous research lead behind those results. Consider a large cohort of agents with finitely many complete valuation types \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_g\) of finitely many good types \(g\). Cap each person’s bundle at \(K\) goods. An allocation is a mass \(x_{t,B}\) of type-\(t\) agents receiving each integral bundle \(B\), with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B} B_gx_{t,B}=q_g\). Individuals still receive indivisible bundles; only the type-level averages are fractional. A precise computational question would be: given this rational instance and \(\beta\), decide whether every feasible EFX mass allocation gives each positive-mass recipient at least \(\beta\) times their continuous MMS. Define that MMS by partitioning the per-capita supply into a mass-one distribution over integral bundles and maximizing the least value in its support.

The regime is credible for large standardized cohorts—for example, many students with a small set of shared valuation types and course-seat supplies that grow with cohort size. It preserves the paper’s EFX-versus-MMS question and gives the authors a recognizable analogue. In this model I would expect the sharp universal factor still to be \(10/17\): the paper’s lower-bound charging argument applies to bundle values, and its finite tight examples are themselves instances of the mass model. But that is an extension of a structural theorem, not evidence about the complexity of the certification problem; the paper does not establish whether that problem is tractable or hard. That gap is the weakest point and prevents this from counting as an anchored continuous-computation case under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchors are not computational results of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. Theorem 3.5 and Theorem 3.7 prove universal fairness guarantees; Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.3 give tight examples and an exact constant; Theorem 5.2 establishes an equivalence with a scheduling price of anarchy. None classifies an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity result. That is a decisive gap for this paper as an input to a programme about the complexity landscape.

The proponent’s cohort model is coherent. Agents can have finitely many complete valuation types, receive integral bundles, and appear as masses \(x_{t,B}\); per-capita supplies make the resource constraint meaningful. EFX and PMMS depend on valuations and bundles, not named identities, so I would not reject the model on that ground. Nor does the indivisibility of each person’s bundle rule it out. The problem is what the model asks: extending a universal guarantee is a continuous structural theorem, while computing an instance-specific worst EFX-to-MMS ratio is a new optimization problem over supply-feasible allocations. The paper does not study that problem or motivate its computational significance. It cannot serve as the computational mirror of one of these results just because a computational question can be built around them.

That applies to the other apparent anchors too. The tight family is a construction, not an algorithmic task, and the fixed-\(n\) machine-covering correspondence is an identity between extremal ratios, not a complexity classification. A high-multiplicity version of any of these could be mathematically sensible; that would support the regime, but it would not supply the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s eligibility criterion, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proposed population model is plausible, and a separate computational study of instance-specific fair-allocation guarantees might be worthwhile. What the paper itself offers, however, is no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
