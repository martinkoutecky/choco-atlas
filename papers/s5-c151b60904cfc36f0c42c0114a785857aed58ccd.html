The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a computational complexity result. Its closest computational result is empirical: Algorithm 2 finds Condorcet domains of size \(1082\) on \(10\) alternatives and \(2349\) on \(11\), improving the previous records \(1069\) and \(2324\). These are lower bounds from a heuristic search, not proofs of optimality or complexity classifications; the paper says finding the largest domains remains open. The two corollaries in Section 6 are unnumbered and concern restrictions of domains, not computational complexity. So there is no eligible named anchor under the stated requirement.

The strongest population-level mirror I can make is a proposed version of the paper’s maximum-domain problem. Let a type be a complete ranking \(\pi\) of \(n\) alternatives. Given a reference population with rational type masses \(\rho_\pi\), choose retained masses \(x_\pi\in[0,\rho_\pi]\) to maximize \(\sum_\pi x_\pi\), subject to the condition that the support \(\{\pi:x_\pi>0\}\) is a Condorcet domain. A solution is the vector of retained masses. In a very large consultation with many people sharing each ranking type, this asks for the largest share of an available electorate that can be admitted while guaranteeing transitive majority relations for every odd finite election among the admitted types. With a uniform reference population, \(\rho_\pi=1/n!\), the optimum is exactly \(f(n)/n!\), where \(f(n)\) is the largest Condorcet-domain size. Thus the paper’s search for large domains supplies the matching empirical lead.

This mirror preserves the paper’s core question: how much preference diversity can coexist with the Condorcet property? The uniform reference population also gives every ranking equal weight, matching the paper’s cardinality objective. I would expect the mass version to inherit any hardness of maximum-domain selection; the exact uniform case embeds the discrete problem. The paper establishes no such hardness, however, so the complexity remains open. Natural follow-up questions include whether weighted reference populations admit useful algorithms, and whether the support constraint can be handled through the paper’s triple and restriction structure.

The scenario is strained at the paper’s larger values of \(n\): the uniform reference population contains all \(n!\) ranking types, so a high-multiplicity interpretation requires a population much larger than \(n!\). A real electorate with fewer types gives a more plausible regime, but then the problem is a weighted generalization rather than the paper’s exact objective. Also, at an optimum each selected type receives all its available mass: fractional retention does not improve the solution. The continuous model therefore gives a faithful population interpretation, but it offers no evident continuous-optimization advantage.

So this is a defensible modeling lead for the maximum-CD search reported in the paper, especially its \(10\)- and \(11\)-alternative discoveries. It is not a case anchored in a qualifying numbered computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the missing anchor: this paper proves no named computational complexity result. Its computational contribution is a heuristic algorithm and empirical lower bounds: domains of sizes \(1082\) and \(2349\). Those are discovered examples, while finding the largest domains remains open. The paper’s definition of \(f(n)\) is an extremal problem over sets of rankings; it supplies no complexity classification to continuize. Under the programme’s stated criteria, that is a decisive mismatch.

The proposed population version also exposes the mismatch. If \(x_\pi\) is the retained mass of ranking \(\pi\), the Condorcet-domain condition depends only on whether \(x_\pi\) is zero or positive. With every \(\rho_\pi>0\), an optimum therefore takes each \(x_\pi\) to either \(0\) or \(\rho_\pi\). The problem reduces to choosing a subset \(D\) that maximizes \(\sum_{\pi\in D}\rho_\pi\). Under uniform \(\rho\), this is exactly \(f(n)/n!\), a rescaling of the paper’s count. Under nonuniform \(\rho\), it becomes a weighted domain-selection problem: the masses affect the objective, while the Condorcet guarantee still quantifies over arbitrary odd profiles on the selected types.

There is a coherent scenario for that weighted problem: an institution could choose which preference rankings its ballot system admits, maximizing the share of a reference electorate whose rankings remain admissible while ensuring cycle-free aggregation. Its relevance depends on a policy choice absent from the paper: that the institution may restrict admissible preferences, and that matching the observed population’s mass is the right measure of preference freedom. The resulting question may merit independent study. It adds a population-selection objective to the paper’s domain search; it does not continue a population-size parameter or mirror a named computational result.

The negative case is strongest on programme fit and weakest as a universal claim. The paper provides no multiplicity axis in its optimization input: \(n\) counts alternatives, and the search assigns rules to triples. Still, I cannot rule out that weighted Condorcet-domain design is worthwhile in a suitable institution. Its case would rest on the added policy model, rather than on a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
