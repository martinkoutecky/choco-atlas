The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is conditional: the paper has a plausible continuous-population mirror, but it does not contain a qualifying anchor under your rules.

In the supplied text there is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. Examples 1–4, the mechanism-properties table, and the empirical convergence claims do not qualify. The closest computational statements are unnumbered claims in Sections 4 and 10 that exact RSD probabilities are \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-complete/\(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard and that checking envy, efficiency, and manipulability can be NP-hard. Those claims are cited from Aziz, Saban, and others rather than proved or named in this paper. Thus, strictly, the paper has no eligible anchor and no compliant “one continuous problem per anchor.”

If that anchor restriction is relaxed, my lead mirror would be:

Continuum RSD Marginal Computation. Fix \(m\) objects and let \(T=S_m\) be the set of complete rankings. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of agents with ranking \(t\). Give each object capacity \(1/m\), so the unit-mass population receives one object in total. For a large integer \(K\), form the replicated finite market with \(K\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(K/m\) copies of every object. Let \(A^{K,\mathrm{RSD}}_{t,j}\) be the probability that a type-\(t\) agent receives object \(j\) under RSD, and let \(A^{\mathrm{PS}}_{t,j}\) be the corresponding type-level allocation under probabilistic serial. Define

\[ A^{\infty,\mathrm{RSD}}_{t,j} = \lim_{K\to\infty} A^{K,\mathrm{RSD}}_{t,j}. \]

Given \((m,\mu)\) and precision \(L\), compute \(A^{\infty,\mathrm{RSD}}\) and \(A^{\mathrm{PS}}\) to additive error \(2^{-L}\), and decide whether PS stochastically dominates RSD for every type. With a common risk parameter \(\alpha\), the same output can compare

\[ W(A)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{j\in M}A_{t,j}u_\alpha(t,j). \]

The continuous object here is unmistakably the population distribution \(\mu\), not merely the fractional assignment matrix. The matrix is only the necessary representation of what each type receives. The decision/output variable is the type-level marginal assignment; the computational objective is to recover it and the induced welfare or dominance comparison.

A convincing regime is large course or school assignment: many students, a fixed collection of schools or course categories, and relatively few distinct complete rankings induced by programme, timetable, or commuting constraints. If the effective support of \(\mu\) is \(q\), the intended regime is \(K\gg q\), with \(m\) fixed. The paper’s own discussion of large-capacity markets and the asymptotic equivalence of PS and RSD supports this as a natural scenario rather than an artificial relaxation.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. The PS allocation is obtained by a finite event-driven eating process. In the balanced high-multiplicity regime, the random priority fluctuations disappear and the RSD marginals converge to a deterministic flow; the paper cites precisely this kind of PS/RSD equivalence. Thus the finite-market \(\#\mathrm{P}\) difficulty is plausibly a population-multiplicity effect that vanishes in the continuum, while the ranking structure and the mechanism comparison remain intact.

The weakest point is substantial: the mirror replaces the paper’s one-copy market by a replicated-capacity limit, and that may erase the very RSD–PS differences the paper empirically studies. Moreover, the only suitable hardness statement is not a numbered result of this paper. So this is a credible continuous analogue of the underlying matching problem, but not a valid anchored positive case under the prompt’s strict grading rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is formal: this paper has no eligible computational anchor. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting a complexity result. Its actual contributions are empirical comparisons, utility-model experiments, and summaries of known axiomatic properties. The claims that computing RSD probabilities is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard and that checking manipulability or envy is NP-hard are unnumbered citations to other papers, not results established here. Under the stated gate, there is therefore no paper result to which a continuous question can be attached.

The proposed “Continuum RSD Marginal Computation” is a sensible adjacent problem, but it is not a mirror of a result in this paper. It changes the paper’s one-copy market into a replicated-capacity market with \(K/m\) copies of every object. That co-modification is not forbidden by the programme, but it is a substantive new model: the paper’s distinctive setting is precisely scarce, one-copy supply. If capacities remain fixed while the population grows, the served mass tends to zero; if capacities scale with the population, one has moved to the many-copy regime already discussed in the cited literature; if the number of objects also grows, there is no stable fixed finite type space of rankings. The proponent has therefore found a plausible high-multiplicity assignment model, not a continuous version of this paper’s computational contribution.

There is also no canonical continuum RSD supplied by \(\mu\) alone. Finite RSD is a uniform random permutation of named agents. An atomless population has no first agent in such a sequential process. The proposed definition repairs this by choosing a particular replication sequence and a particular random-priority construction. That can define a limit, but then the object being studied is an asymptotic regularization of finite markets, not an intrinsic mechanism on the continuous society. Different priority or capacity regularizations need not represent the same question.

The paper’s manipulability results fare worse. A unilateral report change in a normalized \(K\)-agent population changes the society by

\[ \mu'=\mu+\frac{1}{K}\bigl(\delta_{t'}-\delta_t\bigr), \]

which vanishes as \(K\to\infty\). Thus an individual cannot change the aggregate allocation in the continuum. Giving a deviating agent positive mass turns the problem into coalition manipulation; retaining an individual deviation makes the market effect disappear. Either way, this is no longer the paper’s finite-agent strategyproofness question. The reported fractions of manipulable profiles likewise require a second-order distribution over societies, not merely one continuous society \(\mu\).

Welfare comparisons and type-level envy could certainly be defined over \(\mu\), and I would not make the mistaken objection that fractional assignments are somehow outside the programme. Nor would I claim that a known PS/RSD limit is uninteresting merely because its answer is equality. Those are legitimate caveats. But they only establish that one can formulate a new high-multiplicity matching problem. They do not rescue a computational anchor from this paper.

So the strongest honest verdict is narrower than the literal universal claim: there is no worthwhile *anchored continuous mirror of this paper*. The paper may inspire a separate continuous assignment project, but the proponent has not shown that this paper contains the computational result required to motivate it. If the rubric insists on “no worthwhile mirror in any conceivable scenario,” that stronger claim is not defensible: one-sided matching genuinely has natural high-multiplicity regimes.
