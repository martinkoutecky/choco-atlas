The continuous mirror question

Given sample access to a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\delta_{a_t}\) over locations \(a_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), can a randomized algorithm using \(q=O(1/\epsilon^2)\) independent draws, with no dependence on \(d\) or \(\tau\), return \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\) such that \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\mu_t\|a_t-x\|_1]\leq(1+\epsilon)\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^d}\sum_t\mu_t\|a_t-y\|_1\)?

The model it lives in

Types are residents’ ideal locations \(a_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), with mass \(\mu_t\); the mechanism draws \(q\) residents and returns a facility location \(x\). Its objective is expected per-capita social cost \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\mu_t\|a_t-x\|_1]\), compared with the minimum over all facility locations.

What the mirror covers

Covers the one-facility coordinate-wise median approximation in \((\mathbb{R}^d,\|\cdot\|_1)\); it leaves the tree and multiple-facility results and the auction results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(O(1/\epsilon^2)\) sample guarantee be made nonasymptotic and uniform over finitely supported \(\mu\), including atoms and ties?
  • Is the \(1/\epsilon^2\) sample dependence necessary for this expected-cost guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is for a continuous population median approximation problem. Let a type be a resident’s ideal location \(a_t\in\mathbb Q^d\), which completely determines their preferences over facility locations under \(\ell_1\) distance. The society is a finite-support measure \(\mu\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A plausible regime is a city with many residents concentrated at a much smaller number \(\tau\) of shared residential locations: \(n\gg\tau\). Mass means fraction of residents. The decision is where to place one facility, \(x\in\mathbb R^d\), and its per-capita social cost is
\[ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\|a_t-x\|_1. \]
The best location is a coordinate-wise median of the population.

The anchor is Theorem 3.11, “High-Dimensional Sampling Median,” proved in this paper. It says that for agents in \((\mathbb R^d,\|\cdot\|_1)\), for every \(\epsilon>0\), sampling \(c=\Theta(1/\epsilon^2)\) agents and returning their coordinate-wise median gives, in expectation as \(n\to\infty\), a \(1+\epsilon\) approximation to the optimal social cost of the full-information median. The sample bound does not depend on \(d\).

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. Population Median Approximation: A society is a finitely supported rational probability measure \(\mu=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\delta_{a_t}\) over \(\mathbb Q^d\), with finite first moment. The mechanism has access to independent draws of resident types from \(\mu\), but can inspect only \(q\) reports. For each \(\epsilon>0\), is there a randomized algorithm using \(q=O(1/\epsilon^2)\) draws, independent of \(d\) and \(\tau\), that returns \(x\) with
\[ \mathbb E[\mathrm{SC}_\mu(x)]\le (1+\epsilon)\min_{y\in\mathbb R^d}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)? \]
The sample coordinate-wise median is the candidate algorithm; a solution is an algorithm and a proof of that guarantee. This is tractable in the sample-query sense: after drawing \(q\) vectors, computing the coordinate-wise median takes polynomial time in the sample and dimension. The result would not establish a new polynomial-time separation for an explicitly supplied mass list: with the full list and weights available to the solver, weighted coordinate-wise medians already give the exact optimum.

This is a credible mirror of the paper’s facility-location question. The type, distance-based preferences, random sample, chosen facility, and social-cost objective all carry over directly; the population measure makes the high-multiplicity interpretation explicit. It covers the single-facility median approximation result, not the tree and multiple-facility limits or the auction results. Those auction results depend on communication with identifiable bidders, which does not transfer naturally to a mass of agents.

The weak point is also the reason to keep the claim narrow. The paper already studies finite societies as \(n\to\infty\), and its sample-access model is central to the result. Writing the society as \(\mu\) may be a faithful continuous formulation, but it may add little beyond the paper’s asymptotic theorem. The mirror is strongest when the question is sample complexity under limited elicitation; as a computational problem with an explicitly known \(\mu\), it is routine. Further questions include whether the \(1/\epsilon^2\) sample dependence is necessary, how to state a finite-population bound uniformly in \(\tau\) and bit length \(L\), and which high-probability guarantees retain the dimension independence of the expected-cost result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a model fork. If the rational support list \((a_t,\mu_t)\) is the input, the problem is ordinary optimization over that list: its weighted coordinate-wise median minimizes \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu\). To make limited information matter, the mirror instead gives the mechanism sample access to \(\mu\). That is a sample-query problem, not the explicit-support computational problem at the centre of the ChoCo model.

But this sample-query version closely matches the paper’s own result. For rational \(\mu\), take populations of size \(n\) with exactly \(n\mu_t\) residents of each type, along a sequence of \(n\) divisible by the mass denominators. Sampling a fixed number of residents without replacement converges to independent draws from \(\mu\). The paper’s rank-limit analysis and Theorem 3.11 already establish the corresponding asymptotic approximation guarantee. A finite-sample bound uniform over \(\mu\) might sharpen that analysis, but it looks like a technical extension rather than a new continuous computational problem.

This is the best negative case: the explicit-support version adds no limited-information question, while the sample-access version is essentially the paper’s own asymptotic result. It does not defeat the anchor under the brief’s rules, though. The paper has a named algorithmic result, the location types and mass objective make sense, and its finite-to-continuous limit is itself a result. I can’t honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives here.
