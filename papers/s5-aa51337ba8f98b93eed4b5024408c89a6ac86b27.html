The continuous mirror question

Given candidates at rational positions \(x_c\), a target \(c^\star\), a movement amount \(\delta\), and voter types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), positions \(x_t\), and directed contact intensities \(\lambda_{tu}\), choose seed masses \(s_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t s_t\le\beta\). Define limiting activation as the least fixed point reached from \(s\) of \(a_u=\mu_u-(\mu_u-s_u)\exp(-\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_{tu}a_t)\). Activated mass of type \(t\) moves toward \(x_{c^\star}\) by at most \(\delta\); compute the resulting plurality shares \(q_c(s)\) and return feasible seed masses whose increase in margin, \(q_{c^\star}(s)-\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c(s)-(q_{c^\star}^0-\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c^0)\), is within \(\varepsilon\) of its supremum.

The model it lives in

A continuum of voters with masses \(\mu_t\) over political and network types, a contact kernel \(\lambda_{tu}\), seed-mass decisions \(s_t\), and the target candidate’s change in plurality margin as the objective.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 optimizes over vertices in a supplied graph, whereas the mirror replaces that graph with an exchangeable typed network; the proponent acknowledges this restriction but does not establish that the finite cascade’s expected margin converges to the proposed fixed-point objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the single-campaign, exact-view seed-selection problem in a typed stochastic-block regime. It leaves aside arbitrary realized networks, noisy-view experiments, multiround campaigns, and the PageRank heuristics.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove that normalized expected margins in the finite directed independent-cascade block model converge to the stated fixed-point objective, including the relevant parameter regime.
  • Determine the complexity of selecting seed masses as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), and whether Theorem 1’s conditional approximation guarantee has a mass-allocation analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s one-campaign problem on a high-multiplicity, typed social network. The population is the continuous object: masses of voters occupy a small number of political and network roles, and the manipulator chooses a mass of seeds. The interaction kernel is a necessary co-modification to say how influence passes between those roles.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It considers the paper’s Independent Cascade model when every voter’s view of each candidate’s position equals that candidate’s true position. The paper’s algorithm uses the seed budget to greedily maximize influence on \(M\), the voters who do not currently support the target \(c^\star\) but would support it if influenced. The theorem states that its output \(\hat S\) satisfies
\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathrm{MoV}(\hat S)] + X(\hat S) \;\ge\; \frac{1}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right) \max_{S:\,|S|\le B}\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathrm{MoV}(S)]. \]
Here \(X(S)\) is the expected maximum, over opponents \(c\ne c^\star\), of the number of voters who initially do not vote for \(c\) but would do so after receiving the message for \(c^\star\). Thus the theorem gives a constant-factor approximation when \(X(\hat S)=O(\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathrm{MoV}(\hat S)])\). The qualification matters: the guarantee is not unconditional.

Here is a continuous problem that mirrors that result.

Typed Continuum Election Manipulation (lead). An instance has candidates at rational positions \(x_c\in[-1,1]\), a target \(c^\star\), a rational movement amount \(\delta\), a rational seed budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), and a finite set \(T\) of voter types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational political position \(x_t\). For this mirror of Theorem 1, voters have exact views of candidate positions, so their rankings are by distance from \(x_t\) to \(x_c\). A type also records its network role: for each pair of types \(t,u\), the input gives a rational contact intensity \(\lambda_{tu}\).

The contact intensities define a high-multiplicity sequence of finite instances: with \(N\) voters, there are \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), and each potential directed edge from type \(t\) to type \(u\) transmits independently with probability \(\lambda_{tu}/N\), for sufficiently large \(N\). This is the paper’s Independent Cascade process on a typed network. Its continuum limit describes final activation by the least fixed point reached from the seed masses \(s_t\):
\[ a_u^{(0)}=s_u,\qquad a_u^{(k+1)} =\mu_u-(\mu_u-s_u) \exp\!\left(-\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_{tu}a_t^{(k)}\right). \]
Here \(0\le s_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t s_t\le\beta\), and \(a_u\) is the mass of type \(u\) eventually reached by the campaign.

An activated voter of type \(t\) moves to
\[ x'_t=x_t+\min(\delta,|x_{c^\star}-x_t|) \operatorname{sign}(x_{c^\star}-x_t). \]
Let \(q_c(s)\) be candidate \(c\)’s final vote share: the unactivated mass of each type votes as before, and its activated mass votes for the nearest candidate to \(x'_t\). Let \(q_c^0\) be the initial share. The task is: given these data and a rational \(\varepsilon>0\), output feasible seed masses \(s\) whose change in margin of victory is within \(\varepsilon\) of the supremum:
\[ \Delta(s) =q_{c^\star}(s)-\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c(s) -\left(q_{c^\star}^0-\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c^0\right). \]
The requested solution is the allocation \(s\), together with its induced limiting activation and vote shares. A natural complexity target is polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\), where \(\tau=|T|\) and \(L\) is the input bit length.

A plausible regime is a large platform campaign with millions of users but tens or hundreds of model-relevant audience cells: users in a cell share a political position, candidate views, and network role, while the matrix captures how likely information is to pass between cells. Seed budgets of a positive fraction of the population are plausible here; the paper itself studies budgets of \(5\%\), \(10\%\), and \(15\%\). Each type is complete for this model: users in it are indistinguishable in their preferences and their modeled interaction probabilities. The population is much larger than the support size, and the campaign allocates audience mass rather than choosing people at one-person granularity.

I think the authors would recognize this as a mirror of their seed-selection problem: it keeps plurality, the target candidate, movement toward that candidate, network diffusion, a seed budget, and change in margin of victory. The fixed-point equation is the limiting description of their campaign’s outcome; the computational question remains how to choose the seeds. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s single-campaign, exact-view setting. It does not claim to cover the paper’s noisy-view experiments, multiround campaigns, or PageRank heuristics.

I would treat the continuous optimization as a Class C candidate, with the classification open. The arbitrary-graph hardness discussed in the paper does not automatically transfer to a model with only \(\tau\) exchangeable network roles. The apparent source of difficulty is instead the coupled fixed-point activation and the max-opponent margin objective. It is possible that the type-level problem has useful concavity or diminishing-returns structure, but that needs to be established. The immediate questions are whether the theorem’s approximation guarantee has a mass-allocation analogue, including a continuum version of its \(X\) term; whether the objective admits an efficient approximation algorithm; and how well finite high-multiplicity instances approximate the continuum optimum.

The paper says its zero-noise, two-candidate, large-\(\delta\) subcase inherits hardness from Wilder and Vorobeychik [36]. It does not state that claim as a numbered result here, so I do not use it as an anchor. My weakest point is the network restriction: the paper allows detailed fixed graphs, whereas this mirror assumes exchangeable users within network roles. That limits the mirror’s scope. I still think the typed regime is a sensible version of the authors’ problem, rather than a change to the objective that made it easy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a named computational result, so the “nothing computational to mirror” objection does not apply. The strongest case against its mirror is that its optimization is driven by the realized network, not just by the distribution of voter opinions. The algorithm chooses seed vertices, and its guarantee rests on reachability in the graph’s live-edge realizations. Two voters with the same political position can have very different influence because one sits on a bridge or reaches a different set of voters.

That makes the proposed type description incomplete for the paper’s stated problem. Political position, block membership, and average contact rates do not preserve the neighborhoods and path overlaps that determine which seeds are best. The fixed-point recurrence describes expected activation in an exchangeable block network with type-based seeding. It does not, by itself, describe optimal seed selection on the paper’s known, realized graph. To preserve that graph and its individual seed choices, a type would need to encode enough network position to distinguish many vertices; then the support can grow with the population.

A stronger mirror could make the network itself a stochastic block model or graphon, with types recording political and latent network roles and the manipulator allocating seed mass across them. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem. But it changes the network input and the seeding information available: the finite theorem selects among vertices in a supplied graph, while the mean-field version optimizes over roles in an exchangeable network. The difference matters to the theorem’s central question. There is also a smaller mismatch: the proposed vote shares use limiting activation masses, while Theorem 1 evaluates the expected margin after a random cascade. Concentration may bridge that gap in a suitable block model, so it is not decisive.

This defeats the proponent’s proposed recurrence as a direct mirror of Theorem 1 on the paper’s arbitrary network instances. It does not defeat every plausible mirror. A large platform modeled by a stochastic block network is a credible setting, and mass seeding there is meaningful. Because that setting could still produce a worthwhile computational question, I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no continuous mirror is worth pursuing. The negative case is strongest for the paper’s realized graph model and weak at the broader “in any scenario” claim.
