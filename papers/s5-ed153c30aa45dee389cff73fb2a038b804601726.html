\[ M(D)=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b+1}\int_{-1}^{b} C_b(D)\,db, \]
and the authors apply that score to district shapes. That is a named mathematical definition, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s contribution is a shape measure and an empirical study; it explicitly sets population aside when measuring shape. So there is no result here that I can honestly present as an anchor under the requested rules.

The best candidate I can construct from the paper’s redistricting motivation is Continuous-Population Compactness Redistricting. An instance would give a state map divided into \(\tau\) adjacent precinct regions \(Q_t\), a population mass \(\mu_t\) for each precinct type \(t\), a desired number \(k\) of districts, and a population-balance tolerance \(\epsilon\). A type is a large cohort in one precinct sharing the political and demographic attributes relevant to the model; its mass is its fraction of the state’s population. The regime is many residents—say, hundreds of thousands or millions—represented by far fewer precinct types. A solution partitions the precincts into \(k\) connected districts \(D_1,\dots,D_k\), each with mass within \(\epsilon\) of \(1/k\), and maximizes \(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k M(D_j)\). The output is the precinct-to-district assignment.

This uses the paper’s actual circularity measure and its concern with redistricting, while making population mass central to the constraints. I would expect the problem to remain hard if its difficulty comes from the combinatorics of connected precinct partitions: changing the number of residents represented by each precinct does not remove that structure. But that is a prediction about this proposed problem, not a conclusion supported by a named result in the paper.

The plausibility limit is substantial. The authors would likely recognize this as a natural optimization extension of their redistricting application, but their paper measures the shapes of already drawn districts; it does not optimize boundaries or balance population. Thus this candidate does not mirror a result they prove. It is also possible to evaluate the paper’s score for a district while population stays irrelevant, but that would make continuization of the society incidental.

So the positive case is that the paper’s redistricting setting can motivate a credible continuous-population optimization problem using \(M\). Its weakest point—and under the requested anchor standard, the decisive one—is that the paper supplies no computational result for that problem, or for any other problem, to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. It defines \(M(D)\), a geometric score for a planar region, and reports measurements on selected congressional districts. It proves no algorithmic or complexity claim about redistricting. The proposed optimization therefore adds a new problem rather than continuizing one established here.

Take that proposal in its strongest form: partition precinct regions into connected, population-balanced districts while maximizing their average \(M\). It is coherent and plausibly relevant to the authors’ redistricting motivation. But \(M(D)\) depends on the district’s geometry; the paper explicitly sets population aside. Changing the population masses while holding the boundaries fixed leaves every score unchanged. Population can enter the proposed problem’s balance constraints, but the measured object remains the map.

A finer population continuum does not close that gap. If districts remain unions of precincts, the boundary choices are still geographic partition choices; continuous masses only alter the balance constraints. If the model permits fractional assignment of locations, it needs new rules to ensure the results are coherent geographic districts. That becomes a continuum geometric partition problem whose central object is not the paper’s treatment of a society as a distribution over voter types.

The article’s redistricting context makes this a plausible new research direction, and that is the negative case’s weak point: such an extension could be worthwhile on its own. But the proponent’s candidate is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper’s actual contribution is already a computation over shapes, while its population data do not define the score.
