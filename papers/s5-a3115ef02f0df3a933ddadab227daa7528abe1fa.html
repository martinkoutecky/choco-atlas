The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: it proves no result about a problem being in \(\mathrm{P}\), hard, fixed parameter tractable, or similar. The closest result is Corollary 1, proved here from Theorems 2 and 3: when buyers use maximal strategies, the selling mechanism implements in subgame-perfect equilibria in the strong sense the maximum competitive equilibria. Equivalently, its equilibrium outcomes have seller-optimal stable payoffs and maximum equilibrium prices. That is an equilibrium characterization, not a complexity result, so it cannot honestly serve as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest adjacent positive case is a continuous-mass version of the assignment market whose target outcome is the one characterized by that corollary. Consider buyer types \(b\in B\), each with rational mass \(\mu_b\), and seller types \(s\in S\), each supplying rational mass \(\nu_s\). A type records the full valuation vector \(a_{bs}\) for the objects, so agents of a type are identical for the market’s purposes. Even if the total numbers of buyers and sellers are very large, the supports \(B\) and \(S\) could be small. This fits a replicated market for standardized jobs and workers, where each side has many agents but relatively few valuation and object types.

Call the problem Continuous Seller-Optimal Assignment. Given those type supports, masses, and nonnegative rational values, find a mass matching \(x_{bs}\), prices \(p_s\), and buyer utilities \(u_b\) forming a competitive equilibrium with seller-optimal prices. Each buyer type’s mass is assigned among object types and the outside option; assignments use at most each seller type’s supply. Buyers receive only objects maximizing their surplus \(a_{bs}-p_s\), and any unsold supply has price zero. Among equilibria, the requested price vector is componentwise greatest, and the output includes a compatible matching. The result I would expect is Class A: the total-surplus assignment LP and its dual give a direct route to computing an equilibrium and selecting seller-optimal prices. The natural parameters are \(|B|\), \(|S|\), and the input bit length, rather than the number of individual agents.

This mirrors the market outcome in Corollary 1, not its strategic implementation. The paper’s mechanism relies on individually chosen prices and buyers acting in sequence; a mass matching does not itself preserve that strategic structure. Whether a precise nonatomic pricing game can recover the corollary’s implementation claim is a further question, as is what happens when extra costs or prices split types and enlarge the support.

That limitation is the weak point, and it matters: the continuous assignment problem appears naturally mirrorable, but it does not expose a discrete hardness result whose status might change under continuization. The defensible positive claim is therefore narrow: this paper’s seller-optimal market outcome has a plausible continuous-population counterpart. It is not an anchor-based case that the paper contributes to the computational complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–3 and Corollary 1 characterize the outcomes of a strategic mechanism: under maximal buyer strategies and the strong equilibrium concept, its outcomes are precisely the maximum competitive equilibria. They make no claim about the complexity of finding those outcomes, or about any other computational problem. The proponent acknowledges this, so there is no qualifying anchor for a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The proposed continuous assignment problem is sensible on its own. A market with many buyers and sellers but few valuation types is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and a mass matching with competitive prices has a natural LP formulation. But that formulation keeps the paper’s target outcome while dropping its distinctive contribution: sellers individually choose prices, buyers choose sequentially, and seller deviations are tested against maximal continuation behavior. The LP computes a price-taking market equilibrium; it does not capture the paper’s strategic implementation result.

The stronger versions run into a modeling fork. In an atomless market, an individual seller controls zero supply, so a unilateral price deviation cannot have the market effect that the paper’s strong-equilibrium test examines; the sequential choices of individual buyers also need a new aggregate interpretation. Treating each type as a bloc that sets a common price can restore strategic influence, but changes the players and their actions: the bloc is a coordinated price setter, not many identical sellers independently choosing prices. Keeping the original individual players avoids that change, but then the ordered sequence and unit-level deviations remain part of the input; a mass distribution alone does not describe them.

So the negative case is strong for the proposed mirror as a computational contribution of this paper: it is a plausible continuous assignment problem, but not a continuous complexity result anchored in the paper’s claims. I would not argue that continuum assignment markets are unworthy in general, or that the LP’s likely tractability makes the question uninteresting. The weak point is the universal claim: a cohort-level selling game could be worthwhile as a new research direction. It would, however, be a new strategic model rather than a mirror of this paper’s computational result—which the paper does not have.
