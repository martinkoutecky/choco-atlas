Its main findings are empirical: prices predict later ratings, in-class traders contribute information, manipulation appears limited, and BMM prices were more stable than LMSR prices. The paper describes market-making algorithms, but gives no computational problem with a complexity result. That is the decisive gap for a continuous *mirror*.

The strongest rescue is to formalize manipulation as a minimum-mass control problem: given a distribution of students by rating eligibility, information, trading budget, and action costs, find the least mass whose reports and trades can move a market to a target. A large online course could make that population plausible; treating prices or costs as type-level properties is no objection. But the paper reports observed manipulation and asks whether it occurs. It does not define or analyze an optimal attack problem. That formulation may be a worthwhile new project, but it is not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

The same distinction applies to optimizing market-maker parameters or modeling how much insider mass makes prices informative. Those could be useful questions, but they introduce computational objectives absent from the study. So the case against a mirror is strong for this paper, though it cannot rule out a separate computational project on robust instructor-rating markets.
