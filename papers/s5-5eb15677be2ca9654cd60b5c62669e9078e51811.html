Theorem thm:NP-USW

The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many teacher types \(T\), student categories \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-teacher category supplies \(\sigma_g\), quantiles \(\tau_t\), and values \(v_t(g)\), consider finite multisets \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\) as integral bundles. Choose nonnegative masses \(x_{t,b}\) with finite support satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=\sigma_g\) for every \(g\). What is the supremum of \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}v_t(b)\), and is it attained by a feasible finite-support allocation? Equivalently, for a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether some feasible allocation has welfare at least \(q\).

The model it lives in

A configuration allocation assigns mass \(x_{t,b}\) of each teacher type \(t\) to whole bundles \(b\) of student categories, conserves each type mass and per-capita supply, and maximizes per-teacher utilitarian welfare.

The objection that survived

The proponent does not address that vanishing type mass can absorb fixed supply in bundles whose sizes diverge, so a maximum may fail to exist; the mirror should ask about the supremum and attainment.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the unrestricted utilitarian welfare hardness result, Theorem thm:NP-USW. It leaves the balanced utilitarian results, egalitarian welfare results, and identical-valuation results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can pricing over finite bundle configurations be solved in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|G|\), and the binary input length?
  • Which instances have an attained optimum, and how can near-optimal continuum allocations be rounded to finite high-multiplicity instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is for a continuous version of the paper’s unconstrained utilitarian welfare problem. The supplied text does not render theorem numerals: it identifies the result by the label thm:NP-USW in §4.1.1, so I cite that label rather than guess a number.

That theorem is proved in this paper, by a reduction from Exact3Cover. It says that, for goods and any instance with \(\max_i \tau_i<1\), finding an allocation that maximizes utilitarian social welfare is NP-complete. The theorem covers both heterogeneous quantiles and the homogeneous case; the reduction is the authors’ proof, while the paper cites prior work for Exact3Cover’s NP-hardness. This is a useful anchor because the question is whether that discrete hardness persists when the population is represented by masses.

Call the mirror Quantile-Type Continuum Allocation. Its instance has a finite set \(T\) of teacher types and a finite set \(G\) of student categories. A teacher type \(t\) specifies the complete information relevant to this problem: its quantile \(\tau_t\) and its value \(v_t(g)\) for each student category \(g\). The input gives each type a rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and each category a rational supply \(\sigma_g\) of students per unit of teacher population. Supplies scale with the population.

A bundle \(B\) is a finite multiset of student categories: each teacher still receives an integral bundle of indivisible students, and \(v_t(B)\) is exactly the paper’s quantile value for that bundle. The decision variable \(x_{t,B}\geq 0\) is the mass of type-\(t\) teachers assigned bundle \(B\). A feasible allocation satisfies \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,B} b_g x_{t,B}=\sigma_g\) for every category \(g\), where \(b_g\) is the number of category-\(g\) students in \(B\). The question is to maximize \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}v_t(B)\), the per-teacher utilitarian welfare. A solution is a finite list of bundle configurations and their assigned rational masses; fractional mass means splitting a population of indistinguishable teachers across whole-bundle assignments, not giving an individual a fractional student.

The intended regime is a large tutoring or school network with many teachers sharing a relatively small number of full valuation profiles and quantiles, and many students falling into a small number of categories that each teacher type values alike. Thus the number of teachers \(N\) and students \(m\) can be large while \(|T|\) and \(|G|\) stay comparatively small. For a finite instance with \(N\) teachers and \(m_g\) students of category \(g\), the dictionary is \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(\sigma_g=m_g/N\): a discrete allocation gives a continuum allocation, and allocations whose masses lie on the \(1/N\) grid map back. The continuous problem admits finer splits. The paper’s quantile-welfare question is otherwise intact: allocate all goods to maximize the sum of agents’ quantile values. The authors already study heterogeneous quantiles and identical valuations, which makes repeated agent profiles a natural instance regime. This mirror concerns the population; the fact that assignments are described by masses does not turn the students into divisible outcomes.

I would provisionally expect this problem to be Class A, with limited confidence. Its natural formulation is a configuration LP over type-and-bundle assignments. Its key algorithmic question is whether, for each teacher type and a vector of student-category prices, one can efficiently find a bundle maximizing quantile value minus its price. If that pricing problem is tractable in \(|T|\), \(|G|\), and the binary input length \(L\), the LP may yield an exact algorithm; otherwise, the mirror could be continuum-specifically hard. The follow-up questions are whether pricing is polynomial for all \(\tau_t<1\), and how well an optimal mass allocation can be rounded to a finite high-multiplicity allocation.

The weakest point is substantial: the theorem’s X3C reduction encodes combinatorics in distinct valuation patterns and item identities. It does not establish hardness in a regime with few teacher types. Splitting each type’s mass across bundle assignments may also remove the integrality that makes the reduction work. So I would not claim hardness transfers. The positive case is that the scenario is plausible, the mirror preserves the paper’s valuation and welfare objective, and the resulting pricing question is a concrete computational problem the authors could recognize. The paper’s discussion of quantile preferences over randomized outcomes is not itself this mirror: here the population, rather than the outcome space, is continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed mirror’s unconstrained allocation model. The paper’s thm:NP-USW is a real computational anchor, but its large-population limit can lose an optimum even in the simplest possible type regime.

Take one teacher type of mass \(1\), with quantile \(\tau=0\), and two student categories: category \(a\), valued at \(1\), and category \(b\), valued at \(0\). Let each category have supply \(1\) per teacher. For any integer \(k\geq 1\), assign mass \(1-1/k\) bundles consisting of one \(a\), and mass \(1/k\) bundles consisting of one \(a\) and \(k\) copies of \(b\). This uses all supplies and gives per-teacher welfare \(1-1/k\). But no feasible allocation attains welfare \(1\): positive supply of \(b\) must go to positive mass of teachers, whose minimum-value quantile is then \(0\). Thus the proposed continuum problem has supremum \(1\), but no maximizing allocation.

This is the limit of a finite allocation in which \(N-1\) teachers each receive one \(a\), while one scapegoat receives the remaining \(a\) and all \(N\) copies of \(b\). As \(N\) grows, the scapegoat’s population share vanishes while its bundle grows without bound. The paper’s own \(n/(n-1)\)-approximation for unconstrained utilitarian welfare makes this escape route especially visible. A configuration LP over finite bundles does not capture the limiting scapegoat as an ordinary positive-mass configuration.

A uniform class-size cap would prevent this escape, but it changes the unconstrained problem. It points toward the paper’s balanced-allocation results instead. Those offer a plausible alternative mirror: bounded bundles, type masses, and per-capita supplies give a well-defined configuration problem. Likewise, replacing “maximum” with “supremum” may repair the proposed model, though it leaves open what an optimal allocation means and how to round one.

So the best negative case is that the proponent has not yet specified a well-posed continuum version of the anchor, and the natural unrestricted version has a non-attainment defect. That is a substantial problem for this particular mirror. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: a bounded-bundle version remains plausible, and the proposed supremum problem could still be computationally meaningful.
