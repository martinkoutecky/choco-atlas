The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one: Border and Jordan describe a very natural high-multiplicity population, but the paper contains no qualifying computational anchor.

The closest structural anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It says that for \(N\) voters with separable quadratic preferences over \(\mathbb R^m\), a choice function is straightforward and unanimity-respecting if and only if it decomposes coordinatewise:
\[ C(\langle G^i\rangle) = \bigl(c_1(\langle I_1(G^i)\rangle),\ldots, c_m(\langle I_m(G^i)\rangle)\bigr), \]
where every \(c_j:\mathbb R^N\to\mathbb R\) is itself straightforward and unanimity-respecting. Corollary 7.1, also proved here, extends this to separable star-shaped preferences and identifies the coordinate rules as uncompromising. Proposition 7.3 gives the boundary: on a sufficiently rich domain of nonseparable quadratic preferences, every straightforward unanimity-respecting rule is dictatorial.

These are substantial theorems, but they are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. The paper gives no named theorem asserting membership in P, NP-hardness, parameterized tractability, an approximation guarantee, or even an algorithmic formulation whose complexity is analysed. Statements about Gibbard, Moulin, or related work are cited background, not results proved here. Thus there is no legitimate computational anchor in the sense required by ChoCo.

There is nevertheless a credible substantive mirror. Consider a large population of residents choosing a policy vector \(x\in\mathbb R^m\). A complete type is \(t=(p_t,a_t)\), where \(p_t\in\mathbb Q^m\) is the ideal point and \(a_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\) specifies separable quadratic utility
\[ u_t(x)=-\sum_{j=1}^m a_{tj}(x_j-p_{tj})^2. \]
The population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types, with \(\tau\ll n\). This is plausible for repeated cohorts of residents or employees who share the same policy ideals and issue salience. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\); it is not an outcome-space relaxation.

A precise candidate problem is *Continuous Coalition Manipulation for Coordinatewise Quantile Rules*. The input consists of \(m\), the finite type set \(T\), the distribution \(\mu\), a finite set \(R\) of admissible reported ideal points, rational quantile levels \(q_1,\ldots,q_m\in(0,1)\), and transfer costs \(\gamma(t,r)\). The rule is
\[ C_q(\nu)_j = \inf\left\{z: \sum_{r:r_j\le z}\nu_r\ge q_j\right\}, \]
so it is a coordinatewise order-statistic rule of exactly the kind covered by Theorem 6.1 and Corollary 7.1.

The action is a mass transfer \(y_{t,r}\ge0\), moving mass of true type \(t\) to report \(r\), with
\[ \sum_{r\in R}y_{t,r}\le\mu_t. \]
The post-report distribution is
\[ \nu = \sum_t\left(\mu_t-\sum_r y_{t,r}\right)\delta_{p_t} + \sum_{t,r}y_{t,r}\delta_r. \]
The objective is to minimize
\[ \sum_{t,r}\gamma(t,r)y_{t,r} \]
subject to every type supplying positive manipulated mass weakly preferring \(C_q(\nu)\) to the truthful outcome \(C_q(\mu)\), with at least one strict preference. Equivalently, the solution is a minimum-cost profitable coalition of population mass.

For fixed \(m\), this candidate looks plausibly tractable: the possible output coordinates are finite, one can enumerate candidate outcome vectors, and for each vector the quantile and mass-transfer constraints are linear while the preference constraints are convex quadratic inequalities. With variable \(m\), the coordinate combinations may create a genuine combinatorial problem. So I would expect a Class A result parameterized by \(m\), with open questions about unrestricted \(m\), arbitrary phantom-voter rules, and nonseparable preferences.

The authors would likely recognize this as their subject matter: ideal-point types, order-statistic or phantom-voter mechanisms, unanimity, and manipulation all survive. The necessary modification is that an individual has zero mass in an atomless society and therefore cannot change a continuous aggregate rule. The honest analogue is manipulation by a positive-mass cohort, or by a coalition of mass at most \(\beta\). That is the weakest point: it changes individual strategy-proofness into a coalition notion.

My conclusion would therefore be: a natural continuous-population research question exists, and it could generate new computational results around the paper’s mechanism class. But it is not a compliant positive mirror of this paper under ChoCo’s anchor rule. The paper contributes axiomatic structure, not a computational problem whose discrete complexity can dissolve or transfer under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s gate: Border and Jordan supply no computational anchor. Theorem 6.1, Corollary 7.1, and Proposition 7.3 are axiomatic characterizations and impossibility results. None defines an input-output optimization problem, a decision problem, an algorithm, or a complexity claim. Thus there is no discrete computational problem here whose high-multiplicity relaxation could transfer hardness, dissolve NP-hardness, or expose a pricing problem. Any computational problem attached to the paper must be newly invented.

The deeper obstruction is that the paper’s central predicate does not survive population continuization in a nontrivial form. Straightforwardness asks whether one voter can profit by changing their report while all other reports remain fixed. For a nonatomic society with distribution \(\mu\), changing one individual’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Consequently every distributional choice rule is individually strategy-proof: the deviating voter cannot change the outcome at all. This is not a substantive continuous analogue of the paper’s theorems; it makes their key axiom vacuous.

The strongest rescue is the proponent’s mass-coalition formulation. But that changes the object in several essential ways. The paper has no coalition, transfer cost, admissible report set, target outcome, or minimum-cost objective. Its quantifier

\[ \text{“for every voter and every misreport”} \]

becomes

\[ \text{“choose a positive-mass coalition and optimize its profitable deviation.”} \]

That may be a worthwhile new ChoCo problem, but its computational content comes entirely from the added optimization model. Theorem 6.1 contributes only the choice of a coordinatewise rule; the theorem neither asserts nor suggests a minimum-cost coalition problem. Adding arbitrary \(\gamma(t,r)\), finite report sets, and quantile levels makes this a computational study of a newly designed mechanism, not a continuous mirror of Border and Jordan.

Theorem 6.1 is therefore not rescued by choosing a plausible cohort interpretation. Repeated cohorts with common ideal points and salience parameters are a perfectly legitimate high-multiplicity regime; the problem is not that such types are implausible. The problem is that the finite theorem’s proof derives its structure from unilateral, identity-specific deviations. In the continuum, those deviations have zero measure. If an entire type-block is instead treated as one strategic unit, then the strategic agents are no longer voters but coalitions or types. That is a defensible re-modelling, but it asks for a new block-strategyproofness theory.

Corollary 7.1 does not provide a stronger anchor. Its “phantom voters” are fixed parameters of a generalized-median rule, not population agents. One can certainly represent them as phantom mass and obtain weighted quantile rules. But that is a natural measure-theoretic reformulation of an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. Asking whether a supplied distributional rule has a phantom-mass representation would again be a newly introduced recognition problem, absent from the paper.

Proposition 7.3 fares no better. Its conclusion that sufficiently rich nonseparable quadratic domains force dictatorship is also purely axiomatic. Under a nonatomic interpretation, individual strategyproofness again becomes vacuous. Under a positive-mass-block interpretation, the question becomes whether coalitional incentive compatibility forces dictatorship. That could be mathematically interesting, but it is a different impossibility theorem with different quantifiers and strategic objects, not a continuization of Proposition 7.3.

One could preserve individual influence by giving each voter a positive atom, but then one has retained a discrete electorate, perhaps with repeated weighted types. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity representation, but it does not produce the programme’s characteristic continuum in which individual mass is infinitesimal. Alternatively, one can take a limit of finite generalized-median mechanisms, but the limit preserves outcome formulas while losing the paper’s incentive predicate. The interesting finite-agent content lies precisely at the scale that disappears.

So the honest verdict is not that no related continuous question can be imagined. The cohort-and-quantile problem is sensible, and a paper on mass-strategyproofness or coalition manipulation could be worthwhile. The stronger conclusion is that none of the paper’s actual named results supplies the computational problem that would make such a question a ChoCo mirror. The proposed anchor survives only by replacing the paper’s individual strategyproofness problem with a new optimization problem; under the programme’s rules, that is not enough.
