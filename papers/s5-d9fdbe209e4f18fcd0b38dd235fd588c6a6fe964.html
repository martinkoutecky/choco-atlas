The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational distribution \(\mu\) over advertiser types \(t=(v_t,B_t)\) and per-capita inventory \([0,\kappa]\) with a piecewise rational, strictly decreasing click-through profile \(\theta(q)\), determine whether there is a measurable allocation coupling advertiser mass to inventory, together with value bids, that implements the allocation under the mass-quantile version of BCSP(price). Require each inventory unit to be assigned, each type to supply at most \(\mu_t\) mass, assigned advertisers to afford their prices, and almost every assigned advertiser to weakly prefer their assignment to every affordable placement and to receiving none.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution over value-budget types, matched to proportionally scaled placement mass with click-through profile \(\theta(q)\). The decision variables are a mass coupling between types and placements and a measurable bid schedule; the schedule orders advertiser mass by bids and charges each rank the next-lower bid quantile.

The objection that survived

The paper’s distinct-budget assumption excludes positive-mass repeated types from its existence guarantee, and the proposed mass-quantile mechanism needs a finite representation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s existence and implementation of envy-free assignments under BCSP(price); it leaves the Nash-equilibrium results for the other mechanisms and public budgets aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which finite-support distributions with repeated budget types admit an envy-free assignment implementable by mass-quantile BCSP(price)?
  • Can such an assignment and bid schedule be computed in time polynomial in the support size, profile description, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow: budget-aware GSP stability has a natural population-level analogue, but this paper does not provide a named computational-complexity result of the kind the brief asks for. Its theorems concern whether equilibria or envy-free assignments exist. The closest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: *there exists an envy-free assignment realizable under BCSP(price).* Its proof combines the existence result in Lemma 3 with Lemma 5, which shows that every envy-free assignment can be implemented by bids under that mechanism. The construction is algorithmic in spirit, but the theorem gives no running-time classification.

A plausible mirror would model a large market of advertisers by a distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(v_t,B_t)\), where each type specifies value per click and budget. Normalize advertiser mass to one. Let the available placements have total mass \(\kappa\leq1\), indexed by \(q\in[0,\kappa]\), with a decreasing click-through-rate profile \(\theta(q)\). A solution is a measurable allocation coupling \(\pi\) between advertisers and placements, together with a per-click price \(p(q)\) and bids that implement the allocation under a quantile version of BCSP(price). Every placement must be assigned; each advertiser receives at most one placement; assigned advertisers must afford it; and almost every advertiser must weakly prefer their assigned placement to every affordable placement at its price, as well as to taking no placement. The implementation requirement asks the bids to produce that allocation and price schedule under the mechanism.

One credible regime is a large ad market in which advertiser mass and comparable inventory both grow: for example, many advertisers and placement opportunities across similar keyword, region, or time contexts. Advertisers with the same value and budget share a type; more generally, the population has a distribution of these parameters. The number of advertisers can then be large relative to the number of distinct or parametrically described types. This retains the paper’s budget, ranking, price, and envy concepts, and the authors could plausibly recognize the question as a continuum version of their stability problem.

I would expect an existence analogue to be approachable by extending the paper’s price-decrease construction, and a finitely represented version may admit a tractable search method. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a complexity result established by this paper. For a genuinely atomless input, a complexity classification also needs a finite representation of the type distribution and slot supply. The further questions are whether envy-free outcomes always exist for such distributions, whether BCSP(price) can implement them, and whether finite markets approximate the continuous solutions.

The main weakness is substantial. The paper assumes pairwise distinct budgets, while a finite-support population with repeated advertisers gives many agents the same budget. Preserving distinct budgets pushes the mirror toward an atomless budget distribution, with potentially infinitely many types and no immediate finite input encoding. Also, keeping the paper’s number of slots fixed while the advertiser population grows makes slot mass vanish; the proposed regime must scale inventory too. Those choices can be defensible co-modifications, but they make this a less direct and weaker computational mirror.

So this paper offers a serious positive lead for continuizing stability and envy-free implementation. It has no named NP-hardness, tractability, or parameterized-complexity result to anchor a ChoCo complexity claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor, but it establishes an existence property: an envy-free assignment can be realized under BCSP(price). Lemma 3 gives a finite price-lowering construction, and Lemma 5 turns an already-given envy-free assignment into bids. The paper states no computational problem or running-time result for either construction. A continuum version asking whether an envy-free assignment exists would be an equilibrium-existence question. Asking for an algorithm to compute one would add a search problem that the paper does not study.

There is also a sharper mismatch with ChoCo’s high-multiplicity model. An advertiser’s complete type must include at least their value and budget. Positive mass on a type therefore means repeated budgets. Yet the existence result assumes pairwise distinct budgets; the paper’s Figure 1 shows that envy-free assignments can fail when budgets repeat. A finite-support population with many advertisers per type leaves the theorem’s domain. Keeping budgets distinct makes each budget a separate type, so the population no longer has the finite-support multiplicity the mirror needs. An atomless distribution over budgets could be mathematically coherent, but it requires a new input representation and a new computational question.

The best salvage is to study whether envy-free assignments can be computed in a continuous market with repeated types and budgets. That could be worthwhile research. It would concern a regime where the paper’s existence theorem does not apply, and the paper provides no complexity result to anchor the question. An atomless-budget formulation likewise opens a new continuum equilibrium problem rather than continuizing Theorem 3’s stated result.

This is a strong case against a ChoCo complexity mirror of the proposed anchor, though not proof that continuous auction stability is uninteresting. The weakness is that a new computational question about envy-free assignments could still be valuable; its value would have to come from the new model and results, not from a computational contribution made by this paper.
