The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its propositions establish equilibrium properties and comparative statics; none says a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or gives a computational formulation. So I can’t offer a fully qualified, result-anchored positive case.

The closest result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: *the game has a unique monotone equilibrium, and its pivotal state \(\theta^*\) depends on signal precisions \(\sigma_e\) and \(\sigma_f\) only through their ratio \(\sigma_e/\sigma_f\).* That makes the equilibrium a well-defined computational target, but uniqueness and a scaling property do not establish an algorithm or a complexity bound.

The best follow-on question I see is Finite-Support Opinion-Leader Equilibrium Computation. Fix the paper’s uniform state and signal distributions. An instance gives rational signal precisions, an integer sample size \(n\), and two rational finite-support distributions \(\mu^E,\mu^F\), each summing to one. A type is a role and a payoff cutoff \(p\); the role determines the signal precision, and \(p\) determines when the agent prefers action \(A\). Thus \(\mu^E_t\) and \(\mu^F_t\) are the fractions of experts and followers of each type. The coordination outcome is still determined by the fraction of followers choosing \(A\), while each follower observes \(n\) randomly sampled expert actions.

The task is to return, to additive error \(\varepsilon\), the unique equilibrium pivotal state and the type- and observation-contingent signal cutoffs that support the monotone equilibrium. The population regime is a large product-adoption market or protest movement with many agents but few distinct role-and-bias types; each type’s mass is a rational share of its group. The objective is equilibrium computation, not optimization.

My tentative expectation is Class A: with finite support, uniform noise, and a single pivotal-state condition, an algorithm might evaluate the equilibrium condition and locate its unique root in time polynomial in the number of types, \(n\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\). That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Proposition 1; polynomial-time evaluation of the condition and its bit complexity still need proof. It also raises the finite-to-continuous dictionary question: does the equilibrium of a finite-agent version with repeated types converge to this threshold as population size grows?

The serious weakness is that the paper already makes the population continuous. This proposal adds a computational question to that continuum model; it does not show a discrete hardness result dissolving under continuization. The authors could recognize the equilibrium-computation problem as natural, but under the programme’s strict anchor test it remains an unanchored extension, not a demonstrated continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1, the proponent’s anchor, proves uniqueness of a monotone equilibrium and says the pivotal state depends on signal precision through a ratio. It gives no complexity bound or algorithm for a computational problem. Propositions 2–4 are also equilibrium comparative statics. The proposed equilibrium computation is a new algorithmic question attached to Proposition 1, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed question is coherent, but it does not continuize the population: the paper already models a continuum of experts and a continuum of followers, with heterogeneous payoff cutoffs represented by integrable functions. Finite-support distributions over those cutoffs specialize that existing model. The paper also reduces equilibrium to a scalar pivotal condition and proves its solution is unique. For the proposed finite-support setting, evaluating that condition over types and observed action counts looks like a plausible computational follow-up; the proponent’s polynomial-time expectation may be right. Establishing the bit complexity could still be useful, but it would be computation on an already continuous equilibrium model, without a discrete-to-continuous bridge or a computational theorem from this paper to anchor it.

A stronger proposal might optimize costed discounts to early adopters, using the paper’s discussion of marketing and Proposition 3’s results on expert bias. That could become a worthwhile control problem over a continuous population. But it adds an intervention and cost model absent from the paper. It is a new computational project, not a mirror of a named computational result here.

So the negative case defeats the anchor actually offered, and the obvious intervention variant does not restore a paper-specific mirror. The broader claim that *no* computational question arising from this continuum model could be worthwhile is weaker: the programme expressly seeks computational questions missing from analytic continuum work. I would reject this paper as a computational-result mirror, while leaving open the value of a separately motivated equilibrium or control study.
