The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying anchor under your standard. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithms 1–3 specify procedures; the claims that the token algorithm often improves task completion and uses about an order of magnitude fewer messages are empirical findings from simulations, not results that place a problem in P, prove hardness, or classify it another way. The paper also cites Gaston and desJardins’ earlier work, but that is neither a numbered result in this paper nor a complexity classification.

The strongest plausible population regime is a large robot or sensor fleet with many agents but relatively few skills and repeated deployment roles. A continuous model could assign mass to those agent types and represent their links with a graphon; the decision would be a rewiring policy, and the objective could be long-run task throughput subject to per-agent degree and communication limits. But this would extend the paper toward network-design optimization. Aggregating agents by skill alone would lose information the paper’s algorithms use: individual neighborhoods determine coalition formation and token movement. Whether the authors would recognize this optimization problem as theirs is therefore uncertain.

So there is a possible continuous-population research direction, but it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror anchored in this paper’s named computational results. There is no anchor per which to state a prover-ready question or predict whether hardness transfers, dissolves, or arises specifically in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the missing anchor: this paper states no computational complexity result. Algorithms 1–3 describe procedures, while Figures 1–7 report finite simulations of throughput and message counts. Those findings may motivate further experiments or analysis, but they do not identify a complexity question whose continuous version this paper has answered or left open.

The proposed graphon mirror also has a representation problem at its core. Coalition formation and token movement depend on particular edges, local neighborhoods, and the evolving states of individual agents. A society described only by masses of skill types loses that structure. Putting each agent’s neighborhood into its type preserves it only by making the number of types grow with the network. A dense graphon is a poor fit for the paper’s bounded-degree setting: as the population grows, its normalized edge density vanishes. Sparse limits can preserve local neighborhoods, but then the object to track is a distribution of evolving, marked local graphs, not the finite-support society in ChoCo’s central model.

One could instead pose a continuous network-design optimization problem—for example, choose a rewiring policy to maximize expected task throughput under degree and communication limits. But the paper does not define that optimization problem: it compares decentralized adaptation algorithms, rather than specifying an optimal policy, its input, or a complexity measure. Defining those would create a new problem inspired by the paper, not a mirror of one of its results. A direct limit of the algorithms’ dynamics may be mathematically interesting, but if it asks about limiting dynamics or equilibria, it falls on the analytic side of the boundary ChoCo draws.

The negative case is strongest for the claim that this paper supplies an anchored ChoCo mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim that no useful continuous-population computational study could grow from the paper. A model with a few agent roles and type-conditioned link rules might be a worthwhile extension, and the authors could recognize it as related. But it would need substantial new modeling choices, and it would not rescue the paper as a source of an existing computational result to continuize.
