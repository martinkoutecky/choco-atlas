The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result of the required kind: no theorem or proposition classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or otherwise. Propositions 1–10 give implementation characterizations and applications, not complexity results.

The closest seed for a population mirror is Proposition 11, proved in the paper: if individuals share a long-term goal and their willpower levels satisfy \(\sum_{i\in I} k_i \ge |X|\), then the social choice rule selecting that goal’s top choice is Nash implementable. A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large group making repeated collective decisions, with types recording each member’s short-term craving and willpower. But a continuum changes the strategic argument: an individual has zero mass and cannot make the pivotal deviation used in the proposition. Defining implementation for that population would require additional modeling, and would be an exploratory question rather than a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

So I can’t honestly provide the requested one-question-per-anchor case. The paper’s implementation results may motivate a separate continuous-population project, but they do not supply the required computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–10 characterize which social choice rules are implementable or apply those characterizations to welfare concepts and behavioral models. Proposition 11 establishes existence of an implementing mechanism under a condition on willpower. None poses an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. The paper’s objects are states, choice functions, rules, and equilibrium outcomes—not computational instances with a running time or complexity boundary.

Proposition 11 is the best candidate for a population-based question. Its natural version would describe a society by the distribution of members’ long-term goals, short-term cravings, and willpower, then ask whether the common goal can be implemented. But the theorem’s mechanism depends on a feature that a continuous population removes: a named individual can unilaterally report an arbitrarily high intensity and force the outcome. In an atomless society, one person’s deviation does not change the distribution of reports. An outcome rule based on the pointwise maximum could detect that deviation, but then the rule depends on a measure-zero report that the society’s distribution does not encode. Making a positive-mass group pivotal instead requires a coalition or cohort deviation, changing the equilibrium question.

The theorem’s threshold also counts individuals: \(\sum_i k_i \ge |X|\). Since the paper assumes \(k_i\) is positive, this holds automatically once the finite population has at least \(|X|\) members. A mass-normalized capacity condition could avoid that collapse, but it would be a new resource-allocation model; it would not restore the theorem’s individual incentive argument.

So I would reject Proposition 11 as a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror. There may be a worthwhile project on behavioral implementation in nonatomic societies, but it would need a new equilibrium notion and a new problem statement. That is an adjacent research direction, not a computational result this paper already provides for continuization. My negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s stated remit; it cannot rule out the broader value of that separate project.
