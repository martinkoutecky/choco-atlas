The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 3

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 3 — survived: The type-mass best-response problem preserves the college’s offer decision, and finite support permits sorting types and splitting the boundary mass.
  • Theorem 4 — survived: The population version remains a recognizable calibration problem, with finite-support cutoff changes handled by checking the resulting breakpoints.

The continuous mirror question

Given colleges \(P\), a finite set of applicant types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), a fixed state \(s_i\), fixed rival strategies, type utilities \(u_{i\theta}=v_\theta+e_{i\theta}\), acceptance probabilities \(\pi_{i\theta}=\pi_i(s_i,v_\theta)\), per-capita capacity \(\kappa_i\), and penalty \(\gamma_i>\max_\theta u_{i\theta}\), compute offer fractions \(x_{i\theta}\in[0,1]\) maximizing \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta x_{i\theta}u_{i\theta}\pi_{i\theta}-\gamma_i\max\{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta x_{i\theta}\pi_{i\theta}-\kappa_i,0\}\).

The model it lives in

Each type \(\theta\) records the applicant attributes relevant to the college’s utility and acceptance probability; the applicant population is the distribution \(\mu\). Colleges remain individual decision makers. For a fixed state and rival strategies, set \(y_\theta=\mu_\theta x_{i\theta}\pi_{i\theta}\), so \(0\le y_\theta\le\mu_\theta\pi_{i\theta}\). Since \(\gamma_i>\max_\theta u_{i\theta}\), an optimum takes accepted mass in descending \(u_{i\theta}\) order up to \(\kappa_i\), splitting the boundary type if needed. Sorting gives an exact \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) algorithm once the acceptance values are available.

What the mirror covers

Covers the individual college offer decision in Theorem 3 and the state calibration in Theorem 4. It leaves preference learning and the incentive, stability, and fairness results uncontinuized.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the bit complexity when type utilities, masses, capacities, and acceptance probabilities are given as binary rationals?
  • When \(\pi_i\) and the state distribution \(F_i\) are specified by succinct models, what is the complexity of mean calibration across the finite-support cutoff breakpoints?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large decentralized admissions or labor market where one side is a small set of institutions and the other is a much larger cohort. Take a year of applicants with standardized credentials and a limited set of application and fit profiles. A type \(\theta\) records the public score \(v_\theta\), each college’s fit \(e_{i\theta}\), and the type’s relevant acceptance behavior. The cohort is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types; \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(\theta\). Colleges remain individual decision makers, while their quotas scale with the cohort size: \(\kappa_i\) is college \(i\)’s capacity per unit of population.

This is a plausible regime when \(n\), the number of applicants, is large and the number of complete profiles \(\tau\) is much smaller. The paper’s own elite-college example is less convincing: individualized essays and fit may make nearly every applicant a different type. A standardized admissions system or a labor market with repeated credential and compatibility profiles gives a stronger case.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, for a fixed state \(s_i\), college \(i\)’s strategy of offering to arms above a cutoff \(\hat e_i(s_i,v)\) is near-optimal for the expected payoff in Eq. (6). The cutoff is
\[ \hat e_i(s_i,v)=\min\{\max\{\hat b_i(s_i)-v,0\},1\}, \]
so it selects by latent utility \(v+e_i\), decreases with \(v\), and has slope \(-1\) wherever it is between \(0\) and \(1\). If a threshold \(b_i\) makes the expected number of accepted offers equal the quota, it is used; otherwise the theorem chooses between the adjacent thresholds \(b_i^+\) and \(b_i^-\) by comparing the boundary arms’ expected utility with the penalty for exceeding quota. The expected payoff gap is bounded by
\[ 0\le \max_{B_i\subseteq A_{T+1}} U_i[B_i]-U_i[\hat B_i(s_i)] \le UE^\dagger, \]
where
\[ UE^\dagger = \left[\min_{j\in B_i^-(s_i)}(v_j+e_{ij})\right] \left[q_i-\sum_{j\in B_i^-(s_i)}\pi_i(s_i,v_j)\right]. \]
The theorem also says \(UE^\dagger=0\) with a continuum of arms when \(\pi_i(\cdot,v)\) is continuous in \(v\).

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuum College Best Response. Its instance is a finite set of colleges; a rational type distribution \(\mu\) over applicant profiles \(\theta\); a fixed state \(s_i\); a fixed strategy for the other colleges; an acceptance probability \(\pi_i(s_i,v_\theta)\) for an offer to type \(\theta\); a per-capita quota \(\kappa_i\); and a penalty \(\gamma_i\). College \(i\) chooses \(x_{i\theta}\in[0,1]\), the fraction of type \(\theta\) it offers to. Its per-capita expected payoff is
\[ U_i(x_i;s_i)= \sum_\theta \mu_\theta x_{i\theta}(v_\theta+e_{i\theta})\pi_i(s_i,v_\theta) -\gamma_i\max\left\{ \sum_\theta \mu_\theta x_{i\theta}\pi_i(s_i,v_\theta)-\kappa_i,\ 0 \right\}. \]
A solution is an offer fraction for each type that maximizes this payoff. Each applicant still receives an integral offer decision; \(x_{i\theta}\) records the share of a large, homogeneous group offered admission. The natural solution is a cutoff in \(v_\theta+e_{i\theta}\), with fractional offers at the boundary when needed. With rational finite support and given acceptance values, sorting the \(\tau\) types and scanning the cutoff candidates gives a direct algorithm. This is Class A: mass at the boundary removes the finite-population gap, matching Theorem 3’s continuum-arms clause.

A second useful anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It characterizes the state that maximizes the agent’s average expected payoff. Writing \(S_i\) for the uncertain true state and \(\partial B_i(s_i)=\lim_{\delta\to0^+}(B_i(s_i-\delta)\setminus B_i(s_i))\) for the marginal set, the theorem gives the maximizing \(s_i\) through
\[ \Pr(S_i\ne s_i) \sum_{j\in\partial B_i(s_i)} (v_j+e_{ij})\,\mathbb E[\pi_i(S_i,v_j)\mid S_i\ne s_i] = \gamma_i\Pr(s_i<S_i\le1) \sum_{j\in\partial B_i(s_i)} \mathbb E[\pi_i(S_i,v_j)\mid s_i<S_i\le1]. \]
The paper’s proof says the solution is the unique maximizer. Its continuous mirror, Continuum Mean Calibration, uses the same type distribution and per-capita payoff, along with a distribution \(F_i\) over the uncertain state and a representation of \(\pi_i(s,v)\). It asks for the state \(s_i\) and induced offer fractions \(x_i(s_i)\) maximizing
\[ \mathbb E_{S_i\sim F_i}\!\left[ \sum_\theta \mu_\theta x_{i\theta}(s_i)(v_\theta+e_{i\theta})\pi_i(S_i,v_\theta) -\gamma_i\left( \sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{i\theta}(s_i)\pi_i(S_i,v_\theta)-\kappa_i \right)_+ \right]. \]
For a smooth population of types, the theorem’s marginal sums become integrals over the mass entering the offer set as \(s_i\) changes. This is a one-dimensional calibration problem; its expected direction is tractable when the state distribution and acceptance functions can be evaluated efficiently. With finitely many types and cutoff jumps, the direct objective can be compared at its breakpoints.

These mirrors cover the colleges’ individual offer decisions and the calibration of their uncertain state. They do not claim a continuous complexity result for learning the acceptance functions, or for the market’s full incentive, stability, and fairness properties. The paper’s sentence that exact finite-arm optimization is NP-complete is not a numbered theorem, so it is not an anchor here. The named anchors are algorithmic optimization results, rather than named hardness results.

The weakest point is that the paper already states an exactness result for a continuum of arms in Theorem 3. That makes the lead mirror highly recognizable to the authors, but also means it cannot be presented as a wholly unstudied continuous variant. The additional computational question is whether to classify the high-multiplicity version in terms of \(m,\tau,L\), including its finite-support and per-capita-capacity variants. The other weakness is the regime itself: if complete types must include genuinely idiosyncratic fits and preference behavior, \(\tau\) may grow almost as fast as \(n\). The positive case holds best for markets with many repeated, outcome-relevant profiles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I grant the strongest regime for a mirror: a small number of colleges facing a large cohort with repeated, standardized applicant profiles. The negative case does not need to rely on individualized essays making the number of types too large. It rests on the fact that the two proposed anchors already have almost exactly the structure the continuous version would ask about.

For Theorem 3, the paper explicitly says that its cutoff strategy is optimal when there is a continuum of arms and the acceptance probability is continuous in score: \(UE^\dagger=0\). That is already a population-continuum result. The finite-support version in the proposal is a direct mass formulation of the same optimization. For a type \(\theta\), write \(u_\theta=v_\theta+e_{i\theta}\) and \(z_\theta=\mu_\theta x_{i\theta}\pi_i(s_i,v_\theta)\). Then \(z_\theta\) is accepted mass, and the payoff is
\[ \sum_\theta z_\theta u_\theta-\gamma_i\left(\sum_\theta z_\theta-\kappa_i\right)_+, \qquad 0\le z_\theta\le \mu_\theta\pi_i(s_i,v_\theta). \]
Since \(\gamma_i\) exceeds every \(u_\theta\), there is no gain in exceeding capacity. The optimum takes accepted mass from types in decreasing \(u_\theta\) order until it reaches \(\kappa_i\), splitting the boundary type if needed. Thus the proposed offer fractions are obtained by sorting the \(\tau\) types and filling the quota. This is the paper’s cutoff argument with mass in place of individuals, and it gives an \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) algorithm once the input values are available. The finite-support extension handles atoms that the theorem’s continuum clause does not spell out; fractional offers at the boundary are the natural extension.

Theorem 4 gives little additional computational substance for this mirror. Its calibration condition already balances marginal utility against marginal over-quota penalty. Replacing the marginal-arm sums by mass-weighted sums or integrals leaves the state as a single scalar decision. With finite support, evaluating the condition involves the types entering or leaving the cutoff; the population lift adds weights, not a new combinatorial search over applicant populations. A formal runtime bound would need an encoding and evaluation model for \(F_i\) and \(\pi_i\). Without that, any difficulty lies in representing those functions, not in continuizing the society.

A richer question about the joint market, learning type-level acceptance behaviour, or equilibrium and stability could still be worth studying. It would need its own computational anchor: it changes the target beyond the individual best response and mean calibration raised here. For these two anchors, the case against is a collision with the paper’s own continuum result and a direct finite-support extension, rather than an objection to the plausibility of high-multiplicity admissions markets.

This negative case is strongest for the proposed anchors, not a proof that no worthwhile continuous mirror of the paper exists.
