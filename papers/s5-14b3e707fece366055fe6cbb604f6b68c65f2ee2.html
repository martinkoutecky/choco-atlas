The case FOR (proponent)

Under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: it proves neither a runtime bound nor a hardness classification. Its closest anchors are named universal-feasibility results. They support a positive mirror of the paper’s allocation question, though not a claim that the paper has charted a complexity landscape.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large network of clinics receiving indivisible kits from a few standardized categories. There are \(N\) clinics, but only a moderate number of complete valuation types: clinics of type \(t\) have the same monotone valuation \(v_t\) over kit categories. Category \(j\) has about \(N a_j\) copies, with \(a_j\in[0,1]\), so stock scales with the population. The type masses \(\mu_t\) sum to \(1\). Each clinic still receives an integral bundle; only the fraction of clinics of each type receiving each bundle can be fractional.

Here is a continuous version of the paper’s thinned benchmark. For \(d\) kit categories, let \(X_j\) be independent indicators with \(\Pr[X_j=1]=1-e^{-c a_j}\), and let \(X=\{j:X_j=1\}\). This is the population limit of sampling each physical copy independently with probability \(c/N\), then recording whether at least one copy of each category was sampled. Define
\[ \tau_{q,c}(v_t,\mathbf a) =\min\{r\in v_t(2^{[d]}):\Pr[v_t(X)\le r]\ge q\}. \]
An aggregate allocation is a set of masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq[d]\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}=a_j, \]
and \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(v_t(S)<\tau_{q,c}(v_t,\mathbf a)\). These are finite LP constraints once the type valuations and bundle benchmarks are listed.

The lead mirror is Continuous Thinned Quantile Feasibility: does there exist a universal \(c>0\) such that every finite type set, mass distribution, per-capita supply vector, and monotone type valuation profile admits such an allocation at \(q=e^{-c}\)? Its anchor is Theorem 1.5, proved in this paper: there is a universal constant \(c>0\) for which the \(c\)-thinned \(e^{-c}\)-quantile share is universally feasible for every finite profile of monotone valuations. In that finite problem, each of \(n\) agents gets an integral bundle from a partition of the goods, and the benchmark samples each good independently with probability \(c/n\).

I expect the mirror’s answer to be positive, and the explicit aggregate formulation gives a plausible tractability route. At finite scale, apply Theorem 1.5 to repeated copies of the types and co-scaled category supplies, then take a convergent subsequence of the type-bundle allocation masses. The binomial category-presence probabilities converge to \(1-e^{-c a_j}\). Since the number of types and bundles is fixed in this regime, the aggregate variables lie in a bounded finite-dimensional space. This is an existence and LP-formulation argument; it is not a runtime theorem from the paper.

A second, complementary question is Continuous Thinned Quantile Sharpness: for fixed \(c\) and \(q>e^{-c}\), can one always produce a continuous instance where no fair aggregate allocation exists? Proposition 1.8, proved here, says that in the finite model the answer is yes: for every \(c\in(0,1]\) and \(q>e^{-c}\), some instance fails universal feasibility. Its example has \(n-1\) goods, \(n\) identical agents, and value \(1\) exactly for a nonempty bundle; every agent’s benchmark is \(1\), but there are too few goods.

The continuous witness is just as direct. Take one type, one category, supply \(a<1\), and \(v(S)=1\) exactly when \(S\) contains that category. Choose \(a\in(-\ln(q)/c,1)\). Then the benchmark is \(1\), since its probability of value \(0\) is \(e^{-ca}<q\), while supply \(a<1\) cannot give the category to the entire population. The obstruction transfers from the finite scarcity example; constructing it is immediate, not a hardness result.

This case covers the thinned-share feasibility theorem and its upper boundary. It does not mirror the RMMS comparison or the conditional unthinned result of Theorem 1.7. Its weakest point is also its limit: the clinic-type model and co-scaled supplies are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but the finite theorem is already an existence result for every population size. The continuous feasibility claim is therefore close to a limit consequence of the paper, not a new complexity classification. The authors could recognize the allocation question as theirs; a computational-complexity claim would need a separate algorithmic problem and a specified valuation representation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchors are existence results, not computational results. Theorem 1.5 proves that every finite profile admits an allocation meeting the thinned quantile shares; Proposition 1.8 gives a profile where universal feasibility fails above a threshold. Neither studies the complexity of finding an allocation, or defines an algorithmic input model. Turning either into such a question would add a computational problem that the paper does not anchor.

The clinic model is nevertheless a legitimate population mirror. In its strongest natural form, use finitely many complete valuation types, integral bundles of kit categories, and per-capita supply constraints \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le a_j\). Take \(N\) clinics, \(\lceil Na_j\rceil\) copies of category \(j\), and sample each physical kit with probability \(c/N\). Category \(j\) then appears in the benchmark with probability \(1-(1-c/N)^{\lceil Na_j\rceil}\), which converges to \(1-e^{-ca_j}\) from above. For monotone valuations, the finite benchmarks are at least the limiting ones. Theorem 1.5 supplies finite fair allocations; compactness of the finitely many type-and-bundle masses gives a fair aggregate allocation in the limit. That is a sound discrete-to-continuous bridge, and the programme’s stated scope allows such bridges to matter.

But the bridge establishes existence, not a new complexity landscape. Once share eligibility is specified, aggregate feasibility is a finite LP with one variable per type and bundle. With explicit valuation tables, its size is polynomial in the table size; a harder algorithmic question would need a chosen succinct or oracle representation, which the paper does not provide. So the proponent’s own account—that this is not a runtime theorem—marks the gap with ChoCo’s computational aim.

Proposition 1.8 also has a valid continuous counterpart: use one type, one category, and supply \(a\in(-\ln(q)/c,1)\), possible when \(q>e^{-c}\). The benchmark is \(1\) because \(e^{-ca}<q\), while supply \(a<1\) cannot satisfy the entire population. This is a faithful mirror, but its obstruction is just a capacity inequality. It adds no computational question.

So the negative case is strong only under ChoCo’s stated computational scope: neither anchor is a complexity result, and the natural aggregate feasibility formulation does not itself expose a nontrivial algorithmic problem. It is not an honest argument that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any broader sense. The existence bridge for Theorem 1.5 is mathematically real and could be valuable as a formal dictionary result; dismissing it merely because the finite theorem already implies it would contradict the programme’s own treatment of discrete-to-continuous identities.
