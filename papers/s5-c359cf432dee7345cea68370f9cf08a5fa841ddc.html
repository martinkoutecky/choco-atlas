What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are asymptotic equilibrium results, and the remaining named results support the analysis rather than state computational claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that a double auction is naturally described at population scale. A type could include a trader’s side, valuation schedule, capacity, and utility parameters; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of traders of type \(t\). Type contingent bid strategies generate aggregate demand and supply curves, and the outcome is the clearing price and allocation. A plausible regime is a large market of small buyers and sellers trading standardized goods, with values dispersed across a common range. The paper’s Walrasian comparison gives a natural welfare benchmark.

The closest named results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says that, for single unit demands and supplies, under no asymptotic gaps, no asymptotic atoms, and \(z\)-independence, every nontrivial equilibrium becomes asymptotically efficient. Uniformly over such equilibria, efficiency losses vanish faster than \(1/n^{1-\alpha}\) for each \(\alpha>0\), and the fraction of expected surplus lost relative to a Walrasian market shrinks as \(1/n^{2-\alpha}\). Theorem 2 extends this result to multiple unit demands and supplies, assuming no asymptotic gaps for first unit values and no asymptotic atoms across values. These results make the continuous market a credible description of the limit the authors study.

But neither theorem is a computational result. They establish asymptotic properties of equilibria; they give no complexity classification or algorithm for a computational problem. The continuum model also puts pressure on the mirror: if each trader has zero mass, an individual cannot affect the price, so a price taking equilibrium may erase the strategic price impact central to the paper. And because exact values are part of a trader’s type, the paper’s dispersed value assumptions do not obviously yield a regime with few repeated types.

So the positive case is for a continuous population as a natural *limit object*. Under the requested anchor rule, I cannot turn that into a valid anchor based computational mirror: the paper contains no named result asserting that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is decisive under the anchor rule: Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are not computational results. They bound the asymptotic welfare loss of nontrivial equilibria; neither states a complexity classification or gives an algorithmic problem. The proponent describes natural limit objects, then concedes they cannot anchor a computational mirror.

The best computational question one could add to Theorem 1 would be to compute or decide a nontrivial equilibrium, or its welfare gap, from a high-multiplicity market description. But the theorem’s assumptions resist that description. With any fixed finite type space containing interior values, a type with positive mass puts \(\Theta(n)\) traders in an interval of length \(1/n\), contrary to no asymptotic atoms, which bounds that expected count by a constant. No asymptotic gaps also requires values throughout the interior. In the paper’s grid example, values therefore give \(\Theta(n)\) distinct types. Exact trader types do not repeat at growing multiplicity, so the usual compression to a moderate support \(\tau\) is absent.

Theorem 2 adds multiple-unit demand and supply, which can sensibly be included in a trader’s type. It does not fix the mismatch: it inherits the same asymptotic equilibrium question and likewise states no computational result. Sorting marginal values to compute a surplus-maximizing allocation would be a legitimate algorithmic task, but it would compute the Walrasian benchmark, not the strategic equilibrium property these theorems establish.

There is a further obstacle to the best rescue. The paper’s \(z\)-independence condition concerns the joint distribution of traders’ values. A mass distribution over individual types does not encode that dependence. Adding a full joint prior could make equilibrium computation well-posed, but it abandons the compact society description that motivates ChoCo. And in a nonatomic market each individual has zero mass, so the price-impact incentives at the heart of the finite-market proof disappear. Giving a positive mass of a type the power to deviate together would change the strategic unit.

I cannot rule out every worthwhile algorithmic project on large auctions: one could formulate a new equilibrium-computation problem with richer input data. The negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper’s proposed mirrors. Neither named theorem supplies a computational anchor, and the most natural continuizations either discard the theorem’s strategic mechanism or lose the high-multiplicity compression.
