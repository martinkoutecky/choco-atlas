The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a defensible positive computational mirror anchored in this paper: it contains no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational class. It is a survey of Bayesian mechanism implementation, and its theorems characterize when mechanisms exist or what conditions implementation requires.

The nearest formal results are Theorem 1, proved here, which says that under Assumptions 1–7 an allocation rule is Bayesian implementable iff it is incentive compatible; Theorems 4 and 5, which give related characterizations for pure-exchange and economic environments, drawing on earlier work; and Theorem 6, whose cited result says that an interim-efficient, incentive-compatible allocation is essentially implementable by an essentially cheap-talk-proof mechanism under its stated assumptions. None analyzes the computational complexity of deciding or constructing those mechanisms.

Nor does the paper supply a population-continuum anchor. Its common prior \(q\) is uncertainty over profiles of types, not mass of society holding each type. Example B has a continuous interval of possible valuations, but only a buyer and a seller; continuous private information is not a high-multiplicity population. Turning the finite strategic players in these theorems into a continuum of agents would change the equilibrium problem—including the role of the uninformed player in Theorem 1.

So there is no qualifying anchor, no per-anchor continuous computational problem to hand to a prover, and no tractability or hardness direction I can honestly assign. The paper is useful background on implementation theory, but claiming it establishes a continuous-population complexity result would overstate what it does.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorems 4 and 5 characterize implementability; Theorem 6 characterizes a form of essential implementation. None states a complexity bound for deciding implementability or constructing a mechanism. Turning one into a problem about verifying conditions or finding mechanisms would be a new algorithmic question, not a computational continuation of a result in this paper.

Theorem 1 is the closest candidate, but its prior \(q\) is over profiles in \(T_1\times\cdots\times T_N\), not a population distribution \(\mu\) over agent types. Its allocation and incentive constraints are indexed by named players, and its construction depends on an uninformed player who can respond to the reports of the informed players. Replacing those players with masses requires deciding how profile correlations, individual deviations, and the uninformed player’s role work in an atomless society. That could define a new model, but the theorem supplies no computational question about it.

The same issue applies to Theorems 4 and 5. Their pure-exchange allocations specify what each individual receives, and Bayesian monotonicity tests player-specific interim preferences under \(q\). A high-multiplicity version would need an aggregate allocation and an appropriate mass-level incentive condition. Those may be worth studying, but they are additional modelling choices, not mirrors of computational claims in these theorems.

Example B does not provide a population anchor either: its valuation space is continuous, but the auction has one buyer and one seller. A market with continuous masses of buyers and sellers could be a worthwhile computational subject, yet it would be a new large-market mechanism problem. Likewise, Theorem 6’s “essential” criterion compares interim utilities for each player and type; an aggregate criterion would change what counts as implementation.

So none of the proposed anchors survives as a continuous computational mirror of this paper. The universal claim is weaker at its outer edge: the paper could inspire a new computational theory of atomless mechanism implementation. But that possibility does not give this survey a computational result, or a continuous-population question already anchored in one of its results.
