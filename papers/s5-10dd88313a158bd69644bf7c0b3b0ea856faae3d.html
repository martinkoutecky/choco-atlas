The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), candidate preference orders, a tie-breaking priority, a default winner, and a distribution \(\mu\) over the supported complete voter rankings with binary rational masses summing to \(1\), decide whether there is an active set \(A \subseteq C\) that is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium under the paper’s lazy-candidacy preferences and whose plurality winner is a designated candidate \(c^\star\).

The model it lives in

Voter types are complete rankings \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\); each candidate remains an individual player choosing whether to run. For an active set \(A\), each candidate’s plurality score is the total mass ranking them first among \(A\). The decision object is the binary participation vector, and the question is whether an equilibrium vector has winner \(c^\star\); there is no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s NP-completeness results for Lazy∃NE and Lazy∃wNE. It leaves the paper’s equilibrium characterizations, parameterized results, and withdrawal-only dynamics aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of the mass version in the support size \(\tau\), including whether it admits an algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\) alone?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the winner-specific existence problem. Its regime is a large electorate with recurring complete ballots: for example, a large association or union election where many voters share each ranking over a growing slate of candidates. A voter type is a complete ranking \(t\) of the candidates, and its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. The instance lists the \(\tau\) supported rankings and their binary rational masses, summing to \(1\). This is plausible when the electorate is much larger than the number of distinct ballots. The candidate slate remains finite: its members still make the paper’s individual run-or-abstain decisions.

For an active set \(A\), define candidate \(c\)’s plurality score as the total mass of voters who rank \(c\) first among the candidates in \(A\). Resolve ties by the paper’s fixed priority order, and use its designated default winner when \(A=\varnothing\). Keep the candidates’ preference orders and the paper’s laziness rule exactly: a candidate prefers an outcome they rank higher, and, if the outcome is unchanged, prefers abstaining to running.

This gives two precise questions. Mass-Lazy∃wNE, my lead, takes this instance and a designated candidate \(c^\star\); it asks whether some active set \(A\) is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium whose winner is \(c^\star\). A solution is such an \(A\). Mass-Lazy∃NE omits \(c^\star\) and asks whether any pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists; a solution is an equilibrium active set. In either problem, equilibrium means that no single candidate can improve by changing their run-or-abstain action.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that both Lazy∃NE and Lazy∃wNE are NP-complete, even when every candidate has self-supporting preferences—that is, ranks herself above every other candidate. The paper proves the result by reduction from a restricted Exact 3-Cover problem, citing Gonzalez (1984) for that source problem. This is a useful anchor because the hardness is already present without reluctant leadership: it concerns lazy participation itself.

The mirror preserves the election and the strategic question. Given a discrete voter profile with \(n_t\) voters of ranking \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), where \(n=\sum_t n_t\). Every plurality score is then divided by the same positive factor \(n\), so winners and ties are unchanged. Candidate preferences, deviations, and the laziness rule are unchanged too. Thus the discrete and mass versions have exactly the same equilibrium active sets. Conversely, equilibrium verification in the mass version takes polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational bit length \(L\): guess \(A\), calculate the winner, and check each candidate’s one possible deviation. The natural expectation is therefore NP-completeness by transferred hardness (Class B), not tractability created by continuization. The reduction’s combinatorics remain in the candidate slate and the interactions among ballot types.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: voter counts become proportions, while plurality, candidacy, candidate preferences, and lazy abstention retain their meanings. This is a direct high-multiplicity formulation of their game, not an outcome-space or probability continuization. It also poses the question about the underlying game, not an encoding.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s two equilibrium-existence results, and no broader claim is needed. It generates follow-up questions about algorithms parameterized by support size \(\tau\), and about mass versions of the paper’s withdrawal-only dynamics and their reachable winners.

The weak point is that this is a faithful but modest continuization: it changes how the electorate is represented, and the hardness survives. Moreover, the paper shows the discrete problems are fixed-parameter tractable in the number of candidates \(m\), so the hardness requires \(m\) to grow; a fixed, small slate may be easy. That limits the regime where the hardness is practically salient, but it does not make the mirror less faithful to the authors’ question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror adds little new structure to this particular game. For a fixed active set, Plurality is just a sum over ballot types, and each candidate still has only the same run-or-abstain decision. The mirror of Theorem 3 would therefore be a high-multiplicity encoding of the equilibrium problem, rather than a new continuous strategic model.

But that objection does not defeat the anchor. For every finite voter profile, replacing each count \(n_t\) by mass \(n_t/n\) preserves every score comparison, winner, deviation, and equilibrium. Conversely, any rational mass vector can be scaled by a common denominator to recover an integer profile. The input encodings differ—scaling a binary rational vector into explicit voters can be exponentially large—so this is not a claim that the computational problems are identical. It is exactly a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. Large electorates with recurring ballot types are a plausible regime, and the voters’ full rankings capture everything the model uses about them. The candidates remain individual strategic players, as the mirror requires.

The same response applies to the proposed W-dynamics follow-up and Theorem 7: with the electorate represented by ranking masses, every intermediate Plurality score and withdrawal incentive is preserved. The finite candidate sequence does not make the voter population finite in the mirror.

So the honest negative case is about likely incremental yield: the mirror may mainly re-express known hardness, and its most immediate analysis may be a transfer rather than a new optimization technique. Under ChoCo’s stated criteria, that is not enough. A transferable hardness result is a legitimate boundary result, and a high-multiplicity formulation is not a novelty collision. I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists here; Theorem 3, at minimum, survives the attempted objections.
