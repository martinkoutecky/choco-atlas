The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies a strict school preference order and an ordered priority class at each school, run mass-proposing DA, splitting a type’s mass when a cutoff passes through it. For each baseline cohort \((t,r)\), find rational assignment masses \(x_{t,r,s}\) that conserve the cohort, respect capacities, and assign no mass below its DA school. Require a positive improvement whenever DA is inefficient; require priority violations against type mass only where that mass is unimprovable or benefits in the assignment; and require that any justifiable DA improvement Pareto-dominating the output strictly expands its beneficiary mass, allowing relabeling of interchangeable agents within a cohort. Can this be done in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A school-choice population with finitely many complete preference and priority-class types, rational mass, and per-capita school capacities. The decision variables \(x_{t,r,s}\) transfer mass from each type’s DA-assigned school \(r\) to a final school \(s\); the task is to find an assignment satisfying Theorem 2-style improvement, justifiability, and constrained-efficiency guarantees, with no welfare objective beyond those guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a focused one: the paper’s algorithmic result for sequentially expanding justifiable improvements. It is a population mirror of the school assignment problem itself; the result about ex ante consent is a separate mechanism design impossibility, not the computational anchor I would choose.

The best regime is a large citywide school assignment run with many applicants but relatively few recurring combinations of school preferences and priority relevant characteristics. A type \(t\) records a complete preference ranking and the characteristics that determine its priority at every school. For example, many applicants may share a preference list, residence zone, sibling status, and feeder school. The population has \(N\) applicants but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(\tau\ll N\). School capacities are represented as seats per capita. A school’s priority order ranks the types; where a capacity cutoff passes through a type, the continuum model admits the required fraction of its mass.

This is the key co-modification: the population becomes continuous, so a type’s mass can be split among schools while each person still receives one whole school place. The split represents different subgroups of otherwise identical applicants, not fractional school assignments or a lottery over outcomes. The regime is most convincing when preferences and priority characteristics really do recur at scale.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that when DA is inefficient, every SJBC+ outcome (1) Pareto-dominates DA; (2) is justifiable; (3) cannot be Pareto-dominated by another justifiable matching unless that matching strictly expands the beneficiary set; and (4) includes all beneficiaries of JBC. It also proves that an SJBC+ outcome can be computed in time polynomial in \(|I|\) and \(|S|\). This is a named polynomial-time result, though the paper gives no named hardness result of comparable kind.

I would ask for a polynomial-time solution to the following continuous problem, which I would call Mass-SJBC+. The input is a set of \(m\) schools and \(\tau\) types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), a strict preference order \(\succ_t\) for each type, and a priority order \(\triangleright_s\) over types for each school. Run student-proposing DA on the mass: rejected mass applies to its next school, and each school keeps the highest-priority mass up to capacity, splitting a type at a cutoff if needed. This gives the baseline assignment \(d\).

A candidate output is a rational mass table \(x_{t,r,s}\), where \(x_{t,r,s}\) is mass of type \(t\) that had DA assignment \(r\) and ends at \(s\). It must conserve each baseline cohort, respect school capacities, and send no mass to a school worse than its DA assignment:
\[ \sum_s x_{t,r,s}=d_{t,r},\qquad \sum_{t,r}x_{t,r,s}\le \kappa_s,\qquad x_{t,r,s}=0\ \text{if }s\prec_t r. \]
Some positive mass must strictly improve.

For the SJBC+ guarantees, the output should also include a strongly justifiable seed \(J\), inclusion-maximal among strongly justifiable DA improvements, and a final assignment \(x\) whose beneficiaries include those of \(J\). Here \(U\) is the mass that cannot benefit in any DA improvement, and \(B(x)\) is the mass assigned strictly better than under DA. A priority violation against a mass of type \(u\) occurs if \(u\) prefers a school \(s\) to its final assignment and positive mass of a lower-priority type occupies \(s\). The seed \(J\) may have such violations only against \(U\); the final assignment may have them only against \(U\cup B(x)\). Finally, if another justifiable assignment Pareto-dominates \(x\), it must strictly expand \(B(x)\), up to null sets and identification of interchangeable agents across assignments. This last condition is the continuous version of Theorem 2’s constrained-efficiency guarantee.

I expect this problem to be tractable. Once the population is represented by type masses, its assignments and trades can be expressed with a number of rational variables polynomial in \(m\) and \(\tau\). Capacitated matching or flow methods seem like plausible tools for the mass version of the paper’s augmenting-path steps. The difficult part is preserving the endogenous justifiability condition while aggregating people; a flow formulation alone would not settle that. The target complexity should be polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. This would be meaningful beyond Theorem 2’s polynomial time in the explicit population size: replicating a rational mass vector into individuals can take time polynomial in a denominator that is exponential in \(L\).

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains DA, Pareto improvements, school capacities, priority violations, beneficiaries, and the paper’s justifiability test. Theorem 1’s structure of strongly justifiable trades supports the seed idea. The mirror covers the SJBC+ algorithmic result, not the consent results, simulations, or every normative claim in the paper.

The weak point is that strict, individually tie-broken priorities can make almost every applicant a distinct type, so \(\tau\) may approach \(N\). The mirror is strongest in regimes where priority-relevant categories recur and within-category cutoff splitting is an acceptable fluid treatment. If the authors regard an individual lottery number as essential to the problem, this type model may lose the low-support regime that makes continuization useful. A further question is whether continuous assignments can be rounded to finite cohorts while retaining justifiability and the beneficiary guarantees, or only approximating them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only computational anchor in the positive case. The strongest objection is that its justifiability test depends on the paper’s strict, individual priority orders. If a type is a complete description of an applicant, it must include their position in each school’s priority order. With individual tie-breaks, applicants who share preferences and priority categories still have different types; the support can therefore grow with the population. The proposed compression works only by leaving that tie-break information out.

The proposed repair—order priority classes and split mass at cutoffs—gives a coherent fluid model, but it changes the priority structure. Applicants within a class are now tied. That matters here: the paper distinguishes its results under strict priorities from improvement cycles arising under weak priorities, and frames JBC as addressing the strict-priority case. Theorem 1’s strongly justifiable seed and Theorem 2’s beneficiary-based restrictions both depend on who outranks whom. The class model may be a worthwhile extension, but it does not directly mirror those results under the paper’s stated assumptions.

Adding a lottery rank to each type would preserve strict priorities, but then an exact finite-support representation generally loses the compression: distinct ranks produce distinct types. Representing ranks by a continuous distribution could restore a continuum, but it requires a different input and complexity model from the paper’s finite list of rational type masses. There is also a repair needed in the proposed mass formulation: a type can be split across final assignments, so violations and beneficiary status must be defined for submasses, not for a type with one “final assignment.” Expanding the state to include baseline and final schools can address that ambiguity, but it does not solve the priority-support problem.

This is the strongest case against the mirror: exact strict priorities and a small set of complete types are difficult to have at once. It does not, however, establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large district with recurring, legally meaningful priority classes and no individual tie-break may be a plausible regime, and its mass version could retain the paper’s core justifiability question. The objection is strongest if the authors regard strict individual tie-breaks as essential; without that premise, the negative case is weak, and Theorem 2’s anchor may survive as a recognized extension to cohort priorities.
