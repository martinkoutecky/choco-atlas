Theorem 6.1

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type support \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu\), and pair-signal laws \(P_{tu}\) on common signal spaces, each task pairs a focal type \(t\) with a peer drawn independently from \(\mu\), producing law \(P_{t,\mu}=\sum_u\mu_uP_{tu}\). Given \(\Phi\) and admissible score classes \(\mathcal K_t\), construct report-based learners and bound the task samples needed for the resulting pairing mechanisms to make truth-telling a \((\delta,\epsilon)\)-Bayesian equilibrium for every cohort \(t\); determine how the bounds depend on \(\tau\), the mass encoding length, and the score-class complexity.

The model it lives in

A population with type masses \(\mu\), where a focal agent of type \(t\) receives repeated i.i.d. peer-signal pairs from \(P_{t,\mu}\). The mechanism learns a type-specific score from those reports and uses it in the paper's \(\Phi\)-pairing payment rule.

The objection that survived

The type masses enter only through the aggregate pair law \(P_{t,\mu}\), so the existing theorem may apply without recovering or computing over those masses; its sample bound counts tasks, not population size.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.1's learned pairing-mechanism guarantee and Theorem 7.4's score-learning sample bound when priors arise from type-mass mixtures. It does not extend the paper's known-prior result, nonexistence result, or other multi-agent proposals, and it supplies no general running-time bound.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the score-class complexity and task-sample bound be controlled uniformly over the mass-weighted laws \(P_{t,\mu}\), including cohorts with small positive mass?
  • For explicitly represented score classes, can the empirical risk maximization be implemented with a running-time bound in \(\tau\), the mass bit length, and the class parameters?

The case FOR (proponent)

A modest positive case is available: the paper has named results on sample complexity and mechanism construction, though no theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its strongest mirror is therefore about learning peer-prediction scores for a large population, not about transferring a hardness result. The paper’s continuous signal spaces do not by themselves supply the mirror; the population must be the continuous object.

A plausible regime is a large panel of recurring-task reviewers. A type is a reviewer’s complete task-relevant profile: their signal-generation channel, report alphabet, and stable information parameters. Reviewers of one type are indistinguishable for the mechanism. If \(N_t\) of \(N\) reviewers have type \(t\), their population mass is \(\mu_t=N_t/N\); the regime has \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of types with positive mass. The platform samples a reviewer from a focal cohort and, on each task, matches them to a peer drawn from the population without revealing the peer’s type. Write \(P_{tu}\) for the task-level joint signal law when the focal reviewer has type \(t\) and the peer has type \(u\). The focal reviewer’s pair of signals then has law
\[ P_{t,\mu}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal T}\mu_u P_{tu}. \]
Fresh random matching across independent tasks makes these pair observations i.i.d. This is a population model: \(\mu\) determines the mixture of peer types, while each worker still receives an integral signal and submits an ordinary report.

Theorem 6.1 is the lead mechanism anchor, proved in this paper. It says that if a learning algorithm \(L\) produces a \((\delta,\epsilon)\)-accurate scoring function using \(S_L(\delta,\epsilon)\) samples for a family of pair-signal priors, then the paper’s learning-based \(\Phi\)-pairing mechanism uses \(2+S_L(\delta,\epsilon)\) tasks and is \((\delta,\epsilon)\)-truthful. Under stochastic relevance it is \((\delta,\epsilon)\)-informed-truthful; with finite signal spaces and the stated strict-convexity assumptions, it is \((\delta,\epsilon)\)-strongly truthful. These guarantees are proved here. In the paper’s definition, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), truth-telling’s expected payment is within \(\epsilon\) of the relevant truthful reference mapping, and every strategy’s expected payment is bounded above by that mapping.

The continuous mirror I would hand a prover is Mass-Indexed Peer-Prediction Design. Its instance consists of a finite type support \(\mathcal T\), population masses \(\mu\), a matching rule, and for each focal cohort \(t\), an unknown i.i.d. task-pair law \(P_{t,\mu}\). It also specifies \(\Phi\) and a learner that estimates an ideal scoring function from task-pair samples. The question is to construct, for each focal cohort, a detail-free payment mechanism whose reports-based learning and scoring procedure has the truthfulness guarantee of Theorem 6.1 for every admitted \(P_{t,\mu}\). A solution is the learned score together with the bonus-and-penalty payment rule, using the stated number of tasks. For finite signal spaces, stochastic relevance and the theorem’s convexity conditions give the strongest version, approximate strong truthfulness. The type cohorts are known platform groups, but the signal prior is not supplied to the mechanism.

The learning premise has a concrete anchor in Theorem 7.4, also proved here. For a distribution \(P\) over signal pairs, the theorem assumes the function class \(\mathcal K\) contains the \((P,\Phi)\)-ideal score and that the relevant generalized entropy-with-bracketing and variance bounds for \(\mathcal K\) and \(\Phi^*(\mathcal K)\) are finite. Then the empirical risk maximizer from Equation (13) is \(\epsilon\)-accurate with probability at least \(1-\delta\) after \(M=O(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) samples, with the hidden constants depending on those bounds. This is a sample-complexity theorem, not a worst-case running-time theorem.

Its population mirror is Mass-Indexed Ideal-Scoring Learning. Given the type masses and matching process that generate each \(P_{t,\mu}\), the task-sample oracle, \(\Phi\), and suitable classes \(\mathcal K_t\), output scores \(\widehat K_t\) satisfying, with probability at least \(1-\delta\),
\[ \mathbb E_{P_{t,\mu}}[\widehat K_t(X,Y)] -\mathbb E_{P_{t,\mu,X}\otimes P_{t,\mu,Y}}[\Phi^*(\widehat K_t(X,Y))] \geq D_\Phi(P_{t,\mu}\Vert P_{t,\mu,X}\otimes P_{t,\mu,Y})-\epsilon. \]
The expected direction is sample-tractable under Theorem 7.4’s assumptions. Applying it separately to each of \(\tau\) cohorts gives a total sample bound by a union bound when the complexity constants are uniform; that is an inference from the theorem, not a result stated in the paper. If the function classes have an explicit, efficiently optimized finite-dimensional representation, the mirror looks tractable in the usual algorithmic sense too. The paper does not establish that running-time claim in general.

These are credible mirrors because the paper already studies paired signals, heterogeneous agents, detail-free learning of scoring functions, and mechanisms for more than two agents. It is natural for its authors to ask how the score-learning and payment guarantees behave when the peers are drawn from large, repeated-task cohorts with fixed type proportions. This covers the paper’s scoring-function learning and pairing-mechanism results; it does not claim to continuize outcomes, or to derive a complexity result for every result in the paper.

The weakest point is that the paper counts tasks, not workers: Theorem 7.4’s \(M\) is a number of i.i.d. task samples, and its prior \(P\) is already a distribution over signal pairs. Replacing \(P\) by a type-mixture \(P_{t,\mu}\) is a meaningful population interpretation, but it is not a new theorem about computational hardness in \(\tau\) or population size. The mechanism also needs stable, identifiable cohorts and an i.i.d. matching process; arbitrary hidden heterogeneity could invalidate the common-prior model. Finally, the continuous-signal case does not automatically inherit strong truthfulness, since the paper’s strong-truthfulness condition requires finite signal spaces. The case survives as a focused population mirror of the paper’s learning and mechanism-design questions, with a clear statistical result and a narrower, conditional claim about running time.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors are real results, so I would not argue that the paper lacks named theorems. The difficulty is that Theorems 6.1 and 7.4 concern learning from repeated task samples under a pair-signal prior. They do not make population size or type masses part of the computational problem.

In the proposed reviewer model, the masses enter only through \(P_{t,\mu}=\sum_u\mu_uP_{tu}\). Once that pair distribution is specified, Theorem 6.1 already applies to it: the theorem is stated for a family of priors, and its learner must be accurate across that family. Theorem 7.4 supplies a sample bound when the function class and its complexity conditions are suitable. Running the construction separately for each focal type, with a union bound, is a reasonable extension—but it is repetition over priors, not a new problem in population mass. If the component laws \(P_{tu}\) are known, forming the mixture adds no learning challenge. If they are unknown, observations identify the aggregate \(P_{t,\mu}\), not its decomposition into masses and component laws; the score learner can work directly with the aggregate. In neither case does the proposed mirror need to recover or compute over \(\mu\).

The population size \(N\) also does not supply the samples counted by these theorems. A reviewer still needs the mechanism’s repeated task observations. Increasing the number of reviewers may make random peer matching easier to approximate, but it does not reduce the number of tasks required by Theorem 6.1 or 7.4. Pooling different reviewers’ reports as samples would require a new incentive argument: the paper’s guarantees concern an individual’s reporting strategy and payment. A mass-weighted average payment cannot replace that guarantee without potentially ignoring a low-mass cohort’s incentive to misreport.

A stronger proposal might choose the peer-matching mixture to maximize the minimum information or minimize the worst cohort’s sample requirement. That could be an interesting continuous optimization question, but it changes the task: the population composition becomes a control variable, and the model needs component laws or other structure to evaluate the objective. The paper’s results do not pose or analyze that matching-design problem. It is adjacent mechanism design, not a population mirror of either cited theorem.

There is a further mismatch with ChoCo’s computational focus. Theorem 6.1 transfers a statistical learning guarantee into a mechanism guarantee; Theorem 7.4 bounds the number of samples. Neither gives a general running-time bound for the learner: its function class may be abstract, and the theorem’s complexity conditions are statistical. Thus the analyst’s caveat about running time is central. Rewriting these sample bounds using a mass-indexed family may be useful for applications, but it does not chart a computational landscape in \(\tau\), \(L\), or population size.

This case should not rest on the paper’s continuous signals—that is not population continuization, and the paper is not prior art for that by itself. Nor is there a convincing claim that reviewer populations can never have repeated types. The narrower negative is stronger: in the proposed scenario, mass either disappears into the pair prior already handled by the paper, or becomes an optimization variable that requires a new design question. That is a substantial objection to these anchors as ChoCo mirrors, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population scenario exists.
