The continuous mirror question

For a population of total mass \(N\) with finite support \(T\) of feature-cost types \(t=(x_t,c_t)\), mass \(N\mu_t\), and per-capita budget \(b\), determine whether a truthful, individually rational, budget-feasible direct procurement mechanism can achieve an approximation factor below \(2\). The mechanism acts on individual cost reports and selects a fraction \(q_t\in[0,1]\) of each type, with aggregate design value \(V_N(q)=\log\det\!\left(I_d+N\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_tx_tx_t^\top\right)\) and benchmark \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}=\max\{V_N(q):0\le q_t\le1,\ \sum_t\mu_tc_tq_t\le b\}\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support population of feature-cost types, with type mass \(N\mu_t\), fractional selection rates \(q_t\), \(D\)-optimal value \(\log\det\!\left(I_d+N\sum_t\mu_tq_tx_tx_t^\top\right)\), and individual report-contingent allocation and payment rules whose aggregate payments fit budget \(Nb\).

The objection that survived

An individual deviation has zero mass in the aggregate distribution, so the mirror still needs precise report-contingent per-subject allocation and payment rules that connect individual incentives to the type-level rates.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the strategic Bayesian \(D\)-optimal experimental design results in Theorems 2 and 3. It leaves the general-learning Corollary 1, the non-homotropic-prior extension in Theorem 4, and the non-Bayesian determinant result in Lemma 15 outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a deterministic individual-level mechanism implement the type-level selection rates while satisfying dominant-strategy truthfulness and aggregate budget feasibility?
  • Does the factor-\(2\) mechanism lower bound persist when the selected population can be fractional?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of the paper’s budgeted \(D\)-optimal experiment procurement problem. It keeps the paper’s information objective, strategic costs and budget; it changes the population representation and lets a type’s mass be selected fractionally.

Take a population of total mass \(N\), described by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of complete types \(t=(x_t,c_t)\). Here \(x_t\in\mathbb Q^d\) is the public feature vector and \(c_t\) is the type’s private participation cost. Members of a type have the same features and cost. A natural regime is a large survey or platform population with repeated feature strata and a small menu of participation-cost levels: \(N\) is large while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of occupied strata is moderate.

Let \(q_t\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of type \(t\)’s mass selected. Selecting that mass contributes \(N\mu_tq_t x_tx_t^\top\) to the design matrix, so the value is
\[ V_N(q)=\log\det\!\left(I_d+N\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t x_tx_t^\top\right). \]
With per-capita budget \(b\), total budget is \(Nb\), and the full-information mass benchmark is
\[ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}} =\max\left\{V_N(q): 0\le q_t\le1,\quad N\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c_tq_t\le Nb \right\}. \]
The \(x_tx_t^\top\) term is the same per-subject contribution as in the paper; the factor \(N\) preserves aggregate information as the population grows.

My lead is Theorem 3, proved in Appendix G. Its claim is that no \(2\)-approximate, truthful, budget-feasible, individually rational mechanism exists for the paper’s EDP. The proof uses two orthogonal feature vectors with costs just above \(B/2\): truthfulness and threshold payments prevent the mechanism from buying both, leaving value \(\log 2\) where the optimum is \(2\log 2\).

The corresponding question is Divisible Budget-Feasible \(D\)-Optimal Mechanism: given the mass instance above and a target \(\alpha<2\), can one give a deterministic direct mechanism that works across all such instances, or prove none exists? For each reported-cost profile it returns an allocation rate \(q_i\in[0,1]\) and payment \(p_i\ge0\) per participant. It must satisfy individual rationality \(p_i\ge c_iq_i\), aggregate budget feasibility \(\sum_i p_i\le Nb\), and dominant-strategy truthfulness: for every agent, true cost \(c_i\), and alternative report \(r_i\),
\[ p_i(c_i,\hat c_{-i})-c_iq_i(c_i,\hat c_{-i}) \ge p_i(r_i,\hat c_{-i})-c_iq_i(r_i,\hat c_{-i}). \]
Its approximation guarantee is measured against \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\). I expect Class A for this mass version: the two-agent obstruction no longer directly forces an indivisible choice. With two unit masses, orthogonal features and costs \(B/2+\epsilon\), selecting a fraction \(B/(B+2\epsilon)\) of each type spends exactly \(B\) and gives value approaching \(2\log 2\). That does not prove a truthful sub-\(2\) mechanism exists, but it shows why Theorem 3’s witness may stop working when selection is by mass.

A second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in the paper. For every \(\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1]\), it gives a polynomial-time, \(\delta\)-truthful, individually rational, budget-feasible mechanism for EDP, with
\[ \mathrm{OPT}\le \frac{10e-3+\sqrt{64e^2-24e+9}}{2(e-1)}\,V(S^*)+\varepsilon \ \simeq\ 12.98\,V(S^*)+\varepsilon. \]
The continuous question is Mass-EDP-12.98: construct the corresponding mechanism for the finite-support society, outputting type-level selection rates and transfers, with the same \(\delta\)-truthfulness and budget guarantees and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\le 12.98V_N(q)+\varepsilon\). I also expect Class A here: the mass objective is concave in the selection rates and the budget constraints are linear. A prover would still need to establish type-level monotonicity and payment guarantees; the paper’s individual-level proof does not automatically establish them.

Theorem 3 is my lead because it supplies the paper’s named approximation lower bound and poses a clear test of whether divisibility changes it. It is a mechanism impossibility result, not an NP-hardness theorem. The paper’s EDP NP-hardness via Knapsack appears in unnumbered prose in §3.2, so I am not using it as an anchor. The two questions cover the strategic \(D\)-optimal EDP results, not the paper’s general-learning corollary or its non-Bayesian determinant objective.

The weakest point is the economic regime and its incentive model: exact repeated feature vectors and costs are a stylized assumption, and a continuum mass allocation requires a population-level account of individual reports and transfers. The mirror is most convincing for large recruitment pools with recurring covariate strata and standardized cost levels. Its main research value is then to determine whether the paper’s truthful lower bound survives mass allocation and whether its approximate-truthfulness algorithm can be compressed to \(\tau\) types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The weak point in the proposed case is that its fractional benchmark settles no mechanism question. In Theorem 3, the obstruction is about named subjects’ reports, allocations, and threshold payments. Buying fractions of two orthogonal feature groups may raise the full information value, but it does not show that a truthful, budget-feasible mechanism can buy those fractions. To state that problem, the model must say how each subject’s report affects their own chance of selection and payment.

That leaves a consequential choice of strategic unit. If a type is a block whose members share one private per-capita cost report, then it is the block—not the individual subjects—that is strategic. That could describe agencies selling pools of participants, but it changes Theorem 3’s model. If subjects report individually, a continuum allocation described only by masses does not specify their individual treatment. At finite population size, a single report changes the type distribution by \(1/N\); in an atomless limit, that change disappears. The type-mass formulation therefore cannot simply inherit the theorem’s individual threshold-payment argument. A separate mechanism model might resolve this, but the opponent’s two-type calculation has not.

The same issue affects Theorem 2. Its algorithm uses a \(\delta\)-decreasing estimate whose coordinates are individual costs, then combines that estimate with an allocation rule and threshold payments. Replacing those coordinates by type masses changes what a cost report means: is it one subject’s bid, or a common bid for an entire cohort? The paper’s proof does not answer that, and a block-supplier version would need its own incentive and payment analysis. The proposed concavity of the mass objective establishes tractability of an allocation relaxation; it does not establish the claimed mechanism guarantees.

There is also little new computational content in the nonstrategic mass problem. With known costs, choosing type weights to maximize the \(D\)-optimal information objective under size and cost constraints is already a standard approximate-design problem. The design literature explicitly allows fractional weights and handles cost constraints; for example, [Pronzato and Wang’s approximate \(D\)-optimal design with size and cost constraints](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.2698) studies this formulation. Thus the full-information convex allocation problem is not a strong independent reason to pursue this mirror. That prior art does not answer the strategic private-cost question, so it is not a collision with the proposed mechanism mirror.

My negative case is strongest against the two named anchors: both are results about incentive guarantees for individually strategic subjects, while the proposed mass model has not yet specified a matching individual-level mechanism. It is weaker as a universal claim. Large recruitment pools with repeated covariates and standardized costs are plausible, and a type-level procurement mechanism might be worth studying if the authors accept a cohort or supplier as the strategic agent. I cannot honestly rule out that project; I would say the current proposal has not shown that it is a continuous mirror of Theorems 2 and 3.
