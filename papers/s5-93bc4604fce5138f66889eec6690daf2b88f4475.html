The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about one narrow point: this paper has no numbered theorem establishing a computational complexity result, so there is no theorem to restate one-for-one. But that does not support the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The paper’s central question is itself about collective choice: whether the citizens’ proposals resonated with the wider public, and whether those proposals should have gone to referendum. It reports votes on the proposals and survey evidence about public support. That gives a natural computational problem to formalize.

The stronger mirror would model the wider electorate, rather than treat the Convention’s 159 participants as a population limit. Let each type encode a complete ranking of feasible policy packages and, for a campaigning variant, the cost of changing its position. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the public with type \(t\). For the set \(F\) of packages meeting emissions, budget, and social-justice constraints, one can ask which \(S\in F\) has greatest public support, or what minimum mass transfer is needed for a given package to win a referendum. The package choices remain discrete; the society is the distribution over voter types.

That is not a question imported from nowhere. The article asks whether co-construction can include the broader public, reports that public contributions went unused, finds survey support for measures alongside the citizens’ rejection of referenda, and notes that the combined climate impact of the proposals was not assessed. Choosing a feasible package that can command broad support—or measuring how robust that support is—puts those concerns into a computational social-choice problem. In this macro-public setting, preference and cost types can recur at high multiplicity, so replacing named voters with mass does not discard information the question needs.

The limitations are real: this would not mirror a proved complexity theorem, and the article’s surveys do not provide a complete distribution over preferences for packages. Those points limit how directly the mirror can be derived or populated from the paper. They do not show that the question is unrecognizable or unworthy of the programme. The absence of a theorem rules out a narrow kind of mirror; it does not establish that no useful continuous-population computational question can be built from the paper’s central collective-choice problem.
