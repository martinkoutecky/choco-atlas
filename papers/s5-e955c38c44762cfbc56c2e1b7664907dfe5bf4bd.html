The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(q\in(0,1]\) and happy mass \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), consider finite-support societies with type masses \(\mu_t\), a happy-or-sad label for each type, and symmetric interaction intensities \(w_{tt'}\in[0,1]\), where every type has total interaction mass \(\sum_{t'}\mu_{t'}w_{tt'}=q\). Determine whether all, none, or both of the regular interaction structures with happy mass \(\alpha\) have more than half the population in types whose interaction mass with happy types exceeds \(q/2\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population described by a distribution \(\mu\) over interaction-role types, each type carrying a happiness label and a row of a symmetric interaction matrix \(w\). Type \(t\) is a proponent when \(\sum_{t'\text{ happy}}\mu_{t'}w_{tt'}>q/2\); acceptance requires proponents to have mass greater than \(1/2\). The number of types may vary, and the graphon model is a natural extension allowing a continuum of roles.

The objection that survived

A general interaction kernel may require a continuum of distinct interaction profiles, so the unrestricted graphon model need not have the finite-support type representation used in the programme; no reduction to finitely many role types is established.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 14’s classification of uniformly approving, uniformly disapproving, and mixed classes by support; it leaves the separate security-gap and degree-range analyses unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Are the support thresholds exactly characterized by the proposed \(\max\{1/4,q/2\}\) limit, including boundary cases?
  • Do extremal accepting and rejecting witnesses require only finitely many interaction roles?
  • What remains of the classification when degree stays fixed as population size grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 14, proved in this paper. It gives an exact formula for the finite support threshold
\[ \hmind=\left\lceil \frac{d+1}{2}\max\left\{\frac{n+1}{2d},1\right\}\right\rceil+\mathbf 1_{\{(5,3),(9,5)\}}(n,d), \qquad \hmand=n-\hmind. \]
For odd \(n,d\le n\), it says that the class of \(d\)-regular graphs with \(h\) happy vertices is uniformly disapproving when \(h<\hmind\), mixed when \(\hmind\le h\le\hmand\), and uniformly approving when \(h>\hmand\). This is an exact algorithmic result: the theorem solves the threshold problem by a closed formula, though it is not stated as a P-versus-NP theorem. The paper’s discussion of NP-completeness for majority domination is a citation to outside work, not a numbered result proved here, so I would not use it as an anchor.

My proposed mirror is the Dense Two-Tier Support Profile. An instance is a rational \(q\in(0,1]\) and a rational happy mass \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). A candidate society is a continuum of agents \(x\in[0,1]\), described by a symmetric measurable interaction kernel \(W:[0,1]^2\to[0,1]\) satisfying \(\int_0^1 W(x,y)\,dy=q\) almost everywhere, and a happy set \(H\subseteq[0,1]\) with measure \(\alpha\). Here \(W(x,y)\) is the interaction intensity between agents \(x\) and \(y\). Agent \(x\) is a proponent exactly when \(\int_H W(x,y)\,dy>q/2\); the proposal passes exactly when proponents have measure greater than \(1/2\). The problem asks whether, among all such pairs \((W,H)\), every society rejects, every society accepts, or both kinds exist. A mixed answer should include an accepting and a rejecting witness; a uniform answer should include a proof that no contrary witness exists.

This preserves the paper’s two-stage decision rule and its central question: how much support can suffice for acceptance, and how much can still fail to prevent rejection? A finite \(d\)-regular graph on \(n\) vertices embeds exactly as a step kernel with \(q=d/n\): happy-neighbor counts become interaction mass, and the two strict-majority tests agree. The continuum question therefore asks what happens when support becomes mass and degree becomes a fixed population share. The expected threshold is \(\max\{1/4,q/2\}\), suggested by normalizing Theorem 14 as \(n\) grows with \(d/n\to q\). That is a target for the prover, not a result established by this paper; the exact transition points and their endpoint behavior should be determined.

A plausible regime is a large, dense interaction network in which each person attends to roughly a fixed fraction \(q\) of the population. Many people can share the same interaction role and valuation, with the number of agents far exceeding the number of roles. In the model, a complete type is a role’s interaction profile together with its happy-or-sad valuation; its mass is its fraction of society. Finite-block kernels give literal finite sets of such types, while general kernels allow the full range of regular interaction structures studied by the paper.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: integrating the local support over proponents gives a global support bound, while the cap \(W\le1\) gives a local bound. Those yield the two terms in the predicted threshold; the remaining question is whether simple continuum constructions match them. Further questions include whether witnesses need only finitely many roles, how to characterize exact boundary cases, and what remains true in a sparse regime where \(d\) stays fixed as \(n\) grows. This mirrors the support-threshold and class-classification content of Theorem 14, not the paper’s separate security-gap and minimum-degree analyses.

The weakest point is that the kernel permits fractional interaction intensities, while every finite graph has binary edges. That could make some continuum witnesses unavailable in finite networks. The dense regime and exact step-kernel embedding make the relaxation credible, but they do not prove that its extremal constructions reflect every intended network setting. The paper’s odd degrees also rule out local ties; a continuum model permits them, so endpoint claims need care.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat the anchor. Theorem 14 solves a named threshold problem: given \(n,d,h\), it classifies whether regular graph configurations must reject, must accept, or can do either. Calling it only a structural result would evade the paper’s explicit Problem 1.

The strongest objection is that the network is doing essential work. If a type records only whether an agent is happy, its mass does not determine local majorities. To retain those, a type must record an interaction profile. For general regular graphs, that profile can vary from vertex to vertex, so the number of types can grow with \(n\). The proposed graphon handles this by making interactions a kernel \(W\), but then the object is no longer just a finite-support distribution over voter types. And although finite graphs embed as step kernels, that alone does not show that thresholded local majorities behave continuously at the limit: vertices whose neighborhood support is arbitrarily close to half can change status under vanishing perturbations.

Restricting to finitely many interaction roles gives a finite, computational model, but it covers a chosen block-structured regime. To claim it mirrors Theorem 14’s classification over all regular graphs would require showing that a bounded number of roles suffices. The paper’s cyclic constructions make that a real modeling question, not an automatic consequence of the step-kernel embedding.

That is a limitation of the proposed mirror, not a decisive reason against every mirror. A dense graphon model is a plausible continuous population problem for large networked electorates, and the paper’s authors could recognize it as their question. Under the brief’s standard, the negative case is weak: the type-space objection narrows the mirror, but does not eliminate the credible dense-network scenario.
