The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see a qualifying computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. The paper’s central results are axiomatic characterizations, not claims about complexity, algorithms, or parameterized tractability.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for \(n\) individuals choosing among \(k\ge2\) options, an aggregation procedure on single-vote-or-abstention profiles satisfies universal domain, anonymity, neutrality, and positive responsiveness if and only if it is plurality rule. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that under approval balloting, the corresponding conditions—with optionwise anonymity—characterize approval voting. Both are substantial results, but neither asserts a computational property of an underlying problem.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large electorate with many voters sharing one of a small number of ballot types. For Theorem 1, types could be the \(k\) single-option ballots plus abstention, with mass \(\mu_t\) for each type; the winning set is the option or options with greatest vote mass. Approval-ballot types could similarly be subsets of the options. But determining the winners from those masses is just an argmax calculation. More importantly, the paper does not state that as a computational problem or prove a named complexity result about it. A continuous-population restatement of either characterization would remain an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

So there is no lead mirror problem per the requested standard. The large-electorate regime fits the population-continuity idea well, but it cannot supply the missing computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive case is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes plurality rule by axioms; Theorem 2 does the same for approval voting. Neither formulates a decision or optimization problem, nor claims anything about complexity or algorithms. Under ChoCo’s scope, these theorems provide no computational result to mirror.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity regime here: many voters can share one of the \(k+1\) single-vote ballot types, or one of the \(2^k\) approval types. A continuous version can put masses on those types. But extending the theorems to such masses would still be an axiomatic characterization, which falls outside the programme. Computing the winner from the masses is an argmax calculation, not a computational result established by the paper.

One could build a new problem around the paper’s rules, such as finding the least mass of voters that must change ballots to alter the winner. That might be worthwhile as independent ChoCo research. It would introduce an intervention objective absent from the paper, though, rather than mirror a result the authors proved. The universal claim that no scenario could make such a neighboring problem worthwhile is hard to establish; the narrower conclusion is strong: neither named theorem supports a worthwhile computational mirror of this paper.
