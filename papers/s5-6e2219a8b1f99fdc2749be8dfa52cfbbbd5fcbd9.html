What the mirror covers

The proposed extension would concern continuous allocation or bargaining; it does not mirror a computational result in Theorem 5.1 or the paper's axiomatic results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a valid anchored case under the stated standard: the paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification, an algorithmic running time, or hardness. Proposition 3.3 and Theorems 3.4 and 4.2 concern consistency and axiomatic characterizations. The closest result is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper: for any \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and any zero-monotonic TU-game with \(v(N)\ge 0\), the specified bidding mechanism—where negotiations continue after a first-round rejection with probability \(\alpha\)—has subgame-perfect equilibrium outcomes equal to the \(\alpha\)-egalitarian Shapley value \(\varphi_\alpha(N,v)\). The mechanism adapts one cited from Pérez-Castrillo and Wettstein; the theorem does not analyze the computational complexity of finding that value or an equilibrium.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity research direction: many interchangeable participants drawn from a small set of types, with coalition worth described as a function of the mass of each type. One could ask for a continuous-population version of the egalitarian Shapley allocation or its bargaining implementation. But the paper does not define such a population model, establish a discrete-to-continuous limit, or show how its finite-player bidding mechanism works for a continuum. That makes this a proposed extension, not a mirror of a named computational result. The weakest point is decisive for this task: there is no qualifying anchor from which to state the required continuous computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.1 is the only plausible anchor, and its result is an equilibrium implementation theorem, not a computational result. It establishes that the specified finite-player mechanism has subgame-perfect equilibrium outcomes equal to \(\varphi_\alpha(N,v)\). It gives no input representation, running time, or complexity classification for computing either the value or an equilibrium. The paper’s other named results characterize solution properties. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The strongest population model is still easy to imagine: finitely many interchangeable player types, with coalition worth determined by the masses of those types. For example, if \(v_n(S)=nF(x_S)\), where \(x_S\) records coalition composition, one could study the limit of the Shapley component using random priorities and per-capita payoffs. That could produce a meaningful continuous value. But the paper does not establish that limit or ask how to compute it. To make it a computational problem, one must choose an encoding and restrictions for \(F\); those choices supply the problem’s complexity and make it a new project, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

A continuous version of the implementation is a weaker anchor still. The finite mechanism chooses among named proposers, transfers pairwise bids, and removes one proposer at a time until a finite terminal round. In a mass model, removing one person leaves the aggregate type masses unchanged; the finite sequence of reduced games and its terminal round have no direct counterpart. One could design a new bargaining model for a nonatomic population, but its equilibrium and computational questions would need their own formulation and justification. Theorem 5.1 does not provide them.

So I would reject this paper as a ChoCo mirror. That judgment is about programme fit, not a claim that anonymous continuous cooperative games cannot be interesting: they may be. The case is strongest in showing that such work would start a new computational question rather than continuize one this paper actually studied.
