The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population setting is a large electorate choosing among three projects, where voters fall into two complete utility types: type \(A\) ranks \(A \succ B \succ C\), and type \(B\) ranks \(B \succ A \succ C\). Their payoffs are determined by the paper’s parameter \(x\), and the positional rule by \(\alpha\). If \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\) are their population shares, then ballot masses determine each candidate’s aggregate score. This is a natural high-multiplicity description: many voters, two types, and a rule whose scores aggregate linearly.

The closest result to anchor is the unnumbered equilibrium analysis in Appendix A. For the two-player game, it reduces the strategies to sincere and strategic voting after iterated deletion of weakly dominated strategies. At \(\alpha=\tfrac12\), it gives \((\text{strategic},\text{strategic})\) when \(x\le\tfrac12\), and the two asymmetric sincere/strategic equilibria when \(x>\tfrac12\). For \(\alpha>\tfrac12\), it gives those asymmetric equilibria. These findings are proved in the paper, but they are not a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition, and they make no computational-complexity claim. The experimental results in the numbered tables are empirical findings, not such claims either.

A possible continuous question would take ballot shares \(q_{t,r}\), where \(t\in\{A,B\}\) and \(r\) ranges over the six rankings, and ask for an equilibrium assignment of ballot mass under the \(\alpha\)-positional rule and the paper’s payoffs. The central difficulty is that in an atomless population a single voter cannot change the aggregate scores or winner. Individual best responses therefore lose the pivotal incentives that drive the paper’s manipulation analysis. Requiring deviations by positive masses could make the model responsive, but that would study coalition or mass manipulation rather than the individual strategic behavior in the paper.

So the paper has a credible type structure for a continuous-population model, but it contains no named computational result at all under the required anchor criterion. I would not submit an anchor-specific problem as though it met that criterion. The weakest point is also the decisive one: preserving the paper’s pivotality appears to require keeping committees finite, while making the population itself continuous removes the incentive mechanism the paper studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its main findings are experimental estimates from committees of size \(2,4,8\); Appendix A analyzes equilibria of a two-player game. None states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm. The proponent’s proposed equilibrium question would therefore add a new computational target rather than continue a result in the paper.

The proposed two-type electorate is sensible: voters can be grouped by their complete preference and payoff types, with \(\mu_A=\mu_B=\tfrac12\). But that does not preserve the paper’s central mechanism. In an atomless society, one voter has measure zero. Changing their ballot leaves the aggregate score vector unchanged, so it cannot change the winner or their payoff. Every ballot is then a best response; at a tie, an individual still cannot alter the aggregate tie. The paper’s strategic manipulation depends on the chance that an individual vote is pivotal, especially in its small committees.

One could retain pivotality by keeping finite voters with ballot mass \(1/n\). That preserves the finite game, but the continuous distribution alone loses the score-margin information that determines whether a one-voter change breaks a tie. Retaining that information requires a finite-\(n\) model or additional assumptions about fluctuations near ties. Alternatively, allowing a positive mass to change ballots makes the deviator a coalition or bloc. That is a different strategic actor from the individual voters whose behavior the experiment measures. Neither route supplies a continuous-population version of the paper’s equilibrium finding.

The negative case is strongest on this particular mirror: a natural high-multiplicity electorate exists, but atomlessness removes the individual incentive being studied, while repairs change the model. This is not proof that no adjacent computational question could be worthwhile; it is a reason not to present one as a continuous mirror of this paper.
