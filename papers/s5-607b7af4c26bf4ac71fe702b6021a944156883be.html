The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest pro case is a computational question about the paper’s second-best allocation problem. The paper does not prove a conventional complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Its best computationally relevant anchor is Proposition 3, proved here: there is a unique solution to the mechanism designer’s problem, with \(q_P=f|_{[x_1,x_2]}\) and \(q_I=(1-\beta)\,f|_{[0,x_1]\cup[x_2,x_3]}+\beta\delta_{\diamond}\), where \(\beta\in[0,1)\), \(0<x_1<x_2\le x_3\le X\), \(F(x_2)-F(x_1)=\mu_P\), and \(F(x_1)+F(x_3)-F(x_2)=(1-\beta)\mu_I\). The paper also explains that this reduces the original infinite-dimensional screening problem to two choices.

I would name the mirror Exact Two-Type Continuum Screening. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_P,\mu_I\) summing to one, a positive piecewise-constant rational supply density \(f\) with enough total supply, a rational welfare weight \(\alpha\), and two rational piecewise-polynomial utility functions \(u_P,u_I\). The instance promises that utilities are strictly decreasing and satisfy the paper’s strict ordering of absolute risk aversion. A solution is the exact welfare-maximizing incentive-compatible menu of lotteries \(q_P,q_I\), feasible under \(\mu_Pq_P(x)+\mu_Iq_I(x)\le f(x)\); report its allocation endpoints and \(\beta\), or an exact representation of those values.

The population interpretation is direct: a large appointment or public-housing queue has many people in each of two urgency classes, all with the same utility function within their class. A type is the complete utility function used by the model; its mass is the fraction of eligible people in that class. Thus \(\tau=2\) can remain small as the number of people grows. The lotteries are the paper’s allocation decisions for each type.

I expect this encoded problem to be Class A. Proposition 3 leaves two scalar choices, and the proposed finite input makes the objective and constraints piecewise algebraic. That is a promising route to an exact algorithm. The paper itself does not establish polynomial bit complexity, so proving tractability in the number of supply segments and input bit length is the question—not a result to attribute to the authors. Further questions include the corresponding problem with a variable number of types, an approximation guarantee for finite populations, and whether the continuous optimum can be rounded to an implementable finite assignment while preserving incentive compatibility.

The scope here is only the unobservable-type, two-type problem in Proposition 3. I would not use the first-best characterization, the many-type results, or the market equilibrium as additional anchors: they are not computational results.

The weak point is substantial: this paper already models a continuum of agents with type masses. Proposition 3 characterizes that continuous-population problem; a “mirror” using the same model risks restating it rather than continuizing a discrete problem. The finite encoding above supplies a computational question the paper does not ask, but it is an analyst’s addition, and the paper gives no discrete-to-continuous identity. So this is a plausible complexity project around the authors’ model, but a weak case that the paper itself supplies a new continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. Proposition 3 characterizes the unique optimum of a screening problem; it does not establish a complexity bound or give an algorithm with a stated running time. The paper also starts with a continuous population: the two preference types have masses \(\mu_P,\mu_I\), supply has a density \(f\), and feasibility is expressed in aggregate. Its housing and appointment examples are already framed in terms of groups of agents. So the proposed “Exact Two-Type Continuum Screening” asks about the same continuous population and essentially the same menu \(q_P,q_I\) that Proposition 3 characterizes. Under ChoCo’s collision test, this is already-continuous work, not a new continuous mirror.

A finite-agent version is plausible: many people of each urgency type could receive lotteries, with aggregate feasibility approximating the paper’s constraints. That makes high multiplicity sensible, but its continuous limit is precisely the model the authors already analyze. A rounding or approximation theorem for finite assignments could be valuable; it would be a new bridge from their existing continuous result to a finite problem, not a continuization of a discrete result in this paper. Extending from two or finitely many types to a continuum of preference intensities is also a possible model extension, but it changes the type space rather than supplying the missing mirror.

The proposed rational piecewise-polynomial encoding does define a computational question, but it adds an input model absent from the paper, whose utilities and supply density may be arbitrary continuous functions. The paper’s reduction to two choices is not a polynomial-time or bit-complexity result. An algorithmic study of that encoded problem might be worthwhile on its own; it would concern a model that is already continuous, rather than charting the computational consequences of continuizing the population.

That is the negative case’s limit: it argues strongly against counting this as a ChoCo continuous mirror, but it does not show that no computational follow-up to the paper could be worthwhile.
