What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror uses the h-categorizer inverse from Corollary 8.3 and a finite mixture of rational weight profiles; it leaves the broader family of semantics, the max-based and card-based cases, and the other inverse properties aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is one defensible mirror, centered on the paper’s inverse problem. Corollary 8.3 is the closest named computational anchor: it is proved in this paper, not cited from elsewhere. It identifies the weighted h-categorizer semantics \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{HC}}\) with the \(L_1\)-based semantics, and says that its inverse, reflection, topological reflection, projective preference, and radiality problems all have positive answers. In particular, for a fixed argument graph \(G=(A,D)\), its inverse is

\[ w^*(a)=x(a)\left(1+\sum_{b\in\mathrm{Att}_G(a)}x(b)\right). \]

Thus a proposed grading \(x\) is realizable exactly when this formula gives a weight vector in \([0,1]^A\), and that vector is the unique initial weighting producing \(x\). The corollary also identifies the max-based and card-based semantics as \(L_\infty\)-based and card-\(L_1\)-based semantics; for the card-based case, all five inverse problems hold except topological reflection.

The paper has no named complexity-class result saying that a problem is in P or is hard. The corollary is a constructive inverse result, not a complexity classification. The polynomial-time claim below is a consequence for the population problem I am proposing, under an explicit binary-rational input model.

Consider a large deliberation platform using one shared argument graph \(G=(A,D)\). Each participant has a complete baseline-belief profile \(v^t\in[0,1]^A\): its coordinate \(v^t(a)\) is that type’s initial weight for argument \(a\). Participants with the same profile are indistinguishable for this model. Suppose the platform has a finite list \(T\) of such types, with \(\tau=|T|\), and a society represented by masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The population’s pooled initial weight for argument \(a\) is

\[ \bar w(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t v^t(a). \]

A plausible regime is a very large user community with many repeated belief profiles: the number of people \(n\) is far larger than the number of supported types \(\tau\). This draws on the paper’s own motivation that initial weights represent beliefs and that inverse inference can support user or domain modelling.

Continuous h-Categorizer Population Inverse Realization. An instance consists of an explicitly listed argument graph \(G=(A,D)\), rational type profiles \(v^t\in[0,1]^A\) for \(t\in T\), and a rational target grading \(x\in[0,1]^A\). The question is whether there is a population distribution \(\mu\) over these types such that

\[ \Sigma_{\mathrm{HC}}(G)(\bar w)=x. \]

A solution is any such distribution \(\mu\), together with its pooled weight vector \(\bar w\). This is a realizability and recovery problem, so it has no cost objective. It asks whether the observed community-level acceptability degrees can arise from a mixture of the listed belief types.

Corollary 8.3 gives a direct route to an algorithm. Compute \(w^*\) from \(x\). If any coordinate lies outside \([0,1]\), reject. Otherwise, test whether \(w^*\) is in the convex hull of the listed profiles by solving

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t v^t(a)=w^*(a)\quad(a\in A),\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1,\qquad \mu_t\geq 0. \]

With explicit rational profiles and target, exact linear programming gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(|A|\), \(\tau\), the graph size, and the input bit length \(L\). A rational feasible solution can be returned. I expect this mirror to be tractable. Its computational contribution is the composition of the paper’s analytic inverse with a population-mixture feasibility problem; the paper itself does not establish that complexity bound.

The mirror covers the inverse and recovery result for \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{HC}}\), not the paper’s full family of semantics or all five inverse problems. It leaves natural follow-ups, including whether the same approach works when types are given implicitly, and how to handle noisy observations of \(x\).

The weak point is that the paper studies weights on arguments in a framework, not a population of people. This proposal pools participants’ baseline profiles first and then applies the nonlinear semantics to the shared graph. Averaging each participant’s final grading instead would be a different problem, and Corollary 8.3 would not solve it. The pooled-belief scenario seems plausible given the paper’s user-modelling motivation, but whether the authors would recognize it as a continuous form of their inverse problem is the decisive uncertainty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror adds a population model the paper never specifies. In the paper, \(A\) is the set of arguments: \(\Sigma_G\) maps one vector of argument weights to one vector of acceptability degrees. The proposed society instead consists of people, each with a weight vector, and assumes their weights should be averaged before applying \(\Sigma_G\). That is a consequential choice of collective reasoning rule, not something supplied by Corollary 8.3.

For the h-categorizer, the choice changes the result. On a graph with one self-attacking argument, the fixed point satisfies \(x=w/(1+x)\). For two equally sized types with weights \(0\) and \(1\), pooling weights first gives \((\sqrt{3}-1)/2\); applying the semantics to each type first and then averaging gives \((\sqrt{5}-1)/4\). Those are different collective quantities, and the paper gives no reason to privilege the first.

The most favorable repair is to keep each person’s reasoning separate and average their final degrees. With a listed set of profiles, target aggregate degrees then amount to membership in the convex hull of the profiles’ outputs. That is a sensible population question, but it no longer mirrors the inverse result: Corollary 8.3 recovers one person’s initial weights from that person’s grading, while this question concerns mixtures of outputs. The proposed pooled-weights version uses the corollary, but depends on the added pooling convention.

This leaves a real case against the proposed anchor: its population interpretation is plausible, but the connection to the paper’s inverse problem rests on a modeling choice the paper does not make. I cannot honestly make the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper explicitly studies computable inverse functions and supplies a constructive one, and a large community with repeated belief profiles is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The negative case is therefore weak at the point that matters most: whether the authors would recognize pooled community beliefs as their problem.
