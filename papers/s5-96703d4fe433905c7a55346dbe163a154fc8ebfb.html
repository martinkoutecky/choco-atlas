The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Corollary 1, proved here. The paper does not give a named NP-hardness or complexity-class result; this is an approximation-algorithm result. Corollary 1 says that, for regular seller-cost distributions, choosing \(\alpha_0\approx2.39\) and \(\beta_0\approx2.13\) maximizes the stated bound, and the paper’s sequential posted-price mechanism then achieves at least a \(0.2015\)-approximation to optimal expected buyer utility among ex-post budget-feasible mechanisms. Its input prices are the soft-budget-optimal prices for cost distributions truncated at the buyer’s budget. The mechanism splits sellers into high- and low-price groups: it processes the high-price group in decreasing \(v_i-p_i\) order when that group contributes enough of the posted-price benchmark; otherwise it processes the low-price group in decreasing \((v_i-p_i)/p_i\) order. In either branch, it skips offers that the remaining budget cannot cover. The paper also states that the mechanism runs in \(O(n\log n)\) time, with the posted prices efficiently computable.

A continuous mirror I find plausible is Continuum Ex-Post Utility Procurement. A buyer procures one unit of service from a population of sellers whose total mass is normalized to \(1\). There are finitely many seller classes \(t\in T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A class fixes the service value \(v_t\) and the seller’s cost distribution \(F_t\); an individual seller’s full private type is \((t,c)\), where \(c\) is their realized cost. Assume regular, efficiently represented \(F_t\). The buyer has budget \(b\) per unit of population, corresponding to total budget \(Nb\) in a population of \(N\) sellers.

The problem is to find a DSIC-IR mechanism that allocates each seller either zero or one unit, pays nonnegative transfers, and spends at most \(b\) in total for every measurable profile of cost reports. Its objective is expected buyer utility per unit of population, value procured minus payments. Each individual still gets an integral allocation; only the mass of sellers is continuous. The cost distributions describe the population’s mix of private costs, and the continuum model assumes that this mix is exact.

The concrete candidate solution is a posted price \(p_t\) for each class. In the continuum, exactly \(\mu_tF_t(p_t)\) mass in class \(t\) accepts. The price vector should maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_tF_t(p_t)(v_t-p_t) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_tF_t(p_t)\le b. \]
To enforce the hard budget even on report profiles outside the prior, implement those offers with a fixed-order budget cap: process eligible sellers and stop allocating when the budget is exhausted. Since an individual has zero mass, their report does not change the budget remaining when their offer is reached; the offer remains a threshold rule. On the truthful continuum population, the aggregate spend is the expression in the constraint, so no rationing is needed. Thus the cap ensures ex-post feasibility, while truthful play achieves the full posted-price objective.

I expect this mirror to be tractable under regularity and efficient price computation. The paper’s soft-budget analysis supplies the key upper bound: any ex-post-feasible mechanism is ex-ante feasible, and the regular-cost optimum has an independent posted-price form. In the continuum, the ex-ante-optimal prices already spend exactly their expected aggregate amount, so they can be implemented with the hard-budget cap without losing utility on the truthful population. The natural prediction is therefore exact optimality in the continuum, not merely a \(0.2015\)-approximation. The paper’s Lagrangian calculation suggests computing prices with a common multiplier \(\lambda\), using \(p_t=\phi_t^{-1}(v_t/(1+\lambda))\), then choosing \(\lambda\) to meet the per-capita budget when it is useful to do so.

The scenario is a large procurement market for standardized services—compute capacity is a plausible example—with many sellers in a modest number of service-value and cost-prior classes. The number of sellers grows while the number of classes stays small. This is the same Bayesian, hard-budget utility problem the authors study; the population is what becomes continuous. The mirror covers Corollary 1’s regular-distribution, Bayesian utility result. It does not claim a mirror for the paper’s prior-free welfare section or its generalized objectives.

The weak point is that Corollary 1 is a mechanism approximation guarantee, not a hardness result, so this case cannot show that discrete computational hardness dissolves. The exact-continuum prediction also depends on the exact-law assumption: the type masses and cost distribution are realized without finite-population fluctuations. A prover should establish that the capped continuum mechanism is DSIC and ex-post feasible for arbitrary reports, then prove that the posted-price upper bound remains optimal over all continuum mechanisms. The main follow-up is a finite-population bridge: quantify how the continuum optimum and its no-rationing guarantee change with \(N\), and determine how quickly finite populations approach it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 1 is a genuine computational result to mirror: it gives an efficient mechanism and a constant approximation guarantee. I cannot dismiss it as merely axiomatic, and the fact that it concerns an approximation ratio is no objection.

The proposed mirror does, however, blur seller classes with complete types. A class fixes \(v_t\) and a cost distribution \(F_t\), but a seller’s strategic type also includes their realized cost \(c\). With an atomless \(F_t\), each complete type \((t,c)\) has zero population mass. So the proposed population is not a finite-support high-multiplicity society in ChoCo’s stated model; it is a continuum of distinct private cost types, with no finite input size \(\tau\) to analyze. Recasting costs as a finite set of atoms fixes that mismatch: the society can be listed by class and cost, with a rational mass for each complete type.

But that repair exposes the strongest objection to the anchor. In an exact-mass population, the mass accepting each posted price is fixed. The budget contention that drives the paper’s hard-budget result disappears on the truthful population: the hard-budget cap can handle other report profiles, but it need not ration the truthful one. The resulting optimum follows from the soft-budget posted-price formulation already developed in the paper, with a common multiplier and an aggregate budget constraint. This is a coherent fluid-limit question, but the specific finite-population problem behind Corollary 1—random acceptance and the loss from ex-post contention—has dropped out.

A stronger attempt to retain that content would give the continuum population a random aggregate composition, perhaps through a common market shock. That could make hard-budget contention meaningful again, but it changes the paper’s independent-cost model and adds a new source of uncertainty. Keeping finite-population sampling instead retains the original discrete problem.

This is the best case against the proposed anchor: its atomless version does not fit the programme’s finite-type input model, while its finite-support repair removes the ex-post contention the corollary analyzes. Still, I cannot honestly claim this defeats every worthwhile mirror. The finite-support fluid model is plausible and may yield a useful discrete-to-continuous identity; under the programme’s own standard, that can be a worthwhile result even if the limit is simpler. The negative case is therefore weak at its central point: the mirror survives as a credible fluid-limit question.
