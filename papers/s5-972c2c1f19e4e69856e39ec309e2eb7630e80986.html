The continuous mirror question

Given \(X=[n]\), an initial LMSR state \(w^0\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) trader types, where each type \(t\) trades an interval \(I_t\), process a sequence of cohort orders \((t,\alpha,q)\) with \(0\leq\alpha\leq\mu_t\). Each order updates \(w\leftarrow w+\alpha q\mathbf{1}_{I_t}\). What worst-case time and space are needed to return exact interval prices and costs after each update?

The model it lives in

Interval LMSR with a continuous population of trader types carrying masses \(\mu_t\); a cohort of mass \(\alpha\) trading \(q\) shares per unit mass contributes \(\alpha q\) shares on its type's interval, and the market maker maintains \(w\) and returns exact prices and costs.

The objection that survived

The market maker receives only the aggregate order αq, so the distribution μ and support size τ do not affect the stated running-time bounds; this confines the mirror to cohort order processing.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Corollaries 3.4 and 3.9 for one-dimensional interval LMSR. It leaves the other scoring rules, multidimensional and general set systems, multi-resolution markets, and CFMM results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can batches of cohort orders be processed with running time expressed in \(n\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • What bit-complexity guarantees are possible for rational cohort masses and exact prices?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror I see is mass-aggregated interval LMSR. It continuizes the participants in a prediction market while keeping the paper’s outcome space discrete.

Take \(X=[n]\), representing \(n\) bins for a forecasted quantity such as an index closing value. The population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) trader types. A type identifies an interval security its members trade; assume members share the market terms and budget, while order size is an action. In a plausible regime, \(N\gg\tau\): many traders share each type. A participating mass \(\alpha\le\mu_t\) of type \(t\), whose interval is \(I_t\), submits \(q\) shares per unit mass. The market state changes by
\[ w'(x)=w(x)+\alpha q\,\mathbf 1_{I_t}(x). \]
Starting from \(w^0\), the market-maker data structure must process these mass trades online and return exact interval prices and costs:
\[ p(J;w)=\frac{\sum_{x\in J}e^{w(x)/b}}{\sum_{x\in X}e^{w(x)/b}}, \qquad C_b(w+\alpha q\mathbf 1_{I_t})-C_b(w), \]
where \(C_b(w)=b\ln\sum_{x\in X}e^{w(x)/b}\). The mass update is exactly an LMSR buy with share amount \(\alpha q\), so the paper’s operation algorithms apply directly. For the lower-bound witness, the relevant types can be the \(n\) prefix intervals; each can have positive mass even when the trader population is much larger.

My lead anchor is Corollary 3.9, proved in this paper by reducing from the dynamic partial-sums lower bound cited as Theorem A.4. It says that any LMSR algorithm on one-dimensional intervals over \(X=[n]\) takes \(\Omega(\log n)\) time in the worst case. That bound carries to the mass-trade problem: choose positive mass for each prefix type and let participating mass and per-capita order realize the desired share amount, so each LMSR buy is represented by a cohort trade. Thus the continuous population does not remove the logarithmic cost of maintaining the market.

The companion upper-bound anchor is Corollary 3.4, also proved here. It says that the paper’s partition-tree algorithms support all LMSR price, cost, and buy operations for one-dimensional intervals in \(O(\log n)\) time, using linear space and \(O(n)\) construction time. It therefore gives the mass-trade problem an \(O(\log n)\)-per-operation algorithm. Together, these anchors make the mirror a tight, tractable problem: Class A, with a matching \(\Omega(\log n)\) bound. The lower bound is about the number of outcomes \(n\), not an NP-hardness result about population multiplicity.

The scenario is a large forecasting market where many participants follow the same price bands or thresholds and submit orders on those interval events. The programme’s motivation fits: the market state is driven by a large population, and fractional mass makes cohort order flow natural. The mirror keeps the society as the continuous object; it does not make the outcome space continuous.

I would cover only the interval LMSR operation results in Corollaries 3.4 and 3.9. I would not claim that this mirrors the paper’s pairing securities, other scoring rules, multi-resolution design, or CFMM results.

The soft spot is that the continuous society is an interpretation of the aggregate order flow, while the paper itself takes the market state \(w\) and the online operations as its input. Its bounds depend on \(n\), and do not establish a new dependence on population size or type support \(\tau\). Also, the paper’s operation bounds use its arithmetic model; an \(L\)-sensitive bit-complexity result for rational masses and exact prices remains open. Natural next questions are whether batch population updates can be processed in time parameterized by \((n,\tau,L)\), and how the mirror changes for security systems with unbounded VC dimension.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper’s computational input has no population variable. Its algorithms receive an outcome space \(X\), a current market state \(w\), and online requests \((E,s)\). The requests already allow any real share amount \(s\). If a cohort of mass \(\alpha\) submits \(q\) shares per unit mass on event \(E\), the market receives the request \((E,\alpha q)\). The algorithm never sees how many traders produced that amount, how they are grouped, or what their preferences are. So the proposed mirror has a plausible large-market story, but the named results do not formulate a computational problem over that society.

Corollary 3.4 is a useful implementation guarantee for cohort orders: the \(O(\log n)\) interval structure accepts their mass-weighted trades directly. That makes it a weak continuization anchor. A batch of \(\tau\) such orders can be passed through the data structure as \(\tau\) operations; the theorem’s parameters remain outcome size and range-operation cost, not population size or type support. Faster batch processing could be a worthwhile data-structure question, but the result does not identify a society-level computational challenge.

Corollary 3.9 has the same boundary problem. Its lower-bound witness is an arbitrary online sequence of prefix updates and queries, inherited from dynamic partial sums. Labeling those updates “cohort trades” does not make the lower bound about population multiplicity. If the interval event is part of a trader’s type, the witness uses \(n\) prefix types, so \(\tau=n\); if traders can choose events as actions, the arbitrary request sequence remains the source of difficulty. In either case, the bound is in \(\log n\), with no dependence on the number of agents.

A stronger mirror might give each type a state-dependent demand rule \(d_t(w)\), then ask for prices after the population responds. That makes \(\mu\) matter, but it adds a model of trader behavior and asks about aggregate response or equilibrium. The paper’s theorems concern pricing and state updates for requests supplied to the market maker; they do not analyze how a society generates those requests. If demands are fixed in advance, they collapse to share amounts. If they depend on the market state, the resulting problem is a new extension whose difficulty depends on the added demand model.

This is a negative case about the value of these particular mirrors, not a proof that no interesting population model for prediction markets exists. Large trader populations are plausible, and the paper’s data structures could support them. The case against is that Corollaries 3.4 and 3.9 provide efficient and lower-bound results for real-valued market operations, while a worthwhile continuous-social-choice question needs the population itself to be part of the computational object.
