The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is the paper’s bicameral legislature example. A continuous population of legislators can be grouped by party and chamber: each type is \(t=(p,h)\), where \(p\) is a party and \(h\in\{\ell,u\}\). Give each chamber’s types rational population shares \(r_{p,h}\), summing to one within that chamber, and let \(q_h\) be its approval quota. A coalition of parties \(S\) then has chamber share \(x_h(S)=\sum_{p\in S}r_{p,h}\), and its ordinal value is 2 if it passes both chambers, 1 if it passes only the lower chamber, and 0 otherwise. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many legislators share a party and chamber type, while the number of types is at most twice the number of parties.

The closest named result is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: CP-majority is the unique social ranking satisfying Strict Desirability, Neutrality, Equality of Coalitions, and Consistency After Tiebreaks. That is a result about an axiomatic characterization, however—not a computational result. Theorem 4.2–4.5 are likewise characterizations; the propositions concern axiom relationships or independence. Section 6 computes rankings for a 17-party legislature, but reports no named complexity result. So the paper contains no qualifying computational anchor under the requested rule.

If the programme wants a concrete research question suggested by that result, it is: given the rational population shares and quotas above, compute the CP-majority relation on the parties induced by the coalition values. For each pair \(i,j\), count
\[ d_{ij}=\bigl|\{S\subseteq P\setminus\{i,j\}:v_\mu(S\cup\{i\})>v_\mu(S\cup\{j\})\}\bigr|, \]
and return the total binary relation with \(iR^\mu_{\mathrm{CP}}j\) exactly when \(d_{ij}\ge d_{ji}\). A solution is the full pairwise relation on \(P\). Authors would likely recognize this as a continuous-weight version of their bicameral application, but it is a proposed algorithmic problem, not a result established by the paper.

My tentative expectation is that the coalition combinatorics remain in the number of parties \(m\): each pair’s comparison still ranges over \(2^{m-2}\) coalitions, and replacing seat counts with rational shares does not remove those coalitions. That makes this a plausible hardness-transfer question, conditional on hardness for the corresponding finite computation; the paper establishes neither hardness nor tractability. The soft spot is that the continuous mass describes legislators, while the objects being ranked and combined into coalitions remain parties. If the number of parties is small, the population continuum may add little beyond normalizing the vote weights.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 characterizes CP-majority axiomatically; Theorems 4.2–4.5 do the same for the other rankings. The remaining formal results concern axiom relationships and independence. Section 6 computes rankings for one legislature, but proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Since the paper’s actual contribution is axiomatic, continuizing those theorems would continue an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The proposed CP-majority computation is the best candidate for a new computational question, but it does not repair that gap. With the coalitional ranking given explicitly, one can scan the \(2^m\) coalitions for each pair of parties. With a compact bicameral threshold encoding, the work instead lies in evaluating coalition counts from weights and quotas. That may be an interesting problem, but it is a problem about the encoding of party coalitions; the paper establishes no computational result for it, and replacing integer seat counts with shares does not itself supply one.

The model also puts the population axis in an awkward place. In the paper’s example, the ranked elements are parties, and the coalitions are subsets of parties. Legislators are aggregated into each party’s chamber weights; they are not the elements being ranked. Keeping parties as the ranked elements leaves a finite coalition problem whose weights have been normalized. Ranking legislators instead gives the social ranking a different subject. One could group many legislators by chamber and party type, but then each individual has vanishing mass in the limit. For threshold games, swapping one individual’s contribution changes a coalition’s value only near a quota boundary. At generic fixed quotas, those boundary events disappear under the usual random-subset interpretation; at knife-edge quotas, the answer can depend on finite-size fluctuations or quota rounding. A type-level block swap can preserve a nonzero effect, but then the objects being ranked are blocks, not the original parties or legislators.

There is a related obstacle for all five solutions. CP-majority counts coalitions equally; lex-cel and the \(L(1)\) solutions count coalition occurrences in equivalence classes, with \(L(1)\) also splitting them by size. For a continuous population, there is no measure over coalitions supplied by the population distribution itself. One could add a sampling law over coalition mass profiles, but that is a consequential extra modelling choice, and different choices can yield different rankings. Theorems characterizing the original rankings do not tell us which continuous version to use.

So the proponent has identified a sensible high-multiplicity regime for legislators and a plausible algorithmic question about threshold games. That is the negative case’s weak point: such a question might be worth studying in its own right. But as a mirror of this paper’s named results, it requires choosing a new computational input, a new population interpretation, and often a new coalition measure. The paper offers no computational anchor that warrants those choices under the programme’s scope.
