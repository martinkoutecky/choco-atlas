The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(W\) of positive integer voting weights, rational masses \((\mu_w)_{w\in W}\) summing to one, and an offset \(z\), compute the limiting per-member swing coefficients \(\beta_w=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{N}\,Bz_{w,N}\) and type shares \(P_w=\mu_w\beta_w/\sum_{v\in W}\mu_v\beta_v\). Here \(N\mu_w\) is integral, and the quotas \(q_N\) lie on the lattice generated by \(W\) and satisfy \(q_N=N\bar w/2+z\sqrt N+O(1)\), where \(\bar w=\sum_w\mu_w w\).

The model it lives in

A weighted-majority society has types \(w\in W\) with masses \(\mu_w\); finite populations sample \(N\mu_w\) members of each type. Under the stated quota scaling, the outputs are each type’s first-order per-member Banzhaf swing coefficient and its share of aggregate Banzhaf power.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover the large-population type-level limits of the Banzhaf and Shapley–Shubik indices; they leave aside exact indices for finite games, arbitrary quota regimes, and the paper’s generating-function algorithms.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove convergence and the stated limit uniformly over admissible quota rounding on the weight lattice.
  • Determine how the type-level limits change for off-center quotas, growing support, or finite-size corrections.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible mirror is a large-population limit of type-level voting power. Take a large union or membership organization with many voters but only a few formal vote-weight classes. A type is its voting weight \(w\); mass \(\mu_w\) is the fraction of members in that class. The type is complete for the weighted-majority game because the game uses only voting weights. Here \(\tau=|W|\) stays small while the number of members \(N\) grows.

The paper’s strongest eligible anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for a weighted majority game with \(n\) players and quota \(q\), it computes every player’s Banzhaf index in \(O(n+q\log q)\) time. Each index is the probability that the player is pivotal when every coalition is equally likely. The paper also gives Theorem 3, proved here: it computes every player’s Shapley–Shubik index in \(O(n+\tilde n q\log q)\) time, where \(\tilde n=\min\{n,q\}\); this index is the probability that the player is pivotal in a uniformly random ordering. These are the numbered computational results I would anchor on. The introduction mentions #P-completeness results from other papers, but this paper has no numbered hardness theorem to use as an anchor.

My lead mirror is Continuum Banzhaf Criticality. An instance consists of a fixed finite set of positive integer weights \(W\), rational masses \((\mu_w)_{w\in W}\) summing to one, and a quota offset \(z\). Write \(\bar w=\sum_w\mu_w w\) and \(\sigma^2=\frac14\sum_w\mu_w w^2\). Consider the finite games with \(N\mu_w\) voters of weight \(w\), along population sizes for which those counts are integral, and with quota \(q_N\) aligned to the weight lattice and within bounded rounding error of \(N\bar w/2+z\sqrt N\). The task is to return, for every type \(w\), its first-order per-member swing coefficient
\[ \beta_w=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt N\,Bz_{w,N}, \]
and its share of aggregate Banzhaf power
\[ P_w=\frac{\mu_w\beta_w}{\sum_v\mu_v\beta_v}. \]
Under the fixed-support local central-limit regime, the expected answer is \(\beta_w=w\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-z^2/(2\sigma^2)}\), hence \(P_w=\mu_w w/\bar w\). This is a continuous-population question: the input is the distribution of member types, and the output is how pivotal influence is distributed across those types. No action is optimized; the computational task is to calculate the influence profile.

A second, related mirror is Continuum Shapley–Shubik Pivotal-Mass Computation. Its instance is \(W,\mu\), and an interior quota fraction \(\rho\in(0,\bar w)\). It asks for the limiting aggregate Shapley–Shubik share of each type, obtained from the games with \(N\mu_w\) voters and quotas \(q_N/N\to\rho\):
\[ P_w=\lim_{N\to\infty}N\mu_w\,SS_{w,N}. \]
For fixed bounded weights and support, the expected limit is again \(P_w=\mu_w w/\bar w\). The random-order interpretation makes this an analogue of Theorem 3’s underlying power question, rather than a question about the paper’s generating-function encoding. Both mirrors are Class A under these stated regimes: their limiting profiles have direct formulas.

These limits are plausible for the authors to recognize as large-population versions of their two power-index problems: each finite member’s index is aggregated by type, and the population is the object being continued. The scenario is credible when many members share a small number of formal voting weights. It is also narrow. The Banzhaf mirror requires a balanced quota regime; the Shapley–Shubik mirror requires an interior quota fraction. Neither claims to compute exact indices in an arbitrary finite game.

That restriction is the weakest point. In an atomless population, there is no canonical uniform random subset or uniform random permutation of individual voters. The mirrors therefore define their outputs through limits of the paper’s finite models. Moreover, the normalized type shares collapse to normalized weight mass, so the continuous answers are much simpler than the exact finite indices. The first-order Banzhaf coefficients retain some detail through the quota offset and variance, but this still does not preserve the full finite computational problem. The paper’s cited discrete hardness results do not automatically transfer to these limits; questions about off-center quotas, finite-size corrections, growing support, and when exact hardness survives would need separate treatment.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that these indices are defined by *individual pivotal events*, and an atomless population has no individual who can change a coalition’s outcome. But that case does not defeat both anchors.

Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are explicit computational results, so “there is no named result to mirror” is unavailable. Nor is there a convincing multiplicity objection: a large membership organization with a few voting-weight classes is a plausible regime, and weight is a complete type for this game. The paper’s existing high-multiplicity neighbors support that regime rather than count against it.

For Theorem 1, an individual’s Banzhaf probability tends to zero as the population grows. In the atomless model, it is zero. The proponent’s \(\sqrt N\) rescaling and quota window \(N\bar w/2+z\sqrt N\) recover a nonzero limit, but they turn the task into computing critical-window sensitivity from Gaussian fluctuations. A more direct atomless definition has the same difficulty: the probability that one infinitesimal voter is pivotal is zero, while a derivative of aggregate winning probability depends on how random coalitions are defined. Still, the finite-population limit is a clear, defensible choice of definition. I cannot claim it is not a worthwhile mirror.

For Theorem 3, a continuous ordering accumulates voting weight smoothly and crosses the quota without a pivotal voter. Type-specific pivotal shares therefore require retaining information about the microscopic jump at the crossing. That is a real mismatch with an atomless ordering. But again, the proposed finite-population limit supplies a coherent way to retain that information: it asks which type marks the pivotal jump as the number of members grows. It is an asymptotic version of the index, not the exact finite index, yet it remains a recognizable computational question.

The strongest case against both is thus that the natural continuum object does not directly define either index. The exact problems depend on coalition subsets or permutations of *individuals*; the proposed limits add scaling and quota regimes to extract type-level quantities after individual pivotality disappears. Keeping exact finite indices requires \(N\) and its integer multiplicities, while discarding them requires choosing an asymptotic statistic. That is a genuine boundary to the mirror, but the programme explicitly allows co-modifications forced by population continuization. It does not rule out the limit questions.

I therefore cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case stick. The mirrors are narrow and depend on a chosen limiting regime, but the paper offers two numbered computational anchors and a plausible high-multiplicity setting. On the material given, at least one—and likely both—survive as worthwhile continuous questions.
