No computational result is covered; Propositions 3, 6, and 7 are axiomatic results, as are the paper’s other stated characterizations and preservation claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that taxation has a natural high-multiplicity regime: many taxpayers can share a small number of income types. One could represent the population by masses \(\mu_y\) on income levels \(y\), with a tax amount \(x_y\) per taxpayer of each type and a per-capita revenue constraint \(\sum_y \mu_y x_y=r\), where \(0\le x_y\le y\). That makes a continuous population sensible for this subject.

But the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its numbered results are axiomatic characterizations and preservation claims. For example, Proposition 3 relates progressivity, income-order preservation, inequality reduction, and consistency; Proposition 6 says the minimal-burden operator preserves progressivity and inequality reduction but not merging-proofness; Proposition 7 adds a no-donation-paradox condition under which it preserves merging-proofness. None classifies an underlying computational problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise.

Nor does the paper’s “continuity” axiom mean a continuum of taxpayers: it concerns how a rule responds to changes in incomes or revenue while the population is fixed. Extending its axioms to a mass distribution could motivate new work, but it would not mirror a named computational result here. In particular, the pairwise merging condition needs care: merging two individual taxpayers is not directly represented by changing a measure when each individual has zero mass.

So the positive case reaches a plausible population regime, but not the required computational anchor. There is no qualifying anchor, no lead mirror problem to hand to a prover, and no honest tractability or hardness prediction tied to this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Propositions 3, 6, and 7 establish relations between axioms and preservation properties of operators. They do not define an algorithmic problem with an input, an output or decision, and a complexity question. That gap cannot be repaired by choosing a better population model.

The proposed population model is sensible: let \(\mu\) describe masses of taxpayers by income type, and let a per-capita tax schedule satisfy an aggregate revenue constraint. This could support worthwhile continuous-taxation research. But lifting the paper’s propositions to that model would still produce axiomatic results. For Proposition 3, one could express inequality reduction through a Lorenz curve for the income distribution; checking it on finite support may be straightforward. Neither turns the proposition into a computational task the paper posed.

Proposition 6’s minimal-burden operator is an especially weak anchor. Its definition asks whether one taxpayer’s income is needed to cover revenue after everyone else pays in full. In an atomless population, removing one taxpayer changes aggregate income by zero, so this burden is zero in ordinary interior cases. Treating a whole income type as one taxpayer could preserve a nonzero burden, but changes the tax unit and the operator’s meaning.

Proposition 7 has a related limitation: merging and donation compare the payments of particular taxpayers across problems with different populations. A mass model can define a new group-level or infinitesimal version, but the paper does not supply a computational objective or algorithmic question for it. Even if the authors accepted that reformulation, the result would concern axiom preservation.

So the positive case establishes that a continuous population is plausible for taxation; it does not establish a worthwhile *computational mirror of this paper*. A new optimization problem could be added—for example, choosing a rule to minimize a specified welfare loss—but that objective would come from outside the paper. The negative case is strongest on ChoCo’s stated remit; it does not imply that continuous taxation research itself would be uninteresting.
