The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many lane types \(t\), each with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), unit processing cost \(c_t\), and match value \(z_t\), with equal proposer and receiver mass in each lane and no acceptable cross-lane pairs, choose processed mass \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t c_t x_t\le B\). Does there exist such a choice with \(\sum_t z_t x_t\ge R\)? Each unit of processed mass yields an integral one-to-one match.

The model it lives in

A society has repeated, interchangeable bilateral lane types \(t\) with mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variable \(x_t\) is the receiver mass processed in lane \(t\); processing costs \(c_t x_t\) and yields match value \(z_t x_t\). The platform asks whether it can reach value \(R\) within budget \(B\), or equivalently maximizes \(\sum_t z_t x_t\) subject to the mass and budget constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 7’s isolated bilateral-pair restriction and the budgeted mass-allocation decision; it leaves the general interacting recommendation problem, LA-DA results, stability frontier, and empirical results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does tractability extend to interacting types where receiver competition, display capacities, and rejection chains make the final mass matching depend on the recommendation path?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 7, proved in the paper. It says that Budgeted Recommendation is NP-complete: given costs for processing receiver columns, match values \(z_{mw}\), a budget \(B\), and a target \(R\), decide whether there is a no-duplicate recommendation plan whose total cost is at most \(B\) and whose settled matching has value at least \(R\). Its restriction to empty initial consideration, unit display capacity, and isolated mutually acceptable pairs is weakly NP-complete. The paper proves this by reducing zero-one Knapsack to those isolated pairs: processing a receiver column selects its item, incurs its weight, and yields its value.

I would mirror the isolated-pair restriction, which is the strongest part of the theorem for this purpose. Consider a large placement platform serving many repeated, independently operating bilateral lanes. For example, licensing and location constraints might leave each candidate cohort and placement lane with just one mutually acceptable counterpart type. Within a lane, agents share the preferences, processing cost, and match value relevant to the allocation. There are \(N\) opportunities but only \(\tau\) lane types, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\). A unit of mass still represents an integral candidate–placement match; fractional quantities describe how much of a homogeneous cohort is served.

Call the continuous problem Mass Budgeted Recommendation. Its instance is a list of types \(t\), each with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), processing cost \(c_t\) per unit of receiver mass, and value \(z_t\) per resulting match, along with a total budget \(B\) and target value \(R\). For each type, the proposer and receiver masses are both \(\mu_t\), and that lane has no acceptable cross-type matches. Choose how much receiver mass \(x_t\) to process. The question is whether there is a vector satisfying

\[ 0\leq x_t\leq \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t c_t x_t\leq B,\qquad \sum_t z_t x_t\geq R. \]

A feasible vector is the solution. Processing mass \(x_t\) creates \(x_t\) matches in lane \(t\); no individual receives a fractional match. The equivalent optimization version maximizes \(\sum_t z_t x_t\) subject to the mass and budget constraints.

This is an LP with \(\tau\) variables, so it is solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A): the finite hardness comes from choosing indivisible columns, while the mass version lets the platform serve a fraction of each repeated type. That is a direct continuization of the theorem’s decision variable and objective. Processing remains a platform action, heterogeneous costs and values remain attributes of types, and matching remains one-to-one at the individual level.

The discrete-to-continuous bridge is also concrete. If a finite market has \(n_t\) opportunities of type \(t\), then its normalized mass is \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and its discrete choices correspond to requiring \(x_t\) to be a multiple of \(1/n\). Rounding each continuous choice down to an integer number of opportunities preserves the budget and loses at most \(\sum_t z_t/n\) in normalized value. Thus, with bounded values and moderate \(\tau\), the LP gives an additive approximation to the discrete problem. A fuller account would compare the integer and mass formulations for more general matching components.

The main further question is how far this tractability extends when lanes interact. If receiver types can compete for proposer mass, or a recommendation can trigger rejection chains across types, does an aggregate matching formulation remain an LP, or does its complexity depend on a pricing problem over possible match patterns? That would test whether the gain survives beyond the theorem’s isolated-pair boundary.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 7’s hardness already reduces to Knapsack and needs no matching interaction. The continuous mirror therefore demonstrates that population multiplicity can create hardness in recommendation budgeting, but it does not continuize the paper’s central stability or deferred-acceptance machinery. Its regime is credible only where many genuinely interchangeable opportunities recur across a small number of lanes; idiosyncratic compatibility that makes each pair a distinct type would weaken the high-multiplicity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is the only computational anchor the proponent names, and it is a real one. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror smooths the Knapsack reduction while removing almost all of the matching problem.

In the isolated-pair restriction, an opportunity’s relevant type includes its preferences, cost, and match value. Repeating that type many times and charging per processed unit gives exactly
\[ \max \sum_t z_t x_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t c_t x_t\le B,\qquad 0\le x_t\le \mu_t. \]
That is fractional Knapsack: a standard linear optimization problem whose solution follows by ordering types by value per unit cost. It has no competition for partners, rejection chains, stability constraint, or deferred-acceptance behavior. The claimed rounding bound is the usual loss from rounding fractional quantities down. So the result would show that allowing fractional service dissolves item indivisibility; it would say little about the paper’s matching contribution.

Restoring interaction requires more than replacing counts with masses. In the paper, processing a receiver column changes which pairs are displayed and can change the subsequent DA path. A mass version would need to specify how recommendation mass is distributed across proposer types, how display capacities constrain those flows, and how evolving rejections and retained authorizations determine the final matching. That could define a worthwhile new problem, but the isolated-pair LP does not establish its formulation or complexity. Its simplicity comes precisely from removing those interactions.

There is also a modeling choice hidden in the LP. It assumes costs scale per unit of receiver mass. If the relevant action instead has a fixed cost for opening a type or lane, the action remains indivisible and the Knapsack structure survives. That alternative is not forced by the paper, so it cannot invalidate the per-unit mirror; it does show that the tractability claim belongs to a particular cost model.

The negative case is therefore limited. The repeated bilateral-lane scenario is coherent, and the programme permits the per-capita cost co-modification. I would argue that its mirror is too generic and too far from stable matching to be a strong ChoCo result, but I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: under the programme’s stated standard, this simple Class A result may still count.
