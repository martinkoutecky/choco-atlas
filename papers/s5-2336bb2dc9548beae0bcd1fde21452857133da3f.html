What the mirror covers

The proposal concerns only Proposition 10’s implication from maximum preference weight to a most-preferred trajectory; it leaves the broader preference language and ASP implementation results without a surviving population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: continuous population planning with preferences over one shared public plan. The paper is explicitly single-agent, but this keeps its central object—a goal-achieving trajectory ranked by PP preferences—and lets a population’s distribution over preference types determine which shared plan is selected.

My anchor is Proposition 10, proved in this paper. It says that for a general preference \(\Psi\), any trajectory \(\alpha\) with maximum paper-defined weight \(w_\Psi(\alpha)\) is a most-preferred trajectory under \(\Psi\). The paper uses this result to justify maximizing preference weights when computing a plan. This is an optimization-correctness result, not a runtime bound. The paper gives no named result that classifies the problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise; Proposition 10 is the relevant algorithmic anchor because the paper offers no comparable hardness result.

Call the mirror Continuous PP Public-Plan Selection. An instance consists of a planning domain \((D,I,G)\), a maximum plan length \(n\), and a finite support \(T\) of preference types. All types rank the same feasible set \(\mathcal A_n(D,I,G)\) of valid trajectories of length at most \(n\) that achieve \(G\). Each type \(t\) has a PP general preference \(\Psi_t\) and a binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The mass is the fraction of users with that preference type; repeated users are indistinguishable in everything this planning choice uses.

The decision variable is one actual trajectory \(\alpha\in\mathcal A_n(D,I,G)\). Using the paper’s weight function as the specified common score scale, the objective is
\[ \max_{\alpha\in\mathcal A_n(D,I,G)} \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,w_{\Psi_t}(\alpha). \]
Any maximizing trajectory is a solution. The plan remains an ordinary integral sequence of actions: only the population is continuous. The use of PP weights as an interpersonal scale is part of this mirror’s social aggregation rule; the paper itself ranks plans for one user and does not prescribe how to compare different users’ preferences.

A plausible regime is a municipality choosing a shared sequence of transport-service or emergency-response actions. Many residents may have the same PP preferences over safety, cost, comfort, or timing, while the city has many residents and relatively few distinct preference types: \(N\) is large and \(\tau=|T|\ll N\). This fits the high-multiplicity premise when each type includes everything relevant to evaluating the common plan. It would fit poorly if users had materially different feasible-plan sets that the model failed to represent.

I expect hardness to transfer if the underlying discrete plan-search problem is hard. With \(\tau=1\) and \(\mu_t=1\), the objective reduces to the paper’s most-preferred-trajectory problem, up to a positive constant. Thus population aggregation does not remove the combinatorial search over trajectories. That is a prediction for this mirror, not a complexity theorem proved by the paper. Further questions include whether useful algorithms can depend on the support size \(\tau\) rather than the number of people, and whether any column-generation approach helps when the trajectory set is large; its pricing step would itself need to find a high-scoring feasible plan.

The scope here is only the paper’s preference-based selection of goal-achieving trajectories. It does not mirror the PP language’s full expressive theory or claim a complexity result for the ASP encoding. The weakest point is the shared-plan social objective: the paper assumes one user, so authors might view aggregation across users as an extension rather than their original problem. The case for accepting it is that the feasible trajectories and PP rankings stay intact; the new element is a continuous distribution over the users whose preferences guide a common planning choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 10 is the paper’s closest anchor, but it says something narrower than a computational result about planning: if \(w_\Psi\) is an admissible encoding of a preference order, maximizing it returns a most-preferred trajectory. Theorem 4 and the encoding results nearby establish similar semantic correctness claims. None classifies the complexity of finding such a trajectory, or gives a running-time bound for the ASP method. The paper leaves that cost to the planning domain, horizon, and solver. So there is no complexity result here whose continuous-population counterpart the proposed model would extend.

The proposed shared-plan objective also adds a social choice rule the paper does not supply. PP’s weights preserve each user’s ordering; they do not establish interpersonal comparability. Summing \(w_{\Psi_t}\) therefore treats the construction’s numerical scale as social utility. An equivalent PP preference could have a different weight representation and change the winning plan. An ordinal repair—say, choosing among trajectories by mass-weighted pairwise majority—would avoid that problem, but it adds a new aggregation rule. Proposition 10 does not justify its outcome.

That ordinal repair is the strongest version of the mirror, and it changes the problem’s subject. The paper models one agent, one initial state, one goal, and a trajectory that agent can execute. If people plan separately, their states, goals, and feasible trajectories may differ; population mass then just describes a distribution of individual planning instances. If a municipality instead chooses one public plan for everyone, there is a coherent collective-planning problem, but the paper does not model that shared plan or the society that selects it. Its main computational question would come from the added collective choice, not from continuizing a result in PP.

The negative case is strongest against a *direct* mirror of Proposition 10: the result is order-theoretic correctness, and its population version needs new social semantics. It is weaker against the universal claim. A continuous distribution of stakeholders choosing a shared public plan is plausible, and studying its computational complexity could be worthwhile. I would argue that this is a new collective-planning problem inspired by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its named results; I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would recognize it as close enough.
