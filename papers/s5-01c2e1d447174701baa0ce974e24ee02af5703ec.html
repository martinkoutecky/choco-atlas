What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns maximum-total-reward matching; Theorems 3.4, 4.4, and 5.5 themselves provide only characterizations or existence results, and no computational results are left for a mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a high-multiplicity workforce being reorganized across departments: many workers share a small set of complete types—such as role, contract, and peer-effect payoff—and department capacities scale with the workforce. A continuous model could assign type masses \(x_{tH}\) to departments, with \(\sum_H x_{tH}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tH}=\kappa_H\). If workers’ rewards depend on the type composition \(z_H\) of their department, the objective is aggregate worker and department reward. That preserves the paper’s peer effects and central restructuring question while making type-level mass the decision variable. A concrete computational follow-up would ask for an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal allocation when the mass, capacities, and peer-effect reward functions are rationally encoded.

But this is a proposed follow-up, not a valid anchor under your criteria. The closest result, Theorem 4.4 (proved here), says that a matching maximizes the sum of workers’ and departments’ rewards if and only if it is compensation stable: no alternative matching increases the combined reward of the workers whose department or peers change and the departments whose assigned workers change. Theorem 5.5 (also proved here) says every compensation matching game has a nonempty core. These are structural and existence results; neither classifies a computational problem or gives an algorithm. Theorem 3.4 is likewise a characterization: for strict matching problems, Pareto optimality is equivalent to contractual exchange stability.

So this paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, and I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem *per anchor* or assign one of the programme’s three complexity classes. The positive case is that the paper’s maximum-reward problem has a plausible continuous-population formulation that its authors could recognize. Its weakest point is decisive for this task: the paper does not establish a computational anchor for that formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.4 characterizes Pareto optimality by contractual exchange stability; Theorem 4.4 characterizes maximum total reward by compensation stability; Theorem 5.5 proves that a compensation matching game has a nonempty core. None gives a complexity classification or an algorithm, and the paper does not specify a computational encoding for its reward functions. Theorem 4.4’s connection to maximizing total reward makes it the closest anchor, but asking for an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal mass allocation would add a computational problem to the paper rather than mirror one it studies.

There is a substantial modeling obstacle too. The paper permits arbitrary rewards \(r_i(S,H)\) and \(\pi_H(S)\) over named workers and coworker sets. In that general model, a worker’s complete type includes their rewards for every relevant group, so workers will usually have distinct types and the support will grow with the population. A type-composition allocation \(x_{tH}\) cannot preserve those rewards without extra assumptions. Making rewards depend only on a small number of role types and their department composition is plausible, but it imposes symmetry that is not present in the paper. The resulting optimization problem could be useful; it would also be a new, narrower problem.

The same distinction applies to each theorem. A mass version of Theorem 3.4 would be a structural characterization, not a computational result. A mass version of Theorem 4.4 could motivate an optimization problem, but that problem’s input and payoff structure must be supplied. A mass version of Theorem 5.5 would need its own definition of coalition values and blocking by measurable groups; the finite-player proof does not settle those choices. None of these objections rules out a worthwhile follow-up, but each separates that follow-up from a computational result established in this paper.

The strongest positive scenario survives those modeling objections: a large workforce with a few shared role and contract types, scaled department capacities, and peer rewards depending on type composition. That is a credible high-multiplicity model, and I cannot honestly argue that its maximum-reward problem is intrinsically uninteresting. So the negative case is strong only in the narrower sense that this paper supplies no computational anchor for the proposed mirror. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
