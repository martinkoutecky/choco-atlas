The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the school-zoning problem, rather than of GeoKmeans’s particular iterative algorithm. There is a serious limitation up front: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. It does say in the background that regionalization is NP-hard, citing Megiddo and Supowit (1984), but that is an unnumbered general claim, not a result proved for the paper’s school-redistricting model. The experiments in Table 2 are empirical comparisons, not formal complexity anchors. So there is no eligible named anchor under the requested rule.

A plausible lead question is Continuous-Mass School-Zoning. Take a district partitioned into \(\tau\) fixed SPA polygons, with their rook-adjacency graph. For one school level, each type \(t\) is an SPA together with its geometry, adjacency, and school-level data. Its rational mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of the district’s pupils; normalize the masses to sum to \(1\). Each school \(k\) has a fixed location, a seed SPA, and a capacity \(q_k\) expressed in the same normalized units.

The task is to assign every SPA type to one school, keeping each school’s seed and requiring each school’s assigned SPAs to form a connected region. Among such assignments \(a\), minimize a weighted combination of the paper’s three criteria:

\[ J(a)=\lambda_b B(a)+\lambda_p P(a)+\lambda_c C(a), \]

where

\[ B(a)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \left|1-\frac{\sum_{t:a(t)=k}\mu_t}{q_k}\right|, \qquad P(a)=\sum_t\mu_t\,d(\operatorname{cent}(P_t),s_{a(t)}), \]

and

\[ C(a)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \left(1-\frac{4\pi\,\operatorname{area}(U_k)} {\operatorname{perimeter}(U_k)^2}\right). \]

Here \(P_t\) is the polygon for SPA \(t\), \(s_k\) is school \(k\)’s location, and \(U_k\) is the union of SPAs assigned to it. The weights are fixed nonnegative inputs. This keeps the article’s fixed geometries and whole-SPA assignments, while treating pupil counts as population masses. The scenario is a growing school district with many thousands of pupils distributed over hundreds or a few thousand SPAs; pupils in the same SPA and school-level category are indistinguishable under the paper’s location and capacity model. It is a credible high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every school-planning setting has one.

The authors would likely recognize the underlying zoning question: connected school regions, capacity balance, and proximity. The fit is less exact for the algorithm. GeoKmeans uses feasibility checks and adaptive weighting; it does not optimize this particular weighted sum. The question therefore concerns the redistricting problem GeoKmeans addresses, not a continuous restatement of its procedure.

My expectation is hardness transfers if the corresponding fixed-SPA discrete optimization problem is hard: integer pupil counts \(n_t\) give masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the cell assignments and connectivity constraints remain. But the paper’s generic NP-hardness sentence does not prove hardness for this precise objective, so that expectation needs an independent reduction. The next questions are whether balance and proximity admit efficient approximation when \(\tau\) is moderate, how topology changes the tractability of the mass formulation, and whether allowing SPA fragmentation creates a distinct, tractable relaxation.

The weakest point is also the decisive one: there is no qualifying named result to anchor this proposal, and the paper does not specify a single global objective combining its reported metrics. This makes the mirror a plausible research question arising from the paper, but not a positive case grounded in one of its formal computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. The paper reports an empirical heuristic and cites a general NP-hardness claim about regionalization, but proves no complexity bound for GeoKmeans or for a specified school-zoning objective. That leaves no formal anchor for the proposed continuous question; it would be a new problem inspired by the paper.

The proposed Continuous-Mass School-Zoning also leaves the central decision discrete. Each SPA, with its distinctive geometry and place in the adjacency graph, must be assigned whole to one school. Thus the support consists of the spatial units themselves, and the combinatorial search is over their assignments. Pupil masses weight the balance and proximity criteria, but do not make those assignments fractional. This is a sensible high-multiplicity representation of the data, yet the continuous population does not remove or replace the graph-partition problem that governs the zoning.

The stronger mirror would let a school boundary cut through an SPA, assigning fractions of its pupil mass to different schools. That fits the paper’s suggestion that fragmenting polygons might improve plans. But it also requires specifying how cuts affect land geometry, contiguity, compactness, and the input representation of population density. With a finite mesh, the problem returns to a graph partition over spatial units; with a continuous domain, the computational question is a new geometric-partitioning problem, not a continuous version of a result this paper establishes.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit: the paper offers no named computational finding, and its proposed mirror either retains the same discrete spatial decisions or changes the model substantially. It is not airtight. The authors’ interest in polygon fragmentation makes a continuous-density zoning problem plausible and potentially worthwhile on its own. What the paper does not give us is a result whose continuous mirror would directly advance the continuization programme.
