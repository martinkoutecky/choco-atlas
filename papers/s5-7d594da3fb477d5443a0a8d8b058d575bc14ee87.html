The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 classifies equilibria of a bilateral game. The paper’s other central findings concern AME and mean-field accuracy, phase transitions, and symmetry breaking. None states the complexity of a computational problem. So the case for offers no named computational result to mirror.

The strongest follow-on would ask for the complexity of computing an AME steady state from rational inputs. That could be a worthwhile new question, but the paper does not specify a computational task with an input, output, and accuracy requirement, or establish an algorithmic result. For fixed maximum degree, the AME is already a finite system of differential equations, with \(4\sum_{k=0}^{k_{\max}}\binom{k+3}{3}\) state variables. Studying the complexity of solving it would add a computational layer to the paper’s existing continuous model; it would not mirror a result in the paper.

The population type choice also exposes a tension. Types based on degree and current strategy omit much of the neighborhood structure that matters to realized outcomes. The paper’s clearest example is substitutable activities: individual network runs often end with either A or B dominating, while the AME captures their symmetric average. A richer type that preserves seed placement and neighborhood structure may recover that variation, but risks making agents’ network positions part of their types, with no useful high multiplicity. Asking for ensemble averages avoids that problem, but the AME already supplies an analytic treatment of those aggregates.

This is a strong case against a *mirror* of this paper, rather than proof that no related computational project could be worthwhile. The AME complexity question might be worth pursuing on its own; it is simply a new question, with its value not established by the paper’s results.
