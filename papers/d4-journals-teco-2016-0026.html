The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) customer types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational complex demands \(d_t\), nonnegative rational values \(v_t\), and rational per-capita capacity \(C\), choose allocated masses \(0\leq\lambda_t\leq\mu_t\) to maximize \(\sum_t\lambda_t v_t\), subject to \(\left|\sum_t\lambda_t d_t\right|\leq C\). For instances whose demands lie in the angle regimes of Theorem 4.1, determine whether a feasible \((1-\varepsilon)\)-approximation can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\), and whether either inapproximability barrier persists.

The model it lives in

A distribution over exact customer types, each specified by its per-customer complex demand and value; \(\lambda_t\) is the mass receiving that type's full demand. Normalize a discrete instance's total capacity \(C_N\) to per-capita capacity \(C=C_N/N\); maximize \(\sum_t\lambda_t v_t\) subject to \(0\leq\lambda_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\left\|\left(\sum_t\lambda_t\operatorname{Re}(d_t),\sum_t\lambda_t\operatorname{Im}(d_t)\right)\right\|_2\leq C\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1's inapproximability question for the specified demand-angle regimes; it leaves the paper's truthful mechanism results and approximation algorithms unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a bit-complexity analysis for multiplicative approximation of this mass-allocation problem across the angle regimes in Theorem 4.1.
  • Quantify how solutions round to finite cohorts when each type's allocated mass must be an integer multiple of \(1/N\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is for Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper by a reduction from SUBSUM. The theorem says that, unless \(P=NP\), (i) for any \(\delta>0\), CKP\([\pi/2+\delta,\pi]\) has no \((\alpha,1)\)-approximation when \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\) have polynomial-length descriptions; and (ii) CKP\([\pi-\delta',\pi]\) has no \((\alpha,\beta)\)-approximation when \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) have polynomial-length descriptions and \(\delta'\) is exponentially small in the number \(n\) of users. Here an \((\alpha,\beta)\)-approximation must achieve value at least \(\alpha\) times optimum while using capacity at most \(\beta C\).

I would call the mirror Mass Complex-Demand Knapsack. Its input is a finite support of \(\tau\) types, each with a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), a rational complex demand \(d_t\), and a nonnegative rational value \(v_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); it also has a rational per-capita capacity \(C\). The question is to choose allocated masses \(\lambda_t\) to maximize
\[ \sum_t \lambda_t v_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\leq\lambda_t\leq\mu_t,\qquad \left|\sum_t\lambda_t d_t\right|\leq C. \]
The \(\lambda_t\) are the solution: each is the mass of type \(t\) receiving its full per-customer demand. Thus the model does not give each customer a fractional power bundle; it allows a fraction of a large cohort to receive the whole bundle. For an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate solution, require feasibility and value at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon\sum_t\mu_t v_t\).

A plausible regime is a large demand-response programme with millions of customers in standardized load-profile and contract-value cohorts. A type is the exact per-customer complex load and value; the number \(N\) of customers can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct types. If the discrete capacity is \(C_N\), the continuous version uses per-capita capacity \(C_N/N\). Grouping \(n_t\) identical customers gives \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); the discrete allocation choices for that type lie on a count grid, and the continuous mirror replaces that grid with the interval \([0,\mu_t]\). This is the high-multiplicity relaxation.

The expected answer is tractability, including in the angle regimes of Theorem 4.1. With variables \(\lambda_t\), the constraint is
\[ \left\|\left(\sum_t\lambda_t\operatorname{Re}(d_t),\, \sum_t\lambda_t\operatorname{Im}(d_t)\right)\right\|_2\leq C, \]
so the problem is a second-order cone optimization problem with rational input and \(\tau\) allocation variables. Standard SOCP methods give an arbitrarily accurate optimizer in time polynomial in the support size, input bit length, and requested precision. The angle restrictions do not break that convex formulation. The discrete hardness exploits all-or-nothing subset selection; allowing a cohort’s mass to split removes that particular combinatorial barrier.

I think the authors would recognize this as a continuous-population version of their apparent-power allocation problem: the same per-customer load, value objective, and aggregate capacity constraint remain. The paper has complex-valued demands, but its society is still a finite set of users with binary allocation decisions; that is not already a continuous-population model. This case covers Theorem 4.1 only. It does not claim a continuous mirror of the paper’s truthful mechanism results.

The weakest point is that the SUBSUM instances proving Theorem 4.1 can have many distinct demands, so they need not inhabit the high-multiplicity regime where \(\tau\ll N\). The mirror therefore does not carry over that hardness; it asks what happens to the same allocation question when large cohorts of genuinely identical users can be divided by mass. That is a real change in the feasible allocations, and the proposed smart-grid regime is what makes it plausible rather than a purely mathematical relaxation. It also raises follow-up questions: how truthful mechanisms behave when a type’s mass is split, how the mirror extends to multi-minded load menus, and how well its allocations round back to a finite cohort.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only substantial negative line against Theorem 4.1 is that its SUBSUM reduction is built from individually chosen demands \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) and one cancellation demand. Those instances need not have repeated types, so the hardness witness itself may sit outside a high-multiplicity regime. On that narrow reading, Mass CKP changes the instance family that carries the theorem’s hardness.

But that does not defeat the better mirror. Large demand-response programmes can have cohorts with the same load profile and contract value; \(\lambda_t\) then means that a fraction of each cohort receives its full demand. With per-capita capacity, this is a coherent continuous population model. Its second-order cone formulation is also the tractability question the programme is meant to expose: a changed answer is not a defect, and an easy answer is not by itself a reason to reject a well-posed mirror.

I therefore cannot make a strong case against this anchor. The reduction’s lack of repeated types limits what it says about high-multiplicity hardness, but it does not show that the continuous population problem is implausible or unworthy of study. Theorem 4.1’s mirror survives.
