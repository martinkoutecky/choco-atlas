What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the programme’s anchor requirement: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 7 and Theorem 8 give structural characterizations of roughly weighted disjunctive and conjunctive hierarchical games. They do not assert that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Although their criteria might support an efficient recognition algorithm, that would be a consequence to establish, not a result the paper names.

The setting does suggest a population mirror: let the types be hierarchical levels, let \(\mu_i\) be each level’s population mass, and describe a coalition by how much mass \(x_i\in[0,\mu_i]\) it contains. One could ask when the resulting fractional threshold access structure has a rough weighted representation. But that is a possible structural question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The paper’s characterization theorems therefore do not support the requested computational case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorems 7 and 8 characterize which hierarchical games are roughly weighted; Theorems 3 and 4 characterize weightedness. None states the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper studies no algorithmic task such as manipulation, control, or optimization.

The strongest reconstruction is coherent: make hierarchy levels the types, give them masses \(\mu_i\), represent a coalition by \(x\in[0,\mu]\), and ask whether one vector of weights separates winning from losing mass coalitions. But that is a new continuous representability classification. Turning it into a decision or search problem would add a computational task the paper never studied, so it would not mirror one of its results.

There is also a limit problem with that reconstruction. Most of Theorem 7’s exceptional roughly weighted cases depend on fixed integer gaps or small class sizes—such as gaps of one or two, or a level of size two or four. When the population grows with positive mass in each level, those features vanish after normalization: the gaps become zero mass differences, and the small levels have zero limiting mass. In the family \(n_3=k_3-k\), for example, the three thresholds converge to \(\kappa,\kappa,\kappa+\mu_3\); the third condition adds no separate population-scale boundary because \(x_3\le\mu_3\). The conjunctive cases inherit the same issue by duality. The \(k_1=1\) family leaves a zero-mass passer, which would require a separately chosen boundary convention such as \(x_1>0\).

I would not claim that no one could find a worthwhile study of continuous rough-weighted access structures. The paper’s repeated player classes make high multiplicity a sensible regime, which supports trying one. The stronger, defensible negative is narrower: the proponent has not identified a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper, and the most natural mass limit sheds much of the finite-player structure its characterizations explain.
