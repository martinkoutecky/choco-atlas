What the mirror covers

Theorem 3.1 and Propositions 6.1 and 6.5 give stochastic waiting-time bounds or limits, not computational results. The proposed type-level mean-field question replaces the paper’s finite-network stochastic process with deterministic class dynamics.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a ChoCo mirror on. Its named results concern the speed of a stochastic process, not the running time or complexity of solving a computational problem.

For example, Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, bounds the expected time \(T_q\) to reach adoption mass \(q<p\) by \(7.2/(p\alpha)\bigl(1+\ln(p/(p-q))\bigr)\), uniformly over dynamic networks. Proposition 6.1, also proved here, gives the limiting threshold time \(T_q\to \alpha^{-1}\ln(p/(p-q))\) for linear response on large regular networks. Proposition 6.5 proves an exponential lower bound in the network degree for regular networks when \(r(q)<q\). These are substantive named results, but none classifies the complexity of computing an answer.

A plausible nearby continuous-population question would take \(\mu\) over finitely many agent classes with shared payoffs and response function, and give a row-stochastic interaction matrix \(K\). If \(x_t(s)\) is the adoption fraction in class \(t\), the mean-field model is
\[ \dot{x}_t(s)=r\!\left(\sum_{t'}K_{tt'}x_{t'}(s)\right)-x_t(s), \qquad x_t(0)=0. \]
Given \(\mu,K,r\), and a target \(q\), one could ask for the first time \(H_q\) when \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t(s)\ge q\), for example by computing an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation. A large platform or patient population divided into a few interaction and behavior classes is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The authors might recognize this as a continuous counterpart: the paper itself uses a mean-field approach for Proposition 6.1.

But that is an analytic mean-field question, not a computational-complexity mirror anchored in this paper’s results. The main theorem concerns finite networks of arbitrary topology, and replacing those networks by finitely many classes also loses structural detail. That is the positive case’s weakest point, and it is decisive under the requested anchor criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper studies a stochastic process, not a computational problem. Its named results bound or characterize how long adoption takes; they do not ask for an algorithm to find a solution to an instance. That matters here because a continuous population can be a natural description of the agents without producing the kind of computational question ChoCo is meant to chart.

Theorem 3.1 is the best anchor for a mirror: given classes of agents, their interaction matrix, a response function, and a target adoption mass \(q\), ask for the first-passage time or an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to it. That is a coherent model, and large populations with repeated agent classes are plausible. But it is a new algorithmic wrapper around a dynamical question. The theorem itself gives an explicit, network-independent bound, while the paper’s broader setting permits arbitrary response functions and changing networks without specifying a finite encoding for them. To make complexity meaningful, one must choose how those functions and dynamics are represented and restricted. A finite-class ODE with an explicit response function could support such a study, but it would be a newly specified numerical reachability problem, not a computational result mirrored from Theorem 3.1.

Proposition 6.1 is less promising as a computational anchor. It already takes a large-population limit for regular networks and gives the limiting time \( \alpha^{-1}\ln\!\bigl(p/(p-q)\bigr) \). In the continuous model suggested by the proponent, that is precisely the simple scalar mean-field trajectory. Its computation is immediate. Generalizing to multiple classes and nonlinear responses may create harder questions, but then the extra complexity comes from the enlarged model, not from the proposition’s result.

Proposition 6.5 points to a real limitation of a naive continuum mirror. Its exponentially long waiting time is a finite-population rare-event phenomenon: when \(r(q)<q\), the deterministic mass trajectory need not reach \(q\) at all. The fluid limit therefore erases the finite waiting-time quantity the proposition bounds. A better version could retain stochastic fluctuations and study a large-deviation exponent, but that is an asymptotic analysis of rare events, not a complexity classification for a social-choice computation.

The proponent is right about the main weakness in this negative case: there *is* a sensible high-multiplicity regime here. Adoption fractions are central to the paper, and its regular-network limit is an explicit population limit. Nor does network position rule out types in every instance: block-structured or regular populations can have repeated positions. The stronger objection is narrower. In the arbitrary-network results, a type must preserve the interactions that affect an agent’s updates; in general that can leave as many types as agents. Restricting to a few exchangeable classes makes the continuum tractable, but shifts the focus away from the paper’s arbitrary and evolving networks.

So I cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous model exists in any scenario. A finite-type hitting-time problem could be worth studying on its own. The negative case is that it is not a strong ChoCo mirror of these results: the anchors are analytic waiting-time claims, the most natural continuum treatment is mean-field dynamics, and turning that into a computational-complexity programme requires adding a problem and an input model the paper does not supply.
