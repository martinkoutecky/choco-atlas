The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant anchored case under the requested standard: the paper has no numbered result classifying the complexity of an underlying problem. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3, Corollary 1, and Theorem 4 establish strategic properties of mechanisms—whether they are NOM or very obviously manipulable. They give no hardness, tractability, or parameterized-complexity result to mirror.

The closest seed is Theorem 4, proved here. It states that with substitutable preferences and contracts, HDAM and every efficient mechanism that Pareto-dominates DDAM, including EADAM, are very obviously manipulable, even in a one-to-one market. The paper demonstrates this with a two-doctor, one-hospital example: a doctor’s misreport improves its outcome for every preference of the other doctor. This is a strong, named result, but it is not a computational-complexity anchor.

If the anchor rule were relaxed to allow a qualitative mechanism theorem to seed a new computational question, the most promising lead would be Cohort Very-Obvious Manipulation in Continuum Contract Matching. A market would specify finite sets of doctor and hospital types, rational masses for each type, complete rankings over finitely many contract categories, substitutable hospital choice rules, and capacities scaled to the population. A mass-valued version of hospital-proposing deferred acceptance would assign contracts to types; each individual would still receive an integral contract, while the type’s assignment is represented by the fractions receiving each contract. The question would be whether there is a positive mass \(q\) of one doctor type and a false report such that the cohort’s contract distribution is weakly better in the first-order stochastic-dominance sense under every report distribution of the remaining population, and strictly better in at least one case. A solution is the cohort mass and misreport witnessing this property.

A plausible regime is a large centralized residency market: many applicants share a relatively small set of preference types, and programs have public, substitutable preferences and capacities. Here \(n\) could be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types remains moderate. This continuizes the population; fractional type-level assignments are the natural aggregate description, with capacity scaling as part of the matching model.

The weakness is also substantial. Theorem 4 concerns a single doctor’s unilateral deviation. A positive-mass cohort acting together is a coalition, and stochastic dominance over fractional assignments is an added solution concept. The theorem’s two-doctor example does not establish that its manipulation persists for a cohort in a continuum market. In an atomless model, a single doctor may have no effect at all; retaining a nontrivial individual deviation requires an atom or a separate focal-agent construction. So this is a plausible research lead, but I can’t honestly classify it as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard from this paper.

Thus my positive case is limited: Theorem 4 offers a clear mechanism phenomenon that could motivate a population-based extension, and the matching regime is plausible. But the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and the proposed extension changes the strategic unit from one doctor to a cohort.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s opening concession is decisive for the ChoCo brief: this paper states no complexity, algorithmic, or optimization result. Theorems 1–4 classify mechanisms by whether doctors can manipulate them obviously; none asks how hard it is to compute a mechanism, find a manipulation, or verify one of those properties. Theorem 4 is a strong strategic result, but adding a computational problem around it would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 4 is also the best anchor to test against a continuous population. Its example depends on one named doctor’s report changing the hospital’s sequence of offers and the rejection of another doctor’s contract. In an atomless population, one doctor has zero mass, so changing that doctor’s report does not change aggregate offers or assignments. The individual-manipulation question then degenerates: the mechanism has no population-level response to the deviation. If instead a type has enough mass to affect the outcome, the strategic actor is a cohort, not the individual doctor in the theorem. Preserving an individually pivotal doctor by giving each person a distinct type would make the support grow with the population, removing the high-multiplicity regime.

The proposed cohort version is the strongest repair, but it changes the object being studied. It replaces unilateral manipulation with coordinated coalition deviation, and replaces an individual doctor’s ranking of contracts with a comparison between distributions of contracts, such as first-order stochastic dominance. The paper’s two-doctor example establishes nothing about whether such a cohort manipulation exists or persists in a continuum market. One could study that new problem, but it would need its own model and justification; it is not an algorithmic consequence or mirror of Theorem 4.

Matching can plainly have sensible high-multiplicity regimes, such as large applicant pools with a small number of preference types. That supports continuizing matching populations in general. It does not resolve the mismatch here: the paper’s results concern the strategic effect of one person’s report, while the natural continuum either erases that effect or turns it into a coalition problem. On this paper, the negative case is strongest at that boundary. The cohort question may be worthwhile as adjacent work, but the paper supplies no computational anchor for the continuous-complexity programme.
