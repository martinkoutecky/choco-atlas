The continuous mirror question

Given programs \(P\), a finite set of agent types \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), type rankings of acceptable programs, program rankings of types in priority tiers, and nonnegative per-unit program costs \(c(p)\), choose \(x_{tp}\ge 0\) on mutually acceptable pairs with \(\sum_p x_{tp}=\mu_t\) for every type. Require that there are no types \(t,u\) and programs \(p,q\) with \(x_{tq}>0\), \(x_{up}>0\), \(p\succ_t q\), and \(t\) in a strictly higher priority tier than \(u\) at \(p\). Minimize \(\sum_p c(p)\sum_t x_{tp}\) (or decide whether this value can be at most a given rational budget \(B\)); measure complexity in \(|P|\), support size \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\), and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A large school or course allocation with finitely many programs and cohorts whose members share program rankings and priority tiers; rational cohort mass is assigned through \(x_{tp}\), at per-unit cost \(c(p)\), while envy is tested between types receiving positive mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's \(\textsf{MINSUM}\) strong NP-hardness and \(7/6-\epsilon\) inapproximability claims. It leaves the polynomial \(\textsf{MINMAX}\) result, the approximation algorithms, and the experiments unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper's Set Cover and vertex-cover reductions establish the two Theorem 2 hardness guarantees for rational-mass cohorts under this positive-mass envy condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is continuous minimum-sum envy-free matching. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper’s Section 5 and referred to there as Theorem~\(\ref{thm:smfq\_hardness}\). It says that \(\textsf{MINSUM}\) is strongly NP-hard even when every agent lists exactly \(f\ge2\) programs, both sides’ preferences obey master lists, and there are only two distinct program costs. It also says that, unless \(P=NP\), \(\textsf{MINSUM}\) cannot be approximated within \(7/6-\epsilon\), for any \(\epsilon>0\), even with three distinct costs.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. There is a finite set \(P\) of programs and a finite set \(\mathcal T\) of agent types. A type specifies an agent’s complete ranking of acceptable programs and that type’s priority at each program; each program ranks the types it accepts. The input gives a rational mass \(\mu_t\ge0\) for each type, with \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t=1\), and a nonnegative cost \(c(p)\) per unit of mass assigned to program \(p\). An allocation is a set of nonnegative masses \(x_{tp}\), only on mutually acceptable pairs, satisfying \(\sum_p x_{tp}=\mu_t\) for every type: all population mass is assigned.

The allocation is envy-free if there are no types \(t,u\) and programs \(p,q\) such that \(x_{tq}>0\), \(x_{up}>0\), type \(t\) prefers \(p\) to \(q\), and program \(p\) gives type \(t\) higher priority than type \(u\). The objective is to minimize \(\sum_p c(p)\sum_t x_{tp}\). The decision version asks whether an envy-free allocation of cost at most a given rational budget \(B\) exists. Its input size and running time should be measured in \(|P|\), support size \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational data. This is a population mirror of the paper’s underlying minimum-cost matching problem; the variables describe assigned mass, not an encoding to solve.

A plausible regime is a large school district or university course allocation: thousands of students, a relatively small set of schools or courses, and cohorts sharing both ranked choices and priority categories. Programs retain their discrete identity and each cohort receives an integral assignment at the individual level; the type-level allocation records the fraction of that cohort sent to each program. Program costs remain per-student resource costs, as in the paper. This is population continuization, with the mass-valued assignment as the necessary co-modification.

I expect the hardness to transfer for this problem. The paper’s Set Cover reduction can be read with each set-agent role and element-agent role as a type of equal mass. If the population is replicated \(K\) times, these types represent \(K\) indistinguishable agents each, while the distribution over types stays the same. Fractional allocation does not obviously dissolve the cover choice: if positive element mass is assigned to a set-program, that program’s higher-priority set-agent type must be assigned entirely to that program, or that type would envy the element mass. The selected set-programs therefore still cover every element, and their activation costs still encode the cover size. The inapproximability reduction has the same useful feature: positive edge-agent mass at a vertex-program forces the higher-priority vertex-agent roles for that vertex away from the zero-cost program, so activated vertices cover every edge. Uniformly scaling all masses scales the objective and budget together, leaving approximation ratios unchanged. This gives a concrete route to the two parts of Theorem 2, rather than an assumption that discrete hardness automatically survives.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s \(\textsf{MINSUM}\) hardness results. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s polynomial \(\textsf{MINMAX}\) result or its approximation algorithms. The main soft spot is the priority model: indistinguishable agents of a type tie within that type, whereas the paper assumes strict rankings over named agents. A setting with genuine priority categories makes this a natural co-modification; if individual tie-break positions are essential, they must enter the type definition and can make \(\tau\) large. I think the authors could recognize this as their problem in a cohort-based regime, but that priority interpretation is the point a referee should scrutinize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the right anchor, and the strongest objection is that its strict program rankings make the proposed high-multiplicity regime hard to obtain without changing the priority model. An agent’s type must include their preferences and their priority at every program. Two agents with the same nonempty preference list share a program, where the paper’s strict ranking distinguishes them. So in the paper’s model, repeated complete types are unavailable; keeping those rankings leaves the support size growing with the population.

The cohort mirror repairs this by giving each cohort a shared priority tier. That is a coherent model, and the set-cover argument survives its exact envy-free interpretation: positive element mass at a set’s program forces the higher-priority set-agent cohort entirely onto that program. But the repair replaces strict individual priorities with ties between cohorts. In applications where lottery numbers or other individual tie-breaks determine priority, those details are part of who envies whom; collapsing them into a tier changes the problem’s central relation. The paper’s master lists do not resolve this issue: they order named agents, including agents that the mirror would need to treat as interchangeable.

There is also a choice of fractional envy semantics. Requiring that no positive mass of one type be envious of positive mass of another preserves the reduction’s all-or-nothing activation. A proportional fractional relaxation, closer to the paper’s LP constraints, can permit partial activation and no longer has that argument; the paper already formulates an LP relaxation of this kind. The exact tier-based mirror is therefore the more promising new question, but its suitability rests on an application regime with genuine priority tiers. That regime is plausible, so this is a weak negative case rather than a universal refutation: I cannot honestly claim the anchor fails under every author-recognizable model.
