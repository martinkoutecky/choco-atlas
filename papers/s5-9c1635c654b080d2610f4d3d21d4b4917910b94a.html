The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), permitted arcs \(A\), exactly three sink types \(S\), and a cap \(\lambda\), does there exist a nonnegative flow \(x_{tu}\) on \(A\) such that \(\sum_{u:(t,u)\in A}x_{tu}=\mu_t+\sum_{r:(r,t)\in A}x_{rt}\) for every \(t\notin S\), and \(\mu_s+\sum_{r:(r,s)\in A}x_{rs}\leq\lambda\mu_s\) for every \(s\in S\)? Equivalently, can an atomless population route all represented vote mass to sinks while keeping each sink’s average load per member at most \(\lambda\)?

The model it lives in

Types represent interchangeable members with the same permitted delegation roles; \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population mass. Variables \(x_{tu}\) route vote mass along allowed arcs, and the objective in the optimization version is to minimize the maximum sink load per member.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 counts literal sink vertices and uses person-specific arcs; its three-sink hardness construction does not cover three positive-mass sink types with aggregate per-member load caps.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers feasibility and optimization for role-typed delegation populations with three sink types. It leaves aside the paper’s arbitrary person-to-person delegation graphs and their graph-specific hardness and parameterized results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the type-mass flow formulation exactly equivalent to an atomless role-based delegation process, including acyclic resolution?
  • How closely can optimal mass flows be realized by finite populations with fixed type counts, and what load increase is needed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is for a federation or large membership platform with many members but a small set of recurring delegation roles. A type records a member’s role, whether they are a final delegate, and exactly which roles they may delegate to. Members of one type are interchangeable, and may delegate to any member of an authorized type. There are \(n\) members but only \(\tau\) types, with \(n\gg\tau\). For a concrete version, use three final-delegate roles, echoing the paper’s three-sink result.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: Resolve Delegation is NP-complete even with only three sink vertices, and is therefore para-NP-hard parameterized by the number \(t\) of sinks. The paper proves this by reducing from Two Vertex Disjoint Paths, whose NP-completeness it cites as reference [5]. The underlying question is whether every non-sink voter can choose one permitted delegate so that no sink ends up responsible for more than \(\lambda\) voters.

Call the mirror Three-Role Type-Mass Resolve Delegation. An instance consists of a finite set of types \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), permitted arcs \(A\subseteq T\times T\), and a load bound \(\lambda\). There are exactly three sink types \(S=\{s_1,s_2,s_3\}\), with no outgoing arcs. Each mass unit still represents a member who chooses one delegate; the continuum lets a type’s members divide across their permitted choices. The question is whether there is a routing of the population mass through the type graph such that every unit of mass reaches a sink and each sink type’s average represented-voter load is at most \(\lambda\).

A candidate solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_{tu}\) on the permitted arcs, where \(x_{tu}\) is vote mass forwarded from type \(t\) to type \(u\). For each non-sink type \(t\), it must satisfy \(\sum_{u:(t,u)\in A}x_{tu}=\mu_t+\sum_{r:(r,t)\in A}x_{rt}\). For each sink type \(s\), it must satisfy \(\mu_s+\sum_{r:(r,s)\in A}x_{rs}\leq\lambda\mu_s\). The left side is the total mass represented by that role; dividing by \(\mu_s\) gives its average load per member. Since any eligible member can delegate to any member of the target type, incoming mass can be routed to the subgroups that chose each outgoing role. Circulations can be removed, leaving paths to sinks. Thus the aggregate flow represents members making individual delegation choices, while the choices of different members of one type produce fractional type-level totals.

I expect this problem to be tractable. Minimizing the maximum average sink load uses the same balance equations with a variable \(z\), and constraints \(\mu_s+\sum_r x_{rs}\leq z\mu_s\). This is a linear program with size polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|A|\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational input. A finite expansion with \(n_t\) members of type \(t\) has \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); for a singleton sink type, the average-load ratio becomes the ordinary number of voters represented by that sink.

The paper gives a close precedent in Theorem 6: it proves that the optimization version with fractional delegation of votes has a linear-programming formulation and is polynomial-time solvable. Theorem 6 concerns fractional forwarding among named voters; the mirror here takes the society itself as rational masses over interchangeable types. That makes the typed, high-multiplicity formulation a separate question, while the paper’s own LP result supports the expected direction.

The weakness is clear: Theorem 1’s reduction imports an arbitrary Two Vertex Disjoint Paths graph, with separate vertices for its source vertices. It establishes no hardness result for instances with many members per type and small \(\tau\). This mirror covers the paper’s Resolve Delegation problem in a role-structured population regime; it does not cover the arbitrary person-to-person graphs behind the reduction. The connection still seems credible because the mirror preserves the single-delegate choice, transitive delegation, sink load, and the paper’s central resolution task. A useful next question is how closely an optimal mass flow can be rounded to a finite delegation graph with fixed type counts and a small load increase.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to the proponent’s Theorem 1 anchor is that “three sink types” does not preserve the theorem’s parameter \(t=3\), which counts sink vertices. If a positive-mass sink type represents many people, its members are many sink vertices in the expanded graph, so \(t\) grows with population size. If the mirror instead keeps three literal sink individuals, each has mass \(1/N\) in a population of size \(N\); at fixed \(\lambda\), the normalized capacity of all three sinks tends to zero. Theorem 1 sets \(\lambda=17n\), so that particular degeneration can be avoided by scaling the cap or treating the sinks as special zero-mass hubs. But those are different modelling choices, not the stated three-role type model.

There is a related loss of structure. The reduction for Theorem 1 relies on individual vertices and their particular graph connections to encode two vertex-disjoint paths. A type-level permission graph gives every member of a type the same choices, effectively replacing individual connections with complete connections between types. To retain each person’s exact delegate options, those options must be part of their type; in the reduction, that means \(\tau\) grows with the graph. So the theorem’s hardness result does not establish hardness—or even a close parameterized analogue—for the proposed small-\(\tau\), three-role mirror.

The proposed role-based model is still coherent. Its flow constraints are also closely related to Theorem 6: in an explicitly expanded graph, aggregate the fractional delegation variables by type to get the type-level balances; conversely, a type flow can be distributed uniformly among eligible members. With symmetric type permissions, this is a natural aggregation identity. I would not call Theorem 6 a collision by itself: it has a finite named population, while the mirror uses a succinct rational-mass description. That distinction could itself be a result.

So the negative case defeats the claim that Theorem 1’s three-sink hardness anchors this particular mirror, but it does not rule out a worthwhile continuous-population question. The role-based regime is plausible, and the connection to Theorem 6 leaves room for a meaningful identity result. The honest case against a mirror in any scenario is weak.
