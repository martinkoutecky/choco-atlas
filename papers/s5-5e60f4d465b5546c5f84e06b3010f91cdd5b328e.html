The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive lead is a continuous population version of the paper’s staged winner computation, but it does not meet the required anchor test. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or any similar class. Its named theorems concern axioms: for example, “Solid Coalition preserves in multi-stage voting” and the results that committee monotonicity, candidate monotonicity, and consistency fail for rational multi-stage score rules. The appendix likewise proves axiomatic claims. The introduction says computational aspects are not the focus; using an ILP to implement Chamberlin–Courant in the experiment is not a complexity result. So there is no eligible result to restate as an anchor.

A plausible *new computational question* would be Continuous Multi-Stage Winner Computation. In a large election with \(N\gg\tau\), let each type be a complete candidate ranking and let \(\mu_\rho\) be its rational population mass. Given \(C\), the stage sizes \(k_1>\cdots>k_t\), and the paper’s \((\beta,\vec{\gamma})\)-rule, compute one valid shortlist path \(S_0=C,S_1,\ldots,S_t\), where each \(S_r\) maximizes \(\sum_\rho \mu_\rho f_{\rho,r}(S)\) among the size-\(k_r\) subsets of \(S_{r-1}\). The path is the solution certificate. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of their actual voting procedure: rankings remain intact, mass replaces voter counts, and the committee is still selected at every stage. For \(\ell_1\) rules, each stage reduces to selecting the top \(k_r\) candidates by aggregate score, so this restricted computation looks tractable. The \(\ell_{\max}\) case requires solving the corresponding committee-scoring optimization; the paper’s ILP implementation does not establish its complexity.

The nearest theorem for support is “Solid Coalition preserves in multi-stage voting,” proved in the paper: if every stage rule satisfies Solid Coalition, then the multi-stage rule does too. Replacing voter counts by ranking masses gives a direct continuous statement. But that remains an axiomatic result, and the proposed winner-computation problem is an extension beyond what the paper proves. I would present it as a promising research lead, not as a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a *result-anchored* continuous mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its theorems concern axioms: Solid Coalition is preserved, while committee monotonicity, candidate monotonicity, and consistency fail in the stated settings. The approval appendix is axiomatic too. The ILP used to compute Chamberlin–Courant in the experiment is an implementation detail, not a complexity theorem.

The closest proposed anchor, “Solid Coalition preserves in multi-stage voting,” has a clear population version: replace “at least \(n/k\) voters” with mass at least \(1/k\). The proof carries over to ranking types and mass. But that is an axiomatic statement, not a question about computational complexity or algorithms. The same scope problem defeats continuous versions of the paper’s other axiom results; making the population continuous does not turn those claims into computational ones.

The proposed winner-computation problem is a stronger candidate. Given rational masses on \(\tau\) ranking types, one could compute a valid sequence of committees by maximizing each stage’s aggregate score. That is a sensible high-multiplicity formulation, but it is a new computational question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper supplies the rule and its axiomatic analysis, not a claim about the complexity of computing its output.

There is an even closer computational extension: assign costs to changing one ranking type into another, then ask for the least mass transfer that changes the final committee. That fits the paper’s discussion of manipulation and the programme’s interest in bribery or winner robustness. It also has a plausible large-election setting with many voters but relatively few ranking types. Still, the paper proves no result about that task.

So the negative case is strong on provenance: none of the cited theorems qualifies as a computational anchor. It is weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The winner-computation and mass-transfer questions are plausible continuations; dismissing them as uninteresting would overreach. They should be described as new computational work inspired by the paper, rather than continuizations of its results.
