The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete interaction types \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), binary type values \(w_{ab}\), an integer \(k\ge2\), and a rational threshold \(q\ge0\), decide whether there are allocations \(x_{a\ell}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_{\ell=1}^k x_{a\ell}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_{a\in T}x_{a\ell}=1/k\), and \(\sum_{b\in T}w_{ab}x_{b\ell}\ge q\) for every type \(a\) with \(x_{a\ell}>0\) in every coalition \(\ell\). Determine the complexity in the input bit length, number of types, and number of coalitions.

The model it lives in

A society consists of rational masses \(\mu_a\) of types whose members share the same values \(w_{ab}\) for members of each type \(b\). The decision variable \(x_{a\ell}\) assigns type mass to one of \(k\) coalitions, each of mass \(1/k\); the objective is to meet the egalitarian threshold \(q\) for every type present in a coalition. Excluding an individual’s value for themself changes the finite-population utility by a vanishing amount after normalization.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1. It leaves aside Theorem 2, the symmetric-game result for egalitarian value \(2\), the utilitarian welfare bound, and the heuristic and simulation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • As a function of the number of types, coalitions, and input bit length, is Equal-Mass Egalitarian Partition polynomial-time solvable or hard?
  • Does fractional splitting of each type’s mass eliminate the support choices that underlie hardness, or can hardness be established directly for the mass model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s equal-sized coalition hardness result. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, states that DecisionEgalitarianEqalCS is NP-complete: given an ASHG, positive integers \(k\) and \(\delta\), decide whether there is a \(k\)-coalition structure with equally sized coalitions and egalitarian value at least \(\delta\). The proof establishes hardness already for simple ASHGs, reducing from the general decision problem using hardness cited from Bang-Jensen and Christiansen [6]. The theorem statement does not specify that \(k\) is fixed, so I would not make that stronger claim from the theorem alone.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large training programme that must place participants into \(k\) equal-capacity cohorts. Participants fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of recurring collaboration types; a type fully specifies how its members value members of every other type. There may be thousands of participants but only dozens of types. This preserves the paper’s central concern: form exactly \(k\) equally sized coalitions while maximizing the utility of the worst-off participant. It is a more structured ASHG than the general model, but a credible instance regime for it.

Here is the continuous problem I would give a prover. Call it Equal-Mass Egalitarian Partition. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_a>0\) summing to \(1\), a nonnegative type interaction matrix \(w_{ab}\in\{0,1\}\), an integer \(k\ge2\), and a rational threshold \(q\ge0\). The mass \(\mu_a\) is the fraction of the population of type \(a\); \(w_{ab}=1\) means each type-\(a\) participant values each type-\(b\) participant. Decide whether there are allocations \(x_{a\ell}\ge0\), for \(a\in T\) and \(\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\), such that
\[ \sum_{\ell=1}^k x_{a\ell}=\mu_a,\qquad \sum_{a\in T}x_{a\ell}=\frac1k, \]
and, for every \(a,\ell\) with \(x_{a\ell}>0\),
\[ \sum_{b\in T}w_{ab}x_{b\ell}\ge q. \]
A solution is the allocation matrix \(x\): it says how much of each type goes into each cohort. The last expression is utility per unit of population, so \(q\) is the normalized counterpart of the discrete threshold \(\delta\). In a finite population of size \(N\), the natural comparison is \(q=\delta/N\), up to the vanishing self-exclusion term for same-type pairs.

I expect this problem to be a continuum-specific hardness candidate, not to inherit Theorem 1’s NP-hardness automatically. The occupied type–coalition pairs create a support choice, and the combinatorics may survive in the number of types and in \(k\). But the continuous model lets a type’s mass split across cohorts; the discrete reduction cannot simply be rescaled and declared to work. That is the central question for a prover: does this fractional freedom make the problem tractable, or does the support structure keep it hard? A polynomial-time result might come from a structural theorem that removes those support choices; a hardness result would need to establish hardness for the type-mass model itself.

The soft spot is exactly that gap between the anchor and the mirror: Theorem 1 concerns indivisible agents and does not prove hardness when agents fall into repeated interaction types and their mass can split. The regime is still recognizable as the authors’ problem because it retains their objective and coalition constraints, but the proposed result is a restricted, asymptotic analogue—not a consequence of their theorem. I would cover Theorem 1 only. Theorem 2’s absolute out-degree threshold of \(1\) and fixed coalition-size lower bounds do not give as clean a population-normalized mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The serious objection is that Theorem 1’s hardness is built on an arbitrary graph of named agents. Its reduction adds agents whose utility depends on which original agents they join. In a sparse friendship model, an agent’s neighbors are specific people, and grouping agents by broad characteristics does not preserve those utilities. To get the proposed type matrix \(w_{ab}\), one must instead assume that every member of type \(a\) values every member of type \(b\) identically. That makes the graph block structured, often dense, and changes the instance regime behind the hardness result.

This also weakens the proposed bridge to the paper’s data. If each person has only a bounded number of valued neighbors as the population grows, normalizing utility by population size makes the discrete threshold tend to zero. The proposed continuum utility \(\sum_b w_{ab}x_{b\ell}\) instead captures value from a positive fraction of the population. It fits settings with recurring roles and type-wide compatibility, but that is a substantive modeling choice, not a limit forced by the student-friend examples. The training-cohort scenario makes the choice plausible; it does not make it representative of the paper’s motivating cases.

Still, neither point defeats the better mirror. Block-structured ASHGs are a credible regime for assigning recurring roles to fixed-capacity cohorts, and the authors could recognize that as their problem. The proposed allocation question is well-defined: a type may split across coalitions, each coalition has mass \(1/k\), and every type receiving positive mass must meet the egalitarian threshold. Its support conditions are combinatorial; one can enumerate type–coalition supports and solve an LP for each. That gives an immediate parameterized approach, but leaves a genuine complexity question as \(\tau\) grows. A tractable result, or hardness for this mass model, could both be worthwhile.

So the strongest case against is limited to relevance and the lack of an automatic connection between Theorem 1’s reduction and the continuous model. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists: the equal-mass mirror survives in a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the objection that its answer may differ from the discrete theorem is not a reason to reject the question.
