The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a defensible anchored computational mirror in this paper. It contains no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, W[1]-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in the paper: any judgment-transformation function satisfying universal domain, rational co-domain, consensus preservation, minimal relevance, and independence/systematicity must be the identity function. That is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 is also an axiomatic impossibility result, about judgment aggregation, and its proof is attributed to other work. Neither qualifies as an anchor under your criteria.

Section 7 does describe constrained minimal revision functions that can produce single-peaked output profiles, but it gives no numbered computational result for them. One could imagine a separate continuous research question: represent a society by masses over complete rational judgment sets, allow mass transfers from pre-communication to post-communication sets, and minimize total revision distance subject to a desired property of the output profile. That would be a new computational problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its named computational results. I would not present it as a qualifying positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central point is decisive for an anchored mirror: the paper contains no numbered result about the computational complexity of a problem. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility result about judgment aggregation; Theorem 2 is an axiomatic impossibility result about judgment transformation. Neither specifies an algorithmic problem, an input encoding, or a complexity claim. Theorem 1’s being attributed to earlier work makes it an even weaker anchor here.

The strongest attempted rescue is to turn Theorem 2 into an algorithmic question about large deliberating groups. Let types be complete rational judgment sets, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population with input type \(t\), and let mass transfers describe post-communication judgments. That is a coherent population model, but it loses a condition central to Theorem 2: minimal relevance asks whether changing one individual’s input can affect that individual’s output. In an atomless society, a one-person change leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Replacing that condition with responsiveness to a positive-mass change gives a different theorem; treating each type as an individual gives up the high-multiplicity interpretation. A transfer plan can track how much mass moves between types, but it cannot restore the theorem’s single-person comparison.

The same distinction rules out Theorem 1 as an anchor. A distribution over judgment types could support a computational problem about collective rationality—for example, finding the smallest mass of judgments to revise so majority judgments become consistent. But that would add an optimization objective and algorithmic question to an axiomatic result. It would be a new problem, not a computational mirror of Theorem 1.

The best new candidate is the paper’s constrained minimal revision idea. For a large public deliberation, one could represent rankings by their population masses and minimize the mass-weighted revision distance needed to obtain a single-peaked profile, choosing the axis as part of the optimization. That asks a sensible question in continuous units: what fraction of the public would need to change its ranking? But the paper presents this as a class of transformation functions and a possible account of deliberation-induced meta-agreement. It gives no computational result for it. The proposed optimization would require the researcher to choose the cost model, the target property, and an encoding; it cannot serve as an anchor to a result in this paper.

So I find the negative case strong against every proposed mirror: neither numbered theorem supplies the computational result the programme is meant to continuize, and the best algorithmic reformulation changes the question. I would not claim the broader statement that no worthwhile continuous research question can be built from this paper. The mass-revision problem is plausible enough to merit consideration on its own, even though it does not rescue the case for a continuous mirror here.
