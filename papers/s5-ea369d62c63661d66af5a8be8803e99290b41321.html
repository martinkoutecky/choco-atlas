The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying named anchors in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its main computational finding is an unnumbered simulation: with 1,000 students, 20 programs, quotas of 50, uniformly generated student preferences and grades, the authors report 159 student objections and 56 unstable matches, then conclude that the simulated Armenian procedure produces unstable outcomes under those assumptions. That is an empirical result, not a named complexity result. The paper also cites Gale and Shapley’s guarantee that their algorithm produces stable matchings, but that result is external and unnumbered here. I would not present either as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest positive case is therefore a candidate mirror of the paper’s stability-audit question, rather than a mirror anchored in a theorem. In a large national admissions cohort, let a student type \(t\) specify a complete ranking of programs and an exam-score priority band. Let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of applicants. Each program \(u\) has per-capita capacity \(\kappa_u\), and \(x_{t,u}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(u\). Types must be complete: students grouped together have the same preferences and priority as far as the market uses them. The regime is plausible when many applicants share preference lists and score bands, so the cohort size is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported types. Program capacity scaling with population is the natural per-capita version of the paper’s quotas.

A precise audit problem is Maximum Blocking Mass\(_\infty\). Its instance is the rational type distribution \(\mu\), program capacities \(\kappa\), program priority ordering over types, and a proposed mass matching \(x\), such as the output of a continuous version of the admissions procedure. For each type-program pair \((t,u)\), define
\[ b_{t,u}=\min\!\left\{ \sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}x_{t,v},\ \kappa_u-\sum_r x_{r,u}+\sum_{r:\,u\text{ prefers }t\text{ to }r}x_{r,u} \right\}. \]
The first term is the mass of type \(t\) assigned somewhere it likes less than \(u\); the second is the seats \(u\) can offer that mass, through vacancies or by replacing lower-priority assigned types. The objective is to maximize \(B=\max_{t,u} b_{t,u}\), returning a maximizing pair and mass. The outcome is stable exactly when \(B=0\). This makes “how many applicants can form a blocking objection?” the continuous counterpart of the paper’s objection check. Fractional assignment by type is a co-modification of the matching model, not a move to continuous outcomes as the programme’s goal.

I expect this audit problem to be tractable: for finite support, compute the assigned and acceptable masses for each type-program pair and take the maximum, using time polynomial in the support size, number of programs, and rational input length. Further questions would be whether a faithful continuous version of the Armenian two-stage procedure can be specified, whether its output is stable for every such distribution, and how much mass must be reassigned to make an unstable output stable. The mirror covers only the paper’s claim about auditing the stability of the admissions procedure; it does not turn the paper’s broader discussion of matching theory or its Gale–Shapley recommendation into computational results.

The soft spot is the type regime. The paper’s simulated preferences are random, and it describes grades as distinct; either feature can make the number of complete types approach the number of students. The mirror is most convincing for a different but plausible admissions regime with recurring preference lists and score bands that programs genuinely treat alike. Also, the paper does not specify its simulated procedure in enough detail to define its continuous version uniquely. So this is a credible mirror of the question the paper investigates, but not a strong theorem-to-theorem continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no qualifying anchor. The paper has no numbered result about computational complexity; its main computational finding is an unnumbered simulation reporting 159 student objections and 56 blocking pairs in one randomly generated instance with \(1{,}000\) students and 20 programs. The Gale–Shapley guarantee is cited background, not a result of this paper. That leaves no paper-owned complexity result to continuize.

The proposed Maximum Blocking Mass problem is a sensible way to audit a *given* fractional matching: it asks whether some positive mass of a type and a program can block. But the paper’s question is whether the Armenian admissions procedure produces a stable matching. Those are not the same computational task. The paper describes a main stage and an additional stage, then says its simulation uses a mechanism “similar” to the admissions algorithm; it does not specify enough of the simulated procedure to determine its output \(x\). Computing \(B\) from an arbitrary \(x\) cannot supply that missing mechanism. A measure closer to the reported count of blocking pairs could be defined, but it would face the same gap.

The strongest repair would specify a population-scaled version of the two-stage procedure, group students by complete type—including preferences and the priorities the programs use—and ask whether its output has blocking mass. That could be a worthwhile study. But the paper’s simulation assumes distinct grades, so including exact grade in the type makes its sampled students essentially all different types. Repeated preference and score types could yield a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but that would be a new regime and require a newly specified mechanism. It is not established by the paper’s result.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper: it offers no computational result to anchor a continuous mirror, and its empirical question cannot be mirrored faithfully without supplying a central missing model. I cannot honestly extend that to prove that no continuous student–program matching problem would be worthwhile; a repeated-type admissions model could be. The evidence here supports “no worthwhile mirror of this paper,” not the universal claim that no such scenario exists.
