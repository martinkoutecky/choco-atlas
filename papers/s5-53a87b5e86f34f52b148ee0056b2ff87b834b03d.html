The continuous mirror question

Given τ worker types with rational masses μ_t and rational linear utilities u_t(q) over team compositions q in the simplex Δ^{τ-1}, promised to satisfy a mass weak top-coalition condition on every nonzero residual mass vector v ≤ μ, compute a finite partition into teams x^j = λ_j q^j whose type masses sum to μ. A partition is mass-SIS if no positive mass can be removed from its teams and reassigned to new teams so that all deviating mass strictly prefers its new composition and all nondeviating mass in teams receiving members weakly prefers the resulting composition. Ask whether such a partition can be computed in time polynomial in the type count, input representation size, and rational bit length.

The model it lives in

An anonymous hedonic game with τ types and rational population masses; each type ranks team compositions in the full simplex, represented by rational linear utilities. Outcomes partition the mass into teams, and SIS deviations may split and reassign mass across teams.

The objection that survived

The finite recipe menu may omit the mixed composition of a team after it receives members, and the stated feasibility promise depends on type support rather than mass proportions; repairing this requires preferences over the full composition space and an explicit mass version of the layering condition.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s existence and polynomial-time computation of an SIS partition. It does not transfer Theorem 3’s Pareto-optimality result or the CNS nonexistence result in Theorem 4.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass weak top-coalition condition guarantee an SIS partition and support a polynomial-time algorithm?
  • Can the full composition preferences and layering condition be represented and checked with a compact input encoding?
  • Can solutions be rounded into useful stable outcomes for finite populations of type-clones?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 2, proved here: every HGWTCP instance admits a strongly individually stable (SIS) partition, and Algorithm 3 computes one in polynomial time. The paper gives no numbered hardness result to use as an anchor; its NP-hardness statement about Pareto-optimal partitions in HGCRP is prior work mentioned in the prose. Theorem 2 is therefore the right computational anchor.

I would mirror it in a large organization that forms project teams from recurring worker profiles. Say there are millions of workers but only \(\tau\) types: a type records a worker’s role and complete preference over team compositions. Workers of the same type are interchangeable for the model. Their mass \(\mu_t\) is their share of the workforce, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime when the number of distinct role-and-preference profiles stays small as the workforce grows.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass-WT-SIS. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), a finite explicit menu \(\mathcal Q\) of team recipes, and for each type \(t\), a weak ranking of the recipes that include \(t\). A recipe \(q\in\mathcal Q\) is a rational vector of type proportions, with \(\sum_t q_t=1\). A team may have any positive mass \(\lambda\), so its type-mass vector is \(\lambda q\). The model assumes preferences depend on the recipe, not on the team’s overall scale; this is the composition-based regime in which team size itself does not change a worker’s ranking.

The instance is promised to satisfy the mass analogue of the weak top-coalition property. For every nonzero residual population vector \(v\leq\mu\), there must be a feasible recipe \(q\), together with an ordered layering of its participating types, such that any type in layer \(k\) that strictly prefers another recipe feasible in \(v\) can form that recipe only with help from a type in an earlier layer. Feasibility here means the recipe’s types have positive residual mass; it can be scaled down to fit. The question is to find a finite partition \(\{(\lambda_j,q^j)\}_j\) with
\[ \sum_j \lambda_jq^j=\mu \]
that is SIS. A blocking deviation is a positive mass of workers rearranged into teams so that every deviator strictly prefers their new recipe, while every nondeviating worker whose team receives deviators weakly prefers the resulting recipe. Workers left behind when others depart need not consent, matching the paper’s SIS notion.

I expect Mass-WT-SIS to be Class A, tractable. With finitely many types and recipes, the layering test is finite. After selecting a weak top recipe for the current residual population, choose its largest feasible scale; that exhausts at least one type. Repeating this step takes at most \(\tau\) rounds. This suggests an exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of listed recipes and preference entries, and their bit length \(L\)—rather than in the headcount. It is the same structural idea as Theorem 2’s greedy construction, expressed in mass variables.

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous-population version: the agents still form coalitions, and the outcome is still judged by strong individual stability. Making the population continuous forces coalition memberships to be represented by type masses, but that co-modification is part of the mirror. It does not turn the question into fractional hedonic utility or continuity of the outcome space.

The scope is deliberately just Theorem 2. I would not treat Theorem 3 as a second anchor here: its strict-preference Pareto argument relies on rankings over coalitions, while the composition-only model makes different team scales equivalent. Whether SIS and Pareto optimality can be obtained together in a suitable continuous model is a further question, not a result this mirror inherits.

The weakest point is also the main modeling restriction: preferences must be anonymous within type and invariant to team scale. That is plausible for standardized project teams valued by role mix, but it excludes settings where headcount or named coworkers matter. There is also a real complexity gap to bridge: the paper’s polynomial bound is in the number of agents and the IRCL list length, not in a high-multiplicity encoding’s \(\tau\) and \(L\). So this is a new continuous algorithmic question, not a theorem already implied by the paper. Its value is that it asks whether the weak-top-coalition structure still yields a stable partition when mass, rather than individual agents, is the input. Further questions include whether the restriction on team size can be relaxed, whether continuous SIS partitions yield useful rounded outcomes for finite clone populations, and how efficiently the mass weak-top property can be recognized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for finding an SIS partition. The strongest case against mirroring it is that the paper’s algorithm operates on an explicitly agent-indexed input. Its IRCL lists rank coalitions of named people, and its weak top-coalition test ranges over residual subsets of those people. The stated polynomial bound is in \(n\) and the list length; the paper gives no compression by preference type.

The proposed Mass-WT-SIS model supplies that compression by adding a finite menu of team recipes and making preferences depend only on proportions. But that menu creates a problem for SIS deviations. If a team of mass \(\lambda\) with recipe \(q\) receives mass \(\delta\) with recipe \(r\), its new recipe is
\[ q'=\frac{\lambda q+\delta r}{\lambda+\delta}. \]
A finite recipe menu need not contain \(q'\), even though the stability test must ask whether the existing members accept their changed team. Preferences over the whole composition simplex would fix this, but that is a materially richer input model, requiring its own compact representation and its own weak-top and SIS definitions. It is not an algorithmic consequence of Theorem 2.

There is another gap in the proposed promise: because any recipe can be scaled down to fit, its feasibility depends only on which types have positive residual mass. Thus the weak-top condition as stated ranges over type supports, not mass proportions. The continuous masses still affect how teams can cover the population, but the paper’s central layering structure has not itself been continuized in a way that uses those masses.

That makes the proponent’s finite-menu formulation a poor demonstration that this particular theorem transfers. It does not, however, defeat the best mirror. Repeated role-and-preference profiles in a large workforce are plausible, and preferences over team compositions could be represented by suitable functions on the simplex. That would pose a genuine continuous-population computational question. The honest negative case is therefore weak: it supports caution about fidelity and input representation, but it cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror of Theorem 2.
