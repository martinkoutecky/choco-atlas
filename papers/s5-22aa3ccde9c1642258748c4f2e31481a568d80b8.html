The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large ethics survey: many respondents rank the options in recurring dilemmas, while the number of distinct rankings remains modest. A type is a complete ranking \(\sigma\) of the relevant alternatives, and its mass \(\mu_\sigma\) is the share of respondents with that ranking. For \(N\) respondents and \(\tau\) observed rankings, the natural regime is \(N\gg\tau\). The decision is the winning set selected by an aggregation rule. This is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors would recognize it: they define \(\pi_A(\sigma)\) as the probability that a random voter has ranking \(\sigma\), and identify it with the permutation process \(\Pi(A)\).

A precise computational question one could pose is Compatible-Process Winner Determination: given \(m\) alternatives, an explicit support of \(\tau\) rankings with binary rational masses summing to \(1\), a specified rule from the paper’s PM-compatible-process results, and a promise that the induced pairwise-majority relation is a total preorder, return the exact winning set. For the rules covered by those results, the winner set is the alternatives that pairwise-majority-dominate or tie every other alternative. Direct pairwise tallying takes \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations, so this proposed problem is tractable. It also raises further questions about recognizing compatibility and computing winners when the process is represented by utility-distribution parameters instead of an explicit ranking support.

That is a concrete question, but it is not anchored to a named computational result in this paper. Theorem 2 characterizes strongly PMD-efficient rules under a PM-compatible, consistent process: they are exactly the rules satisfying the Strict Condorcet Principle. Theorem 5 says that a strongly efficient SCC is stable under a strongly compatible process. Neither asserts a complexity classification. The tallying bound above is a direct algorithm for a newly posed problem, not a result the paper claims. The paper contains no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The central weakness is sharper still: the proposed mass model is already the paper’s model. Its profiles are distributions over rankings, and its results apply to those distributions; a discrete electorate’s empirical profile is just one special case. So the survey scenario supports the *plausibility* of high multiplicity, but turning it into a “mirror” would largely restate an existing continuous-population formulation. I would treat this paper as useful evidence that the regime is natural, but not as a paper with an eligible computational anchor for a ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The survey scenario is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many respondents can share a modest number of rankings, and the resulting shares are meaningful. But it does not supply a continuous mirror of this paper. The paper already defines \(\pi_A(\sigma)\) as the probability that a random voter has ranking \(\sigma\), and identifies it with the permutation process \(\Pi(A)\). On a finite alternative set, that is already a distribution over ranking types—the same population object a continuous society would use. The paper’s results apply to those distributions, not just to named voters. Calling the input a survey with rational masses makes the regime plausible; it does not open a new model for the mirror to study.

The proposed *Compatible-Process Winner Determination* problem is well-defined, but it is not a computational result asserted by the paper. Given explicit support, one can compute the pairwise tallies by weighted counting in \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations, then return the undefeated alternatives promised by the compatibility condition. Theorem 2 supplies the characterization, not a complexity classification. Theorem 5 is likewise about stability when the set of alternatives is restricted; it says nothing about the cost of changing population mass. Neither theorem gives an algorithmic problem whose complexity continuization could reveal. Asking for compatibility recognition or for calculations from a compressed utility model would add a new representation or inference problem; it would not recover a missing computational result from this paper.

A stronger proposal would be to ask how much population mass must be moved to change a winner or destroy compatibility. That fits ChoCo’s population axis and could be a useful project. But it adds an intervention cost and a population-perturbation objective absent from the paper. The paper’s “stability” varies the alternatives while holding the process fixed. Recasting it as robustness to edits in the electorate changes the question, so it does not rescue Theorem 5 as a computational anchor.

The negative case is strongest on eligibility: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and its central aggregation model already uses distributions over rankings. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile project could grow out of the paper. A mass-edit robustness problem is plausible, but it would be a new problem prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
