A distribution over types loses that singleton; making the manipulator a positive-mass cohort changes unilateral manipulation into group manipulation and changes the guarantee.

What the mirror covers

Theorem 2 is the sole proposed anchor, and no continuous-population mirror survives for it; the paper’s other manipulation strategies are not advanced as anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Theorem 2, the paper’s min–max manipulation result. It is proved here, though its construction uses rank-maximal matching machinery and a switching-graph characterization cited from earlier work. In full: if \(p\) is the highest-ranked feasible \(f\)-post in the manipulator’s true preference list, Algorithm HP constructs a falsified list \(H_p\) that minimizes the worst true rank the manipulator can receive across all rank-maximal matchings. Moreover, every min–max strategy has the same guarantee: every rank-maximal matching assigns the manipulator to \(p\). The theorem certifies the algorithm’s correctness and optimality; the paper does not present it as a separate complexity-class theorem. Its construction makes polynomially many rank-maximal matching and forced-pair tests, giving a plausible algorithmic anchor. The paper offers no comparable hardness result.

My lead mirror is Continuous Min–Max Rank-List Manipulation. Consider a large centralized placement market—say, graduate placements—with \(m\) post categories and many applicants but only \(\tau\) distinct preference and eligibility types, where \(\tau\ll n\). One positive-mass cohort has identical true preferences and eligibility, and a representative controls the common list submitted for that cohort. Posts have per-capita capacities \(q_p\), so their supplies scale with the population. Each person still receives at most one indivisible post; mass records the fraction of a type receiving each post.

Formally, the instance gives a finite post set \(P\), type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s acceptable posts and true ranking, per-capita capacities \(q_p\), and a focal type \(s\) with \(\mu_s>0\). For a proposed complete report \(\hat\succ_s\), let \(x_{tp}\ge 0\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(p\), and \(u_t\ge 0\) its unmatched mass. Require \(\sum_p x_{tp}+u_t=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tp}\le q_p\). The central authority lexicographically maximizes the rank totals \(R_i=\sum_{t,p:\,\operatorname{rank}_t(p)=i}x_{tp}\), using \(\hat\succ_s\) for type \(s\) and truthful rankings for other types. The question is to find \(\hat\succ_s\) minimizing the worst true rank the focal cohort can receive across all lexicographically optimal assignments; count being unmatched as worse than any listed post. A solution is the report, its achieved worst-rank guarantee, and a proof that no report gives a better guarantee. This retains the paper’s decision variable—the submitted ranking—and its adversarial quantifier over every rank-maximal outcome.

This is a coherent high-multiplicity extension. For rational masses and capacities, clearing denominators gives a finite clone market: each type becomes many identical applicants, and each post category gets the corresponding number of slots. For a fixed report, the assignment constraints form a transportation or flow polytope; its integral vertices and rank-maximal faces mean that positive mass assigned to a post corresponds to a clone receiving it in some rank-maximal matching. So fractional mass describes proportions of people receiving whole posts, rather than fractional posts. That gives a concrete bridge to the paper’s matching semantics.

I would provisionally expect Class A: for a fixed report, rank-maximal assignments and whether the cohort can receive a given post are flow questions, and Theorem 2’s construction suggests that the report search may be organized through polynomially many structural tests. Extending its critical-rank and forced-pair arguments to positive-mass cohorts is the central unproved step; the theorem itself does not establish that extension. Further questions include whether those structural tests survive when many identical applicants report together, what the running time is in \(m,\tau\), and input bit length, and whether the guarantee has a compact certificate.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a high-multiplicity extension of their min–max problem: rank-maximality and strategic ranking remain central. The weakest point is also clear: their manipulator is one applicant, while this mirror gives control to a positive-mass cohort. A lone applicant’s mass vanishes in an atomless population, so an individual deviation would not affect the aggregate matching. The cohort interpretation is natural for coordinated applications, but it is a substantive change of strategic actor. This case covers Theorem 2 alone; it does not claim that the paper proves a new continuous algorithm or that its other manipulation strategies have been mirrored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives an optimal report for the sole manipulator \(a_1\), judged by the worst post that *that individual* can receive across all rank-maximal matchings. I would not dismiss it merely because the paper gives no complexity-class theorem.

The direct high-multiplicity embedding loses that strategic object. If \(a_1\) remains a singleton in a society of \(n\) applicants, its mass is \(1/n\). Its report can change rank totals by at most \(1/n\), which vanishes in the normalized continuum model. Yet the theorem’s guarantee is sensitive to exactly one possible bad assignment: a single rank-maximal matching that gives \(a_1\) a worse post defeats the guarantee, however many other applicants get better posts. The limit records proportions; it forgets that individual contingency.

Keeping a tagged applicant alongside a continuous background preserves the question’s focus, though it requires tracking the tagged person and the effect of one unit of capacity. That is a finite-agent-plus-continuum model with additional subpopulation information, rather than a model determined by the continuous society’s type masses alone.

The proposed positive-mass cohort is a coherent repair, and its assignment constraints have a sound flow interpretation. It changes the strategic object: a representative now controls a shared report for a whole cohort, whose members’ assignments can split across posts. A guarantee that no positive mass receives a bad post captures an almost-everyone or cohort-level guarantee. It can overlook a single bad recipient: in a finite clone market, one bad assignment among \(n\) members has mass \(1/n\), which disappears in the limit. Preserving the theorem’s exact worst-person criterion would require tracking that recipient separately. The cohort formulation therefore looks like a plausible group-manipulation project, rather than a continuization of Theorem 2’s unilateral manipulation problem.

My case against the anchor rests on that mismatch in the unit of strategic control and the guarantee. Its weakness is the cohort escape: the paper’s authors might reasonably recognize group manipulation as a natural extension of their rank-maximal matching work. I can make a strong case against counting this as a mirror of the stated theorem; I cannot honestly establish that every cohort version would be unworthy of ChoCo.
