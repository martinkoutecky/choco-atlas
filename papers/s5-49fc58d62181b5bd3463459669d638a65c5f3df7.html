What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; Theorems 1 and 2 are axiomatic results, and the caseload optimization is not analyzed in the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that any preference relation over continuous solution functions satisfying continuity, monotonicity, disagreement indifference, and mixture symmetry has a representation \(V(f)=\int f_1(S)f_2(S)\,d\mu(S)\), for a full-support measure \(\mu\) on the space of bargaining games. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that under those axioms the Nash bargaining solution is the unique most-preferred solution. Both are axiomatic characterization results. Neither classifies the complexity of an encoded problem or gives an algorithm for one, so neither can honestly serve as an anchor of the kind requested.

The closest positive lead is a caseload interpretation of Theorem 2. Imagine a large arbitration service handling many bargaining pairs, with repeated, identical kinds of cases. A type is a feasible utility set \(S_i\); its mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of cases of that type. A policy chooses \(x_i\in S_i\) for each type to maximize \(\sum_i\mu_i x_{i1}x_{i2}\). This is a precise continuous-population optimization question, and Theorem 2 gives it an intuitive pointwise answer: choose the Nash outcome for each case type. A natural regime would have many cases and relatively few distinct feasible sets.

That lead is weak as a mirror of the paper’s central question. The paper studies an arbitrator’s preferences over rules defined across the whole space of games; its representing measure is over games, not over a society of bargaining pairs. Treating that measure as caseload mass changes its meaning, and a finite list of case types does not have the theorem’s full-support property. The caseload problem is therefore a plausible adjacent application, but it is not a continuous problem anchored by a named computational result in this paper. Under the requested anchor rule, there is no defensible lead question to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that neither named theorem is a computational result. Theorem 1 represents preferences over solution rules by an integral; Theorem 2 characterizes the best rule under four axioms. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem to mirror. The paper’s main object is an arbitrator’s preference over rules defined across bargaining games—not a population whose members’ actions or attributes are being aggregated.

The caseload interpretation is coherent: repeated arbitration cases could be grouped by identical feasible utility sets, with \(\mu_i\) the fraction of cases of type \(i\). But the measure in Theorem 1 is a representation of the arbitrator’s preferences over games, not a distribution of cases. Replacing it with caseload mass changes its role. A finite list of case types also cannot supply the theorem’s full-support measure over the space of games.

The stronger version—give a caseload distribution \(\nu\) over games and choose a rule \(f\) to maximize \(\int f_1(S)f_2(S)\,d\nu(S)\)—is a legitimate continuous-population question about cases. It still does not mirror the paper’s result: it turns a preference representation into an aggregate objective over a caseload. Theorem 2 says which rule is best under the paper’s axioms; it does not analyze an encoded caseload or its computational complexity. If cases are independent, the population weights introduce no coupling. Adding shared budgets or cross-case constraints could produce a richer mass-allocation problem, but those constraints would be new structure absent from the bargaining model.

I would not argue that repeated arbitration is an implausible high-multiplicity regime. That is the negative case’s weak point: it may support a worthwhile neighboring project. But on the programme’s stated gate, it does not rescue a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. The caseload would supply a new computational problem, rather than continuize one the authors studied.
