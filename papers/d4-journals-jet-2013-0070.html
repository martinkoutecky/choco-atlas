The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case that meets the anchor requirement. The paper contains no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Its results characterize mechanism properties; they do not analyze algorithms or computational complexity.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that when the preference domain allows elevation of pairs and is flexible, a social choice function is truthfully implementable if and only if it is constant, binary implementable, or a lexicographic affine maximizer. Corollary 5.10, also established in the paper, gives a continuous-valuation domain where those domain conditions apply. These are substantial characterization results, but their continuity is in agents’ valuation functions over the alternative space \(A\). The paper’s society is still a finite, labeled set of \(n\) agents with a profile of reports—not a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types.

A natural adjacent scenario would be many residents choosing among public-good levels, with a small number \(\tau\) of shared valuation-function types and masses \(\mu_t\). One could ask how truthful implementation works when the mechanism takes \(\mu\) as input. But if the mechanism depends only on the population distribution, a single agent’s report does not change \(\mu\) in an atomless model; preserving the paper’s individual incentive question therefore requires additional modeling choices. That would be a new continuous-agent mechanism-design question, not a computational mirror anchored in this paper.

So there is no prover-ready continuous problem per qualifying anchor, and no defensible tractability or hardness-transfer prediction. The weak point is decisive for this programme: although the paper studies truthful social choice and continuous valuation functions, its named results do not address computational questions, and its society is not continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper states no computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes which social choice functions are truthfully implementable on rich preference domains; Corollary 5.10 shows that continuous valuation domains satisfy the theorem’s conditions. Neither gives an algorithm, complexity bound, or computational problem with an input representation. The proposed mirror would have to add that problem.

The best candidate is a public-good setting with a rational distribution \(\mu\) over valuation types: compute an alternative maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\sigma_t v_t(a)+q(a)\), with the paper’s lexicographic treatment of zero-weight agents. That is a plausible algorithmic mechanism-design question, but it is not a computational question asserted by Theorem 1. And Corollary 5.10’s continuity concerns each agent’s valuation *over alternatives*; it does not make the population continuous. If the reports are restricted to a finite set of types to get a finite-support input, the rich-domain conditions behind Theorem 1 may no longer hold. If they are not restricted, the mechanism still has to handle the paper’s full preference domain.

The incentive problem is sharper. For a public alternative chosen solely from an atomless population distribution, one person’s report cannot change that distribution. With transfers, truthfulness then forces each person’s payment to be the same across reports, so the individual incentive condition no longer constrains the social choice rule. Keeping a person’s \(1/n\) influence makes the model finite at the strategic margin; making a whole type cohort strategic changes the unit of incentive from an individual to a group. Private-goods allocations could retain individual incentives, but they require an allocation schedule and capacity structure that this theorem does not specify.

So the continuous-valuation results do not supply a continuous-population complexity mirror. One could study algorithms for affine-maximizing mechanisms in large populations, and that may be worthwhile on its own. The honest weakness in the negative case is that the authors might recognize that adjacent problem as theirs. But under the programme’s gate—an identifiable computational question anchored in the paper’s results—it is a new project, not a mirror.
