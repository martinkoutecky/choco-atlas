What the mirror covers

The proposed task covers Proposition 2's large-electorate cutpoint and quadrant vote masses; it leaves Propositions 1 and 3 and the broader equilibrium analysis alone.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that Proposition 2 already identifies a large-electorate limit object. But the anchor requirement has a firm limit: this paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification. Proposition 2 is proved here, but it is an equilibrium-structure theorem, not a result about computing an equilibrium. It is therefore a promising *supporting* result, not a qualifying computational anchor.

Proposition 2 says that under Assumptions 1–3—four parties at the square’s corners, preferences single-peaked in each coordinate with independent peaks, and a continuously differentiable utility function—there is a unique cutpoint \(\theta^-\), defined by the paper’s fixed-point correspondence built from the marginal bliss-point distributions. For every \(\eta>0\), some \(n_0\) exists such that, in every mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium with \(n\ge n_0\), voters at least \(\eta\) below the cutpoint in both coordinates vote for LB; those at least \(\eta\) above it in both vote for RT; voters below it in the first coordinate and above it in the second vote for LT; and voters above it in the first and below it in the second vote for RB. The proposition is proved in the paper’s appendix. It characterizes what voters do in large finite electorates; it does not give an algorithm or complexity bound.

A plausible follow-on is the Limit-Equilibrium Cutpoint and Vote-Mass Problem. An instance is a rational, piecewise-constant density \(\mu\) over bliss points in \([0,1]^2\), with continuous marginals, in the paper’s four-corner model and under its preference assumptions. A type is a bliss point \(\theta\); its mass is its fraction \(d\mu(\theta)\) of society; its action is a vector of vote probabilities \(p_k(\theta)\) over the four parties. The question is to compute the cutpoint \(\theta^-\) and the limiting aggregate vote shares \(q_k=\int p_k(\theta)\,d\mu(\theta)\), where the profile assigns each point to the corner corresponding to its quadrant around \(\theta^-\). There is no objective to minimize: a solution is the cutpoint and shares selected as the limit of finite-electorate Nash equilibria. For continuous marginals, each cutpoint coordinate satisfies \(\theta^-_j=1-F_j(\theta^-_j)\), and the quadrant masses determine the four shares.

This is a credible population mirror because the paper itself studies electorates of increasing size and derives the cutpoint from population distributions. With the stated density representation, the cutpoint equations are monotone one-dimensional equations and the quadrant masses are integrals, so the natural conjecture is tractability. Further questions include whether those aggregate limits are unique for every convergent sequence of finite electorates, and how much of the result extends beyond corner parties.

The weakness is central: in an ordinary nonatomic voting game, one voter cannot change the policy outcome, so every vote may be a best response. The proposed problem preserves the strategic content by defining its solution through limits of finite-game equilibria, rather than ordinary nonatomic Nash equilibrium. That makes it a plausible continuation of Proposition 2, but also means the computational question is new work, not a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the only anchor. It gives an asymptotic description of equilibria, but states no computational problem or complexity result. The proposed cutpoint and vote-mass task adds one: given a distribution, calculate an output implied by that description. That distinction matters for ChoCo’s aim of mapping the computational landscape.

For the proposed atomless, piecewise-constant density, the calculation is direct. Each cutpoint coordinate solves \(\theta_j+F_j(\theta_j)=1\); with rational piecewise-constant input, \(F_j\) is piecewise linear, so the root can be found exactly by scanning its pieces. The four vote shares are the masses in the quadrants around \(\theta^-\), obtained by integrating the density there. Continuous marginals give the cutpoint lines zero mass, so they do not leave an unresolved cohort. The result is a small calculation on the input distribution, not a strategic optimization problem. Allowing more general densities would shift the difficulty to the representation or integration oracle.

A direct nonatomic equilibrium model loses the strategic feature the paper studies: one voter has zero mass, so changing their vote does not change the outcome, and every vote is a best response. The proponent’s finite-electorate limit preserves the paper’s strategic meaning, but under the stated continuous-marginal assumptions Proposition 2 already pins down the limiting aggregate shares through the cutpoint and quadrant masses. There is no equilibrium computation left after that limit is taken.

The best rescue is to allow positive mass at a cutpoint, or to ask for a finite-size refinement that retains pivotal effects. Proposition 2 does not determine how such a cohort votes: its \(\eta\)-neighborhood argument no longer makes the cohort’s mass disappear. A set of possible limiting vote shares could be a worthwhile problem, but defining it requires an additional selection or approximation model. That would be a new research question prompted by the paper, rather than a computational mirror established by one of its results. Section 4’s extension to arbitrary party positions does not supply another computational anchor; identifying the convex hull and evaluating the stated extrema are geometric calculations, while the proposition leaves the full equilibrium shares unspecified.

So the negative case is strongest for the proposed atomless mirror: it either has trivial nonatomic incentives or reduces to evaluating a limit that the theorem already characterizes. I would not claim the paper rules out every worthwhile future model. A carefully specified finite-size or atomic refinement might be interesting; its promise is also where the case against is weakest.
