The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), item categories \(J\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), and effectively represented nonnegative, nondecreasing, differentiable concave valuations \(v_t\), choose a finite-support mass assignment \(y_{t,b}\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{J}\). Require \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,b} b_j y_{t,b}\leq q_j\) for each category, and maximize \(\sum_{t,b} y_{t,b}v_t(\sum_j u_{tj}b_j)\). Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in the compressed input size \((\tau, |J|, L, 1/\epsilon)\) returning welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/((1+\epsilon)\max_t\kappa_t)\), where \(\kappa_t\) is the paper’s local multiplicative curvature for \(v_t\) and the category utilities?

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents represented by types and masses, with per-capita supplies of repeated item categories. Each individual receives an integral bundle; the decision variables distribute type mass across bundles, and welfare is the mass-weighted sum of the paper’s concave-additive valuations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the general \(\mathsf{ICA}\) approximation problem anchored by Theorem 1.1. It leaves the integrality-gap result, the additive guarantee, and the smooth asymmetric Nash-welfare and WBB applications outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the bundle-pricing problem for \(\max_b\{v_t(\sum_j u_{tj}b_j)-\sum_j p_jb_j\}\) be solved in time polynomial in the compressed input?
  • Can the paper’s curvature-controlled guarantee be achieved with runtime polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|J|\), and \(L\), independent of the expanded population and item counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s utilitarian welfare problem, \(\mathsf{ICA}\). The excerpt gives no numbered hardness result proved in the paper; its hardness claims are citations to earlier work. So my lead anchor is algorithmic.

Theorem 1.1, proved here, says that for an \(\mathsf{ICA}\) instance with nonnegative differentiable concave valuations, writing \(U_i^{\max}=\sum_j u_{ij}\) and \(r_{\max}=\max_i \bigl(v_i'(0)U_i^{\max}/v_i(U_i^{\max})\bigr)\), there is an algorithm with approximation ratio \((1+\epsilon)\max_i\mu_i\) and running time \(O(mn\ln(r_{\max}/\epsilon)/\epsilon)\). Here \(\mu_i\) is the paper’s local multiplicative curvature, determined by agent \(i\)’s valuation and the utilities of individual items.

A plausible regime is a large allocation program with millions of recipients but a much smaller set of complete profiles: each profile fixes a recipient’s utility for every item category and their concave valuation curve. For example, a platform might have many advertisers using a relatively small menu of bid schedules and soft-budget contracts. Inventory also has repeated categories, with the number of impressions growing roughly in proportion to the advertiser population. The high-multiplicity premise is that agents with a profile are indistinguishable in every parameter the allocation uses.

I would ask about High-Multiplicity Concave-Additive Allocation. The input is a finite type set \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(J\) of item categories, and per-capita supplies \(q_j\). Each type \(t\) has utility \(u_{tj}\) for one item of category \(j\), and a nonnegative, nondecreasing, differentiable concave valuation \(v_t\). For a concrete input model, the curves could be explicit rational functions with rational coefficients. A bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}^{J}\) gives a member of type \(t\) utility \(\sum_j u_{tj}b_j\). The decision variable \(y_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned that *integral* bundle. Feasibility requires \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,b} b_j y_{t,b}\leq q_j\) for every item category \(j\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,b} y_{t,b}v_t(\sum_j u_{tj}b_j)\). A solution is a feasible finite-support mass assignment \(y\).

The computational question I would give a prover is whether, for every \(\epsilon>0\), this problem admits an algorithm polynomial in the compressed input size—type count \(\tau\), category count \(|J|\), bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\)—that returns welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/((1+\epsilon)\rho)\), where \(\rho=\max_t\mu_t\) and \(\mu_t\) is the paper’s local curvature computed from \(v_t\) and the item utilities \(u_{tj}\). This preserves the paper’s individual-level indivisibility: mass may be divided among bundle choices, but each unit of mass receives an integral bundle.

I expect Class A for this curvature-controlled approximation question, especially in small-item regimes where the paper’s curvature guarantee approaches one. The continuous problem has a linear objective over bundle masses, though potentially many bundle choices; its real algorithmic hinge is whether the resulting pricing problem over integral bundles can be handled efficiently. That is a new question, not a consequence of Theorem 1.1.

The mirror is plausible because it keeps the paper’s underlying problem intact: each agent has a concave function of additive utility, items are uniquely allocated, and welfare is the sum of agent valuations. The changes are the high-multiplicity representation of identical agents and the per-capita inventory needed for a population limit. The authors could reasonably recognize this as their problem in a new regime.

The main weakness is that Theorem 1.1 runs in the expanded instance’s \(m\) and \(n\); it does not give a runtime in \(\tau\), \(|J|\), and \(L\). Moreover, compressed bundle pricing could prove hard and put the unrestricted problem in Class C. Some real populations may also have too many distinct full utility profiles to compress. Those points limit the case, but they do not make the mirror artificial: they identify the computational boundary the continuous version would investigate. This case covers the paper’s general \(\mathsf{ICA}\) welfare result, not its separate smooth Nash-welfare and WBB results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives a genuine computational anchor: Theorem 1.1 proves an approximation algorithm for \(\mathsf{ICA}\). So the decisive objection that there is no computational result to mirror does not apply.

The proposed formal question does have a concrete error. It defines \(\rho\) as the largest type mass, then asks for welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/((1+\epsilon)\rho)\). When there is more than one type, \(\rho<1\), so this bound can exceed \(\mathrm{OPT}\). That is a fixable statement error, though: use a separate symbol for the paper’s curvature bound and state the approximation in terms of that bound.

The harder objection is that the mirror needs two repeated structures: many agents with the same complete utility profile, and many items that can be grouped into categories with common utilities across those profiles. For arbitrary \(\mathsf{ICA}\) instances, either assumption may fail, and an exact profile may include utilities for every distinct item. Then the proposed compressed input can be nearly as large as the original instance.

But that objection does not defeat the better version. In a replicated allocation market with a small menu of agent profiles and repeated item categories, each person can still receive an integral bundle. The mass variables simply describe what fraction of each type receives each bundle. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting bundle-mass optimization has a real computational hinge: pricing integral bundles under the category supplies. It is not already answered by the paper’s fractional-item program; fractional items can give an individual a divisible share, whereas the mirror assigns each person a whole bundle. Nor does the cited high-multiplicity work establish that this particular continuous computational question is already solved.

The negative case is therefore weak. The item and agent repetition assumptions may restrict the range of instances, and the proposed approximation bound needs correction, but a plausible regime survives both objections. I cannot honestly argue from this paper that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
