The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\), a support of \(\tau\) rankings \(\pi_t\in S_n\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and fixed positive rational item weights \(w(e)\), find a ranking \(\hat{x}\in S_n\) satisfying \(F_\mu(\hat{x})\le (1.9677+\varepsilon)\min_{x\in S_n}F_\mu(x)\), where \(F_\mu(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t d_U^w(x,\pi_t)\) and \(d_U^w\) is the element-weighted Ulam distance, in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length, for each fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(\pi_t\in S_n\). Each type carries mass \(\mu_t\); the shared item weights \(w(e)\) define the cost of moving an item. The decision variable is a consensus ranking \(x\in S_n\), with objective \(F_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t d_U^w(x,\pi_t)\).

The objection that survived

The paper proves its guarantee for uniformly sampled finite inputs; extending the stated approximation factor to arbitrary rational masses, including repeated sampled types, still requires a proof.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1's element-weighted Ulam 1-median approximation. It leaves the MPC results, other metrics, and the broader framework results outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper's \(1.9677+O(\delta)\) guarantee be established for arbitrary binary rational masses in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length?
  • Can the approximation factor be improved for the rational-mass version?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Weighted Ulam Population Median. A type is a complete ranking \(\pi_t\in S_n\) of the same \(n\) alternatives; the instance gives \(\tau\) supported types with binary rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\). A fixed positive rational weight \(w(e)\) for each alternative specifies the common cost of moving that alternative. For rankings \(x,\pi\), let
\[ d_U^w(x,\pi)=\min_{S}\sum_{e\notin S}w(e), \]
where \(S\) ranges over common subsequences of \(x\) and \(\pi\). The decision variable is a consensus ranking \(x\in S_n\), and its objective is
\[ F_\mu(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,d_U^w(x,\pi_t). \]
The question is: given \(n\), the supported rankings and their masses, and \(w\), output a ranking \(\hat x\) with \(F_\mu(\hat x)\le(1.9677+\varepsilon)\min_{x\in S_n}F_\mu(x)\), for any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length.

My anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper: its Ulam entry says Algorithm 1 gives a \((1.9677+O(\delta))\)-approximation to the 1-median under the element-weighted Ulam metric, in \(O(n^{17})\) time. This is the closest named result to the mirror’s objective. The paper’s cited NP-hardness for Ulam median is not a numbered result proved there, so I do not present it as an anchor. The theorem’s stated input is a finite set of rankings; the mirror asks for the corresponding guarantee when that population is supplied by rational masses over its support.

A plausible regime is a very large preference consultation on a fixed slate: millions of participants rank the same alternatives, many produce identical full rankings, and there are relatively few distinct ranking types. The public item weights are shared across participants, so two participants with the same ranking are indistinguishable for this objective. This is a high-multiplicity regime: the number of agents can greatly exceed \(\tau\). It is also a sensible rank-aggregation scenario, rather than a claim that every electorate has few types.

The authors should recognize this as their problem with a high-multiplicity input. The consensus remains a permutation, the loss remains weighted Ulam distance, and the objective remains the average distance from the input rankings. The key change is that the society is represented by rational mass on types, rather than one equally weighted record per distinct ranking. This also avoids conflating the paper’s phrase “continuous version”—where the median may be any permutation—with a continuous population: \(S_n\) is still finite, while the mirror makes the population the distribution.

I expect the anchored approximation question to be tractable. The paper’s framework samples constant-size sets of input rankings, constructs local candidate medians, and evaluates candidates against sampled rankings. In the mirror, sampling types according to \(\mu\) and evaluating costs against the support are natural replacements; rational masses also permit exact objective comparisons. This is a reason to expect the approximation guarantee to extend, not a claim that Theorem 5.1 already proves the extension. The scope here is the offline weighted Ulam 1-median result; I would not stretch it to cover the paper’s MPC results or its other metrics.

The exact version is a separate question, and the paper cites Ulam 1-median hardness from outside work. Any finite instance can be represented by assigning equal rational mass to its input rankings, so that hardness would transfer to exact optimization on the continuous-population formulation. That does not rule out the stated approximation. Natural follow-ups are whether the factor can be improved for arbitrary rational masses, and how the algorithm’s running time depends on \(\tau\) and the mass bit lengths.

My weakest point is that the paper’s proofs and statements use uniform sampling from a finite set; they do not establish all details needed for arbitrary rational masses, especially when sampling repeats a type or the support is small. Those look like extension issues for the sampling and local-median analysis, rather than a mismatch in the underlying rank-aggregation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is that its continuous version may require almost no new algorithmic work. The objective is just a weighted 1-median:
\[ F_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\,d_U^w(x,\pi_t). \]
Theorem 5.1 already gives a polynomial-time approximation for the uniform finite input. Its framework samples rankings and evaluates candidates by average cost, so sampling from \(\mu\) appears to be the natural extension. For rational masses, one can also view \(\mu\) as multiplicities in a finite electorate; repeated rankings should not invalidate the argument. That makes this look more like a short extension of the existing proof than a new complexity landscape.

But that concern is about how much work the mirror might take, not whether it is a worthwhile question. The paper’s “continuous version” lets the median be any permutation; it does not make the population continuous. The proposed mirror does, and its fixed-slate consultation scenario is plausible: agents with the same ranking are indistinguishable for this objective, and their mass is meaningful. High multiplicity supports the model rather than colliding with it.

I therefore cannot honestly defeat Theorem 5.1 as an anchor. The proponent’s caveat about weighted sampling and repeated types is real, but looks like an extension issue, not a fundamental mismatch. The negative case here is weak; under the programme’s stated standard, this mirror survives.
