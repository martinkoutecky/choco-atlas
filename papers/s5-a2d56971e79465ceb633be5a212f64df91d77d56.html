The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), side, acceptable opposite-side types, weak rankings, and integer per-person quotas \((\ell_t,u_t)\), consider atomless cohorts whose members are interchangeable. A feasible outcome is a measurable matching relation in which almost every person's degree is an integer at most their upper quota. Call it critical if it minimizes \(D(M)=\int_A\max\{0,\ell(a)-\deg_M(a)\}\,da+\int_B\max\{0,\ell(b)-\deg_M(b)\}\,db\); call it relaxed-stable if almost every potential opposite-side pair that blocks satisfies one of Definition 6's endpoint justifications. Does an algorithm polynomial in the number of types \(\tau\), mass bit length \(L\), and quota parameters return a critical relaxed-stable matching with mass cardinality at least \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the maximum?

The model it lives in

An atomless bipartite population over complete types \(t\), each with mass \(\mu_t\), side, acceptable opposite-side types, weak rankings, and integer individual quotas. Outcomes retain integral bundles for almost every individual, while each type may distribute its mass across different bundle patterns; deficiency is integrated over people, and relaxed stability rules out positive-mass blocking deviations under the endpoint conditions of Definition 6.

What the mirror covers

The mirror targets Theorem 1's existence result and its \(\frac{2}{3}\)-approximation for maximum-cardinality critical relaxed-stable matchings, including the stable-matching subcase without lower quotas. It leaves the paper's separate algorithmic details and uncertain-proposal analysis outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a finite encoding over integral bundle patterns yield a polynomial-time \(\frac{2}{3}\)-approximation in \(\tau\), mass bit length, and quota parameters?
  • Does the discrete hardness for maximum-size stable matching persist when each type's mass can split across assignments, or does that freedom make the problem tractable?
  • How closely do the values for rational-mass instances correspond to finite high-multiplicity cohorts?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make the one-to-one stable hospital coverage problem the lead mirror: it gives a named hardness result and a direct bridge to this paper’s critical stable matchings. The broader many-to-many result then supplies a second, proved-here anchor.

Take a large placement system with residents and hospital positions—or students and course sections. A type records an agent’s side, complete preference order over opposite-side types, and per-agent lower and upper quotas. Everyone in a type has the same preferences and quotas; members of a counterpart type are tied. The regime is \(N\gg\tau\): many agents, relatively few such templates. The paper itself motivates matching with ties and lower quotas through residents and hospitals and students and courses, so this asks its matching question in a natural repeated-cohort regime.

My lead anchor is Theorem 13 of GMMY22, cited in the paper’s Section 2.3, rather than proved by these authors. As the paper describes it, Theorem 13 shows that HRT-MSLQ is NP-hard in the one-to-one setting: residents have unit upper quota and no lower quota; hospitals have unit upper quota and lower quota zero or one; preferences are complete and may contain ties; the task is to maximize total hospital satisfaction over stable matchings. The paper’s own Claim 1 supplies the bridge: when hospital upper quotas are one, a matching is stable and critical exactly when it is a stable matching with maximum total satisfaction. I treat Claim 1 as that bridge, not as a separate eligible anchor.

The corresponding continuous problem is Mass-HRT-MSLQ. An instance gives resident types \(i\) and hospital types \(j\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), complete weak rankings over opposite-side types, and hospital lower quotas \(\ell_j\in\{0,1\}\). Each individual has upper quota one; residents have lower quota zero. A solution is a mass matching \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of resident–hospital pairs assigned between types \(i\) and \(j\), with
\[ \sum_j x_{ij}\leq \alpha_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}\leq \beta_j. \]
This is realizable as a matching of individuals in atomless type classes: each individual receives either zero or one partner, while a type’s aggregate matched mass may be fractional.

For each type pair \((i,j)\), define
\[ w^R_{ij}=\alpha_i-\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'},\qquad w^H_{ij}=\beta_j-\sum_{i':\,i'\succeq_j i}x_{i'j}. \]
These are the masses of residents and hospitals, respectively, who are unmatched or strictly prefer the other type to their current partner. The assignment is stable when \(w^R_{ij}=0\) or \(w^H_{ij}=0\) for every pair of types: otherwise positive masses on both sides can form a blocking pair. The objective is to maximize
\[ \sum_{j:\ell_j=0}\beta_j+\sum_{j:\ell_j=1}\sum_i x_{ij}. \]
That is the mass-weighted satisfaction objective; with unit hospital quotas, maximizing it is also minimizing total hospital deficiency. The requested output is a stable mass matching attaining this optimum.

I expect this problem to be Class B, because the cited hardness concerns the pattern of preferences and ties, which remains present across the \(\tau\) types. That is a hypothesis, not a transferred theorem: a reduction must survive the new ability to split each type’s mass among assignments. If that fractional freedom yields a compact formulation instead, discovering that would be a valuable Class A result.

The second anchor is this paper’s Theorem 1, proved here. It says that for any bipartite graph with positive upper quotas, nonnegative lower quotas, and possibly tied preferences on both sides, a critical relaxed-stable matching exists, and a polynomial-time algorithm computes a \(\frac{2}{3}\)-approximation to a maximum-cardinality critical relaxed-stable matching.

Its continuous counterpart is Mass-Max-CRSM. Give each side finitely many types with rational masses, per-agent integer lower and upper quotas, acceptable opposite-side types, and weak rankings over those types. A solution is a measurable matching relation \(M\) in which almost every individual has an integral number of partners, bounded by their upper quota. Define its mass deficiency by
\[ D(M)=\int_A\max\{0,\ell(a)-\deg_M(a)\}\,da+ \int_B\max\{0,\ell(b)-\deg_M(b)\}\,db, \]
and its mass cardinality by \(\lvert M\rvert_{\mathrm{mass}}=\int_A\deg_M(a)\,da\). Call \(M\) critical if it minimizes \(D\) over all matchings. Use the paper’s Definition 6 for relaxed stability: every blocking pair must be justified from at least one endpoint, with the same saturation and nonsurplus conditions. In the continuum, impose this almost everywhere, so null exceptions do not determine the outcome. The task is to find a critical relaxed-stable \(M\) of maximum mass cardinality, or a \(\frac{2}{3}\)-approximation, with running time measured in \(\tau\), the mass bit length \(L\), and quota parameters.

I would tentatively put unrestricted Mass-Max-CRSM in Class C: integral per-agent bundles and the relaxed-stability test can create combinatorial constraints among type-level bundle patterns even after headcount disappears. For fixed per-agent quota bounds, Class A looks plausible through a configuration formulation or a proposal algorithm operating on masses. The theorem’s guarantee gives a concrete target for either result: establish whether its \(\frac{2}{3}\) approximation survives in time polynomial in the type input, or locate the obstruction.

Both mirrors keep the paper’s underlying object: agents on two sides, preferences, individual quotas, stability conditions, and matching. Lower-quota fulfillment remains an individual property in the continuous model; it is measured by the integral of individual deficiencies, not by comparing an average assignment against an average quota. Average assignments can be fractional because a type’s mass is divided among people receiving different integral bundles.

The soft spot is stability under atomless mass. In the finite graph, even one blocking pair violates stability. The mass model ignores null exceptions and treats positive-mass groups able to block as the consequential deviations. That is a real modeling choice. It is defensible for large cohorts with repeated types, and avoids letting a measure-zero individual determine the outcome, but the authors could reasonably ask for a pointwise version or a theorem relating the two.

The main follow-up questions are whether rational mass solutions have finite bundle-pattern certificates, whether discrete high-multiplicity instances approximate their values with a provable error, and whether the hardness reduction survives fractional type allocations. This scope covers the stable-critical special case and Theorem 1’s maximum-size critical relaxed-stable problem; it makes no claim about the paper’s other matching objectives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a convincing universal case against these mirrors. Matching is unusually favorable terrain for continuization: repeated applicant, worker, course, or employer profiles can be a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s individual quotas and preference conditions have natural mass-weighted counterparts. The objections that would be decisive elsewhere—no computational result, no repeated types, or an objective that tracks named individuals—do not rule out that regime here.

The weaker anchor is Theorem 13 of GMMY22: it is cited prior work, not a result proved in this paper. Still, the paper’s Claim 1 connects its one-to-one special case to stable critical matching, so that provenance does not dispose of the mirror. The proposed Mass-HRT-MSLQ is coherent: with unit quotas, type-level assignment masses can represent atomless cohorts, and positive masses on both sides of a strictly blocking type pair give a meaningful stability violation. A stronger objection is that this changes the market’s symmetry: members of each counterpart type must be indistinguishable, often tied, and assignments can split a cohort. That may make the continuous question quite different from the finite instances behind the hardness result. But it is also exactly the high-multiplicity regime, and it does not make the question unrecognizable or uncomputational. The fate of the hardness under splitting is something to determine, not a reason to reject the mirror.

Theorem 1 is harder to defeat. Its maximum-cardinality critical relaxed-stability problem has an evident mass version, using per-capita integral bundles, integrated deficiency, and almost-everywhere relaxed stability. The best objection is semantic: in an atomless society, a single blocking pair has measure zero, and a single agent’s deficit cannot affect aggregate criticality. Requiring stability and criticality only at positive mass therefore replaces pointwise finite-agent conditions with aggregate ones. But the proposed version preserves integral bundles for individuals and gives quotas and deficiency a natural population interpretation. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their problem. A richer model could track distributions over bundle patterns within each type, so aggregate edge masses alone need not carry all the relaxed-stability information; that increases the modeling and algorithmic work, but does not remove the computational question.

So the negative case rests on a real concern—whether atomless, type-anonymous stability is close enough to the paper’s individual-level model to be worthwhile—not on a fundamental obstruction. Both anchors survive their stronger versions. I would not recommend grading this paper against the programme on the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
