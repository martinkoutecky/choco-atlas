The continuous mirror question

Given two answer types \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_A \ge \mu_B > 0\) with \(\mu_A+\mu_B=1\), self-confidences \(\alpha_X \in (0,1]\), and cross-routing shares \(\lambda_X \in [0,1]\), determine the absorbed mass of each answer under the paper’s two-block PPV process and report the winner or a tie. In particular, determine whether \(B\) wins exactly when \(r_A\lambda_A-r_B\lambda_B > \mu_A-\mu_B\), where \(r_X=(1-\alpha_X)/\alpha_X\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of delegates with two complete behavioral types, \(A\) and \(B\), carrying masses \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\). Each unit of type \(X\) sends \(\alpha_X\) to its own absorbing answer, \(t_X=(1-\alpha_X)\lambda_X\) to the other type, and the remaining mass within its own type; the task is to compute the two absorbed masses and their maximizing answer.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a two-cluster continuous version of PPV’s winner problem. Consider a large inference evaluation that runs many independent, fixed-size reasoning batches on the same question. Each batch acts as a delegate. In a two-answer regime, delegates fall into two behavioral types, \(A\) and \(B\): a type records its answer \(p_X\), its common self-confidence \(\alpha_X\), and the share \(\lambda_X\) of its delegated weight sent across to the other answer cluster. Within a type, delegates behave symmetrically. The type masses \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\) are fractions of all delegates, with \(\mu_A+\mu_B=1\) and \(\mu_A\ge\mu_B>0\). Thus the number of delegates can be very large while the support has just two types.

This regime is plausible for a service or benchmark that deliberately runs many replicas per question: the prompt and model settings are shared, and repeated reasoning batches can form two dominant answer clusters with similar confidence and routing behavior. The model preserves both of the paper’s levers: \(\alpha_X\) is When, and \(\lambda_X\) summarizes Whom’s cross-cluster routing. The symmetry assumption is restrictive, but it is exactly the idealization the paper uses for its theory.

The anchor is Theorem 1 (Flip condition), proved in this paper. It considers \(n=k+m\) delegates split into two blocks, with \(k\ge m\), and distinct answers \(p_A,p_B\). Every delegate in block \(X\) keeps fraction \(\alpha_X\in(0,1]\) on its own answer and splits the rest so that fraction \(\lambda_X\in[0,1]\) crosses to the other block; the remaining delegated mass stays within its block. Defining delegation odds \(r_X=(1-\alpha_X)/\alpha_X\), the theorem says PPV selects the minority answer \(p_B\) if and only if \(n(r_A\lambda_A-r_B\lambda_B)>k-m\). In words, the majority flips precisely when its odds-weighted leakage advantage exceeds its raw vote margin.

The corresponding continuous problem is Two-Cluster Continuum PPV Winner. Its instance consists of two answer policies \(p_A,p_B\), rational masses \(\mu_A\ge\mu_B>0\) summing to \(1\), rational self-confidences \(\alpha_X\in(0,1]\), and rational cross-routing shares \(\lambda_X\in[0,1]\). Each unit of mass of type \(X\) sends \(\alpha_X\) to its own absorbing answer, \(t_X=(1-\alpha_X)\lambda_X\) to the other type, and the remainder to its own type. The question is which answer receives more total absorbed mass; a solution returns both absorbed masses and the winner, reporting a tie if the masses are equal.

The theorem’s condition becomes \(r_A\lambda_A-r_B\lambda_B>\mu_A-\mu_B\). The masses themselves have the closed form \(M_A=\alpha_A[\mu_A(\alpha_B+t_B)+\mu_Bt_B]/D\) and \(M_B=1-M_A\), where \(D=\alpha_A\alpha_B+\alpha_At_B+\alpha_Bt_A>0\). These formulas give an exact polynomial-time algorithm in the binary input length. I expect this narrow mirror to be tractable: it asks the paper’s own question—when does delegation overturn the majority?—on a continuous population, with the same PPV process and the same two signals.

The mirror covers the paper’s two-block theory in Section 7, especially Theorem 1 and its winner characterization. The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result; this is its strongest relevant named computational result, and it is an exact decision characterization rather than a P-versus-NP classification. Natural follow-up questions are whether exact winner computation remains tractable with more answer types, heterogeneous confidence within answer clusters, or a general similarity-routing kernel; and how robust the winner is to perturbations in masses and routing shares.

The soft spot is that the paper’s theorem already handles finite block sizes, and the continuous condition follows by writing \(k/n=\mu_A\) and \(m/n=\mu_B\). So this is a narrow, exact high-multiplicity mirror, not a new algorithmic breakthrough. Nor should the two-type symmetry be mistaken for a model of the paper’s full heterogeneous \(16\)-delegate experiments. Still, the continuous object is the population of delegates, its types retain the paper’s behavioral distinctions, and the theorem itself supplies the precise bridge from finite counts to mass shares.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Theorem 1, the two-block flip condition. Its continuous version is natural, but its computational content is thin: the paper already proves an exact winner formula for every finite block size, and the formula depends on the block sizes only through their normalized difference. Replacing \(k/n\) and \(m/n\) by \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\) extends the allowed inputs; it does not expose a new algorithmic problem. That is not an objection merely because the answer is unchanged. The issue is that the theorem characterizes when this particular mechanism flips a winner; it does not identify a computational task whose complexity the continuous model would help chart.

The strongest broader mirror would preserve heterogeneous delegates and their reasoning similarities. Let a type \(x\) record a delegate’s answer, confidence, and embedding features, and let a routing kernel \(K(y\mid x)\) describe its delegation. The absorbed mass for answer \(p\) is then determined by an equation of the form
\[ u_p(x)=a_p(x)+\int K(y\mid x)u_p(y)\,d\mu(y), \]
where \(a_p(x)\) is the mass sent directly to \(p\). With finite support of size \(\tau\), this is an absorbing-chain calculation: solve a linear system, in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. With a genuinely continuous type space, exact computational complexity is not defined until the kernel and distribution have a finite representation; approximation then depends on those representation and regularity assumptions. This could be a useful analysis of a limiting delegation process, but the paper supplies no computational question or structure that turns it into a complexity landscape.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity story here: run many independent fixed-size sample batches on the same question. The obstacle is not that delegates lack plausible types. It is that the paper’s main objective is predictive accuracy against gold answers, while its theorem studies the PPV winner given a constructed matrix. A continuous distribution of unlabeled delegate signals does not itself determine correctness. Adding a task such as finding the least mass to alter to flip the winner would require new intervention costs and rules for how an alteration changes the embedding-based routing kernel. Those choices would define a new problem, rather than mirror a result in this paper.

So the negative case is that the proposed mirror is a scale-normalized restatement of a closed-form mechanism result, while the richer continuous version leads either to routine finite-dimensional linear algebra or to an underspecified operator problem. That makes it a weak fit for ChoCo’s computational programme. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight: a continuous population of delegate types is coherent, and the theorem’s mass formulation is especially natural. If the authors regard continuous-population PPV winner computation itself as their problem, this anchor may survive; the case against is strongest on programme value, not on the legitimacy of the model.
