What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 5.3 alone; it leaves the paper’s acyclicity, APDA, and simple-strategy-proofness characterizations untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow, and there is a qualification about the anchors: this paper gives no named NP-hardness, FPT, or other complexity-class result. The closest anchor is a structural characterization that implies an easy recognition problem. So this can support a continuous computational question, but it is not a complexity result stated by the authors.

My lead is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper. It says that on the unrestricted domain \(L^n(A\cup\{a_0\})\), an FPTTC rule is SOSP-implementable if and only if it is a serial dictatorship. For an explicitly given priority structure, this yields a polynomial-time recognition corollary: check whether every object’s priority ranking is the same ordering of agents. The theorem itself establishes the characterization; the recognition bound is its direct algorithmic consequence.

A plausible setting is a large annual public-housing allocation, with many applicants who share preference rankings and priority tiers, and many units in a small number of housing categories. Normalize the applicant population to mass \(1\). A continuous instance has a finite set \(H\) of priority cohorts, rational masses \(\rho_h>0\) summing to \(1\), object categories \(A\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_a\), with \(\sum_a q_a\leq 1\). A cohort’s complete type includes its ranking of \(A\cup\{a_0\}\) and its priority position for each category. Each unit of mass receives at most one indivisible object; an aggregate assignment \(x_{h,a}\) may split a cohort across categories while each person’s bundle remains integral.

I would hand a prover Mass-FPTTC SOSP Recognition. For each positive integer \(k\) clearing the denominators, replicate cohort \(h\) into \(k\rho_h\) agents and category \(a\) into \(kq_a\) distinct objects. Order agents first by cohort priority and then by a fixed order within each cohort; order copies within each category canonically. Run the paper’s FPTTC rule on these finite markets, requiring members of a cohort to report a common ranking, and aggregate the resulting assignments as proportions. The question is whether the resulting sequence of aggregate rules has a limit and whether that limit is implementable by a finite extensive-form mechanism in which each cohort reports once and truthful reporting is obviously dominant. For the cohort-level dominance test, compare the worst category receiving positive mass after a truthful report with the best category receiving positive mass after a deviation, using that cohort’s common ranking. A solution is a yes/no answer, with an implementing tree and cohort order in the yes case, or a failure witness in the no case.

The expected outcome is tractable, if the serial-dictatorship characterization survives this mass model: the candidate condition is a common priority ordering across categories, now over cohorts, and checking it takes time polynomial in \(|A|\) and \(|H|\). The continuous question is whether splitting mass and scaling object supplies preserve that characterization. It also asks whether the replication limit exists and is independent of harmless within-cohort refinements. This mirrors Theorem 5.3 alone; it does not claim to continue the paper’s APDA or other results.

The case is plausible because it retains the paper’s assignment setting, outside option, fixed priorities, FPTTC rule, and simple-dominance question. Scaling supply with population is a natural co-modification for housing categories with many units. The intended regime has many applicants and relatively few preference-priority cohorts.

The weakest point is that the paper gives strict priorities over named individuals, while the mirror groups agents into cohorts and lets each cohort report as a bloc. That changes who acts in the mechanism and how dominance is evaluated. A referee could reasonably say that the model has replaced individual SOSP with a cohort-level concept. Also, the anchor’s polynomial recognition consequence is inferred from a structural theorem, not stated as a complexity result. If the anchor requirement is read literally to require a theorem explicitly asserting membership in a complexity class, then this paper has no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the gap between the paper’s results and the programme’s target. None of its named theorems gives a complexity bound or an algorithm for a computational problem. Theorem 5.3 characterizes when an FPTTC rule is SOSP-implementable; it implies a recognition algorithm for a finite priority table, but that algorithm just checks whether every object has the same priority ordering. That is a real algorithmic consequence, though a very small one. A continuous version of that check would have little complexity landscape to chart.

More seriously, strict priorities make this paper’s population hard to compress into types. An agent’s priority position is part of the information the rule uses, and every agent has a distinct position in each object’s strict ranking. If priority is included in a complete type, the support therefore grows with the number of agents: \(\tau=n\). Grouping applicants by preference and priority *tier* omits information that determines ownership and assignments. Keeping the individual priority order preserves the rule, but gives up the high-multiplicity regime the mirror is meant to study.

The best repair is to give each cohort a shared priority tier and scale housing supply with population. Scaling supply is a natural co-modification. Shared tiers are more consequential: if they are broken by a strict order within each cohort, members again have distinct types; if they remain tied, the paper’s FPTTC procedure, which assigns each object to a single highest-priority agent before running TTC, needs a new mass version. A continuum of priority ranks could describe aggregate assignment, but the paper’s finite-agent ownership and trading cycles do not directly specify that version.

The proposed cohort-reporting mechanism also changes the strategic question. In the paper, individuals report preferences and SOSP tests an individual’s incentives. In the mirror, a positive-mass cohort reports as one actor, and a rule must compare aggregate assignments—possibly splitting the cohort across several categories. A common ordinal ranking does not by itself say how that cohort evaluates such a split. One could define a collective incentive concept, but that would be a new modeling choice. Keeping individuals as the actors avoids that choice, yet an individual has zero mass in the continuum, so their report cannot change aggregate supply or other agents’ assignments. The resulting incentive problem loses the finite-market interaction that makes the paper’s TTC analysis distinctive.

So Theorem 5.3’s strongest plausible mirror either preserves strict individual priorities and fails to compress the population, or introduces priority ties and cohort-level strategic behavior that the paper does not define. The negative case is not airtight: a continuum TTC model with priority tiers could be a worthwhile extension if the authors recognize it as their problem. But this paper does not establish that recognition, and its one computationally usable anchor offers only a simple structural check.
