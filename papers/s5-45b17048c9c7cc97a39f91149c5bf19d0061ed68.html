The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the paper’s service-firm interpretation: many small firms distribute service across consumer segments, with types defined by complete utility schedules and entitlements. A finite-support population distribution over those types would be a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A population-level claim game is conceivable, but taking firms to a continuum changes their strategic effect on aggregate claims; the paper does not define or analyze that game.

The anchor requirement is decisive here. The paper contains no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or giving an algorithmic result. The nearest candidates do not qualify: Theorem 2.7, proved here, establishes existence of a Nash equilibrium in every finite-estate game; Theorem 3.6, proved here, establishes existence in the infinite-estate game for positive entitlements and positively bounded piecewise-continuous utility functions; and Theorem 3.4, proved here using a cited result about homogeneous claim games, says the finite and step-function infinite-estate games have corresponding equilibria almost everywhere. These concern equilibrium existence or equivalence, not computational complexity, and their continuum is over estates while the players remain finite.

So there is no eligible anchor and no per-anchor continuous problem to state. The firm-population scenario is a possible research direction, but presenting it as a mirror of one of this paper’s computational results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The service-firm interpretation is the strongest lead, and a high-multiplicity regime is plausible: firms could share an entitlement and a complete utility schedule over consumer segments. The best continuous version would treat those firms as an atomless population. But then an individual firm’s deviation no longer affects aggregate claims, so this is a new strategic model; preserving the paper’s finite-player incentives would require a different population representation. That makes the proposed version a possible extension, not yet a mirror of a result in this paper.

The decisive obstacle is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2.7 proves Nash equilibrium existence for finitely many estates; Theorem 3.6 proves existence when estates form an interval; and Theorem 3.4 relates equilibria in those two estate models. None states the complexity of computing an equilibrium or solves another computational problem. The paper’s continuum is over estates, while its players remain finite.

One could formulate an algorithmic question about equilibrium computation in a continuous-firm game, with a high-multiplicity discrete counterpart and a complexity analysis. That may be worth studying, but it would add the computational problem that this paper does not pose. So the negative case is strong as a claim about a *mirror of this paper’s results*. It is weaker as the broader claim that no continuous-population extension of the service-firm idea could be worthwhile.
