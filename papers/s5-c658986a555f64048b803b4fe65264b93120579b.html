The continuous mirror question

For a finite machine-type set \(T\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^2\), a rational type distribution \(\mu\), and two job-class supplies \(q_1,q_2>0\) per unit mass, what is the smallest \(\rho\) achievable by a direct truthful-in-expectation mechanism? The mechanism must specify, for every population distribution \(\mu\) and every individual report \(r\in T\), a lottery over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\) and a payment, satisfy dominant-strategy truthfulness for each machine while holding the other machines’ distribution fixed, balance each job-class supply in aggregate, and guarantee makespan at most \(\rho\) times the unconstrained optimum.

The model it lives in

A unit mass of machines has types \(t=(t_1,t_2)\in T\) with distribution \(\mu\); bundle \(b\) gives a machine processing time \(t\cdot b\). A report-conditioned lottery \(\pi_\mu(r,b)\) and payment \(p_\mu(r)\) must satisfy \(\sum_r\mu_r\sum_b\pi_\mu(r,b)b_j=q_j\) for \(j\in\{1,2\}\), together with truthful-in-expectation incentive constraints for every true type and report at fixed \(\mu\). Minimize the worst-case makespan approximation ratio over such mechanisms.

The objection that survived

The proposed aggregate \(x_{t,b}\) does not define an individual machine’s outcome after a deviation to a report with zero mass; the per-report policy must specify those outcomes and enforce incentive compatibility.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror asks whether Theorem 4’s approximation barrier has an analogue in a high-multiplicity scheduling regime; it leaves the paper’s mechanism characterizations and its other approximation results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the optimal truthful-in-expectation ratio fall below \(2\) in this high-multiplicity regime?
  • How does the optimal ratio depend on \(|T|\) and the binary input length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of unrelated-machine scheduling. Machines are the agents; a machine’s type is its processing-time vector. That is a natural setting for a large cloud or production fleet: \(N\) machines may have only \(\tau\) distinct configurations, with \(N\gg\tau\), while workloads repeat across the fleet.

My anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for two players and two tasks, no truthful mechanism has approximation ratio below \(2\), where the objective is makespan. It is a sharp algorithmic-performance lower bound, rather than an NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. Its appeal as an anchor is that it identifies a precise barrier for truthful scheduling.

Here is a continuous problem that barrier motivates. Call it Two-Class High-Multiplicity Truthful Scheduling. The public domain is a finite set \(T\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^2\) of machine types, and the workload consists of two classes of identical jobs, with \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) jobs per unit mass of machines in class \(j\). The reported society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Type \(t=(t_1,t_2)\) means that a class-\(j\) job takes \(t_j\) time on that machine.

A schedule assigns every machine an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^2\). At the type-mass level, \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) machines receiving bundle \(b\), with
\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,b} b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\quad(j=1,2). \]
Thus the schedule may split a type’s mass across integral bundles, while each machine still gets whole jobs. Its makespan is \(\max_{x_{t,b}>0}t\cdot b\).

The question is to find the smallest \(\rho\) for which there is a direct truthful mechanism, operating over such societies, that assigns bundles and payments and guarantees makespan at most \(\rho\) times the optimal makespan. Use truthful-in-expectation mechanisms, with mass splits representing the continuum implementation of lotteries over integral bundles; each machine’s utility is its payment minus its processing cost, \(p-t\cdot b\). A solution is the mechanism together with its guarantee, or an impossibility proof for a proposed \(\rho\).

I would expect this lead problem to be tractable in a fixed-support regime, with configuration-LP methods as a plausible route; the main obstacle is expressing truthfulness across reports, not enumerating two-job bundle patterns. I would not assume Theorem 4’s factor \(2\) transfers. Repeated workloads and mass-level splitting change the feasible schedules, so a first question is whether the truthful ratio can fall below \(2\). Further questions include whether the ratio changes when randomization is removed, whether exact optimality is strategyproof, and how the problem scales with \(\tau\) and the binary encoding of \(q\).

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 4 concerns two indivisible strategic machines and two tasks. This mirror has a large machine population, repeated tasks, and mass-level allocation. The paper’s authors could recognize it as a high-multiplicity extension of their scheduling problem, but their theorem does not establish the continuous result; the point is to ask whether its barrier survives that extension.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4’s \(2\)-approximation barrier is a weak anchor for a population continuum: it concerns exactly two strategic machines and two indivisible tasks. Theorem 3 extends the task count, but still keeps two machines. So neither result studies what happens as the machine population grows.

The best repair is the proponent’s large-fleet scenario: many machines drawn from a small set of processing-time types, with workload scaling per machine. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and scaling the workload with the population is a natural co-modification. The trouble is that the proposed aggregate allocation \(x_{t,b}\) records masses, while truthfulness concerns what one machine gets after changing its report. A single machine has measure zero, so its deviation leaves \(\mu\) and \(x\) unchanged; for a report type with zero mass, \(x\) does not specify that machine’s allocation at all. One can add per-machine lotteries and impose incentive constraints, but then those individual outcomes and their feasibility must be specified separately from the mass allocation. The aggregate model alone does not carry the paper’s dominant-strategy requirement.

Keeping a finite fleet of size \(N\) restores one-machine deviations, but also restores the finite perturbations of the reported distribution that disappear in the continuum. Replacing those deviations with whole-cohort deviations changes the incentive requirement; adding a tagged-machine model creates a different continuum mechanism-design problem. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they are not immediate continuizations of Theorem 4.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and it is not decisive. The high-multiplicity fleet is plausible, and the authors could recognize a carefully specified version as their scheduling problem. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every scenario; the real weakness is that the proposed mass-allocation formulation does not yet preserve the theorem’s central strategic feature.
