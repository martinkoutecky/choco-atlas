The case FOR (proponent)

Under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no named computational result. Theorem 1, proved here, says that a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists under A1–A3, A5 and A6 when the parties have equal interest in power. Proposition 2, also proved here, says that under those assumptions the pure-strategy equilibrium is unique. Proposition 3 and Theorem 2 establish better-reply security and existence of a mixed-strategy equilibrium. These are equilibrium results; none gives a complexity classification or an algorithm. I can’t honestly present them as qualifying computational anchors.

The strongest adjacent problem I can defend is an unanchored computational follow-up to Proposition 2: *Homogeneous-Motivation Continuum Equilibrium Approximation*. An instance specifies rational piecewise-linear CDFs \(F\) and \(H\), with rational breakpoints and coefficients, a rational \(\beta\), party ideals \(\theta_1<\theta_m<\theta_2\), and a shared office value \(\chi\). The paper’s assumptions A1–A3, A5 and A6 are promised to hold. Voter type is ideal point \(\theta\), and mass \(dF(\theta)\) consists of voters with that ideal point. Each party chooses a platform \(x_i\in[0,1]\) and receives the paper’s expected payoff
\[ \pi_1(x_1,x_2)=p(x_1,x_2)\bigl(\psi(x_1,x_2;\theta_1)+\chi\bigr),\qquad \pi_2(x_1,x_2)=(1-p(x_1,x_2))\bigl(\psi(x_2,x_1;\theta_2)+\chi\bigr), \]
where \(p\) is defined from \(F\) and \(H\) as in the paper. Given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), output rational platforms \((x_1,x_2)\) such that neither party can gain more than \(\varepsilon\) by unilaterally changing its platform. The solution is therefore an \(\varepsilon\)-Nash equilibrium of the underlying platform game, not an encoding of it.

A plausible regime is a very large electorate described by a stable distribution of voter ideal points, with the number of voters far exceeding the number \(\tau\) of pieces needed to describe \(F\) and \(H\). The paper’s model makes that aggregate description natural, and authors would recognize the proposed task as computing an equilibrium of their platform game. I would investigate a Class A route for approximation: each party has a one-dimensional action, the model has explicit distribution functions, and Proposition 2 supplies uniqueness. The paper does not establish such an algorithm or bound its running time in \(\tau\) and input length \(L\). Useful further questions include whether the heterogeneous-motivation game permits efficient approximate mixed-equilibrium computation, and how these equilibria relate to those of finite electorates.

The soft spot is substantial: this is a new computation problem on a model whose population is already continuous, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result. Also, the paper uses a continuum of ideal-point types, whereas ChoCo’s central formulation groups agents into a finite set of complete types. Binning ideal points would make the representation finite, but voters within a bin would not have identical preferences. So this is a plausible computational extension of the paper’s analysis; under the stated anchor and high-multiplicity requirements, I cannot make it a fully compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Proposition 2 prove existence and uniqueness of a pure equilibrium; Proposition 3 and Theorem 2 prove existence of a mixed equilibrium. None defines a computational problem or gives an algorithm, complexity bound, or hardness result. Under the requested anchor rule, that leaves no compliant anchor.

The proposed \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium task is a plausible algorithmic question, but it does not supply the missing mirror. Its input \(F\) already describes a continuum of voter ideal points; \(H\) describes electoral uncertainty. The parameter \(\tau\), counting pieces of those functions, is not the support size of a high-multiplicity society, and the input has no finite electorate size \(n\) whose repeated voter types are being compressed. Computing platforms for this game would study an already-continuous model, with complexity tied to the encoding of its distributions and its two strategy variables.

A stronger candidate would start with finite electorates—perhaps voters grouped into repeated ideal-point types—and ask whether their equilibria can be approximated by the continuum model. That could be a worthwhile project, but it adds a finite-electorate game the paper does not analyze. Its main results assume a continuous \(F\); they do not establish a discrete-to-continuous identity or complexity statement for a finite game. The proposed bridge would therefore need a new result to anchor it.

The negative case is strongest on the scope of this assignment, not as a claim that equilibrium computation is uninteresting. An algorithm for the paper’s continuous game may merit study in algorithmic game theory. The material here does not make it a continuous mirror of a computational social-choice result.
