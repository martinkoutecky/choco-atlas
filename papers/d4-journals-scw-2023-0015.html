What the mirror covers

The proposed mass analogue covers Proposition 1's even-\(m\) claim; it leaves Proposition 2 and Propositions 3–6 unaddressed, and none of these results is computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a strict computational mirror. Propositions 1–6 establish social-choice properties; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Proposition 2, for example, characterizes when the majoritarian compromise rule selects socially acceptable alternatives for odd \(m\), depending on the finite voter count \(n\). It is a theorem about outcomes, not computational complexity.

The closest positive case is a population version of Proposition 1, proved in this paper: with an even number of alternatives, every majoritarian compromise winner is socially acceptable. A plausible regime is a large referendum or election where many voters share the same complete ranking, so the population has \(N\) voters but only \(\tau\) distinct rankings, with \(\tau \ll N\). A type is a strict ranking \(t\) of \(A\); its rational mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population.

For a candidate \(a\), let \(\pi_t(a)=m-r(t,a)+1\) be the satisfaction of type \(t\). Define the best majority satisfaction by

\[ q(\mu)=\max\left\{s\in\{1,\ldots,m\}:\exists a\in A,\ \sum_{t:\pi_t(a)\ge s}\mu_t\ge\frac12\right\}. \]

Let \(B_\mu=\{a:\exists t,\ \mu_t>0,\ \pi_t(a)=q(\mu)\}\), and let \(g_\mu(a)=\sum_{t:\pi_t(a)\ge q(\mu)}\mu_t\). The continuous majoritarian compromise winners are the candidates in \(B_\mu\) maximizing \(g_\mu(a)\). A candidate is socially acceptable when the mass ranking it above the line is at least the mass ranking it below the line.

That gives a precise population analogue: given an explicit finite support of rankings and rational masses, compute the continuous majoritarian compromise winners and determine whether every winner is socially acceptable. For even \(m\), the Proposition 1 analogue is expected to hold by the same mass-based argument. This is a sensible continuous extension of the authors’ rule: voters are grouped only when they are indistinguishable under the rankings the rule uses, and majorities become mass of at least one half.

The weakness is decisive for the programme’s purpose: this is a mirror of a structural outcome theorem, not a named computational result. With support given explicitly, the quantities above can be computed by summing masses over rankings and thresholds. The paper neither studies that computation nor establishes a complexity result about it. So this is a credible population analogue, but it cannot honestly serve as a qualifying complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–2 characterize when majoritarian compromise winners are socially acceptable; Propositions 3, 4, and 6 establish that relation on restricted preference domains. None studies an algorithmic problem, complexity, or optimization task. Proposition 1 is therefore a faithful anchor for a population theorem, as the other analyst shows, but not for the programme’s computational question.

The population model itself is sensible here. The rule is anonymous, and a ranking is a complete type for everything the paper uses. In a large election with many voters per ranking, ranking masses are an honest description of the society. I would not object that the model loses identities, or use prior high-multiplicity work against it. But those points establish that a continuous extension is well-posed; they do not give the paper a computational contribution. Replacing voter counts by masses can continue the paper’s structural statements without producing a computational question the paper posed.

The stronger mirror to consider is not just the even-\(m\) result: one could extend Proposition 2’s odd-\(m\) characterization, or the restricted-domain results, to distributions over rankings. Those remain theorems about the relationship between two social-choice concepts. Alternatively, one could study winner determination or the minimum mass of voters whose rankings must change to alter a majoritarian compromise outcome. The latter could lead to interesting continuous optimization, but it adds an intervention problem, with costs and an objective absent from this paper. It is a plausible follow-on project, not a mirror of one of the paper’s results.

So the paper is a weak fit for a programme specifically charting computational complexity under population continuization. The honest negative case is weaker than the requested universal claim, though: I cannot rule out a worthwhile future computational project built around this rule. What I can say is that this paper’s results do not themselves supply one.
