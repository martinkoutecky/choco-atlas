The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) and a distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses over a finite support of complete rankings, assume every pair has a strict majority. Under asynchronous rate-one updates, each unit of mass swaps a uniformly chosen adjacent pair when its order opposes the fixed majority tournament \(G_\mu\). Determine whether the ranking distribution \(\nu_s\) converges to a point mass \(\delta_\pi\); output the consensus ranking \(\pi\) if it does, or a directed cycle in \(G_\mu\) otherwise.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity version of ITR-VOT: ranking types carry rational population mass, and asynchronous updates transfer mass between types through adjacent swaps that follow the strict majority directions. The input is the sparse support and its masses; the computational task is to decide point-mass convergence and return either the resulting order or a cycle certificate.

The objection that survived

Theorem 8 gives no running-time bound for classifying a finite input by its convergence criterion, so its contribution is a dynamical characterization rather than an explicit complexity analysis.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s local-consensus process, anchored on Theorem 8, proved here. The theorem says that Algorithm 1 reaches a unanimous ranking exactly when the initial majority tournament \(G_n\) has no directed cycle. With an odd number of voters, each pair has a strict majority direction. Every permitted adjacent swap reinforces that direction, so the process cannot remove a cycle.

Here is a continuous version that keeps the same update rule. Call it Mass ITR-VOT Consensus. An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a sparse distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings in \(S_m\), with positive rational masses summing to one. Require that every pair of candidates has a strict majority under \(\mu\), as in the paper’s odd-voter assumption. The type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the population currently holding it. Define \(G_\mu\) by directing \(a\to b\) when more than half the mass ranks \(a\) above \(b\).

Each agent has a rate-one update clock. When it rings, if the agent’s current ranking has an adjacent pair \(a,b\) in the order \(a\) then \(b\), while \(G_\mu\) has \(b\to a\), the agent swaps them; choose uniformly if several such pairs are available. Otherwise the agent stays put. Let \(\nu_s\) be the distribution of current rankings at time \(s\). The question is whether \(\nu_s\) converges to a point mass \(\delta_\pi\). A solution is either the consensus ranking \(\pi\), or a directed cycle certifying that consensus cannot occur.

The majority tournament stays fixed during this evolution: every swap moves mass toward the existing majority direction for that pair, and leaves all other pairwise comparisons unchanged. Consequently, if \(G_\mu\) is acyclic, it has a unique total order \(\pi\); each agent’s allowed swaps sort toward \(\pi\), so \(\nu_s\to\delta_\pi\). If \(G_\mu\) contains a cycle, its directions persist, and a population concentrated on any one ranking would induce a transitive tournament. Thus the mass cannot converge to a single ranking. This gives the continuous problem a polynomial-time solution: sum the rational masses to construct \(G_\mu\), then test it for cycles and output either \(\pi\) or a cycle. The work is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(\tau\) is support size and \(L\) is the input bit length. I expect this to be tractable, in the spirit of Class A.

A plausible regime is a large public deliberation platform where many participants begin with one of a modest number of complete-ranking templates and revise adjacent choices in response to public pairwise majorities. There may be millions of participants but only dozens or hundreds of observed ranking types. The paper’s distance-based process already assumes voters can use population-wide pairwise counts, so replacing counts by exact fractions is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of its question. The continuous object here is the population; the random asynchronous updates are inherited from the paper.

The mirror covers Theorem 8’s consensus criterion, not the Borda gossip results or the paper as a whole. Its soft spot is that it is a mean-field version of a process already organized around aggregate pairwise counts, and its computational payoff is modest: the answer reduces to weighted-tournament cycle detection. It does not show that a difficult discrete problem becomes easy through continuization. Still, it poses the same agents’ update question with fractional population masses, and the finite and continuous versions agree on the central yes-or-no criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a fit argument. This paper studies whether two stochastic update processes reach consensus and how quickly; it does not study the complexity of changing, controlling, or evaluating an election outcome. Theorem 8 characterizes the dynamics through the majority tournament, but the paper gives no running-time or complexity result. A decision problem can be extracted—given the profile, decide whether the process reaches consensus—but that is already a step beyond the result the authors prove.

That distinction does not invalidate the proposed mirror. With strict pairwise majorities, a distribution over rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity instance, and the majority tournament is well-defined from its masses. The rate-one clocks also give a reasonable continuous-time version of selecting an eligible voter. The proposed convergence question is precise, and Theorem 8’s reasoning appears to carry over. So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor by claiming that its type space is incoherent or that the continuous dynamics degenerate.

The better version one might propose is to ask how much mass must change rankings to make the tournament acyclic, thereby making consensus possible. That could be a meaningful computational robustness problem, but it adds an intervention the paper does not study: its voters update under a fixed rule, and the authors do not ask how to alter the initial society. It is a plausible follow-on to the paper, not a continuous counterpart of one of its results.

That leaves a narrow negative case: the mirror may be worthwhile as a short high-multiplicity reformulation, but it does not expose the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo is built to chart. This is a weak objection to the mirror itself. The proposed question is well-posed, and the paper’s focus on dynamics does not give me a sound basis for claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
