The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a candidate mirror of the paper’s *underlying dimension problem*, but the paper has no qualifying named computational anchor. Its headline claim, that the EU Council game has dimension at least \(8\), is unnumbered. Lemmas 1 and 2 are numbered, but they give a cover implication and a non-separability test; neither asserts an algorithm or a complexity classification. So I cannot present a continuous problem as mirroring a named result of the required kind.

The plausible setting would be a large federation or association with many voting units and relatively few types. A type \(t\) specifies a unit’s complete relevant attributes: its voting entitlement and its population weight \(q_t\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of units of type \(t\), and let \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the fraction included in a coalition. Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=1\). A direct continuous analogue of the paper’s EU rule declares \(x\) winning when
\[ \left(\sum_t x_t\ge .55\ \text{and}\ \sum_tq_tx_t\ge .65\right) \quad\text{or}\quad \sum_t x_t\ge 25/28. \]
Define its continuous dimension as the minimum \(k\) for which the winning region, over the entire box \(\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), equals an intersection of \(k\) weighted rules \(\sum_t a^j_tx_t\ge b_j\), with \(a^j_t\ge0\). The concrete question is: given rational type data and \(k\), does such a representation exist, and, if so, produce it? Its objective is to minimize the number of weighted games.

That is a recognizable extension of the paper’s subject: it asks how many weighted voting rules are needed to represent the same coalition rule. But its likely answer exposes the main weakness. Intersections of linear threshold regions are convex, while this continuous winning region is nonconvex. For the EU data, one can take a coalition of mass \(0.55\) with population share \(0.65\), and a coalition consisting of the least-populated \(25\) members, which wins by the \(25/28\) clause despite having population share below \(0.65\). Their midpoint loses: its member mass is below \(25/28\), and its population share is below \(0.65\). Thus no finite intersection of weighted rules represents this fractional-coalition game exactly.

So this candidate would most likely yield a continuum-specific representational obstruction, not a transferred hardness result or a rich complexity classification. It covers only the dimension question, not the paper’s particular lower-bound proof: the proof’s \(15\) discrete losing coalitions and failure of a \(7\)-cover do not directly carry over to fractional coalitions. The weakest point is precisely that fractional membership changes the object enough to make exact dimension infinite for a simple geometric reason. I would offer this as a plausible research question about what continuization does to voting-game dimension, but not as an anchored mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s central claim, \(\dim(W_{\mathrm{EU}})\ge 8\), is a lower bound for one finite voting game, not a complexity result. Lemmas 1 and 2 provide tools for that bound; neither classifies an algorithmic problem. One could ask how hard it is to compute voting-game dimension, but this paper does not answer that question. So there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuization.

The proposed dimension problem is still the natural extension to examine. A large federation with many similar voting units is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not reject it on population grounds. But if coalition membership is divisible, the exact EU-style rule does not have a finite continuous dimension as defined in the proposal. Its winning region is nonconvex. For example, take a fractional coalition \(x^A\) meeting the \(55\%\) member and \(65\%\) population thresholds, and \(x^B\) consisting of the least-populated \(25\) of the \(28\) equal-mass member types. The latter wins the \(25/28\) clause while falling below \(65\%\) of the population. Their midpoint meets neither winning branch: its population share is below \(65\%\), and its member mass is below \(25/28\). Yet every weighted rule describes a halfspace, and every intersection of halfspaces is convex. No number of weighted rules can represent this region exactly.

That geometry also removes the paper’s specific contribution. Its \(15\) losing coalitions and failed \(7\)-cover establish a careful finite lower bound. In the divisible model, a losing midpoint inside the convex hull of winning coalitions already blocks any exact intersection representation. The resulting infinite dimension is immediate, rather than a computational classification or a continuous version of the paper’s \(7\)-cover argument.

There are possible repairs, but each changes the question. Restricting coalitions to whole types restores an atomic, high-multiplicity game rather than a continuous coalition space. Measuring the size of a union or Boolean combination of thresholds, or allowing approximation, could define new research problems; the paper’s EU rule already has a short threshold formula, and an approximation question would need an error measure the paper never motivates. Those might be worth studying on their own, but they do not rescue a mirror of this result. The negative case is strongest on that narrow claim: the population regime can be sensible, but the paper’s dimension parameter is incompatible with its direct continuous extension. I cannot honestly rule out every independently invented continuous voting-complexity question.
