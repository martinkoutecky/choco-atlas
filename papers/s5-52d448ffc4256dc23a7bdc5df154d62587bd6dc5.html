The continuous mirror question

Given finite student types \(S\) with rational masses \(\mu_s\) summing to \(1\), and finite school types \(H\) with rational masses \(\nu_h\) and per-school capacities \(\kappa_h\), compute the student-optimal stable assignment flow \(x_{sh}\). Each complete student type records its public rating, full realized shortlist of school types, post-interview ranking, and utility vector; school types use the common public-score priority ordering from the school-choice setting. Require \(0\le x_{sh}\), \(\sum_h x_{sh}\le\mu_s\), \(\sum_s x_{sh}\le\nu_h\kappa_h\), and \(x_{sh}=0\) for schools outside type \(s\)'s shortlist; the flow must have no blocking type pair.

The model it lives in

A finite-support, mass-valued version of the paper’s school-choice interview market: rational masses of complete student and school types, with capacities scaled to seat mass, and a student-proposing DA assignment flow over the realized interview shortlists.

The objection that survived

The theorem’s match-probability and equilibrium guarantees rely on the random shortlists and local proposal fluctuations; the repaired type-mass question does not preserve those guarantees.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computation of the school-choice DA assignment and unmatched mass. It leaves aside Theorem 3.1’s quantitative equilibrium and probability bounds, the residency results, and the experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the student-optimal stable flow be computed in time polynomial in the numbers of types and the input bit length?
  • Under what distribution over complete shortlist types can the paper’s match-probability bounds be recovered for the mass model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It is a quantitative guarantee for an interview-selection strategy, not a standard complexity classification. For sufficiently large \(n\), with \(n\) students and \(n/\kappa\) schools of capacity \(\kappa\), take \(a=5\), \(\Theta(1)\le k\le\Theta(\ln n)\), and \(\alpha=2(4a+1)\ln k/k\). Each student interviews \(k\) schools selected from the rating cone \([r(d)-a\alpha,r(d)+a\alpha)\cap[0,1)\), then students and schools rank their interview partners as specified in the paper, and student-proposing DA is run. The theorem says this outcome is an \(\varepsilon\)-Nash equilibrium for every student with rating at least \(a\alpha\), regardless of the remaining students’ strategies, with \(\varepsilon=O(\ln k/k)\). For each such student and each school with rating at least \(a\alpha\), their respective non-match probabilities are at most \(\exp(-4k/(4a+1))\) and \(\exp(-3\kappa\ln k/2)\).

A natural population regime is repeated school admissions with many students in a small number of exam-score bands and many schools in a small number of program classes. A student type records the student’s complete relevant information: score band, ranking of school types, and interview rule. A school type records its priority ranking over student types and its capacity. The masses are rational fractions of the cohort; capacities scale with the population. Thus the number of students can be large while the numbers of types remain moderate. Fractional mass in the model means that a type’s students are split across outcomes; each individual still receives an integral assignment.

I would pose the lead mirror as Continuous Type-Mass Interview DA. An instance consists of finite student-type and school-type sets; rational masses \(\mu_s\) and \(\nu_h\); per-school capacities \(b_h\), with total seat mass matching student mass; strict rankings \(\succ_s\) and \(\succ_h\); and a rational interview-edge mass \(e_{sh}\) for each eligible type pair. For example, \(\sum_h e_{sh}=k\mu_s\), with edges restricted to the permitted rating cone. The task is to compute the student-optimal stable matching flow \(x_{sh}\), along with each type’s unmatched mass and average utility. A valid solution has \(0\le x_{sh}\le e_{sh}\), respects student mass and school seat capacities, and has no blocking type pair: there cannot be positive interview-edge mass left over, positive student mass of type \(s\) preferring \(h\) to its assignment, and positive capacity at \(h\) willing to accept \(s\). This asks for the underlying DA outcome in a population model, rather than a continuous encoding of the algorithm.

I expect this finite-support flow problem to be tractable: a type-level version of student-proposing DA should operate over the finitely many rankings, edge types, and rational mass breakpoints. Further questions are whether it admits a polynomial algorithm in the support size and bit length, whether the cone strategy’s non-match and utility bounds have a meaningful mass analogue, and whether such a solution gives useful preprocessing or approximation for discrete high-multiplicity cohorts.

The case is strongest for the school-choice side of the paper. Theorem 3.1 studies precisely student applications, public school priorities, and DA, making the repeated-cohort scenario plausible to the authors. The mirror covers its mass-valued matching computation; the theorem’s \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium and probability bounds are targets for further analysis, not consequences I claim follow automatically. The weakest point is that the paper’s guarantees rely on individually random utility values and a sparse random interview graph. Aggregating those into type-level edge masses may lose the local randomness that drives the theorem. So this is a credible continuous computational question, but transferring the theorem’s guarantees is a separate and substantive problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The first problem is a mismatch with what Theorem 3.1 proves. It gives ex ante equilibrium and match-probability bounds for one randomized interview strategy; it does not classify the complexity of a decision or intervention problem. The proposed stable-flow task is a computational question, but it is a new question about computing a matching, not a continuous mirror of the theorem’s contribution.

There is also a fidelity problem with the proposed input. Edge masses \(e_{sh}\) record how much interview traffic runs between each pair of types, but not which interviews occur together for each student. Deferred acceptance depends on those joint shortlists and the resulting rejection paths. Expanding types to include each student’s full shortlist, post-interview ranking, and relevant priorities repairs that problem. But the stable-flow computation is then already very close to an existing continuous framework: Arnosti models a measure over student types with preferences and school-specific priorities, fractional assignments, incomplete lists, and finite school capacities, and gives a deferred-acceptance analogue for finding stable outcomes. His Poisson-vacancy version also makes individual-level probabilistic predictions for finite-capacity markets. [Arnosti, “A Continuum Model of Stable Matching with Finite Capacities”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)

A stronger mirror would preserve the paper’s random interview process and ask for continuous limits of its non-match and \(\varepsilon\)-Nash bounds. That could be interesting, but it is a stochastic matching analysis, not the proposed stable-flow computation. The paper gives no society-level optimization problem or complexity result for that process; its central results are performance claims about a prescribed strategy on random markets. And in the paper’s exact model, continuously drawn ratings and interview-dependent shortlists make complete types nearly unique. Compressing them into moderate support requires changing or approximating the model. Grouping schools instead can erase the separate capacity and local proposal fluctuations that drive the reported bounds.

So I find the negative case strong against this anchor: the flow problem is not what Theorem 3.1 studies, and its repaired form overlaps existing continuous stable-matching work. The universal claim is weaker. Repeated cohorts with shared score bands and genuinely repeated preference-and-shortlist types are plausible, so I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous extension.
