The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), each type has a group \(g(t)\), eligible departments, a strict preference order, and a department-priority profile. Assume the eligible type–department pairs admit a common total order consistent with each type’s preferences and each department’s priorities. Given rational lower and upper quotas for departments and group–department pairs, and assuming a feasible complete allocation exists, does there exist a polynomial-time algorithm in the input bit length and the numbers of types, groups, and departments that finds a rational allocation \(x_{t,d}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{d}x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) satisfying all quotas and limited stability? For the stability test, reset each group–department upper quota to its realized mass \(\bar q_{g,d}=\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}\). A block exists if some positive mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(d_0\) prefers an eligible department \(d\), and a lower-priority type \(t'\) in the same group has positive mass at \(d\), so that for some \(\varepsilon>0\), moving \(\varepsilon\) of \(t\) from \(d_0\) to \(d\) and leaving \(\varepsilon\) of \(t'\) unassigned respects the upper bounds. As in the paper’s blocking test, this check concerns upper-bound feasibility of the resulting partial assignment; the complete allocation itself satisfies the lower bounds.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete applicant types, with a fractional assignment \(x_{t,d}\) of each type’s mass to eligible departments. Types encode group, preferences, and priority profile; quotas constrain department and group–department masses, and limited stability is tested after resetting group–department upper bounds to realized masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the limited-stable allocation guarantee in Theorem 4.1. It leaves Theorem 3.10, the other algorithm, and the experimental results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the ordered-pair greedy argument extend to rational type masses while preserving the stated limited-stability condition?
  • How do type-level priority ties and lower quotas affect the exact stability characterization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is Type-Mass Limited-Stable Department Allocation, based on Theorem 4.1. The paper proves that theorem itself: assuming a feasible complete assignment exists and there is a total order on eligible student–department pairs that agrees with each student’s preferences and each department’s priorities, Greedy-Alloc runs in time polynomial in the number of students and departments and returns a limited-stable complete assignment. “Limited-stable” means stable after each group–department upper quota is reset to the number actually assigned there. The theorem covers the paper’s department and group–department upper and lower bounds; it does not require dropping lower quotas.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large, centralized program-allocation system with tens of thousands of applicants but relatively few recurring profiles. A type records an applicant’s group, eligible departments, preference order, and priority profile at every eligible department. For example, applicants might share one of a finite set of preference rankings and priority bands. The important condition is that these profiles admit the theorem’s common ordering of eligible type–department pairs. Then the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(t\), with \(\tau\) much smaller than the applicant count. This is an instance regime, not a claim that every university allocation problem has few types.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), groups \(g(t)\), eligible departments, strict type-level preferences and department priorities, and rational lower and upper quotas for departments and group–department pairs. Require the common total order from Theorem 4.1 on eligible type–department pairs, and assume the quota constraints admit a complete allocation. A solution is a rational matrix \(x_{t,d}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,d}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to department \(d\). Every type’s mass must be assigned, and the department and group–department quota bounds must hold.

For stability, reset each group–department upper quota to its realized mass, \(\bar q_{g,d}=\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}\), just as the paper resets that quota to the realized student count. A blocking pair is a type \(t\) with positive mass at a less-preferred department \(d_0\), and a preferred department \(d\), for which some positive mass can move to \(d\): either within the upper bounds, or by displacing lower-priority mass already assigned there, while preserving the paper’s upper-bound feasibility conditions. The question is whether one can compute a complete allocation with no such positive-mass block, or report infeasibility. Each unit of population still receives one department; \(x_{t,d}\) is the fraction of a type’s mass receiving it. Population is the continuous object here, not the assignment outcome.

I expect this problem to be tractable, with a running time polynomial in the number of departments, groups, types, and quota bit length \(L\). The theorem’s greedy logic suggests processing the ordered type–department pairs and using exact rational flow or LP optimization to decide how much mass can be committed while preserving a feasible completion. The stability argument needs to be proved for type-level mass and within-type indifference; Theorem 4.1 does not establish that continuous result. Further questions include whether the common-order condition can be weakened, how to incorporate lower quotas into the blocking test, and when a continuous solution can be rounded to a discrete stable assignment.

The authors should recognize this as their allocation problem: it retains the same groups, preferences, priorities, quotas, complete assignment requirement, and limited-stability concept, while replacing counts of indistinguishable applicants by masses. The paper itself does not prove the continuous complexity result; Theorem 4.1 is the named anchor for asking it. This is a narrow scope claim: it mirrors that theorem, not both proposed algorithms or the experimental findings.

The weak point is the type compression. The paper’s 2015 and 2016 applications involved 951 and 921 students, and individual grades or tie-breaks can make nearly every applicant a distinct type. My proposed regime requires finite, consequential priority profiles and the common-order condition; if a real system relies on idiosyncratic strict rankings, the number of types may grow with the population and the high-multiplicity benefit disappears. That limits the mirror’s reach, but not its coherence in systems with genuinely repeated profiles and finite priority classes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 4.1’s tractability rests on a narrow alignment condition: one strict total order on student–department pairs must agree both with every student’s preferences and with every department’s priorities. In ordinary matching markets those orderings pull in different directions. The paper’s actual cohorts also have only about \(900\) students, and if grades or tie-breaks distinguish applicants, making each type complete can leave almost every applicant in a different type. The theorem’s own application therefore gives little evidence that its assumptions and a small support \(\tau\) occur together.

That objection weakens the mirror but does not defeat it. A stronger version groups applicants with the same group, preferences, and priority band, and uses type-level ties where those applicants are interchangeable. In a large program with recurring preference profiles and finite priority bands, this is plausible; a common order on type–department pairs can still exist. The resulting mass-allocation question retains the paper’s groups, quotas, complete assignment, and limited-stability idea. The necessary change from strict individual priorities to type-level tiers is a natural restatement, not a fundamental break.

Nor can I dismiss Theorem 4.1 as lacking a computational result: it proves a polynomial-time algorithm for a named allocation problem. The paper does not already solve the continuous-population version. High-multiplicity matching work would support the regime, rather than count against it.

So the negative case is weak. The mirror may be narrow, and its assumptions may be uncommon outside specially aligned markets, but the improved type-mass version is coherent and potentially worth asking about. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario makes it worthwhile; the proposed anchor survives.
