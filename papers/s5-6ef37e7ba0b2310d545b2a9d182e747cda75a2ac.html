The continuous mirror question

Given a finite project set \(P\), a finite support \(T\) of nonempty approval sets, and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute a weighted NASH allocation \(x^N\in\arg\max_{x\in\Delta(P)}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(x)\), where \(u_t(x)=\sum_{p\in A_t}x_p\). For this allocation, determine the sharp worst-case value of \(\rho=\min_{t\in T}u_t(x^N)/W^*\), where \(W^*=\max_{x\in\Delta(P)}\min_{t\in T}u_t(x)\), over all such societies.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over approval-set types, with mass \(\mu_t\) on type \(A_t\); each type receives utility \(u_t(x)=\sum_{p\in A_t}x_p\) from the shared project-budget division \(x\). The NASH allocation maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(x)\), and its egalitarian efficiency is compared with \(W^*=\max_x\min_t u_t(x)\).

The objection that survived

The opponent derives that the ratio is at least \(\min_t \mu_t\), so the proposed mass-floor follow-up has a useful baseline bound; this limits its novelty but does not defeat the unrestricted mirror.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is a narrow mirror of the paper’s NASH rule and its egalitarian efficiency guarantee. The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result. Its best computational anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved here: for \(n\) agents, the NASH rule’s worst-case normalized egalitarian welfare lies between \(2/n-1/n^2\) and \(2/n\). The proof establishes both bounds and optimizes NASH through a convex program. This is a performance guarantee, not a theorem that the problem is in P; I would not claim more.

A credible regime is a large municipal participatory-budgeting process: many residents, but relatively few distinct approval sets over the project list. A type is an approval set \(A_t\subseteq P\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that set. For example, a city may have hundreds of thousands of residents but only hundreds or thousands of recurring approval types. The budget division \(x\) remains a distribution over projects, as in the paper; the continuization is in the population, represented by rational masses.

This preserves the authors’ problem closely. If \(n_t\) agents have approval type \(t\), their common utility is \(u_t(x)=\sum_{p\in A_t}x_p\), and the discrete NASH objective is \(\prod_t u_t(x)^{n_t}\). Taking its logarithm and dividing by \(n\) gives \(\sum_t (n_t/n)\log u_t(x)\). So for \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), the continuous weighted objective is exactly the normalized discrete one. The society is aggregated by complete problem types; the model does not discard distinctions the paper uses.

I would pose the lead mirror as the Continuous Approval NASH problem. An instance is a finite project set \(P\), a finite support \(T\subseteq 2^P\setminus\{\emptyset\}\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For \(x\in\Delta(P)\), define \(u_t(x)=\sum_{p\in A_t}x_p\). The task is to output a NASH allocation
\[ x^N\in\arg\max_{x\in\Delta(P)}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(x), \]
and determine its sharp worst-case egalitarian efficiency ratio relative to \(W^*=\max_{x\in\Delta(P)}\min_{t\in T}u_t(x)\). A computational solution can return a rational allocation within a requested objective tolerance; a performance result should give a tight bound on \(\rho(I)=\min_t u_t(x^N)/W^*\).

I expect the allocation problem to be tractable: it is weighted concave maximization over a simplex, and the egalitarian benchmark is a linear program. But the finite-\(n\) guarantee does not carry over as a positive constant when masses can be arbitrarily small. With two projects, let a mass \(1-\varepsilon\) approve only the first and mass \(\varepsilon\) approve only the second. NASH assigns masses \(1-\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon\) to the respective projects; the egalitarian optimum splits the budget evenly. Thus \(\rho=2\varepsilon\), and the unrestricted continuous guarantee is \(0\). Setting \(\varepsilon=1/n\) gives the paper’s \(2/n\) upper-bound example. This is a continuum-specific collapse of the performance guarantee, not evidence of computational hardness.

The mirror covers Theorem 4.5 and the NASH computation behind it, not the paper’s other fairness axioms or voting rules. Further questions include the sharp guarantee when every type has mass at least \(\alpha\), and a formal discrete-to-continuous dictionary for the rule and its efficiency ratio.

My weakest point is that the anchor is a named performance result, not a named complexity classification. Also, an arbitrarily small positive-mass type still counts in egalitarian welfare, even as its weight in the NASH objective becomes negligible. That changes the guarantee sharply. The mirror nevertheless preserves the paper’s rule, utilities, and efficiency comparison, so it remains a recognizable question for its authors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 4.5 is an efficiency bound, not a complexity result. The paper already computes NASH by maximizing a concave objective over the project simplex. Grouping agents with the same approval set merely replaces their repeated log-utility terms by \(\sum_t \mu_t\log u_t(x)\). That makes the proposed task a compressed version of the paper’s existing convex program; it introduces no pricing problem or other evident algorithmic obstacle. This weakens the claim that the mirror would chart a new computational landscape.

The egalitarian comparison also reacts sharply to rare types. With two projects and approval types of masses \(1-\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon\), NASH allocates those same fractions to the projects. Its minimum utility is \(\varepsilon\), while the egalitarian optimum is \(1/2\), so the ratio is \(2\varepsilon\). The unrestricted continuous guarantee is therefore zero. This exposes a mismatch between a mass-weighted objective and a minimum over every positive-mass type; the finite bound’s dependence on \(n\) reappears as sensitivity to the smallest type mass.

A stronger version would restrict attention to societies where every type has mass at least \(\alpha\). That avoids the rare-type collapse, but leaves the same weighted convex program. Its first-order optimality condition already gives a basic bound: if \(x^N\) is NASH and \(x^*\) is egalitarian-optimal, then \(\sum_t \mu_t u_t(x^*)/u_t(x^N)\le 1\), hence \(u_t(x^N)\ge \mu_t W^*\) for every type. Thus the ratio is at least \(\min_t\mu_t\). This makes the repaired mirror look more like a parameterized welfare analysis than a new algorithmic problem. It is a real weakness, though it does not rule out a sharper result.

The negative case cannot go much further. A large participatory-budgeting electorate with many residents sharing approval sets is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and an approval set captures everything about an agent that this paper’s utility model uses. The mass model preserves the rule and benchmark exactly for rational population proportions. Neither outcome-space continuity nor existing high-multiplicity work defeats it: the former does not continuize the population, and the latter supports the regime’s relevance.

So I can argue that this is a low-novelty computational anchor, especially if the unrestricted guarantee is the main attraction. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proposed mirror is well-posed, retains a plausible population regime, and asks a question the authors would likely recognize.
