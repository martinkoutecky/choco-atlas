What the mirror covers

The proposed batch model covers aggregate task allocation; it does not mirror the named results on SEV, SEVB, or VCGEV truthfulness and payment bounds.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I believe is a batch version of the paper’s stochastic task-allocation problem. The paper itself, however, gives no valid complexity anchor under your rule. Theorem 1 says SEV is incentive compatible for assigned players but not for unassigned players; Theorem 2 says SEVB is incentive compatible; Theorem 4 says VCGEV is incentive compatible; and Theorems 3 and 5 bound total payments. Those are named results, but none establishes a problem’s complexity or gives a computational formulation that the paper analyzes. I can’t honestly present them as anchors for a complexity mirror.

The candidate mirror is Batch Stochastic Task Allocation\(_\infty\). Consider a large contractor workforce handling a steady cohort of similar projects, each with the paper’s \(m\) sequential tasks and deadline \(d\). A type \(t\) specifies a worker’s task role, complete execution-time distribution \(f_t\), public cost, and any other parameters used by the problem. Let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of the workforce, with \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\). Assume there are \(N\) workers, \(\tau\) types, and a fixed project load of \(\lambda N\) projects; plausibly \(N\) is large while \(\tau\) is moderate.

For each tuple \(\mathbf t=(t_1,\ldots,t_m)\) of eligible types, let \(q_{\mathbf t}\) be the number of projects per worker assigned that tuple, and let
\[ p_{\mathbf t}=\Pr[E_{t_1}+\cdots+E_{t_m}\le d]. \]
Choose \(q_{\mathbf t}\ge 0\) to maximize
\[ V\sum_{\mathbf t}q_{\mathbf t}p_{\mathbf t}, \]
subject to \(\sum_{\mathbf t:\,t_j=t}q_{\mathbf t}\le\mu_t\) for each type \(t\) and task position \(j\), and \(\sum_{\mathbf t}q_{\mathbf t}\le\lambda\). Each project still gets an integral worker type for each task; \(q\) describes the aggregate allocation across many projects. A solution is an optimal mass allocation \(q\) and its expected reward. This states a computational question about the underlying allocation, not about an encoding.

This scenario has a credible high-multiplicity story: many workers in each role have the same capabilities and costs, and the platform allocates across many similar projects. It also makes the necessary co-modification explicit: project demand scales with workforce supply. For fixed \(m\), explicit type sets, and explicit finite duration supports, enumerating the \(\tau^m\) tuples gives a finite LP, so I would expect tractability in that regime. With variable \(m\), the tuple space grows exponentially; pricing, exact deadline-probability computation, and possible hardness transfer become the central questions. The paper establishes none of those results, so this is a proposed direction, not an extension of a theorem.

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies one project, and its main contributions concern truthful reward mechanisms. Scaling to a cohort preserves the task-allocation objective but does not automatically extend SEVB or VCGEV truthfulness. In an atomless model, an individual report may have no effect on aggregate allocation, making individual incentive claims trivial unless the mechanism is designed carefully. So I would offer this as a plausible continuous mirror of the allocation subproblem, not claim that it mirrors the paper’s named theorem results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1, 2, and 4 establish incentive properties; Theorems 3 and 5 bound payments. None states a complexity claim or analyzes an algorithm. Computing those payments or optimizing task assignments could be worthwhile questions, but they would be newly chosen questions, not computational results from this paper.

For a single project, taking the workforce to a continuum also fits poorly. Each of the fixed \(m\) tasks is assigned to at most one worker. Once a type has positive mass, more mass of that type does not provide additional task capacity; but an atomless society cannot itself supply a whole worker. And the incentive theorems concern identifiable players: who is assigned a task, whose execution time is observed, and who receives a reward. A unilateral report changes no type’s mass. Aggregate masses alone therefore cannot express the deviations those theorems rule on. Keeping a tagged individual makes the mechanism meaningful, but the model is no longer just a distribution over types.

A more sophisticated single-project mirror might take a type-level limit of the Shapley values. But the coalition value saturates once there is an eligible worker for each task. As a function of normalized masses, it is flat away from the boundary where a type first appears; a derivative-based continuous allocation can miss the pivotal contributions that the finite-player permutations capture. Recovering those effects requires tracking a finite individual perturbation, such as a \(1/N\) atom, and thus retaining finite-population information.

The proposed batch model avoids that problem by scaling the number of projects with the workforce. It is the strongest positive case: its aggregate allocation LP could be a worthwhile computational question. But it adds shared capacity constraints across many projects and drops the paper’s coalition rewards and individual truthfulness guarantees. That makes it a plausible new direction in stochastic task allocation, rather than a mirror of the paper’s named results.

So the negative case is strongest under the stated anchor standard: this paper supplies no computational theorem, and its central incentive claims do not pass directly to a mass-only population. It is weaker against the batch extension. If the programme accepts the paper’s allocation subproblem as enough of an anchor, I cannot honestly rule out that extension as worthwhile.
