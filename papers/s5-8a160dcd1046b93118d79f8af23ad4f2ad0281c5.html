The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result: no theorem or proposition classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The strongest nearby anchor is Proposition 4, proved in this paper. It says that the Pareto frontier of robustly Pareto-improving category rankings consists of a single ranking, \(I_{PF}\): pool each maximal interval on which the ability distribution, restricted to that interval, first-order stochastically dominates the uniform distribution there; leave all other types as singletons. Here “robust” means weakly beneficial for every type for every admissible cost function and prize distribution, and strictly beneficial for a positive measure of types for some such functions. This is a useful structural characterization, but it is not a complexity result.

The best positive case is a computational problem suggested by that proposition: Robust Pareto-Pooling Computation. An instance is a unit-mass applicant population with ability \(x\in[0,1]\), represented by a strictly positive, piecewise-constant rational density \(f\), and its CDF \(F\). Applicants with the same ability have the same preferences and costs; their utility is \(xy-c(t)\), with common increasing cost function \(c\). The input describes \(F\) by \(r\) rational breakpoints and density values, where \(r\) is small compared with the annual applicant count. The designer chooses a monotone category ranking: a finite set of ability intervals to pool, assigning the seats corresponding to each interval by lottery, while leaving other types unpooled. The task is to output the unique robust Pareto-frontier ranking \(I_{PF}\), with its interval endpoints. A valid solution is precisely the collection of maximal intervals satisfying
\[ \frac{F(x)-F(a)}{F(b)-F(a)}\leq \frac{x-a}{b-a} \quad\text{for every }x\in[a,b], \]
for each pooled interval \((a,b]\), together with singleton categories for the remaining types.

The motivating regime is a large centralized admissions cohort: perhaps millions of applicants, represented by a relatively compact estimated ability distribution. Mass means the fraction of applicants in an ability range; the number of applicants can be much larger than the number of parameters encoding that distribution. The agents’ effort or test performance remains part of the model, and the policy still pools their performance ranks. This is a plausible computational question for the authors: they characterize exactly which poolings help applicants and, in Appendix F, find a robust policy by grid search.

I would expect this computation to be tractable for the stated input representation: with a piecewise-constant density, the FOSD condition is a comparison between a piecewise-linear CDF and an interval chord. But the paper does not prove a running-time bound; that classification would be new work. Further questions include computing the Pareto frontier when the prize distribution is known rather than robustly quantified over, and bounding how a computed policy’s guarantees change when \(F\) is estimated from finite applicant data.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already uses a continuum of abilities and characterizes the robust policy analytically. So this is a computational extension of a problem already formulated in a continuous-population model, not a clear case where continuization dissolves discrete hardness. The proposed question covers Proposition 4’s policy-computation problem; it does not mirror the paper’s equilibrium, estimation, or experimental results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case rests on a collision, not merely on the absence of a complexity theorem. The paper already makes the population continuous: contestants are represented by an ability distribution \(F\), seats by a prize distribution \(G\), and the large-contest allocation is described through their quantiles. Proposition 4 then characterizes the unique robust Pareto-frontier policy \(I_{PF}\) using maximal intervals on which the restricted \(F\) first-order stochastically dominates the uniform distribution. Appendix F even computes an example by grid search.

That defeats the proposed anchor and its natural upgrades. Computing \(I_{PF}\) from a rational density, or computing the Pareto frontier for a known \(G\) and more general category rankings, would add an algorithmic analysis to an already continuous-population model. Propositions 1–3 and Appendix A already characterize those policy comparisons in that model. Exact algorithms and running-time bounds could still be a separate research project, but they would not supply a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.

There is no numbered computational-complexity result here to mirror. That absence alone would not settle the question; the stronger point is that the paper already studies the population distribution and the pooling policies continuously. The negative case is weakest at the boundary: an exact complexity study of policy computation may be worthwhile on its own. The paper does not, however, leave a discrete population problem awaiting continuization.
