What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; the proposed task concerns finding a weighted Condorcet winner, while the paper's named results concern preference encodings, inversion counts, Bruhat adjacency, and domain structure.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that single-peaked rankings form a natural finite type space for a large electorate. Fix the axis \([n]\); each type is a complete single-peaked ranking, represented by a sign sequence of length \(n-1\). In a large election with a stable candidate axis and many repeated ballots, the society can be given by rational masses \(\mu_t\) over \(\tau\) observed types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of voters.

A concrete follow-up is Weighted Single-Peaked Condorcet Winner. Its input is the fixed axis \([n]\), an explicit support \(S\subseteq\mathrm{SP}([n])\), and binary rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). It asks for a candidate \(c\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{t\in S:\,c\succ_t d}\mu_t\geq \tfrac12 \quad\text{for every }d\ne c. \]
A solution is such a candidate, a weak Condorcet winner. The expected result is tractability in \((n,\tau,L)\): aggregate the masses by peak and find a weighted median. This extends the paper’s majority-domain theme to a society represented by masses, and the sign representation makes the types concise.

The nearest named result is Theorem 4, attributed to Theorem 1 of Puppe [18]. It states that \(\mathrm{SP}([n])\) is a connected, minimally rich Condorcet domain of maximal width, and hence is semi-connected; the paper gives a proof for parts of this known result. That is relevant structural support, but it does not classify a computational problem. Theorem 1 proves the unique sign representation, and Theorem 3 proves that the Bruhat edges correspond exactly to changing the first sign or swapping adjacent opposite signs. Proposition 2 gives the inversion-count formula from negative positions. These are combinatorial results, not complexity or algorithm results. The paper’s comment that the representation may help design algorithms is prospective.

So there is a plausible continuous-population question adjacent to the paper, but there are no qualifying computational anchors in the paper. The weighted-winner problem is an extension a reader could formulate from Theorem 4’s subject; the paper does not state or solve it. That gap is the weakest point and keeps this from being a strong ChoCo mirror under the required anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its Theorems 1, 3, and 4, and Proposition 2, describe or count single-peaked rankings and their domain structure. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. That is decisive against calling a proposed population problem a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

Theorem 4 is the closest anchor, but it concerns a property of the domain: every profile drawn from \(\mathrm{SP}([n])\) has an acyclic majority relation, alongside richness, width, and connectivity properties. The proposed weighted Condorcet-winner problem asks a different question about one measured electorate. It can be sensible, but it does not continuize Theorem 4. If the support contains all of \(\mathrm{SP}([n])\), the domain properties do not depend on the masses; if it contains only some rankings, the relevant object is the support domain, again not its mass distribution. The weighted-median algorithm uses peak masses and does not need the paper’s sign representation or Bruhat results.

The best stronger candidate I can construct is continuous swap bribery over single-peaked rankings: give each ranking a mass, use the Bruhat edges as allowed conversions, and ask for the least-cost mass transfer that makes a target candidate win. This is a natural high-multiplicity election model. But it adds the intervention, cost objective, and winner constraint; the paper studies none of them. Its edge description could help formulate or solve that new problem, but it is not itself a computational result whose continuous counterpart the paper supplies.

The same mismatch applies to the other anchors. Theorem 1 encodes each individual ranking as a sign sequence; putting a distribution over those sequences does not create a new population-level claim. Proposition 2 gives a ranking’s inversion count, and Theorem 3 describes moves between rankings. One can use these as ingredients in a mass-transfer or optimization problem, but doing so supplies the computational question from outside the paper.

So the strongest negative case is that the weighted-winner proposal is an adjacent follow-up, not a mirror, and the paper’s actual results have no population variable for continuization to act on. I cannot honestly claim that every computational problem over a large society of single-peaked voters would be unworthy; the swap-bribery version shows why that universal claim is weak. What the paper does not provide is a qualifying computational anchor for such a mirror.
