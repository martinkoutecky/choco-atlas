The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s core question for a large retail network. There is an important qualification: the paper has no named computational result in the sense your anchor rule requires. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic running time. Its numbered results concern existence and properties of cooperative games. So there is no fully qualifying computational anchor here.

The closest anchor is Theorem 9, proved in the paper, with its proof deferred to the online appendix. It says that for any newsvendor situation in the paper’s model—finitely many retailers with demands of finite expectation, positive selling prices, purchasing costs and penalty costs, \(p_i\ge c_i\), and dedicated stock levels—the associated cooperative game is totally balanced and has a nonempty core. In other words, every coalition game has a stable profit division. The authors note that the stochastic-programming duality approach of Chen and Zhang (2009) includes their case, but Theorem 9 is presented as their result.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large retail network with many small outlets and a modest number of outlet types. A type \(t\) specifies the outlet’s demand or shock exposure, purchasing cost \(c_t\), selling price \(p_t\), penalty cost \(g_t\), and dedicated stock level \(e_t\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of outlets of type \(t\). This fits the paper’s premise that retailers with the same relevant parameters can pool inventory. It is credible where repeated outlets serve similar markets, though outlet similarity is an instance-regime claim, not a property of retailing in general.

Here is a precise candidate problem, Continuum Dedicated-Stock Core. Take a finite set \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\). For a finite-scenario version, give each type a nonnegative rational demand rate \(d_t^\omega\) in scenario \(\omega\), with rational probability \(\pi_\omega\). A coalition is described by its mass vector \(x\), where \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). It orders \(q\) units before the demand scenario is known, then allocates \(a_t^\omega\) units to each type’s mass after the scenario is realized. Its value is

\[ V(x)= \max_{\substack{q\ge \sum_t x_t e_t\\ a_t^\omega\ge x_t\min\{d_t^\omega,e_t\}\\ \sum_t a_t^\omega=q\ \forall\omega}} \sum_\omega \pi_\omega \sum_t\left[ -c_ta_t^\omega +(p_t+g_t)\min\{a_t^\omega,x_td_t^\omega\} -g_tx_td_t^\omega \right]. \]

A solution is either a certificate that no core allocation exists, or type-level payoffs \(\phi_t\) per unit of mass satisfying

\[ \sum_t\mu_t\phi_t=V(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t x_t\phi_t\ge V(x) \quad\text{for every }x\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]. \]

This asks whether the stable profit division in Theorem 9 survives when coalition sizes vary continuously, and, if it does, to construct a division. The demand rates can represent common market shocks; with purely independent idiosyncratic demand, the continuum aggregate may instead become deterministic. That is a natural co-modification of the demand model when outlets are replaced by mass.

I would expect this finite-support, finite-scenario version to be tractable. For a proposed \(\phi\), the most profitable blocking coalition maximizes \(V(x)-\sum_t x_t\phi_t\) over \(x\). Put \(x\), \(q\), and the scenario allocations into that optimization, and replace each minimum by a variable \(z_t^\omega\) constrained by \(z_t^\omega\le a_t^\omega\) and \(z_t^\omega\le x_td_t^\omega\). The resulting separation problem is a linear program. The grand-coalition value is also an LP; with rational input, ellipsoid or related separation machinery gives a plausible polynomial-time route to finding a core allocation or detecting that none exists. This is a proposed complexity result, not one established by Theorem 9.

The scope is deliberately just Theorem 9’s core existence result. The paper’s monotonicity theorems could motivate separate continuous questions about payoff divisions as type parameters change, but they are not needed for this mirror.

The soft spot is also the central limitation: the anchor is not computational. The candidate turns the paper’s existence theorem into a computational problem; it does not continue an algorithmic result the authors already proved. The authors may recognize the model as a natural continuum version of their inventory-pooling game, but the complexity question is an added research direction. That is a defensible positive case for a mirror, not a claim that this paper already supplies a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 9 says that the finite-retailer game is totally balanced and has a nonempty core; Theorems 10–12 give conditions for monotonicity. None states an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational decision problem. The model also permits demand distributions specified only by finite expectation, without an input representation from which a running time could be measured.

The proposed Continuum Dedicated-Stock Core is the best attempt to repair that gap, and its retail-outlet scenario is credible. But it turns Theorem 9’s existence claim into a new task: compute a core allocation, or certify that none exists, for a newly specified mass game. It supplies its own finite type and scenario encoding and its own computational question. The paper does not ask that question or establish the computational result being continued. In the proposed encoding, maximizing \(V(x)-\sum_t x_t\phi_t\) can be written as one LP over coalition masses, orders, and scenario allocations. That observation does not make the question unworthy; it does show that its computational content comes from the new formulation, not from a computational bottleneck identified in the paper.

A closer mirror of Theorem 9 would ask whether the continuum game has a nonempty core. That is a worthwhile mathematical question, but it is an existence question, not the kind of computational question the programme is charting. Turning it into core computation changes the deliverable. The monotonicity theorems offer no better computational anchor: they concern how core payoffs compare as game parameters change, not how to compute or classify an algorithmic problem.

I would not argue that retail outlets cannot form high-multiplicity types; the proponent has a plausible regime, and the stochastic model’s details can reasonably be co-modified. The negative case rests on the narrower point that a plausible continuum extension is not, by itself, a computational mirror of this paper. Its weakness is that the resulting core-computation problem may still be useful to ChoCo as an independent direction. Under the stated anchor standard, though, this paper provides no named computational result for that mirror to continue.
