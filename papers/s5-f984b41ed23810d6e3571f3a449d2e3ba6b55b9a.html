The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a continuous mirror is weak: this paper has no named computational result to anchor one. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity bound or algorithmic classification. Its central contribution is an incentive problem illustrated by specific league results and seasons.

The nearest numbered result is Dagaev and Sonin’s Proposition 3, cited in Section 3 and said to be reproducible under the paper’s stated setup: a domestic championship played as a two-round home-away round robin, with the Champions League and Europa League as knockout tournaments. It says, in effect, that qualification is strategyproof if and only if every vacancy is allocated based on the domestic round-robin standings. That is a rule characterization from another paper, not a computational result proved here, so it does not qualify as an anchor under the requested standard.

One could imagine a population model of clubs across many comparable leagues, grouped by sporting and qualification type, and ask what share of cases admit a profitable lose-instead-of-win deviation. But that would change the paper’s question. Its manipulation is unilateral: one named club’s match result can affect its qualification. In a nonatomic population, one club has zero mass and cannot change the outcome; allowing a positive-mass group to deviate instead makes the question about collective manipulation, which the paper does not study.

So I would not write down a continuous problem per anchor: there are no eligible anchors, and inventing a population-level incidence problem would overstate the connection. The paper may motivate a new computational project, but it does not supply the named computational result needed for a credible ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no result in this paper for ChoCo to mirror: it states no computational problem and proves no algorithmic or complexity result. Its contribution is a diagnosis of a qualification rule, examples from three seasons, and a policy recommendation. The nearest formal result is Dagaev and Sonin’s Proposition 3, which the paper cites and applies; it characterizes when a rule is strategyproof, but makes no computational claim. Treating it as an anchor would both credit this paper with someone else’s result and stretch “computational” beyond the programme’s scope.

The paper’s manipulation is also specifically unilateral: one named club’s match result can change that club’s qualification. In an atomless population of clubs, one club has zero mass, so its result cannot move the aggregate standing or qualification cutoff; strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Giving a positive mass of clubs the same deviation preserves an effect only by changing the question to collective manipulation. A richer club type does not fix this: once the type records the schedule, standings, and other details that make a club pivotal, the relevant object is still a finite league of individually consequential teams.

The strongest alternative is a continuum of comparable league-seasons, asking what fraction permit this kind of manipulation. That could be a sensible empirical or policy question, but it aggregates finite cases rather than continuizing the society inside the paper’s problem. The paper supplies no computational result about that aggregation to mirror. A large single league with massless clubs has the same pivotality problem; retaining pivotal clubs as atoms yields a hybrid model, not a continuous-population mirror of the result here.

So the proponent raises no eligible anchor, and the only plausible candidate does not survive scrutiny. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous study could ever be devised is harder to establish: someone could formulate a useful population-level study of manipulation across leagues. But that would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational result, because the paper has none.
