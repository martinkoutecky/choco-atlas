What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover the ex-ante sample-size guarantees in Theorems 4 and 8. They leave the deterministic and fair-selection distortion bounds, Fair Greedy Capture results, and experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Theorem 4’s \(O(\varepsilon^{-2}\ln(m/\varepsilon))\) panel-size guarantee extend to independent draws from every finite-support metric society?
  • Does a matching sample-size lower bound hold for distributional societies, and how does it depend on support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is in the paper’s panel-size guarantees. There is a qualification: the paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. Its closest anchors are named sample-complexity results. If “computational result” requires a runtime or complexity-class claim, this paper has no qualifying anchor. Under the broader reading that includes algorithmic sample-size guarantees, the following two results make a coherent case.

Let a type \(t\) be a complete metric location: its distances to every other type and every alternative determine everything the model uses about an agent. Let the society be a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). For \(m\) alternatives \(C\), define the population cost of \(c\) by
\[ SC_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t d(t,c), \]
and let \(c_\mu\) minimize that cost. A panel of size \(k\) consists of \(k\) actual agents sampled from this population. Its winner minimizes the sum of those agents’ distances to the alternatives. The distortion is the expected population cost of the panel’s winner divided by \(SC_\mu(c_\mu)\). This keeps the paper’s central question intact: how large must a sampled panel be for its decision to reflect the whole society?

My lead question is Continuous Uniform-Sortition Sample Complexity. Given \(m\) and \(\varepsilon\), determine the least \(k\) such that \(k\) independent draws from any finite-support metric society \(\mu\), followed by choosing the panel’s minimum-cost alternative, have expected distortion at most \(1+\varepsilon\). A solution is a bound on that least \(k\), with a proof covering every allowed society and metric. This is a continuous-population version of uniform selection: the panel remains a finite sample, while the population it samples from is given by a distribution.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(\varepsilon\in(0,1]\) and every number \(m\) of alternatives, a uniformly random panel of \(k=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\ln(m/\varepsilon))\) agents has ex-ante distortion at most \(1+\varepsilon\), for every instance with \(m\) alternatives. The theorem concerns sampling without replacement from a finite electorate; the continuous question asks whether the corresponding guarantee holds for draws from a population distribution. I expect this question to have an efficient, Class-A-style sample guarantee: the paper’s concentration argument has a natural distributional counterpart. That extension still needs proof; Theorem 4 itself does not establish it.

A second worthwhile question is Continuous Minimax Sortition Sample Complexity. Define \(K^*(m,\varepsilon)\) as the smallest panel size for which some panel-selection rule guarantees expected distortion at most \(1+\varepsilon\) across all finite-support metric societies with \(m\) alternatives. The rule may use the society’s distribution and metric when choosing its panel. A solution characterizes \(K^*\) or gives matching universal upper and lower bounds. This asks whether any selection rule can beat uniform sampling’s panel-size requirement in the continuous-population model.

Its anchor is Theorem 8, proved in Appendix A. For \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/30]\), even \(n\), \(m\ge70\), and
\[ k\le \min\left\{\frac n2,\ m-1,\ \frac{\ln(m-1)}{288\varepsilon^2}\right\}, \]
the theorem constructs an \(n\)-agent instance with \(m\) alternatives on which every probability distribution over \(k\)-agent panels has distortion greater than \(1+\varepsilon\). The continuous question is whether the same sample-size floor survives when the society is supplied as a distribution and panel members are sampled from its mass. The result is a lower bound on sample size, not a hardness result: it does not place the underlying problem in a computational-complexity class. I expect an \(\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2}\ln m)\)-type lower bound in the continuous model, but transferring this particular adversarial construction to distributional sampling requires a separate argument.

The authors would likely recognize these as close mirrors of their question. Their motivation is explicitly to test whether a selected panel reaches a decision close to the population’s optimum, and the distribution \(\mu\) states that population directly: mass is a share of society, not a count of named individuals. Plausible regimes include large citizen assemblies whose members fall into a moderate number of metric types defined by relevant attributes. The paper’s guarantees depend on \(m\), not on the number of agents, so expressing the electorate as \(\mu\) preserves the substance of its sample-size question.

This case covers the expected-distortion sample-size results in Theorems 4 and 8. It does not cover the paper’s ex-post guarantees for Fair Greedy Capture, its deterministic-panel bounds, or the empirical section. The lower-bound anchor is valuable because it asks whether the panel-size requirement is intrinsic to worst-case instances, rather than a limitation of uniform selection.

The weak point is that these are sample-complexity questions, not the runtime or parameterized-complexity questions ChoCo is primarily meant to chart. Also, the lower-bound construction can use as many distinct metric types as agents, so it does not establish a benefit in a regime with many agents but relatively few types. The positive case is therefore strongest as a distributional mirror of this paper’s core sampling results; it is not evidence that the paper already contains a computational-complexity result for ChoCo.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor is a computational-complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize. Theorem 4 bounds the number \(k\) of sampled people needed for low expected distortion. Theorem 8 lower-bounds that sample size against every panel-selection rule. Both are sample-complexity statements proved with concentration and anti-concentration arguments. The paper gives no named result about the running time or computational complexity of solving a problem on an instance. Its deterministic, fair-selection, and Fair Greedy Capture results are distortion guarantees of the same kind.

The best version of the first mirror is coherent: represent society by a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\), draw \(k\) agents from it, and ask for the smallest \(k\) that guarantees distortion at most \(1+\varepsilon\). That puts the population on the continuous axis, and sortition from a large population with repeated metric types is plausible. The obstacle is what the question measures. With \(\mu\) supplied, generating a sample and finding its minimum-cost alternative are direct operations; the hard part is bounding the chance that the sample represents the population well. If \(\mu\) is hidden, the question becomes one of statistical estimation, while the paper’s model gives the selection algorithm the agents’ locations. The resulting mirror is a valid statistical question, but it does not chart the computational landscape ChoCo targets.

Theorem 8’s best mirror has the same limitation. One can ask for a minimax lower bound on the number of draws required even by a rule that knows \(\mu\). That would say how large a panel must be, not how hard it is to compute a solution. A version parameterized by support size \(\tau\) might be a useful new sample-complexity result; it would need its own analysis. Turning the question into an optimization problem over selection policies would add computational content, but that would introduce a new task the theorem does not anchor.

Changing the scenario does not resolve the mismatch. Keep a finite panel and a continuous society, and the guarantees concern sampling. Make the panel itself a mass-valued object, and the result concerns a different kind of decision. Ask an algorithm to optimize a selection policy, and the paper’s sample-size bounds no longer supply the computational question.

This negative case has a clear weakness: the distributional sample-complexity mirrors are sensible, close to the paper’s motivation, and may be worthwhile to study in their own right. So the claim that *no* continuous mirror is worthwhile in any broader research setting would be too strong. The case against is specific to ChoCo’s stated remit: these anchors support a continuous statistical mirror, but they do not give the programme a computational problem to classify or solve.
