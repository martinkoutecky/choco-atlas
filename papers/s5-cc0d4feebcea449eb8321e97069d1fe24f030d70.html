The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible named anchor under the rule you set. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity or algorithmic result. The Methods section says rezoning problems are NP-hard in general, and the paper reports simulation outcomes, including a median 14% reduction in the Variance Ratio index. But those are not numbered complexity results; the authors also say their solver generally cannot prove optimality. I can’t honestly turn any of them into an anchor.

There is still a plausible unanchored model-level lead. In a large district, take a type to be a cohort sharing a Census block, demographic group, current school, and travel-time profile. Its mass is its fraction of district students. That fits a high-multiplicity regime: the paper studies districts with thousands of students, and the number of populated block-by-group cohorts can be smaller than the number of pupils.

For each type \(t\) and school \(s\), let \(x_{t,s}\) be the mass assigned there. Require \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\); permit assignments only within the paper’s travel-time limit; cap each school at its allowed size; and require the blocks receiving positive mass for each school to form a connected zone. Ask for an assignment minimizing the paper’s White/non-White Variance Ratio \(V\), computed from the resulting school masses. This keeps the paper’s underlying boundary-redrawing question in view, rather than making the mirror about a fractional encoding.

My prior is that the full problem may be Class C, because contiguity retains combinatorial structure; that is a conjecture, not a result of this paper. Without contiguity, the variable part of \(V\) is a convex perspective objective over a mass-allocation polytope, suggesting a tractable Class A version. The natural follow-up questions are whether contiguity makes the full mass-allocation problem hard, and whether it is tractable when the number of schools or boundary cells is small.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper assigns whole geographic blocks, while this mirror permits mass within a type to split among schools. That extension may be mathematically natural for a continuous population, but the authors might not regard it as their boundary problem. So this is a defensible direction for a positive case, not an anchor-backed one; on the paper’s named-results requirement, there is no anchor to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not count this as an anchor-backed ChoCo case. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational result. Its statement that rezoning is NP-hard is general background; its own CP-SAT runs report simulated outcomes and explicitly do not establish optimality. The 14% median reduction is an empirical finding, not a complexity result. So there is no named result here for the proponent’s mirror to preserve.

The proposed cohort model also has a fidelity problem. The paper estimates student counts for Census blocks, then assigns each block to a school. If a type is block-by-demographic-group and its mass is allowed to split across schools, pupils at the same location can be sent to different schools. That is a mass assignment problem, not the paper’s boundary-redrawing problem. To preserve zoning, the type must retain the block’s location and adjacency information, so the support consists of geographic cells rather than a compact set of interchangeable voter-like types.

There is a stronger faithful formulation: give each block a mass and demographic vector, assign the whole block to one school, and retain travel, capacity, and connectivity constraints. But the paper already aggregates pupils to block counts before optimization, and its segregation indices and capacity limits use proportions and ratios. Normalizing those counts to masses therefore re-encodes the same block-level instances. A complexity theorem for weighted connected partitions could be interesting, but it would be a new result about a new formal model, not a mirror of a named result in this paper. The convex model without contiguity is easier to formulate, but it drops the feature that makes the decisions attendance boundaries.

A continuous spatial-density model with connected zones could still be worthwhile. It would need its own finite input representation and computational questions, and the paper does not supply a theorem to anchor them. So the negative case is strong against calling this paper a demonstrated continuous mirror. The broader claim that no spatial mass formulation could merit ChoCo’s time would go beyond what the paper supports.
