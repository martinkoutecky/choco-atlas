The continuous mirror question

Given finite \(G,T\), rational additive valuations \(v_t\), rational \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), rational per-capita supplies \(q\) with \(\sum_g q_g=\ell\), and fixed \(\ell\), choose \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\) with \(\sum_g b_g=\ell\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\), to maximize the largest \(\lambda\) such that every pair \((t,b),(s,b')\) in the positive support satisfies \(v_t(b)\ge\lambda\max_{u+w=b+b'}\min\{v_t(u),v_t(w)\}\).

The model it lives in

A fixed-\(\ell\) high-multiplicity catalogue market with valuation types \(t\in T\), population masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\), integral bundles \(b\), and mass variables \(x_{t,b}\); maximize \(\lambda\) subject to type-mass, supply, and pairwise \(\mathrm{PMMS}\)-compatibility constraints.

The objection that survived

The positive-support condition makes the mass problem nonconvex: \(\operatorname{supp}(x)\) must satisfy pairwise \(\mathrm{PMMS}\) compatibility, and the proponent does not provide a compressed algorithm or prove that the finite guarantees survive with population-size-independent complexity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 3.9's \(\frac12\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\) construction and common-ordering \(\frac23\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\) construction, with Propositions 3.7 and 3.8 as supporting guarantees. It leaves the broader implication map, tight examples, non-implication results, and existential-only discussion unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\ell\), can the optimal \(\lambda\) and a witnessing mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in the type and catalogue descriptions?
  • Do the \(\frac12\) and common-ordering \(\frac23\) guarantees admit algorithms whose running time is independent of the expanded population size?
  • Does the continuous optimum equal the limit of corresponding finite high-multiplicity instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s \(\mathrm{PMMS}\)-allocation problem. The paper contains no named NP-hardness result; my lead anchor is the constructive algorithmic result:

Corollary 3.9, proved in this paper using earlier algorithms, states that for \(n\ge 3\):

  • the round-robin algorithm, in which agents successively choose their favourite remaining good, produces a \(\frac12\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\) allocation; and
  • if all agents agree on the ordering of goods by value, then a \(\frac23\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\) allocation can be computed in polynomial time.

The first conclusion combines the known polynomial-time construction of an \(\mathrm{EF1}\) allocation with the paper’s Proposition 3.8, which proves that every \(\alpha\)-\(\mathrm{EF1}\) allocation is an \(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\) allocation. At \(\alpha=1\), this gives \(\frac12\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\). The second combines polynomial-time exact \(\mathrm{EFX}\) algorithms under a common item ordering, cited from Barman and Krishnamurty and from Plaut and Roughgarden, with Proposition 3.7, which turns exact \(\mathrm{EFX}\) into \(\frac23\)-\(\mathrm{PMMS}\). Thus Corollary 3.9 is stated here, but its algorithmic ingredients are partly cited from elsewhere.

Here is the continuous mirror I would put before the authors.

There is a finite catalogue \(G=\{g_1,\ldots,g_r\}\) of standardized indivisible goods, with \(q_g\) copies of good \(g\) per capita. Every agent receives exactly \(\ell\) goods, where \(\sum_{g\in G}q_g=\ell\). A voter or agent type \(t\in T\), with \(|T|=\tau\), is specified by its complete additive valuation vector \(v_t(g)\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Thus \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population having valuation type \(t\).

This describes, for example, a national benefits programme serving millions of households, where only a small number of household profiles occur and the programme distributes many copies of a fixed catalogue of indivisible benefits. The high-multiplicity regime is \(N\gg \tau,r,\ell\): there may be millions of agents but only a few dozen valuation types and a small fixed number of goods per agent. For rational \(\mu\) and \(q\), an ordinary finite instance is recovered by taking \(N\) large enough that \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq_g\) are integers.

Let

\[ \mathcal B_\ell=\left\{b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{G}:\sum_{g\in G}b_g=\ell\right\} \]

be the set of integral bundles. The action is a mass assignment \(x=(x_{t,b})\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B_\ell}x_{t,b}=\mu_t \]

for every type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B_\ell}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g \]

for every good \(g\). Hence every unit of population receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

For two bundles \(b,b'\), define the type-\(t\) pairwise maximin share by

\[ \operatorname{pmm}_t(b,b') = \max_{\substack{u+w=b+b'\\u,w\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}}} \min\{v_t(u),v_t(w)\}. \]

The continuous problem is:

Continuous High-Multiplicity PMMS. Given \((G,T,v,\mu,q)\), output a mass assignment \(x\) maximizing the largest \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) such that, whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(x_{s,b'}>0\),

\[ v_t(b)\ge \lambda\,\operatorname{pmm}_t(b,b'). \]

The direct mirror of Corollary 3.9 asks whether one can output such an \(x\) with \(\lambda\ge\frac12\); in the common-ordering regime, it asks for \(\lambda\ge\frac23\).

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. Preferences remain additive, goods remain indivisible for each individual, the fairness condition remains pairwise maximin share fairness, and the output is still an allocation of goods rather than a fractional division of goods. The only co-modification is the necessary one: the supply of each good scales with the population, so that a continuum of agents does not receive nothing simply because the original paper has only finitely many goods.

I expect the fixed-\(\ell\) version to be a plausible Class A problem. A round-robin execution over a huge cloned electorate can potentially be compressed into an event-driven sweep over type masses and item supplies. Equivalently, one can seek a configuration formulation whose columns are integral bundles and whose output is the mass vector \(x\). In the common-ordering case, the \(\frac23\) guarantee should be especially amenable to such compression. The unrestricted version, with \(\ell\) part of the input, is a worthwhile boundary question: the number of possible integral bundle configurations may become large, and selecting a mutually compatible support for the PMMS inequalities could create genuine continuum-specific hardness. It would not be hardness transferred from this paper, since the finite source problem has the positive algorithmic result of Corollary 3.9.

The mirror covers only the algorithmic part of the paper: Corollary 3.9, supported by Propositions 3.7 and 3.8. It does not claim to continuize every implication among \(\mathrm{EF1}\), \(\mathrm{EFX}\), \(\mathrm{MMS}\), and \(\mathrm{PMMS}\). Those propositions can generate follow-up questions, such as whether the \(\frac12\) and \(\frac23\) guarantees survive in compressed form, whether exact continuous \(\mathrm{PMMS}\) is computable, and whether the optimum \(\lambda\) agrees with the limit of finite high-multiplicity instances.

The weakest point is that Corollary 3.9 does not itself provide a compressed high-multiplicity algorithm. The ordinary round-robin algorithm may take time proportional to the enormous population \(N\), and PMMS constraints are global and support-dependent rather than obviously linear. So the positive case rests on a credible but nontrivial bridge: compressing a known finite constructive allocation into mass variables without allowing fractional bundles. That bridge is exactly the computational question the continuous mirror would expose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is weaker than the positive case, and I would not honestly give a confident red verdict. Corollary 3.9 is a genuine named algorithmic anchor: it asserts polynomial-time constructions of \(\frac12\)-PMMS allocations, and of \(\frac23\)-PMMS allocations under a common item ordering. It cannot be dismissed as merely axiomatic or as a literature remark.

The strongest objection is that PMMS does not continuize cleanly into the kind of mass-optimization problem ChoCo is designed to exploit. Preserve the paper’s ex post meaning, and let \(x_{t,b}\) denote the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(b\). For a fixed guarantee \(\lambda\), define a conflict between \((t,b)\) and \((s,b')\) whenever

\[ v_t(b)<\lambda\,\operatorname{pmm}_t(b,b') \]

or the symmetric inequality for \(s\) fails. Then every positive-support pair must be conflict-free. The mass and supply equations are linear, but the fairness condition is a support constraint:

\[ \operatorname{supp}(x) \]

must be an independent set in this compatibility graph. A type receiving an arbitrarily small positive mass of a bundle still imposes all of its pairwise PMMS constraints. Consequently, convex combinations of two fair mass assignments need not be fair. Population mass does not produce an LP relaxation; it leaves a discrete support-selection problem underneath.

This defeats the most attractive version of the proposed mirror. The \(\frac12\) result from EF1 and the \(\frac23\) result from EFX are pointwise implications about already-constructed finite allocations. They transfer automatically once one has selected a compatible integral-bundle support. The genuinely new task is therefore not continuizing Corollary 3.9, but solving a new compatibility-and-supply problem over bundle configurations. A fixed-catalogue round-robin sweep may batch one particular construction, but it does not solve the proposed optimization of the best \(\lambda\), nor the existence of exact PMMS.

The obvious repair does not help the mirroring claim. If one replaces the support condition by expected bundle values, or evaluates PMMS on a type’s average bundle, the formulation becomes more convex but is no longer the paper’s PMMS notion. It is an ex ante or fractional-outcome relaxation. Conversely, retaining integral bundles preserves the authors’ problem but also preserves the combinatorial support obstruction. There is no middle formulation that both retains PMMS and turns the anchor into the kind of continuous LP problem exemplified by the ChoCo programme.

There is also a genuine modelling discontinuity. If the paper’s fixed set of goods is retained while the population becomes atomless, almost every agent receives no goods and PMMS becomes vacuous. Scaling the goods supply per capita avoids that degeneration, but it introduces a repeated-catalogue market absent from the paper. That market is plausible—standardized benefits distributed to many household types—but it is a new fair-division model whose central data are the catalogue and supply rates, not merely a continuous version of the paper’s agent population.

Still, these objections do not establish the requested universal negative. The proponent’s high-multiplicity scenario is sensible, preserves integral bundles for individuals, has an exact rational finite-instance dictionary, and does not rely on identity-specific preferences. The common-ordering case may admit a particularly clean batched construction, while the unrestricted case could yield a meaningful support-selection or configuration-complexity study.

So the strongest honest conclusion is not that no worthwhile mirror exists. It is that this paper is a poor fit for the programme’s flagship continuization story: its only anchor transfers local finite-allocation inequalities, while preserving PMMS forces a nonconvex bundle-support problem, and convexifying that problem changes the fairness notion. The positive mirror survives the formal gate, but it should be treated as a weaker, exploratory Class-A/Class-C boundary question rather than a compelling continuization result.
