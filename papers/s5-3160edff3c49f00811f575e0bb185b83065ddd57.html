The case FOR (proponent)

The strict answer is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise. So it supplies no anchor that satisfies the requested criterion. The closest result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every normalized two-person bargaining set \(S\) in the paper’s class \(\mathcal B\), the Nash solution \(N(S)\)—the point maximizing \(z_1z_2\) over \(z\in S\) with \(z\ge 0\)—is the unique Debreu–Hurwicz equilibrium of the specified socio-legal system, and that the equilibrium action and payoff both equal \(N(S)\). This is an equilibrium characterization, not a complexity result. The following is a credible computational question built around it, but it is a follow-on to Proposition 1 rather than a qualifying complexity anchor.

Call it Population-Generated Nash Bargaining Computation. Consider two large constituencies negotiating over a common project or resource. Each member belongs to one of \(\tau\) types, where a type specifies the person’s constituency, preferences, resource needs, and disagreement payoff. The input gives the types’ rational masses \(\mu_t\), which sum to one, together with the type utilities and the feasible resource allocation rules. The regime of interest has many members and moderate \(\tau\): for example, large worker and owner constituencies with repeated preference and constraint profiles.

A feasible allocation assigns resources to the mass of each type and induces aggregate payoffs for the two constituencies. Let \(S_\mu\) be the resulting normalized set of feasible payoff gains over disagreement, with the promise that \(S_\mu\in\mathcal B\). This promise supplies the smoothness and normalization conditions needed for Proposition 1. The question is: given this instance and a rational \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a feasible type-level allocation and a claim vector within \(\varepsilon\) of the unique Nash point \(N(S_\mu)\). By Proposition 1, that vector is also the unique equilibrium action in the paper’s socio-legal system built from \(S_\mu\). The type-level allocations are masses; they may be fractional even though each individual receives an ordinary allocation. The population distribution is the input that generates the bargaining set, so this is a population mirror rather than a proposal to continuize only the payoff space.

I would expect the approximation problem to be tractable when the induced feasibility and utility description has efficient convex optimization or separation. The Nash product can be optimized over a convex payoff set using the concave objective \(\log z_1+\log z_2\) on positive gains. The computational question then includes whether the population description lets us optimize or separate over \(S_\mu\) efficiently. Further questions include exact output complexity, dependence on \(\tau\) and input bit length, and whether less structured population-generated bargaining sets yield hardness.

The mirror covers only Proposition 1’s support of the symmetric Nash solution. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s implementation theory as a whole, or its Proposition 2 on weak implementation. Its weakest point is that the paper treats \(S\) as the characteristics of two players; it never models the members of the negotiating constituencies as a society. The proposed regime gives those bargaining data a plausible high-multiplicity foundation, and leaves the paper’s Nash-solution question intact, but whether the authors would regard its computational study as their problem is uncertain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 characterizes the unique Debreu–Hurwicz equilibrium as the Nash solution; Proposition 2 gives a weak implementation result. Neither defines an encoded computational problem or establishes its complexity. The proponent is right to call their proposal a follow-on to Proposition 1, rather than a mirror of a complexity result.

The strongest version of that follow-on is coherent: model large worker and owner constituencies by type masses, specify a resource technology and a rule for aggregating members’ utilities, obtain a bargaining set \(S_\mu\), then compute \(N(S_\mu)\). But this puts the population in the construction of the bargaining set. In the paper, \(S\) describes a bargaining problem between two players, and only those two players choose actions or make deviations. Members of the proposed constituencies do not participate in the socio-legal system. To make their masses determine \(S_\mu\), the model must add a production technology and a collective utility aggregator—choices the paper does not study. If \(S_\mu\) is given, the population has already been compressed away; if constructing it is the hard part, the complexity comes from the added allocation model.

Making the constituency members the players would preserve their individual roles, but it would replace the paper’s two-person Nash demand game with a new collective bargaining model. The paper supplies no rule for that change. So the proposed mirror does not retain the computational question of a result in this paper while continuizing its society.

That is the strongest negative case, and it has a limit: a well-specified population-generated bargaining model could still be worthwhile research that the authors might recognize. The material supports skepticism that it is a mirror of this paper; it cannot establish the universal claim that no plausible scenario could make the extension valuable.
