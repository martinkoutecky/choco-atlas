The continuous mirror question

Given an explicit finite acyclic binary game tree \(G=(S,s_0,T,g)\), rational finite-support distributions \(\mu_{i,v}\) over complete terminal valuations \(v:T\to\mathbb{Q}\) for each of two sides \(i\), with \(\sum_{i,v}\mu_{i,v}=1\) and side masses \(\pi_i=\sum_v\mu_{i,v}>0\), define \(U_i(t)=\pi_i^{-1}\sum_v\mu_{i,v}v(t)\). Using the paper’s tie-breaking and preference refinement, compute for every initial budget share \(\beta\in[0,1]\) the Bottom-Equilibrium outcome and a compact exact strategy description for every subgame, such as its budget cutoffs and actions.

The model it lives in

Types are pairs of side and complete terminal valuation, carrying rational mass \(\mu_{i,v}\). Their masses define each delegate’s mean payoff \(U_i\); the decision rules are the delegates’ bids and chosen successors at each state and budget, and the output is the exact outcome map with a compact strategy description.

The objection that survived

Constituents do not bid or choose actions in the paper; the mirror relies on a common cardinal scale and a delegate mandate to maximize mean welfare, which the paper neither explains nor derives. The proponent explicitly scopes the question to organized blocs adopting that mandate, so this limits the mirror’s reach rather than defeating it.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 18’s computation of a Bottom-Equilibrium outcome-equivalent profile for explicit binary games; it leaves the paper’s equilibrium properties, fairness claims, and succinct-valuation results without a population mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the computation’s bit complexity be bounded polynomially in \(|S|\), support size \(\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\), including payoff comparisons and ties?
  • How do the exact budget cutoffs change as the constituency masses vary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is for binary sequential bargaining between two large, organized constituencies. The paper itself points to parties bargaining over policy clauses and budgets reflecting their relative power, so this is close to its stated application. Imagine each side appoints a negotiator, and each negotiator is evaluated by the side’s average welfare over the final policy outcome.

A member’s type is their complete cardinal valuation \(v:T\to\mathbb{Q}\) over the game’s terminal outcomes. Let \(\mu_{i,v}\) be the fraction of the whole society that belongs to side \(i\) and has valuation \(v\), with \(\sum_{i,v}\mu_{i,v}=1\). If \(\pi_i=\sum_v\mu_{i,v}\), the side’s representative has payoff
\[ U_i(t)=\frac{1}{\pi_i}\sum_v\mu_{i,v}v(t). \]
Here mass records the composition of each side; the representatives’ scrip budgets govern bidding. A plausible regime has many members but relatively few recurring valuation types over a fixed policy agenda. Treating members with the same valuation as one type makes their proportions the natural input. This requires a common cardinal welfare scale within each side.

I would call the resulting question Continuous-Population Bottom-Equilibrium Computation. An instance gives an explicit finite acyclic binary game tree \(G=(S,s_0,T,g)\), rational distributions over valuation types for each side, and the paper’s tie-breaking refinement. At each nonterminal state, the two representatives bid from their current shares of a unit budget; the higher bidder pays the other and chooses a child. Their terminal payoffs are \(U_1,U_2\). The question is to compute, for every initial budget share \(\beta\in[0,1]\), the Bottom-Equilibrium outcome and a compact strategy description that realizes it in every subgame. A solution can give the exact budget cutoffs, outcomes, and action rules, rather than an uncompressed listing over infinitely many budgets.

My anchor is Proposition 18, proved in this paper in Section 7. Restated in full: for a binary bidding game structure \(G\), Algorithm 3 computes a profile \(\gamma\) that is outcome-equivalent to a Bottom Equilibrium of \(\langle G,-\rangle\); moreover, Algorithm 3 runs in time \(|S|\cdot\mathrm{poly}(|T|)=\mathrm{poly}(|S|)\). The paper gives no comparable named hardness result for this computation, so this algorithmic result is the right anchor. In the mirror, first compute the rational aggregate utilities \(U_i\) from the type masses, then apply the equilibrium computation to the resulting game. The expected classification is Class A, for explicit game trees and explicitly listed type supports, with running time polynomial in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The paper states its bound in terms of game size; a formal population version should make the bit complexity of the rational arithmetic explicit.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the paper’s computation of the Bottom Equilibrium for binary games, with constituency distributions supplying the two representatives’ utility functions. The paper already allows continuous bids; the mirror’s continuous object is the population distribution that determines those utilities. This does not cover uncoordinated members bidding individually, or make a claim about succinctly represented game trees whose size may be exponential in the agenda.

The weakest point is the representative-welfare assumption. Constituents may disagree about how their cardinal utilities should be compared, and the paper does not model intragroup bargaining or the process that gives a negotiator a mandate to maximize average welfare. The mirror is credible for two organized blocs that adopt that mandate; its continuum mostly gives a natural high-multiplicity description of bloc composition, rather than a new source of computational difficulty. Further questions include whether the result extends to more than two blocs or nonbinary agendas, how to solve succinctly represented policy games, and how the equilibrium cutoffs change when estimated type masses are perturbed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 18 is the strongest anchor here: it is a named algorithmic result, and the proposed average-utility version is mathematically well defined. The problem is that its population is not the population of the game the proposition solves.

In the paper, the strategic agents are two players. Each has one utility function, one budget, and one bid at each node. In the proposed mirror, constituency members never act in the game; their distribution is used to construct the two representatives’ utility functions. Once the averages \(U_1,U_2\) are computed, the equilibrium algorithm receives the same kind of instance as before. The constituency support and masses do not enter its strategic structure.

The strongest version of this scenario—organized blocs with common, comparable welfare scales and delegates mandated to maximize average welfare—is plausible. But it adds a model of representation and collective preference formation that the paper does not supply. The paper’s discussion of parties bargaining supports the application, but does not make their constituents the agents in its computational result. Treating the distribution as a population mirror therefore depends on treating the delegates’ payoff construction as continuization of the society itself. That is the weak link.

There is a dilemma. Keep the delegates as the only players, and the population is exogenous data for their payoff tables. Make constituents the strategic participants so population mass becomes part of the game, and the model is no longer the paper’s two-player bidding game; Proposition 18 does not establish an algorithm for that variant. Neither route gives a clear high-multiplicity mirror of the named result.

This is a substantial scope objection, but not a proof that the extension is unworthwhile. Composition-dependent equilibrium analysis for representative bargaining could be useful. If the reader accepts an exogenous distribution that determines player utilities as continuization of the population, then Proposition 18 survives as a continuous mirror. The negative case rests on why that should not count as continuizing this paper’s computational problem.
