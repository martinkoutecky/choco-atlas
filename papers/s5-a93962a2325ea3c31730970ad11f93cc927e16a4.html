Every anchor argued

The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\), item categories \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and valuations \(p_{tj}\in\{0,1,+\infty\}\), where each category \(j\) is valued \(+\infty\) by at most one type, assign each mass to integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\) subject to \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j\le b_j\), maximizing the minimum utility \(q\); determine its exact or approximation complexity in \(m\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\), including whether the factor-\(2\) barrier persists.

The model it lives in

A continuum of consumers represented by complete types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\); each consumer receives an integral bundle \(z\), while mass may split among bundles. Per-capita supplies are \(b_j\), and \(x_{t,z}\) records the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(z\).

The objection that survived

The paper’s configuration-LP algorithm is described for explicit item lists; its running time does not settle pricing or optimization with binary-encoded per-capita supplies.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7’s InfDegreeOne hardness under the proposed type-level restriction. It leaves Theorem 4’s individual-player DegreeTwo restriction, the degree-two approximation algorithm, degree reduction, and the arborescence and Sherali–Adams results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine exact and approximation complexity in \(m\), \(|T|\), and \(L\) when per-capita supplies are binary-encoded.
  • Establish whether the factor-\(2\) inapproximability of Theorem 7 persists for this mass-allocation model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is mass Degree-Two MaxMin Allocation. Take a finite set \(T\) of complete consumer types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has the same additive valuation vector \(p_{tj}\) over a finite catalogue \(J\) of indivisible goods. Good \(j\) has a rational supply \(b_j\) per person. An allocation is a finite-support collection of masses \(x_{t,z}\ge 0\), where \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\) is an integral bundle:
\[ \sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j\le b_j. \]
The question is to maximize \(q\) subject to \(x_{t,z}>0\) only when \(\sum_j p_{tj}z_j\ge q\). Thus every person gets whole goods and meets the guarantee; only the mass of people assigned each bundle is fractional. For the Degree-Two version, each good type has positive value for at most two consumer types.

This mirrors Theorem 4, proved in the paper: DegreeTwo MaxMin Allocation cannot be approximated within any factor better than \(2\) unless \(P=NP\); the same holds for symmetric instances. The paper’s proof gives a gap of \(2\) versus \(1\), using a bounded-occurrence 3SAT reduction. The continuous question is whether this factor-\(2\) barrier survives when people of each type form a continuum and can be divided among different integral bundles.

A plausible regime is a large regional aid programme with many applicants but a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of standardized need profiles. The inventory of indivisible kits scales with the population, and each kit category is useful to at most two profiles. Thousands of applicants can then share each profile, with \(N\gg\tau\). The type must include every relevant feature, including eligibility and valuation. This is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s fairness objective: maximize the minimum utility across people. The per-person inventory scale and fractional masses of people are co-modifications forced by the continuum; individual bundles remain integral.

A second, narrower mirror is mass InfDegreeOne MaxMin Allocation, the same problem restricted to \(p_{tj}\in\{0,1,+\infty\}\), with each good type valued at \(+\infty\) by at most one consumer type. Theorem 7, also proved here, says InfDegreeOne MaxMin Allocation cannot be approximated within a factor better than \(2\) unless \(P=NP\). Its proof’s construction also has nonzero value on at most two players per item, so it lies in the intersection of InfDegreeOne and DegreeTwo. The continuous question is whether that hardness remains for the corresponding mass allocation model under both restrictions. This is a useful secondary anchor because it asks whether the obstruction persists even with a very restricted valuation scale and unique “infinite-value” recipients.

Theorem 4 is my strongest anchor; Theorem 7 is a tighter restriction of the same broad allocation question, rather than independent evidence of a different phenomenon. For both continuous problems, I would tentatively expect Class C hardness from the integral bundle choices and the combinatorics of types and goods; the configuration formulation exposes a potentially difficult pricing problem for finding a low-cost integral bundle that reaches a target utility. But I would not claim that either theorem establishes this. In particular, a continuum can split a type’s mass among bundles, so the paper’s SAT gap does not automatically transfer. Whether the mass problem admits a factor-\((2-\varepsilon)\) algorithm, or remains hard at that threshold, is precisely the question for a prover.

The main weakness is the degree condition. In the paper, DegreeTwo counts *individual players* interested in each item. In this mirror, the natural sparse condition counts *types*: one good category can be valued by every member of either of two large groups. That is a type-level analogue, not a literal preservation of the per-item degree bound, and the authors could reasonably reject it as a mirror of Theorem 4’s restricted case. They should still recognize the underlying mass MaxMin allocation question as theirs. The scope here is those two allocation hardness results; the paper’s arborescence algorithm and Sherali–Adams results are not population results I would use to strengthen this case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the two anchors collapse to one model, and that model is already represented by the paper’s configuration LP. At a fixed guarantee \(q\), its variables say how much of each player or type is assigned each integral bundle. In a continuous population, those same variables are actual masses of people receiving whole bundles. Group identical players by type and normalize the variables and item supplies by population size: the configuration constraints become exactly the mass constraints in the proposed mirror. There is no integrality gap to round away, because fractional mass is the allocation.

That makes Theorems 4 and 7 poor anchors for a new continuous question. Their hardness concerns assigning whole bundles to a finite set of named players; the paper already develops the configuration LP that describes the corresponding mass allocation. It also explains how to optimize that LP approximately using a knapsack separation oracle. InfDegreeOne changes the permitted valuations, but not this correspondence. The SAT reductions do not establish hardness for mass allocation; the relevant point against the mirror is that its exact formulation is already present in the paper’s machinery.

The stronger version of the proponent’s mirror—many applicants in repeated complete types, scaled inventories, integral bundles, and a degree bound on item categories across types—still has this configuration-LP form. If instead DegreeTwo must mean that each good is valued by at most two named individuals, keeping that condition leaves no meaningful high-multiplicity regime. Grouping repeated profiles restores a sensible regime, but then the paper’s LP already supplies its natural formulation.

This is not a decisive universal case. The paper’s running-time discussion assumes an explicit list of items; it does not by itself settle complexity in the bit length of binary-encoded per-capita supplies and compressed item counts. That compressed version could still be worthwhile. So I would reject Theorem 4 and Theorem 7 as independent anchors, and regard the explicit-item mass version as already covered, but I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile type-level mirror. The negative case is weakest precisely for the proponent’s scaled, compressed-inventory scenario.
