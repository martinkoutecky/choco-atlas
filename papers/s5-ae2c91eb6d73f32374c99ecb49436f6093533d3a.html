The continuous mirror question

Given an axis-ordered set of categories \(R\), finite types \(T\) with strict single-peaked rankings and current categories, and rational masses \(\mu_t\), let \(q_j\) be the mass initially at category \(r_j\). Does there exist a reallocation \(x_{t,j}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,j}=q_j\), assigning each type only to its current or a strictly preferred category, and assigning positive total mass to strictly preferred categories?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with finite support over types defined by a current house category and a strict single-peaked ranking over categories; category supplies scale with population mass, and \(x_{t,j}\) records the mass of type \(t\) assigned to category \(r_j\). The decision is whether a feasible reassignment gives positive mass a strictly preferred category.

The objection that survived

The aggregate problem reduces to cycle detection on the category graph, so the single-peaked Diver scan and its individual-query lower bounds disappear; the proponent acknowledges the lost linear-time advantage but does not address this further simplification.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s CheckPO decision. It does not cover the Crawler results, the Diver’s communication bounds, or the finite-population lower bounds in Propositions 4–5.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the tight complexity of this finite-support CheckPO problem in the number of categories, support size, and rational bit length?
  • Under what replication conditions does a mass-improving reassignment correspond exactly to a discrete improving allocation in a market of interchangeable houses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named hardness result to anchor on, so the strongest honest case is algorithmic. My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: the Diver terminates, runs in \(O(n)\) time, and correctly decides whether the initial assignment is Pareto-optimal. The continuous mirror should ask the same underlying question—whether an existing house allocation can be Pareto-improved—when the population is represented by masses of repeated types.

Call it Continuous CheckPO for single-peaked house markets. Let \(R=\{r_1,\ldots,r_k\}\) be ordered house categories on a common axis. The population is atomless with total mass \(1\), described by a finite support \(T\). A type \(t\in T\) consists of a strict single-peaked ranking \(\succ_t\) over the categories and the category \(e_t\) the type currently occupies. Its mass is a binary rational \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Category \(r_j\) has supply \(q_j=\sum_{t:e_t=r_j}\mu_t\), so the current allocation is feasible and each category’s supply scales with the population.

The question is whether that current allocation is Pareto-optimal. A candidate reassignment is a matrix \(x_{t,j}\geq 0\): \(x_{t,j}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving category \(r_j\). It must satisfy \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_t x_{t,j}=q_j\) for every category. It may assign mass only to its current category or one the type strictly prefers. Define \(\Delta^*\) as the maximum, over these feasible reassignments, of the total mass assigned to a strictly preferred category. Return “not Pareto-optimal” exactly when \(\Delta^*>0\); a maximizing \(x\) is a witness. Individuals still receive one whole house: \(x_{t,j}\) records how much of an atomless type population goes to each category, not anyone receiving a fraction of a house.

A plausible regime is a large housing exchange along a corridor of locations: many households, relatively few preference-and-current-location types, and many interchangeable homes within each location category. For example, \(N\) may be very large while \(k\) and the support size \(\tau=|T|\) remain moderate. The common axis and single-peaked location preferences come directly from the paper’s house-market setting. Having the current category in the type is also faithful to the problem: the allocation being checked is part of the instance, so agents with different current houses are different types.

I expect this problem to be Class A. Its input is explicit in \(k,\tau\), and the rational bit length \(L\); the variables and constraints above form a polynomial-size flow or linear program, so the exact decision is polynomial in \(k,\tau,L\). The continuous-mass reading is natural here because a fraction of a repeated type can be reassigned while every household still gets one house. For rational instances, discrete improving assignments give feasible mass witnesses; conversely, a rational witness can be realized in a sufficiently replicated market. At a particular finite population size, however, a fractional witness may not be realizable without replication.

This scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 1’s CheckPO problem, not the Crawler or Diver mechanisms, strategy-proofness, individual rationality, or the paper’s communication protocol. Nor would I claim that the paper’s finite \(\Omega(n)\) lower bound transfers as a lower bound in population size: after compression, \(N\) is not the input size. The cited probabilistic-assignment work concerns continuity in outcomes; it does not itself give this continuous-population model.

The weakest point is the category model. The paper has distinct houses and strict rankings over them; this mirror groups interchangeable houses by location and ranks the categories. The aggregate Pareto question remains recognizable, but it is not the exact same object-by-object market, and a referee could reasonably ask the authors to defend that coarsening. There is a second limitation: the flow formulation is polynomial even without single-peakedness, so it does not preserve the Diver’s particular linear-time advantage. The more specific follow-up is whether the single-peaked order supports a Diver-like algorithm with a sharper bound in \(k,\tau,L\), and what exact discrete-to-continuous correspondence holds at a fixed replication factor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor: it states that CheckPO can be decided in linear time, and Propositions 4–5 give matching linear lower bounds. So the strongest objection is not that the paper lacks a result to mirror.

The difficulty is that the proponent’s model changes what is being checked. In the paper, houses are distinct, each agent owns a particular house, and the Diver interrogates named agents. In the mass model, houses become interchangeable location categories with scalable supplies, and Pareto improvement means that a positive mass can be reassigned without anyone in that mass being worse off. That is a coherent high-multiplicity market, but it is not the paper’s exact market. If exact house identities and rankings over those houses matter, the endowed house is part of an agent’s type; the support can then grow with the population. Keeping \(\tau\) small requires treating multiple houses as equivalent and changing the object and preference model.

That change also strips away the feature Theorem 1 analyzes. In the category model, draw an edge \(i\to j\) when positive mass initially at category \(i\) strictly prefers category \(j\). A mass-improving reassignment exists exactly when this graph has a directed cycle: a cycle supports a sufficiently small positive transfer, and any feasible improving transfer decomposes into cycles. So the proposed problem reduces to cycle detection on categories, without needing single-peakedness or a Diver-like scan of agents. This is a clean aggregate question, but the paper’s linear-time result and its individual-query lower bound do not transfer to it.

A more faithful continuum of locations avoids grouping houses into categories, but then types must record both preferences and initial locations. For populations spread over locations, that generally requires a non-finite-support input or a support that grows with the population, so the proposed finite-\(\tau\) complexity model no longer applies directly.

These are meaningful limits on the mirror, but they do not defeat it. A large exchange among interchangeable homes is plausible, and the programme explicitly allows supplies to scale and type-level averages to be fractional. The existing nonatomic assignment literature confirms that continuum assignment is an established model, but its LP and equilibrium results do not by themselves answer this paper’s computational CheckPO question. [Gretsky, Ostroy, and Zame, “The Nonatomic Assignment Model”](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01213255). I cannot make a strong universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists; the best negative case is that the proposed mirror is a simpler, category-level extension whose connection to the Diver’s individual-house result is loose.
