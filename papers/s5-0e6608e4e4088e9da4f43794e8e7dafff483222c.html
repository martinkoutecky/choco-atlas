The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. This paper has no numbered result of the required kind: no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserts a computational complexity classification. The paper’s own \(R_f(4)=4\), \(R_p(4)=4\), and \(R_p(5)=5\) appear as computational findings in Section 4, not as named complexity results.

The nearest named result is Theorem 2, cited from Chaudhury et al. [7], not proved here. It says that for \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\), if \(g(y)\) is the smallest integer \(d\) with \(dR_f(d)\ge y\), then every \(n\)-agent instance has a partial \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX allocation leaving at most \(4n/(\varepsilon g(2n\varepsilon))\) goods unallocated. That is an existence bound, not a computational result, so it cannot honestly serve as one of the requested anchors. The paper’s SAT and symmetry methods compute small rainbow cycle numbers, but they do not provide a named complexity classification either.

If a contextual, explicitly unanchored mirror is useful, I would pose High-Multiplicity Partial-EFX Waste Minimization. Take a finite set \(G\) of good categories and a finite, deletion-closed menu \(\mathcal B\subseteq 2^G\) of integral bundles. The input gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s complete valuation \(v_t:\mathcal B\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\). A solution is a mass \(x_{t,A}\ge0\) of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(A\), with \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,A:g\in A}x_{t,A}\le q_g\). Require \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX across the positive-mass support: whenever \(x_{t,A},x_{u,B}>0\), require \(v_t(A)\ge(1-\varepsilon)v_t(B\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in B\). Minimize unused per-capita supply, \(\sum_g q_g-\sum_{t,A}|A|x_{t,A}\).

Here, type means identical valuation for everything the allocation problem uses; mass is the fraction of agents of that type. Many residents receiving standardized public-service bundles, for example, could share a small number \(\tau\) of valuation types while population size grows. Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the mass assigned to each type–bundle pair is fractional. The per-capita supply is a co-modification needed to scale the goods with the population.

That makes this a plausible continuous version of the paper’s EFX motivation, not of its central computation of \(R_f(d)\). Theorem 2 makes vanishing per-capita waste a natural question: its stated bound, divided by \(n\), is \(4/(\varepsilon g(2n\varepsilon))\), which tends to zero for fixed \(\varepsilon\). But that does not prove the same result for the type-and-bundle model above. I would expect mass optimization to be tractable once the allocation support is fixed, while selecting a compatible support may retain combinatorial hardness; the paper supplies no named hardness anchor to settle that prediction. The resulting questions are whether the model always admits zero-waste \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX allocations, whether such allocations can be found efficiently, and how their solutions relate to discrete high-multiplicity instances.

The soft spot is decisive: this candidate is a reasonable high-multiplicity EFX research question, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. Under the requested anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors and therefore no honest per-anchor continuous questions to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point is that the proponent found no eligible result to anchor a mirror. That is accurate: \(R_f(4)=4\), \(R_p(4)=4\), and \(R_p(5)=5\) are computational findings, but the paper states no named complexity result. Theorem 2 is an existence guarantee cited from earlier work, not a computational result proved here. So the paper supplies no anchor for the requested continuous computational questions.

The proposed High-Multiplicity Partial-EFX Waste Minimization problem is a sensible research question: agents can share valuation types, receive integral bundles, and be represented by fractional masses. But it adds a bundle menu, per-capita supplies, and a waste objective. The paper neither studies that optimization problem nor gives a computational result about it. Theorem 2 may motivate asking about asymptotic waste, but that would extend the paper’s EFX motivation rather than mirror its contribution: computing rainbow cycle numbers with SAT.

A closer attempt would treat the rainbow-cycle graph’s vertices as agents and aggregate repeated vertices into types. That aggregation loses the pair-by-pair edge labels on which the cycle condition depends. A finite type distribution does not retain which mappings connect which vertices; in a continuum, pointwise cycle existence is also sensitive to details a distribution over types discards. A graphon or interaction kernel could preserve more of that structure, but then the continuous object is the graph and its relations, not a society distribution of the kind ChoCo studies. Restricting interactions to depend only on types would define a new structured problem, not preserve the paper’s general rainbow-cycle computation.

This makes a strong case against calling any proposed question a continuous mirror of this paper. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any scenario: the EFX allocation model could merit study on its own. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: the paper’s central computational contribution offers no named result to mirror, and the plausible EFX extension is a new problem rather than a mirror.
