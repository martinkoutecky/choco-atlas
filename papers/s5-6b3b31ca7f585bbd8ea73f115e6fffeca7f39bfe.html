The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a computational question about a model this paper already makes continuous. But the paper has no named computational result of the kind the brief requires: no theorem classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 1 proves equilibrium existence; Theorems 2–3 prove core–equilibrium equivalences; Theorem 4 proves a blocking result. These are economic existence and structure results, not complexity results. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I cannot honestly claim that the paper establishes a complexity result to continuize.

The most credible follow-on question is Step-MEE Recognition. Take a finite set of states \(\Omega\), \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and finitely many goods. A type is the complete tuple \((\Pi_t,\pi_t,e_t,u_t)\): its information partition, prior, state-contingent endowment, and utility. Encode the primitives and a supplied type-contingent allocation \(x_t(\omega)\) as rationals, with utilities given by concave piecewise-linear functions. Require feasibility:
\[ \sum_t\mu_t x_t(\omega)=\sum_t\mu_t e_t(\omega) \quad\text{for every }\omega\in\Omega. \]
Ask whether there is a normalized, nonzero state-contingent price \(p\) such that each \(x_t\) maximizes
\[ V_t(y)=\sum_{E\in\Pi_t}\pi_t(E)\min_{\omega\in E}u_t(\omega,y(\omega)) \]
over the type’s budget set
\[ \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)\cdot y(\omega) \le \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)\cdot e_t(\omega). \]
A “yes” solution is a supporting price vector. This asks about the underlying market allocation, not an encoding.

The population regime is plausible: many households in a large market, grouped by a modest number of shared information, endowment, and preference profiles. The paper itself supplies unusually direct evidence that its authors would recognize this population mirror. Section 4.2 constructs a continuum economy with equal treatment within each type. Proposition 2 says that an MEE in the finite economy corresponds to a step-allocation MEE in that continuum economy, and conversely. Theorem 3, proved in the paper, says that under its stated assumptions a feasible step allocation in the associated continuum economy is an MEE if and only if it is in the maximin core. For finite-state computational inputs, Theorem 2 gives the related core–equilibrium equivalence.

Those results make the model and the proposed question credible, but they do not settle its complexity. The expected classification is open; the paper’s existence proofs are not algorithms. The follow-on work would need to specify the encoding and determine whether recognition, equilibrium search, or core blocking admits an efficient method—or hardness result.

The weak point is central: the population continuization is already in this paper, so it is not a new mirror of its economic model. The positive case is that its continuum market raises computational questions the paper does not answer. Without a named computational anchor, however, this remains a plausible research target rather than a full anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with a mismatch in the anchor requirement: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 1 is an existence theorem; Theorems 2 and 3 establish core–equilibrium equivalence; Theorem 4 concerns blocking; and Theorems 5–8 give characterizations and incentive-compatibility results. None defines an encoded computational problem or gives a complexity classification. There is therefore no computational result here to mirror.

The proposed Step-MEE Recognition problem is coherent, but Proposition 2 and Theorem 3 support its *economic model*, not its computational status. They already relate the finite economy to an equal-treatment continuum economy and characterize equilibrium through the core. Step-MEE adds a new encoded input, a decision problem about supporting prices, and an algorithmic objective. Recasting it as equilibrium search instead of recognition makes the computational goal more direct, but it still adds a computational layer the paper never formulated. That might be a worthwhile project; it is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The population premise itself is unusually strong. Section 4 explicitly constructs an atomless economy with equal treatment within types, and Proposition 2 links its equilibria to those of the finite economy. A household type containing preferences, information, endowments, and priors is a plausible complete type; the objection that mass loses individual identity would not hold here. Nor can the case rest on saying the computational answer is already known: the paper supplies no computational treatment of MEE recognition or search. Its existing continuum analysis settles the modeling question, not the algorithmic one.

That leaves a narrow but real objection: the proponent has identified a plausible computational follow-on to an already-continuous economics paper, not an anchored continuous mirror of a computational social-choice result. Under the stated anchor standard, that is enough to withhold a green verdict. The negative case is weak if the programme is also meant to solicit new complexity questions motivated by analytic papers: on that broader remit, this paper’s direct continuum construction makes Step-MEE a credible target, and I cannot honestly claim the population regime or the proposed question itself is misguided.
