What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers Theorem 2’s convergence claim and Proposition 1’s stability claim, but neither survives the change from atomic moves to mass transfers.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for the paper’s local stability algorithm, not for its sum-rate simulations. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification. Its best anchors are two proved-here algorithmic results: Theorem 2 says the proposed overlapping coalition formation algorithm, starting from the initial network structure, is guaranteed to converge. Its proof uses the finite number of coalition structures and the fact that each accepted reallocation raises system payoff and cannot revisit a past structure. Proposition 1 says the final structure is stable under the paper’s switching and independent orders: no SBS has a profitable one-unit reallocation. These are weaker complexity anchors than a P or NP-hard result, but they make claims about what a decentralized procedure computes.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large, standardized campus, hospital network, or industrial site with many SBSs in recurring radio-environment roles. An SBS type would specify everything this model uses: its subchannel units and transmit powers, served-user load, channel gains to user classes and other types, and its coordination costs and power threshold. The population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a small set of such types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of SBSs. This assumes repeated, exchangeable radio roles; it would fit poorly where each SBS has a unique interference neighborhood.

Here is a concrete type-level mirror. Include in the instance a finite catalogue \(\mathcal Q\) of recurring coalition roles in the deployment, with rational radio and cost parameters for those roles. Each SBS still has \(M\) indivisible resource units. An allocation pattern \(\pi\in(\mathcal Q\cup\{\bot\})^M\) assigns each unit to a coalition role or leaves it independent. Let \(z_{t,\pi}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) SBSs using pattern \(\pi\), constrained by
\[ z_{t,\pi}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_\pi z_{t,\pi}=\mu_t. \]
The rate, coalition value, and per-SBS payoff are computed by lifting the paper’s SINR, cost-threshold, and proportional-fairness formulas to aggregate masses. Thus mass may be split across patterns, while each SBS’s own units remain indivisible.

My lead question is Mass-OCF Termination. From the all-independent state, consider every sequence of rational-mass moves that transfers one unit of a positive mass \(\delta\) of SBSs from one role to another, or makes it independent, whenever the corresponding lifted switching or independent order is satisfied. Does every such sequence terminate after finitely many moves? A solution is a yes/no answer for the instance; a terminal state is one with no permitted move. This mirrors Theorem 2’s convergence claim and asks whether its finite-state argument survives when the population is continuous. I would provisionally expect continuum-specific hardness (Class C): the finite-state argument disappears, and a strictly increasing population payoff can still allow an infinite sequence of improving mass reallocations. Theorem 2 itself gives no polynomial bound, even in its discrete setting.

A second, narrower question is Mass-OCF Stability Checking, anchored by Proposition 1. Given a rational state \(z\), decide whether it is stable: for every pattern \(\pi\), resource unit \(k\), and destination role, there must be no rational \(\delta>0\) that can be transferred from \(z_{t,\pi}\) to the pattern differing only by that unit’s destination while satisfying the lifted order. The independent option is checked with the paper’s two strict payoff increases; switching also requires the destination coalition payoff not to decrease. I also expect Class C for the general SINR model, because each candidate deviation changes several coupled aggregate rates. An explicit finite catalogue makes the candidate moves enumerable, but does not by itself make the mass-dependent payoff comparisons easy.

These questions cover the convergence and local-stability claims only. They do not turn the paper’s goal into global sum-rate maximization: the paper’s stated endpoint is a stable local structure. The mass variables arise from continuizing the SBS population; they do not make an SBS’s bundle divisible.

The weak point is the regime assumption. In real deployments, geometry often gives SBSs distinct interference profiles, so treating many of them as identical may be artificial. The case survives in repeated deployment layouts where SBSs share a small number of radio roles, and the proposed questions expose exactly what the discrete convergence proof does—and does not—establish in that regime. The paper’s prior work on overlapping coalition games supports the model’s relevance; it is not itself a continuous-population collision.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest against these two anchors, though it cannot establish that no restricted version could ever be useful. The paper has named algorithmic claims, so I would not say there is nothing to mirror. But Theorem 2 proves only finite termination: with finitely many SBS units there are finitely many coalition structures, and accepted moves strictly improve system payoff. It gives no bound on the number of moves. Proposition 1 adds little computational content: the algorithm stops when none of its allowed moves applies, which is exactly the paper’s definition of stability.

The best regime to try is a large deployment with recurring radio roles. Yet the paper’s payoffs depend on relationships between SBSs: each SUE’s rate depends on channel gains from other transmitters, while a coalition’s power cost depends on its farthest members. Coalition membership and overlap determine which interference is suppressed. A mass distribution over SBS types does not by itself retain those relationships. If a type records each SBS’s full interference profile, most SBSs in a realistic deployment will have different types, so the hoped-for small support disappears. If types are compressed into a few role classes, the model needs extra assumptions about how members of those classes interact and which coalitions they can form.

The proposed fixed catalogue of coalition roles is a reasonable way to make an aggregate model tractable to describe, but it also fixes much of the coalition structure in advance. A catalogue of all possible coalitions would grow with the deployment; a small catalogue describes a restricted network design problem. A periodic layout with a fixed set of local coalition patterns might be a worthwhile model in its own right, but those restrictions are doing the substantive work. They are not supplied by the paper’s general overlapping-coalition model.

Mass-OCF Termination has a further mismatch. Theorem 2’s move is one indivisible resource unit belonging to one SBS. Moving rational mass \(\delta\) at once makes many SBSs act as a block. In an atomless model, an individual SBS has zero mass; preserving unilateral moves therefore requires a different account of how its deviation affects payoffs and coalition membership. The proponent’s “every rational-mass sequence” question instead studies a new bulk-update process whose possible moves and stopping behavior depend on choices the paper does not specify. A finer-grained limit of the finite process would also need an update schedule and a rule for how individual coalition memberships are represented. The result could be interesting, but it would concern those added choices as much as the paper’s convergence claim.

Mass-OCF Stability Checking is weaker still as an anchor. In a given finite coalition structure, the paper already describes how to enumerate a unit’s destinations and compare the resulting payoffs. Under a fixed finite catalogue, the continuous version enumerates a finite set of move templates and checks mass-dependent payoff conditions. To get a richer problem, one must restore the spatial and coalition-membership detail that the type aggregate discarded. That may be a coherent new problem, but it no longer follows directly from Proposition 1’s simple terminal-state claim.

So I would not prioritize this paper for ChoCo in its current form. Its strongest result is a finite-state convergence argument, and its stability result largely restates the stopping condition. A repeated-layout, finite-template model remains a possible narrow exception. That is also the weakness in the universal negative case: such a deployment could give the authors a recognizable population question, but the paper does not identify it, and the assumptions needed to make its continuum dynamics faithful are substantial.
