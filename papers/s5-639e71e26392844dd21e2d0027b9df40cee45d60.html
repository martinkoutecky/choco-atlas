The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(G\), a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and each type's rational per-capita budget \(b_t\) and utilities \(u_{tj}\), assume each type values some good positively and each good is desired by some type. Compute rational prices \(p_j>0\), per-capita allocations \(x_{tj}\geq 0\), and returned money \(s_t\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}=1\) for every good \(j\), \(\sum_{j\in G}p_jx_{tj}+s_t=b_t\) for every type \(t\), and each \((x_t,s_t)\) maximizes \(\sum_j u_{tj}y_j+r\) over \(y_j,r\geq 0\) subject to \(\sum_jp_jy_j+r\leq b_t\).

The model it lives in

A price-taking Arctic Auction with type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita budgets \(b_t\), and utilities \(u_{tj}\). Its variables are prices \(p_j\), per-capita allocations \(x_{tj}\), and returned money \(s_t\), with mass-weighted supply \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=1\). The equivalent RCP maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t\log(\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}+s_t)-\sum_t\mu_t s_t\), up to an input-dependent constant, subject to weighted supply and nonnegativity. The paper's price-taking buyer optimization is preserved, so there is no atomic-player to Wardrop solution-concept shift.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the base Arctic Auction equilibrium algorithm and its running-time theorem, Theorem 4, relying on Theorem 3 for correctness. It leaves aside the RCP characterizations in Theorems 1–2 and the production-cost variants in Theorems 5–8.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a Continuum Arctic Equilibrium problem. The paper treats buyers as a finite population, while its goods are already divisible; the mirror would continuize the buyer population itself.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that, for \(n\) buyers with rational budgets \(m_i\) and utilities \(u_{ij}\), letting \(M=\sum_i m_i\) and \(U=\max_{i,j}u_{ij}\), Algorithm 1 terminates after at most \(O\!\left(n^3(\log n+n\log U+\log M)\right)\) phases and performs at most \(O\!\left(n^5(\log n+n\log U+\log M)\right)\) max-flow computations. Its preceding correctness results establish that the algorithm computes an Arctic Auction equilibrium.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite set \(G\) of goods and an explicit support \(T\) of buyer types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a rational per-capita budget \(b_t\), and a rational utility \(u_{tj}\) for every good \(j\). Assume every type values some good positively and every good is desired by some type. Each good has unit supply in this normalized market. The question is to compute exact rational prices \(p_j>0\), per-capita allocations \(x_{tj}\geq0\), and per-capita returned money \(s_t\geq0\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}=1,\qquad \sum_{j\in G}p_jx_{tj}+s_t=b_t, \]
and, for every \(t\),
\[ (x_t,s_t)\in\arg\max_{\substack{y_j\geq0,\ r\geq0\\ \sum_jp_jy_j+r\leq b_t}} \left(\sum_j u_{tj}y_j+r\right). \]
Thus each type’s members receive the same per-capita bundle, and the mass-weighted allocations clear the market. Cash has utility one, so a member buys good \(j\) only when its price is no greater than the member’s utility for it, matching the paper’s reservation-price condition.

A plausible regime is a large cohort of holders in a restructuring or asset-exchange market: many participants share a small number of budget classes and valuation schedules across a fixed list of asset types. The paper itself motivates Arctic Auctions with individuals exchanging blocked offshore assets. Here the unit supply is per unit of population mass, so the finite counterpart is a replicated market whose supplies grow with the population. That supply scaling is a co-modification of the model, while the continuous object is still the buyer population. The regime needs \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of participants and \(\tau=|T|\) the number of distinct complete types; the type includes both the utility vector and the budget.

This is a natural problem for the authors to recognize: it preserves their buyers, budgets, utilities, price caps, divisible goods, and equilibrium question, while replacing repeated indistinguishable buyers with type masses. In fact, the continuous instance reduces directly to the paper’s finite one. For each \(t\), create one buyer with budget \(\widehat m_t=\mu_t b_t\) and utilities \(u_{tj}\). If that buyer receives aggregate bundle \(\widehat x_t\) and returned money \(\widehat s_t\), set \(x_t=\widehat x_t/\mu_t\) and \(s_t=\widehat s_t/\mu_t\). This maps equilibria both ways. Running Algorithm 1 with \(n=\tau\) therefore gives the expected direction: tractable, Class A, in time polynomial in the number of goods, the explicit support size, and the rational input length, subject to the bit-complexity caveat below. Theorem 4’s stated bounds become the same bounds with \(n=\tau\), budgets \(\mu_t b_t\), and \(M=\sum_t\mu_t b_t\).

The supplied paper contains no named hardness result to mirror. So this is a positive algorithmic mirror, but it cannot show that population continuization dissolves discrete hardness. Its further questions are whether Theorem 4’s exact bit complexity can be stated cleanly in the full denominator bit length, and whether the same type aggregation preserves tractability for the paper’s production-cost variants.

The weakest point is that the mirror largely compresses repeated buyers into weighted finite buyers; it does not reveal a new continuous-only algorithmic phenomenon. Also, Theorem 4’s displayed bound depends on \(\log U\) and \(\log M\), while its rational-input statement invokes bit sizes more generally, so a careful binary bit-complexity claim should make that dependence explicit. Finally, this is market-equilibrium computation: if ChoCo is restricted to voting problems, its fit is less direct, though the population-continuity question itself is well-posed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 4’s mirror is that the proposed continuous instance compresses exactly to a finite Arctic Auction. For each type \(t\), make one buyer with budget \(\widehat m_t=\mu_t b_t\). If that buyer receives aggregate bundle \((\widehat x_t,\widehat s_t)\), the per-capita bundle is \((\widehat x_t/\mu_t,\widehat s_t/\mu_t)\). Market clearing, spending, and each type’s utility-maximizing choices correspond exactly. Theorem 4 therefore applies with \(n=\tau\). This makes the mirror look like a routine closure of the existing algorithm under weighted buyers, rather than a new computational method.

That is a weak objection under this programme’s rules. The programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity aggregation as a meaningful bridge, and an exact dictionary between finite and continuous variants is a deliverable. The paper’s buyers have no identity-sensitive constraints, and its equilibrium outputs are prices and allocations; type members can receive the same per-capita bundle. Large cohorts sharing budgets and valuation schedules provide a plausible regime, with supplies normalized per capita. Neither buyer identity nor the need to scale supplies defeats that scenario.

The paper’s rational convex programs and primal-dual algorithms are computational work on finite buyer sets, so I cannot claim that it has already solved the continuous-population problem. Theorem 4 is a named algorithmic result, and its exact aggregation makes a credible continuous question. A careful mirror should state its running time in the full binary input length, including the masses, but that is a precision requirement, not a reason the question is unworthy. The same aggregation appears available for the production-cost variants.

The skeptical case I can honestly make is about research priority: this paper may yield little new technique because its algorithm already accepts the compressed type representation. That does not establish that there is no worthwhile mirror. The proponent’s anchor survives, so a convincing universal case against this paper is unavailable.
