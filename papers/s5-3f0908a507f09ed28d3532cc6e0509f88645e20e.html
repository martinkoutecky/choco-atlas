The case FOR (proponent)

The best pro-side fact is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: under Assumptions 1–3 (monotone aggregate demand and supply, weak dependence, and identifiability), the DCA’s expected percentage welfare loss converges to zero at rate \(1/n\) as the numbers of buyers and sellers grow proportionally. Section 5 makes the population limit concrete: buyer types are complete marginal-value vectors, seller types are complete marginal-cost vectors, and the auction uses observed dropouts to estimate aggregate demand and supply.

That suggests a plausible population mirror: types are those full value and cost schedules; mass is each type’s share of buyers or sellers; actions are reductions in demand or supply as clock prices move; the outcome is traded mass, prices, transfers, and gains from trade. It is a sensible high-multiplicity market regime, and the paper’s continuous distributions and large-market analysis do not by themselves collide with ChoCo’s computational question.

But this is not a valid anchor under your stated rule. Theorem 2 proves an asymptotic welfare guarantee, not that a computational problem is in \(P\), hard, or otherwise classified. Theorem 1 proves incentive and allocation properties, and Proposition 1 compares welfare; neither is a computational result either. So I cannot honestly give you an anchor-derived continuous computational problem per the requested format. The weakness is decisive for this assignment: the paper offers a real population-level model, but no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate, and its population interpretation is sensible: buyer and seller value schedules can be treated as types, with their proportions forming a society. But the theorem proves an asymptotic welfare bound for a sequence of stochastic finite markets. It defines no computational problem, input encoding, or complexity result. Theorem 1 establishes incentive and allocation properties; Proposition 1 compares welfare. Neither supplies a computational result to mirror. For ChoCo’s purpose, that is a decisive mismatch.

The best version of a mirror would ask how to compute the DCA’s target or reserve price, or how hard it is to evaluate the estimator in equation (6), for a society given by rational masses on complete value and cost schedules. That would be a new algorithmic question. The paper gives no complexity result for it, and its general model allows a parameter space \(\Phi\) without a computational representation, so complexity depends on added choices about how the distributions and estimators are encoded. In a finite-support model, demand and supply are step functions; the strict slope bounds in Assumption 1 that support Theorem 2 generally fail. Preserving those bounds calls for a richer distributional input than ChoCo’s finite support list. A discretization might still be useful, but its approximation guarantees would need new analysis.

So the negative case is strong as a verdict about this paper’s fit for a programme mapping computational results. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile computational study could grow from the auction: the mechanism may prompt one. That would be a new project, though, rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
