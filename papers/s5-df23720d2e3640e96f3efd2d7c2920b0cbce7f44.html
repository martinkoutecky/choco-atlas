What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorem 7’s convex-case bounds on pair deviations in GSP’s lower equilibrium; it does not cover the resource-selection results, the other auction bounds, or the VCG modification.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its theorems or propositions classifies a decision, counting, or optimization problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are analytical claims about the number of profitable coalitions. So there is no anchor that meets your stated test.

The closest positive opening is Theorem 7, proved in this paper: when both CTR and valuation sequences are convex, the number of pair deviations from GSP’s lower equilibrium is \(O(s\sqrt{s})\); if CTRs are \(\beta\)-convex, it is \(O(s\log_\beta s)\); and if valuations are \(\beta\)-convex for \(\beta\ge2\), only neighboring pairs can deviate, so the count is exactly \(s\). This is about the stability score itself, rather than an algorithm for computing it.

A possible follow-up is the Continuum GSP Pair-Deviation Density problem. An instance gives a mass-one population with value distribution \(\mu\), a slot supply of \(\alpha\) per bidder, a decreasing CTR curve \(X(q)\) over allocated rank quantiles \(q\in[0,\alpha]\), and a lower-equilibrium bid schedule. For two independently sampled rank points \(q<r\), call the pair deviating if there are alternative reports that leave both weakly better off and one strictly better off. The problem is to compute the pair-deviation density
\[ \Delta_2=\frac{2}{\alpha^2}\int_{0<q<r<\alpha} \mathbf{1}\{\text{the pair }(q,r)\text{ has a deviation}\}\,dq\,dr. \]
The type is bidder value; \(\mu\) is the population mass over values; the action is the pair’s reports; and the output is the fraction of potential pairs that can deviate. A plausible regime is a large ad market with slots scaling with the number of advertisers and values drawn from a small catalog. Normalizing Theorem 7’s counts by the number of potential pairs gives a density tending to zero in all three convex cases. I would expect the nonatomic version to be tractable, though possibly trivially so.

That normalization is the positive case: the authors might recognize “what fraction of pairs can deviate?” as a natural population-scale version of their stability score. But the weakness is substantial. In a nonatomic market, two individual bidders have zero mass and cannot change the bid distribution or one another’s prices. If each is already at equilibrium, their joint deviation can collapse to the same question as two separate unilateral deviations. Meanwhile, the paper assumes distinct valuations, whereas a high-multiplicity market creates tied value types whose equilibrium bids can depend on rank within a type. Resolving that faithfully may require more than a tie-breaking convention. So this is a plausible new question motivated by Theorem 7, but I would not claim it already passes the authors-recognition test as a continuous mirror of their result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper never poses the kind of computational problem ChoCo continuizes. Its stability score is a statistic—the number of named coalitions that can deviate—and Theorem 7 bounds that statistic under assumptions on valuations and click rates. It does not classify the complexity of computing the score or solve an algorithmic problem. A continuous optimization task built around the score would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed GSP pair-density task is the closest candidate, but its continuum interpretation loses what makes a pair deviation work. In the paper, one bidder’s bid change alters another bidder’s payment through their adjacent ranks. In an atomless market, a pair has zero mass, does not change the bid schedule, and has no immediate successor in the dense ranking. With the market schedule fixed, an SNE’s envy-free property rules out gains from moving to another slot. The remaining price effect is a finite-population, one-position change that vanishes as the market grows. Thus the proposed \(\Delta_2\) is either trivial in the nonatomic model or depends on finite-\(n\) corrections that retain the very discreteness the mirror is meant to remove. Theorem 7’s convex-case count also has vanishing density after normalization, but that observation is secondary; the deeper problem is that the continuum has no pairwise price interaction of the kind being counted.

A better version could study deviations by coalitions of positive mass. But that changes the object: the paper counts coalitions of each integer size, while a continuum has no canonical way to count measurable subsets of a given mass. One could choose a distribution over coalitions, or study the smallest mass that can profitably deviate, but either choice adds a new model and a new stability measure. The same issue appears in the other promising results. The congestion-game bounds rely on the one-agent difference between “full” and “vacant” resources; in an atomless population, a pair cannot change either load. The VCG group-strategyproofness result relies on an individual bidder having positive probability of losing utility after lowering a report; a zero-mass bidder cannot alter the aggregate mechanism.

One could build worthwhile continuous congestion or auction models by specifying mass coalitions, payoff scaling, allocation rules, and equilibrium concepts. I can’t honestly claim that no such model is worth studying in any scenario. But those choices would define new population games, not preserve the paper’s identity-based stability scores as a continuous computational problem. The negative case is therefore strong against this paper as a ChoCo mirror, though it does not rule out related continuum research.
