The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a positive case that meets the anchor requirement: the paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It characterizes a subgame-perfect equilibrium for the two-player bargaining game: at step \(t\), player 1 accepts when \(\alpha_t \ge a_t+\delta(b_t-a_t)/2\), and player 2 accepts when \(\alpha_t \le b_t-\delta(b_t-a_t)/2\), where \([a_t,b_t]\) is the current offer interval. The interval in which both accept has probability \(1-\delta\), so agreement occurs in finite time with probability \(1\). Proposition 2 gives a matrix formula for the thresholds from the negotiation history. These are equilibrium characterizations, not computational-complexity results.

The most plausible population extension would put a distribution over types of two-person bargaining pairs and ask for their equilibrium thresholds and aggregate payoffs. But that makes the population a cohort of games; it does not make the society in this paper continuous. The paper’s continuous element is the random offer, and replacing two players with a large population would change its consensus and punishment mechanism. I don’t think this is a convincing continuous mirror of a computational problem the authors studied.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no complexity result to continue. Proposition 1 characterizes an equilibrium, and Proposition 2 gives a matrix formula for its thresholds; neither studies the complexity of a computational problem. Even the strongest computational reading of these results—compute the equilibrium strategy—has already been answered: given the current interval, the thresholds follow directly, and the interval after a history follows by multiplying the specified \(2\times2\) matrices. The simulations add numerical evidence, not a complexity question.

A distribution over repeated two-person bargaining cases is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not dismiss it as an invalid population. But each case can be solved independently using the paper’s formulas, then weighted by its mass. With finite support, that is just one calculation per type and an aggregation. For the paper’s own identical cases, the support has size one. Adding heterogeneous utilities or discount factors could make the aggregate calculation richer, but any difficulty would come from those newly introduced functions or constraints, not from population continuization.

A stronger proposal is to put a continuum of people into one bargaining procedure. That could be a worthwhile new model, especially given the authors’ stated interest in extending the scheme to several players. But the paper’s equilibrium depends on exactly two players with distinct roles: either player’s rejection changes the interval in a specified way. A continuum version must define what counts as consensus, how the mass of rejecting players changes the interval, and how the pie and utilities extend to many participants. Those choices may produce interesting computational questions, but they are additions to the model, not continuous mirrors of the results proved here.

So the distribution-over-cases mirror is well-posed but computationally thin; the continuum-of-bargainers version may be substantial but is a new problem. That is a strong case against a worthwhile *mirror of this paper*, though not proof that a future multi-player bargaining paper could not support one.
