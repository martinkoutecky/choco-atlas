The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are structural: for example, Theorem 1 (proved here, with its first inclusion also proved by Albers) says the relative interior of the core lies in the partnered core and that partnerships are constant across that relative interior. Corollary 1 gives nonemptiness for balanced games; Theorems 3–6 give further structural characterizations. None classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity or gives an algorithm with a stated running time.

Section 4’s LP characterization and the LP arguments in the proofs do not change that: the paper uses them to prove mathematical properties, without specifying a computational input model, an algorithm, or a complexity bound. So I can’t supply an anchor-compliant continuous problem for this paper. One could propose a computational study of partnered-core allocations in a game whose agents are represented by a distribution of types, but that would be a new research question, not a mirror grounded in a named computational result here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for names no result to mirror, so there is no result-by-result anchor to rebut. That absence is the strongest case against a paper-specific continuous mirror: none of the numbered results classifies a computational problem, states an algorithm, or gives a running time. Theorem 1, for example, relates the relative interior of the core to the partnered core and shows that partnerships stay fixed across that interior. Theorem 3 characterizes partnered payoffs for a class of games; Theorem 6 gives an invariance result when there are at most three partnerships. These are structural claims.

The LP in Section 4 is the closest candidate for an algorithmic anchor. It characterizes the core using one constraint per coalition, and the authors use LP duality to prove their results. They do not formulate a computational task or analyze its complexity. The paper also gives no computational input model. With an explicit characteristic function, the input lists values for as many as \(2^n\) coalitions. A high-multiplicity version would need a new representation of coalition values in terms of types or masses. Any question about finding a partnered-core payoff, recovering the partnership partition, or testing core nonemptiness would then depend on choices this paper never makes, such as how coalition values are represented and accessed.

The strongest proposed mirror would build that representation around a replicated economy with a small number of player types, then study the computation of core payoffs and partnerships for a distribution over those types. Section 7’s connection to replicated exchange economies makes that regime plausible. But it supplies a setting, not a computational result in this paper. Turning its structural claims into complexity questions could be worthwhile work; it would be a new computational cooperative-game project rather than a mirror of a result the authors establish here.

So I see no grounded ChoCo mirror in this paper. The limitation in that conclusion is that the paper cannot rule out a valuable continuous computational project built from its ideas. The case is strong against calling such a project a mirror of this paper, and weaker against the broader claim that no useful project could grow out of it.
