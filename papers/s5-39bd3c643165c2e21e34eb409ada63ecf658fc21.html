What the mirror covers

The proposed belief-type setting is plausible, but the paper’s results concern market mechanisms and do not depend on a population distribution; an aggregate flow or terminal-report objective would be a new question.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a computational formulation. The closest result is Theorem 5.1, proved here: an incentive-compatible scoring-rule market satisfies trade neutralization if and only if it is a generalized cost-function market with an additive-subgroup share space and a quasi-open market; moreover, trade neutralization implies portfolio neutralization. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity result. Theorem 4.3, that no SRM for any quantile satisfies weak neutralization, is also an axiomatic result. The paper’s discussion of elicitation complexity is a direction for future work, not a result.

The most plausible population regime would be a large forecasting platform where many traders share the same belief \(p\) over a finite outcome space \(\mathcal{Y}\). A type would be that belief, together with the paper’s shared assumptions about incentives; \(\mu_p\) would be the fraction of traders with belief \(p\). A trader’s action is to move the current market report from \(r\) to \(r'\), receiving \(S(r',Y)-S(r,Y)\), and their objective is to maximize expected payoff. Such a crowd with relatively few distinct beliefs is plausible.

But mass over belief types does not by itself specify a continuous mirror of the paper’s results. The SRM is sequential: trades depend on the current market state, and the paper’s axioms concern each trader’s available contracts. A distribution of beliefs alone does not determine the order or dynamics of trades. Defining a mass-weighted flow and asking for its equilibrium or terminal report would be a new question, not one of the problems Theorem 5.1 or the other results compute.

So the honest positive case is limited to a plausible high-multiplicity *setting*, not a continuous computational mirror of a named result. There is no lead anchor and therefore no per-anchor prover question to state. The main obstacle is also the weakest point: the paper studies axiomatic properties of market mechanisms, while the programme asks computational questions about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually clean: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. The closest candidate, Theorem 5.1, characterizes which incentive compatible markets satisfy trade neutralization. Theorem 4.3 says no quantile market satisfies weak neutralization. Those are results about market axioms, not the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 3.1 likewise characterizes mechanisms, and the discussion of elicitation complexity is explicitly future work. There is no problem here whose complexity landscape a continuous-population mirror could chart.

A large platform with many traders sharing beliefs is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and a complete type could include each trader’s belief and other relevant attributes. But adding a distribution \(\mu\) over those types does not make the paper’s results population-dependent: its axioms quantify over market states, reports, and trades, not over a society or its mass distribution. Theorem 5.1 has the same content whether there is one trader or a million.

The stronger candidate would aggregate a population of belief types and ask for a collective report or terminal market state. But the paper does not define that population-level objective. Its market is sequential, so mass over trader types alone does not determine the order of trades or resulting state. Supplying an order rule, aggregate objective, or mass-flow dynamics would create a new problem. It might be worth studying, but it would not be a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.

The paper does use distributions over outcomes and real-valued reports. That is continuity in beliefs or reports, not in the population, so it does not supply the missing mirror either. I would argue against spending ChoCo’s effort on a mirror of this paper. The limit of that argument is the universal wording: a new computational market-design problem could be built around a population of traders, but the paper gives no named computational result to anchor it.
