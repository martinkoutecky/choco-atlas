Why no mirror

However, neither named anchor has the required computational character: Theorem 1 is axiomatic, while Theorem 2 is an existence result without a computational claim. Therefore no anchor supplies both required bits.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has a genuine continuous-population mirror, but not a standard ChoCo complexity anchor. The paper contains no named theorem establishing NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), parameterized tractability, or a comparable complexity classification. Its strongest named result is an impossibility theorem. I would therefore lead with Theorem 1, and use Theorem 2 only as a secondary, restricted-domain algorithmic mirror.

The natural regime is a large population answering a fixed agenda of logically interconnected propositions: for example, citizens voting on a constitutional package, employees deciding a safety policy, or a large membership deciding a party programme. A type is a complete, consistent and deductively closed judgment set over the agenda \(X\). Two people have the same type exactly when they agree on every proposition relevant to the aggregation problem. If \(T_X\) is the finite set of such types, the society is a rational distribution \( \mu\in\Delta(T_X) \), where \( \mu_t \) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). For a fixed agenda, the number of types is finite while the population may be millions, so this is a credible high-multiplicity regime.

My lead mirror is Continuous Anonymous Systematic Judgment Aggregation. An instance consists of a finite agenda \(X\), including \(P\), \(Q\), \(P\land Q\), and \( \neg(P\land Q) \), the corresponding finite type set \(T_X\), and the family of rational societies \( \mu\in\Delta_{\mathbb Q}(T_X) \). For each proposition \(\varphi\), write \(q_\mu(\varphi)=\sum_{t:\varphi\in t}\mu_t\) for its population support. The action is to choose one common issue-independent function \(h:[0,1]\to\{0,1\}\), inducing the collective judgment set
\[ F_h(\mu)=\{\varphi\in X:h(q_\mu(\varphi))=1\}. \]
The solution must make \(F_h(\mu)\) complete, consistent and deductively closed for every rational society \( \mu \). This is a feasibility problem rather than an optimization problem: either provide such an \(h\), or provide a rational society witnessing failure.

This is the continuous analogue of the paper’s universal-domain, anonymous and systematic aggregation problem. Anonymity becomes dependence only on population mass, while systematicity becomes use of the same function \(h\) for every proposition. The mirror is not a softened version of the authors’ question: it preserves the demand that every admissible population be accepted and that the collective judgments remain rational.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper:

“There exists no judgment aggregation function \(F\) generating complete, consistent and deductively closed collective sets of judgments which satisfies universal domain, anonymity and systematicity.”

The impossibility transfers directly. Put mass \(1/3\) on each of the three rational types displayed in Table 1. Then \(P\), \(Q\), and \( \neg(P\land Q) \) each have support \(2/3\), while \(P\land Q\) has support \(1/3\). If \(h(2/3)=1\), the collective set accepts \(P\), \(Q\), and \( \neg(P\land Q) \), so deductive closure and consistency fail. If \(h(2/3)=0\), completeness forces the opposite judgments, and the same four-proposition configuration again violates rationality. Thus the continuous model contains a finite-support obstruction; making the electorate infinitely divisible does not dissolve it.

The expected classification is therefore a transferred impossibility, not Class A, B, or C in the ordinary computational sense. The obstruction is already present with three types and rational masses, so it is not continuum-specific. Further questions would include whether the impossibility disappears under restricted agenda structure, unidimensional alignment, approximate rationality, or controlled violations of anonymity or systematicity.

A useful secondary anchor is Theorem 2, cited from List (2001), not proved in this paper:

“Suppose that the number of individuals is odd. On the domain of unidimensionally aligned profiles of personal sets of judgments, there exists a judgment aggregation function \(F\) generating complete, consistent and deductively closed collective sets of judgments which satisfies anonymity and systematicity.”

Its continuous mirror is Median Aggregation on an Aligned Society. The instance consists of an ordered type space \(t_1\prec\cdots\prec t_\tau\), a distribution \( \mu \) over those types, and the condition that for every proposition \( \varphi \), the types accepting \( \varphi \) form a prefix or suffix of the order. Assume additionally that no proposition has exactly half the population supporting it. The required output is
\[ J_\mu=\{\varphi\in X:q_\mu(\varphi)>1/2\}. \]
Under alignment, \(J_\mu\) coincides with the judgment set of a weighted median type, and is therefore complete, consistent and deductively closed. It is computable by summing the masses supporting each proposition, hence in polynomial time in the explicit representation of \(X\), \(T_X\), and the rational numbers in \( \mu \). This is a tractable continuous analogue, although the polynomial-time conclusion is an inference from the theorem’s construction, not a complexity result stated by the paper.

The paper’s authors would likely recognise both mirrors: the objects remain populations of individually rational judgment sets, the output remains a collectively rational judgment set, and the conditions being preserved are exactly their universal-domain, anonymity, systematicity and rationality conditions. The population shift is especially natural for their examples involving governments, public bodies, companies and political groups; a mass consultation over a fixed logical agenda is not an artificial relaxation.

The weakest point is decisive: if ChoCo requires a named computational-complexity anchor, this paper does not provide one. The continuous mirror is mathematically genuine and yields a sharp transferred impossibility plus a median-based tractable restriction, but it does not by itself populate the programme’s NP-hard/\(\mathrm{P}\)/parameterized complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no computational anchor to continuize. Theorem 1 is a theorem about the nonexistence of a rule \(F\) satisfying three axioms; it is not a complexity result about an input instance, an optimization problem, or an algorithm. The proposed continuous version merely changes the quantifiers to

\[ \exists h\;\forall \mu, \]

where \(h\) is a global Boolean response function and \(\mu\) ranges over societies. Once the agenda contains \(P\), \(Q\), and \(P\land Q\), the answer is uniformly negative, witnessed by the same three-type society in every representation. There is no input-sensitive computational problem here: either the impossibility theorem is invoked, or a fixed three-atom counterexample is returned.

That does not make the formulation mathematically invalid. A large public consultation over a fixed logical agenda is a perfectly sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the proponent is right not to object that judgment types erase personal identities. But the continuum contributes no computational structure. The obstruction already lives in three rational types with masses \(1/3\). The model has no mass transfer, objective, target, approximation question, or nontrivial search over exponentially many objects. It is an axiomatic impossibility result rewritten over weighted profiles.

A stronger attempted mirror would ask for the smallest relaxation of anonymity, systematicity, or rationality that restores feasibility. That could certainly be made into an optimization problem, but the objective would be newly invented: one must choose how to measure violations, whether to weight propositions, and whether to optimize over societies, rules, or outputs. It would be a new quantitative theory of judgment aggregation, not a computational mirror of Theorem 1. If the agenda or type space is given succinctly, any resulting hardness would instead come from propositional model counting or the chosen encoding, not from continuizing the population.

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. It is not proved in this paper and, like Theorem 1, is an existence theorem rather than a complexity result. In the proposed continuous version, once the order of types is supplied, one computes every support \(q_\mu(\varphi)\), applies the \(1/2\) threshold, and obtains the weighted median judgment set. That is a direct scan of the explicitly represented distribution, not a substantive computational problem. Hiding the order could produce a new recognition problem—recovering a latent one-dimensional alignment—but that changes the question from aggregation to learning the domain restriction. Similarly, asking for the largest aligned subpopulation or the minimum mass to delete would be an invented intervention problem, not a result anchored in this paper.

So the proponent has established a legitimate continuous restatement of the paper’s axiomatic content. What they have not established is a worthwhile ChoCo mirror. The paper supplies no named computational question whose high-multiplicity version could open a complexity landscape; its two proposed anchors collapse either to a fixed impossibility witness or to trivial weighted-majority evaluation. The negative case is therefore strong on programme fit, though not a proof that no imaginative new judgment-aggregation problem could ever be built around the paper.
