The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\), \(R\), \(\mathrm{attSpan}\), the Steemit parameters \(a,b,\mathrm{regen}\), and a finite rational stake distribution \(\nu\), decide whether every finite-support rational refinement \(\mu\) of \(\nu\) into complete types \(\theta=(s_\theta,\ell_\theta)\), with \(\ell_\theta\in[0,1]^M\), and every initial ordering of the \(M\) posts yields a fully ideal final order under the paper’s honest process. A no instance is witnessed by such a refinement and an initial ordering whose final order is not ideal.

The model it lives in

Types are curator profiles \(\theta=(s_\theta,\ell_\theta)\), with mass \(\mu_\theta\). Each type follows the paper’s honest voting rule and has its per-capita Voting Power tracked; its score contribution is multiplied by \(\mu_\theta s_\theta\). The target ideal score is \(Q(p)=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\ell_\theta(p)\), and the question is whether the protocol’s final ranking orders posts by \(Q\) for every refinement and initial ordering.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s honest convergence characterization and its full-order cutoff consequence; it leaves the simulations and the self-promoting behavior of deviators outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper’s exact full-convergence threshold remain necessary and sufficient for every rational type-mass refinement of a stake distribution?
  • For every nonconstant rational stake distribution, can rational likability profiles witness failure of 1-convergence in the continuous type-mass process?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. This paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as being in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its best anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper, which gives an exact convergence characterization. So the mirror below is a computational verification problem built around that result, not a claim that the paper already contains a standard complexity result.

A plausible regime is a large, round-based curation campaign on a fixed batch of posts: perhaps \(10^5\)–\(10^6\) accounts, with tens or hundreds of recurring combinations of stake level and rating behavior. Coordinated accounts, automated curators, or communities using shared rating policies make that regime more convincing than a population of unrelated readers. A complete type is \(\theta=(s_\theta,\ell_\theta)\), where \(s_\theta\) is Steem Power and \(\ell_\theta\in[0,1]^M\) gives the type’s likability for each of the \(M\) posts. Its mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of curators of that type. The ideal quality of post \(p\) is \(Q(p)=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\ell_\theta(p)\). Thus \(N\) can be enormous while the support size \(\tau\) stays modest.

Theorem 1 is the lead anchor. As stated and proved in the paper: (1) if two players have different Steem Power, Steemit does not \((N,R,M,1)\)-converge; (2) if all players have equal Steem Power, then it \((N,R,M,M)\)-converges when
\[ R-1\ge (M-1)\left\lceil\frac{a+b}{\mathrm{regen}}\right\rceil, \]
and does not \((N,R,M,1)\)-converge when that inequality fails. This is a system-convergence theorem, not a complexity-class theorem, but it is a named result about the computational behavior of the paper’s voting system.

I would hand a prover the following mirror: Continuous Universal Full-Curation. An instance gives \(M\ge2\), the Steemit parameters \(a,b,\mathrm{regen},R,\mathrm{attSpan}\), and a finite rational stake distribution \(\nu\) over stake levels. Ask whether, for *every* finite-support rational society refining \(\nu\) into complete types \((s,\ell)\), and every initial ordering of \(M\) posts, the honest process ends in a fully ideal order. A yes solution is a proof of that guarantee; a no solution is a rational type distribution, likability vectors, and initial ordering that produce a non-ideal final order.

The continuous dynamics preserve the paper’s question: each type’s mass votes according to the same honest rule, with per-agent Voting Power tracked for that type, while its score contribution is multiplied by \(\mu_\theta s_\theta\). At the end of a round, posts are ordered by the aggregate scores. The list remains finite; the population is the continuous object. This is not outcome-space continuity.

I would expect the mirror to be tractable. The conjectured full-order criterion is the theorem’s same simple test: for \(M\ge2\), universal full convergence holds exactly when \(\nu\) has one stake level and the displayed round threshold is met. Equal stake makes fully powered aggregate scores track \(Q\); unequal stake can make the stake-weighted vote signal disagree with that unweighted ideal quality. Establishing the continuous counterexamples for arbitrary rational mass shares is part of the research question, rather than something Theorem 1 already proves. For a specified society, direct type-level simulation takes time polynomial in \(M,\tau,R\), and the input bit lengths; the universal question should admit the smaller parameter test if the conjectured characterization is right.

The paper’s concrete Steemit cutoff follows the same line, but I would not count it as a separate anchor. The mirror covers the honest-convergence analysis in Theorem 1. It does not claim to capture the strategic incentives or the self-promoting behavior shown in the simulations. Natural follow-ups are to characterize universal \(t\)-convergence for intermediate \(t\), and to find the minimum mass of deviating types needed to push a low-quality post into a chosen part of the list.

The weakest point is whether \(\tau\) really stays small for the scenario. Exact likability vectors over a particular slate may distinguish nearly every organic user, making \(\tau\) approach \(N\). A bot or guild regime with shared rating policies is more credible, but narrower. And even there, this is a modest Class A mirror: the paper’s result is already an exact correctness characterization, so the continuous version may mainly give a clean high-multiplicity formulation rather than expose a deep complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor, but it is a correctness theorem for one fixed protocol: it characterizes when honest votes produce an ideal ordering. It does not give the complexity of finding an intervention, optimizing an attack, or deciding an algorithmic problem. Continuous Universal Full-Curation adds a new task by quantifying over every likability profile and every refinement of a stake distribution. As stated, that is a universal theorem to prove, not a finite-instance computational problem. To make it one, the prover would need a restricted family of profiles or a specified robustness objective; either choice adds structure the paper does not supply.

The natural exact type is \((\mathrm{SP},\ell)\), where \(\ell\) is the complete vector of likabilities for the \(M\) posts. For organic readers, those vectors can distinguish nearly everyone. Coarsening them into a few “opinion archetypes” changes either the honest vote or the ideal score, so it is no longer an exact mirror. A fixed batch rated by recurring bot or guild policies is more plausible, and could have small support, but it is a narrow setting: the theorem then concerns massed automated curators with shared ratings, rather than a large crowd of distinct readers.

A stronger algorithmic proposal would be to find the least mass of strategic curators needed to push a post upward by a specified amount, perhaps while requiring a minimum quality gap. That would give mass a meaningful role and avoid relying on near ties. But the paper only simulates self-promoting behavior; it defines no utility, attack budget, or optimization problem for that behavior. This proposal would be a new strategic model, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 1.

I would not use individual identity as an objection: the honest theorem aggregates preferences and stake, and the authors’ identities do not drive it. Nor can I claim the limit destroys the result. Normalizing the finite society’s scores by \(N\) preserves its rankings, and rational masses can represent finite electorates exactly. That is the negative case’s weakness: a repeated batch with a small number of genuinely shared curator types is coherent. My objection is narrower: the paper supplies no computational result for that regime, and its best theorem mirror becomes a universal correctness characterization unless someone adds a separate computational objective.
