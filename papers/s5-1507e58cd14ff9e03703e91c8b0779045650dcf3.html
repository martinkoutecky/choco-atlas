What the mirror covers

The proposed mass model targets the finite Nash/core stability result in Theorem 1; it does not address the paper's \(k\)-tier heuristic, social-conscious stability, friendliness, robustness, or experiments.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1 (Siler 2017), which this paper cites rather than proves: under matchup-oriented preferences, a Nash-stable and core-stable tier list is guaranteed to exist, and one can be found in polynomial time. The paper’s preliminaries also report that Nash and core stability are equivalent in this setting. I would not use the NP-hardness claim in the preliminaries as an anchor: it is not a numbered result here. Theorem 1 is the paper’s clearest named computational result.

Here is a continuous version I would ask a prover to solve: Mass-Stable Tiering. The input is a finite set of matchup types \(T\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and a rational antisymmetric matchup utility \(w:T\times T\to[-1,1]\), with \(w(t,s)=-w(s,t)\). A type is a complete matchup profile: its members have the same utilities against every type. A solution is a finite ordered list of tiers and rational allocations \(x_{t\ell}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t\ell}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to tier \(\ell\), and \(\sum_\ell x_{t\ell}=\mu_t\). Require every listed tier to have positive mass.

Number tiers from bottom to top. A type-\(t\) participant in tier \(\ell\) gets utility \(u_t(\ell;x)=\sum_{r\le\ell}\sum_{s\in T}x_{sr}w(t,s)\), the per-capita version of the paper’s sum over agents in the same or lower tiers. The question is whether one can construct an allocation that is both mass-Nash-stable and mass-core-stable. Nash stability means no infinitesimal participant can improve by moving to another tier. Core stability means there is no positive-mass coalition, drawn from the existing tiers, that can form a new tier at any position and strictly improve every member’s utility. The allocation is finite and rational, but the core condition rules out blocking coalitions of any real positive mass.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large online competitive league assigning participants to ordered matchmaking divisions. There may be millions of participants but relatively few recurring archetypes, where an archetype includes everything relevant to matchup utility—such as build and skill bracket. The tier is a division, and the matchup utility is the expected advantage against participants in that division or lower ones. This assumes participants of a type really are interchangeable for the game’s purposes; if relevant player differences proliferate, the support \(\tau\) grows and the regime weakens. With \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), the utility above is exactly the finite-population sum divided by \(n\), which preserves preferences. Allowing a type’s mass to split across tiers is the natural fluid counterpart of assigning individual participants, not a change to the outcome space.

I expect this problem to be Class A, though that is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The result to seek is a construction polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, perhaps by extending Siler’s sorting structure to weighted types. The central technical questions are whether splitting type mass preserves the existence and Nash/core relationship, and whether a stable solution can be represented with a polynomial number of tiers. The finite algorithm’s polynomial dependence on the number of named agents does not itself answer either question.

This mirror covers the existence and construction of stable tier lists under antisymmetric matchup preferences. It does not claim to mirror the friendliness or robustness measurements, the \(k\)-tier heuristic, or the experiments. Nor does the paper’s probabilistic matchup matrix already make its population continuous: matchup uncertainty and population mass are different modeling choices.

The weak point is the scenario fit. The paper’s motivating Smogon list ranks a collection of distinct Pokémon, not a large population of repeated participants, and splitting a matchup type across tiers may not make sense for a catalog of unique game pieces. The league scenario makes the population regime credible, but it asks authors to recognize their formal tiering problem in a different application. I think that case is strong enough for a real mirror, while the weighted stability theorem remains open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this mirror is that Theorem 1’s high-multiplicity regime is hard to ground in the paper’s actual model. The theorem is a genuine computational result, but it is Siler’s 2017 result, included here as background. Its algorithm handles a finite set of named game pieces with an arbitrary antisymmetric matchup matrix \(W\). A small fixed type space for that model requires many agents to have exactly the same matchup utility against every type. In the paper’s main example, Smogon ranks each game piece once; repeating a piece as a mass of identical copies changes the object being ranked.

The proposed league scenario supplies participants, but it also needs the key modelling assumption: each participant’s preferences over tiers are given by expected advantage against all agents in the same or lower tiers. A league could adopt that rule, yet it is not supplied by the paper’s examples, where tiers classify game pieces. If participant-specific matchup behavior matters, the full matchup profiles may produce nearly as many types as participants. If only a few archetypes matter, the mirror rests on a substantive assumption about how much relational variation the application can ignore.

The mass-stability formulation is mathematically coherent. Its Nash condition treats an individual deviation as having no effect on the aggregate society; its core condition allows any positive mass to block. Allowing \(x_{t\ell}\) to split a type across tiers also lets identical participants occupy different levels. These are reasonable choices for an atomless competitive population, but they make the proposed question a new equilibrium model for mass populations. Theorem 1 itself gives little reason to think its authors would regard that model as the computational problem their finite sorting result addresses.

A stronger version of the mirror would start with a large cohort drawn from a fixed menu of matchup archetypes, normalize utilities by population size, and ask for an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length, alongside a correspondence with finite replicated instances. That is a real and potentially worthwhile question. The negative case cannot rule it out: the finite-to-mass dictionary is direct once the archetype assumption is accepted. Its weakness is that the paper offers little support for that assumption in its motivating applications. The case against this anchor is therefore a concern about fit and scope, not a fundamental obstruction to a continuous mirror.
