The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), target \(p\), registered plurality score masses \(s_c\), a finite set \(R\) of disjoint eligible group-template types, available mass \(\mu_r\) of each type, and bundle composition \(b_{r,c}\) giving the number of \(c\)-supporters per group copy, does there exist an activation mass \(x_r\) for each template such that \(0\le x_r\le\mu_r\), \(\sum_{r\in R}x_r\le\beta\), and \(s_p+\sum_{r\in R}b_{r,p}x_r\ge s_c+\sum_{r\in R}b_{r,c}x_r\) for every \(c\ne p\)?

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution over interchangeable group copies, each with a fixed bundle composition and one activation. Choose mass \(x_r\) of each template to minimize \(\sum_{r\in R}x_r\), subject to the plurality winner inequalities; the resulting problem is a rational linear program.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive-addition branch of Theorem 4 for repeated group templates; it leaves the deletion branch and the paper’s other results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the worst-case integrality gap between this LP and finite template-copy bundle addition, as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and the activation budget?
  • Which template families permit an exact or bounded-additive rounding of an optimal mass solution to discrete group activations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The anchor I would use is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that, parameterized by solution size \(k\), \(C\)-Cons-Add is \(W[1]\)-hard and \(C\)-Cons-Del is \(W[2]\)-hard, even when bundles are disjoint. I take the constructive addition result as my lead: adding voter bundles so that a designated candidate becomes a plurality winner, with a limit on the number of leaders selected.

My proposed mirror is Mass Constructive Bundle Addition. Consider a large membership vote or local election where a campaign hires speakers to recruit their households or work teams. A voter’s type records their plurality favorite and their role in a bundle template; the template specifies the group’s full composition of voter types and the cost of recruiting it. There are \(\tau\) templates, each repeated across many eligible groups. Thus the population is continuous at the group-copy scale, while plurality scores remain masses of voters. The scenario is strongest when the number of group copies is very large and the number of distinct templates is modest.

An instance gives candidates \(C\), target \(p\), initial registered score masses \(s_c\), and, for each template \(r\), available leader mass \(\mu_r\) and a vector \(a_{r,c}\) giving the mass of \(c\)-supporters recruited per unit of that template. Given a budget \(\beta\), ask whether there is a solution \(x_r\), the mass of leaders hired from each template, such that

\[ 0\le x_r\le\mu_r,\qquad \sum_r x_r\le\beta, \]

and

\[ s_p+\sum_r a_{r,p}x_r \;\ge\; s_c+\sum_r a_{r,c}x_r \qquad\text{for every }c\ne p. \]

A solution is the vector \(x\); the optimization version minimizes \(\sum_r x_r\). This preserves the paper’s question: recruit voter bundles, at a cost measured by leaders, until \(p\) is a winner. It changes the unit of choice from individual leaders to leader mass. That co-modification is part of making the population continuous, and is natural when a campaign can recruit a fraction of a very large cohort of identical group templates.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: it is a rational linear program with \(\tau\) variables and \(m\) winner constraints, solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. The paper’s \(W[1]\)-hardness makes this a useful test of whether the difficulty survives when the choice is divisible. The mirror does not claim that the discrete hardness transfers; it asks whether the fractional high-multiplicity version is a sensible and tractable problem.

The authors have a strong reason to recognize it: the paper itself motivates bundles through speakers who bring whole groups into an election. The continuous version retains that intervention and the plurality winner condition, while expressing its scale in masses. It covers the constructive-addition arm of Theorem 4; it does not claim to mirror the deletion result or the paper’s overlapping-bundle cases.

The weak point is that Theorem 4’s hardness construction encodes an Independent Set instance using many candidates and bundle templates, rather than many repetitions of a few templates. Its hardness therefore does not establish that the high-multiplicity regime is hard—or even that the reduction has a natural large-population interpretation. Also, a fractional \(x_r\) can recruit part of a template cohort, which need not correspond to an available action in a small election. Those are reasons to study the mirror’s relationship to finite instances, not reasons to reject the continuous question. A natural next question is how well the LP solution rounds to actual leaders, and when its fractional optimum matches the discrete optimum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves \(W[1]\)-hardness for constructive control by adding disjoint bundles, parameterized by the number of leaders selected. The strongest objection is that the proposed continuum changes the unit of choice. In the paper, a leader activates a whole set of named voters; the proposed \(x_r\) can activate a fractional mass of a bundle template.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. Let each group copy have a fixed composition of voter types and one available activation, and let many interchangeable copies share each template. Then \(x_r\) is the mass of copies activated; each activated copy still brings its whole bundle. For plurality, the template’s composition determines its score contribution, so the winner constraints are linear. This is a continuum of repeated group units rather than a continuum that breaks bundles apart. The paper’s own speaker-and-household motivation makes that a scenario its authors could recognize.

The hardness construction does not show that this repeated-template regime is hard: it encodes graph edges as candidates and vertices as bundle templates. That weakens any claim that the continuous model captures the reduction’s hard instances. But it does not undermine the mirror. The programme asks whether a sensible high-multiplicity version is worth studying, not whether it preserves the paper’s hardness. Nor can I reject the mirror just because its answer is a direct LP; a tractable answer is still an answer to a well-posed computational question.

The best negative case is therefore narrow: the mirror studies divisible recruitment from a finite catalog of repeated group templates, and its LP may be routine once that regime is specified. If realistic recruitment depends on a varied or overlapping social network, the catalog could be a poor model of that setting. But disjoint repeated groups are coherent, the scenario is plausible, and the paper itself supplies its motivation. I cannot make a convincing universal case against this anchor; the negative case is weak here.
