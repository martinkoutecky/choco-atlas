The nearest positive anchor is Theorem 3, proved in the paper. With a uniform prior, signals distributed as \(\mathcal{N}(\pm1,n)\), and agents choosing the action that matches the state, it proves that for every fixed \(t\), the probability that all agents in periods \(2,\ldots,t\) follow the first-period majority action tends to \(1\) as \(n\to\infty\). The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is decisive under ChoCo’s remit: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered theorems and corollaries concern learning rates, error probabilities, and an asymptotic limit. Appendix F describes a computational bottleneck and cites hardness in related settings, but proves no complexity result for this paper’s problem. The social planner’s policy in §7.2 is an open question, not an established result. A continuous planner problem might be worth posing, but it would be a new problem rather than a mirror of a result here.

Theorem 3 is the closest anchor, and the replicated-seller scenario makes a high-multiplicity regime plausible. But the theorem’s effect depends on finite-population fluctuations. With signals distributed as \(\mathcal{N}(\pm1,n)\), each seller’s first-period action is weakly informative: conditional on the good state, the probability of choosing the high action is \(\Phi(1/\sqrt n)\), only about \(1/2+O(n^{-1/2})\). In a finite group, sampling noise in the action share is also of order \(n^{-1/2}\), so the first-period majority has a nonvanishing chance of being wrong. In an atomless population with the same signal law, the share equals its conditional probability exactly. The majority then identifies the state, and the wrong-majority event central to Theorem 3 disappears. A second-order fluctuation model could preserve it, but that would support an analytic stochastic-limit question, not a computational result from the paper.

One could instead make private beliefs part of the type space and seek algorithms for equilibrium computation, or formalize the planner’s intervention problem. Yet beliefs evolve with private signal histories, so the state description grows over time; taking a distributional limit leads toward mean-field learning analysis. Those may be worthwhile directions, but the paper supplies no computational anchor for them. The negative case is strongest on that scope mismatch. It is not a proof that no useful continuous problem can be built from this model: the planner question leaves a real opening if “mirror” is broadened to include new computational problems.
