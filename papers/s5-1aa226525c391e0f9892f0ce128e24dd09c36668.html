The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\), \(k\), and demand-oracle access to two monotone, subadditive MPH-\(k\) valuation types \(v_A,v_B:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), put mass \(1/2\) on each type and supply \(1/2\) of each item. Compute a welfare-maximizing mass assignment \(x_{i,S}\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{i,S}=1/2\) for each \(i\in\{A,B\}\) and \(\sum_{i\in\{A,B\},\,S\ni j}x_{i,S}\le 1/2\) for each \(j\in M\), maximizing \(\sum_{i,S}x_{i,S}v_i(S)\). Determine the worst-case ratio of normalized integral two-bidder welfare, \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{int}}/2\), to this mass optimum, where \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{int}}=\max_{S_A\cap S_B=\varnothing}\bigl(v_A(S_A)+v_B(S_B)\bigr)\).

The model it lives in

A society with two complete valuation types, each of mass \(1/2\), where each mass unit receives an integral bundle and item supplies scale to \(1/2\). The decision variables \(x_{i,S}\) assign mass of type \(i\) to bundle \(S\); the objective is total welfare, and the configuration LP describes feasible mass assignments.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 6.12’s tight integral-to-mass welfare ratio for two subadditive MPH-\(k\) valuation types. It leaves aside the paper’s communication lower bounds, including Theorem 5.1, and the broader protocol results.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite populations with \(N\) agents of each type, what convergence rate relates the normalized optimum to the mass optimum?
  • For \(\tau\) valuation types, how does the tight integral-to-mass welfare ratio depend on \(\tau\) and \(k\)?
  • Which valuation classes admit efficient pricing for the mass-allocation LP as \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length vary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is narrow: the paper’s configuration LP is the welfare problem for a continuum of agents sharing a small number of valuation types. Take two cohorts, \(A\) and \(B\), each with mass \(1/2\). A type is a complete valuation function \(v_i:2^M\to\mathbb R_+\), including all the agent’s preferences and other auction-relevant attributes. Each \(v_i\) is monotone, subadditive, and MPH-\(k\). For each item \(j\), set supply to \(1/2\). A variable \(x_{i,S}\ge 0\) is the mass of type \(i\) assigned the integral bundle \(S\); require \(\sum_S x_{i,S}=1/2\) and \(\sum_{i,S:j\in S}x_{i,S}\le 1/2\) for each item. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{i,S}x_{i,S}v_i(S)\). Every unit of mass gets a whole bundle; only the type-level allocation is fractional. Equivalently, this is a large market with \(N\) agents of each type and \(N\) copies of each item, normalized by \(2N\).

My anchor is Proposition 6.12, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(k\ge2\), the worst-case ratio of integral welfare for two bidders to the optimum of their configuration LP, over two subadditive MPH-\(k\) valuations, is \(1/2+\Theta(1/\log k)\). The paper obtains the upper bound from its rounding result and the matching lower bound from its valuation construction. In the population interpretation, the configuration-LP optimum is exactly the best mass allocation above, up to the common normalization factor. So the proposition measures how much better a high-multiplicity market can do than one integral auction with just two bidders.

I would hand a prover the following problem: given \(M\) and two rationally specified monotone, subadditive MPH-\(k\) valuation types, optimize the stated mass allocation and characterize the worst-case ratio between its optimum and the best integral two-bidder allocation. A solution is an optimal mass assignment and a tight bound on that ratio, including instances attaining the lower bound. For this mirror I expect a Class A optimization problem: it is an LP, and its dual can be solved with a demand oracle when the relevant demand queries are efficient. Proposition 6.12 already supplies the tight ratio for this two-type case. Natural follow-ups are the precise finite-\(N\) convergence rate, the extension to \(\tau\) valuation types, and which valuation classes admit efficient pricing as \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length vary.

The authors should recognize this as their welfare-maximization problem in a high-multiplicity regime: agents still value bundles of the same items, the objective is still total welfare, and the co-modification is that supply scales with population while each agent receives an integral bundle. It also gives a concrete interpretation to the fractional configuration variables as masses of agents assigned to bundles. This is a reinterpretation of their discrete model, not a claim that the paper already studies a continuum population.

The weak point is the scope: Proposition 6.12 concerns an LP integrality gap, not the paper’s main communication lower bound. Theorem 5.1 and Proposition 6.14 hinge on private information held by two bidders; I don’t see a principled way to transfer those communication barriers to this population optimization problem without changing the question. The mirror covers one result emphatically, but it should not be sold as a continuous version of the paper’s hardness theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two-cohort construction is a legitimate mirror candidate. Take types \(A\) and \(B\), each with mass \(1/2\), and scale each item’s supply to \(1/2\). Then the configuration LP is exactly the welfare-maximization problem for this mass allocation, up to normalization. Rejecting it just because the paper has two bidders, or because a type fixes a valuation, would be a mistake.

The trouble is what Proposition 6.12 says in that model. Its numerator becomes the optimum welfare of the continuum market; its denominator remains the optimum of the original two-bidder integral auction. So the proposition’s ratio is a comparison between different population regimes, not a complexity result about computing welfare in the continuum. The mass-allocation problem is the configuration LP itself, and Proposition 6.12 already gives the stated worst-case ratio. The paper also explains how to solve that LP with polynomial communication using demand queries. If valuations instead arrive as circuits or value oracles, the computational bottleneck is the cost of demand or pricing queries; population continuization has not isolated it.

Theorem 5.1 does not provide a stronger anchor. Replicating each bidder into a cohort and scaling supply makes distributions over bundles feasible at the aggregate level. That is precisely what removes the integral-allocation barrier on which the communication lower bound is based. Preserve the two-bidder allocation problem and there is no population to continuize; use the natural high-multiplicity allocation and the target becomes the configuration LP. The rounding guarantee in Theorem 6.9 has the same issue: in the mass model, aggregate allocation can realize the fractional configurations directly, so the rounding gap is no longer the question.

One could study finite populations, many valuation types, or the pricing complexity of particular valuation classes. Those might be worthwhile projects, especially since repeated buyers with shared valuations are a sensible market regime. But they extend the paper’s fixed two-player communication problem and make the valuation encoding and number of types central. I would not claim the universal negative is airtight; the honest case against is that the one strong mirror, Proposition 6.12, becomes a known integrality-gap comparison, while the more computational extensions are new problems rather than continuizations of this paper’s results.
