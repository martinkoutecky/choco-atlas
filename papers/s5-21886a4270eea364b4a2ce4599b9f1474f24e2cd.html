What the mirror covers

A repeated-committee population model could extend the paper’s analysis of how individual patience relates to group discount factors; it would not cover a named computational result, and the chat-conformity findings would require a specified behavioral protocol.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a population-level extension of the paper’s question: in large organizations with many repeated three-person committees making similar intertemporal choices, members might come from a relatively small set of patience and discussion-behavior types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of members of type \(t\), and let committee types be triples of member types. Given a specified discussion protocol and a rule mapping each triple to its common group choice, one could ask what fraction of committees choose the median member’s preferred option. This is a plausible continuous-population question about the paper’s subject.

But it is not anchored in a qualifying result from this paper. The authors report that, across 35 three-person groups, regression and Bayesian analyses support an influence for the median-patience member; their chat analysis also finds that the person speaking third tends to follow the majority. These are empirical findings, not numbered theorems, lemmas, propositions, or corollaries asserting a computational complexity result. The paper gives no result about an underlying problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its equations and model comparisons do not supply such an anchor.

The candidate committee question also needs a specified model of discussion: the paper’s observed choices depend partly on chat order and conformity, not just on the distribution of patience types. Once a protocol and type-to-outcome map are supplied, the aggregate is a finite calculation; making group formation itself an optimization problem would add a problem the paper does not study. So I would not present this as a continuous mirror of a named result, or assign it a Class A/B/C verdict. The paper contains no named computational result to mirror. The plausible population scenario is the positive case’s limit: it extends the empirical aggregation question, but does not connect to ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive weakness is the one the proponent identifies: this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its equations are statistical models, and its findings concern experimental evidence for the median-patient member’s influence and third-speaker conformity. It gives no algorithmic problem, complexity classification, or optimization task. Changing the population regime cannot supply that missing anchor.

Repeated three-person committees are the strongest natural population scenario. A faithful model could give each member a type containing patience and discussion behavior, then use a specified protocol to predict group choices. But the paper’s actual estimand is the relationship between members’ elicited discount factors and the group’s elicited factor; “the fraction of committees that choose the median member’s option” is a new proxy. Even if we preserve the original estimand, a finite-support population and fixed protocol yield aggregate moments by summing over typed triples and speaking orders. That can support a worthwhile empirical study, but it does not pose the computational question this programme is meant to classify.

A richer protocol might make prediction harder, and committee assignment or speaker-order design could create genuine optimization problems. Those would be new problems introduced by the added protocol or intervention. The paper neither studies nor establishes a result about them. So the proponent has a plausible high-multiplicity extension, but not a surviving ChoCo mirror. The negative case is strong on that boundary; it would be too broad to claim that no continuous empirical extension of the paper could ever be worthwhile.
