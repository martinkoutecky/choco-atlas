The continuous mirror question

Given proposer and receiver populations distributed over finite supports with rational shares \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), population ratio \(\rho\), pairwise dating rates \(\delta_{ab}\), proposer capacities \(c_a\), receiver exposure caps \(q_b\), and exposure weights \(w_{ab}\), compute the mass allocation prescribed by ECDA. Process type pairs in descending \(\delta_{ab}\), with a fixed tie-breaking order, allocating up to the remaining proposer capacity \(c_a\mu_a\) and receiver exposure budget \(\rho q_b\nu_b\); for \(w_{ab}=0\), the receiver budget does not constrain that pair.

The model it lives in

A continuum society represented by distributions over finite supports of complete proposer and receiver types. The decision variable \(x_{ab}\) is recommendation-link mass between types; proposer capacity scales to \(c_a\mu_a\), receiver exposure capacity to \(\rho q_b\nu_b\), and the output is the mass version of ECDA's dating-rate-sorted greedy allocation. Expected dates can be evaluated as \(\sum_{a,b}\delta_{ab}x_{ab}\); the mechanism computes an allocation rather than optimizing that measure.

The objection that survived

The paper does not show that its personalized production predictions yield moderate exact type support, so the aggregation benefit is conditional on a recurring-type market regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover the allocation-computation results in Theorems 1 and 2. They leave the effective-date evaluation, cap tuning, simulations, and field experiment outside the computational mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove a finite-market-to-mass correspondence for the type-pair greedy allocation, including ties, fractional capacities, and the vanishing per-individual pair bound.
  • Determine when real prediction systems admit moderate exact support, or provide guarantees for approximate aggregation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s exposure-constrained deferred acceptance, because it already treats receiver capacity as a budget on expected likes or dates and allows fractional proposals. The paper’s fractional recommendation matrix is still indexed by named users; the mirror makes the population itself continuous.

Consider a daily platform market with proposer types \(A\) and receiver types \(B\). A type is a complete behavioral profile for this problem: its members share the relevant eligibility, cognitive capacity, predicted login and like behavior, and the same dating rates and exposure weights against every opposite-side type. Let \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) be the type shares on the two sides, each summing to one, and let \(\rho\) be the receiver-to-proposer population ratio. The input also gives dating rates \(\delta_{ab}\), proposer capacities \(c_a\), receiver exposure caps \(q_b\), and exposure weights \(w_{ab}\). For the paper’s two versions of ECDA, \(w_{ab}\) is either the expected like weight or \(\delta_{ab}\), the expected date weight.

The decision variable \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of recommendation links from proposer type \(a\) to receiver type \(b\), measured per proposer in the whole market. Thus \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le c_a\mu_a\), and an exposure cap requires \(\sum_a w_{ab}x_{ab}\le \rho q_b\nu_b\). The task is to compute the allocation prescribed by the mechanism, not to maximize an objective: these theorems do not say that ECDA optimizes effective dates. Its output can be evaluated by expected dates, \(\sum_{a,b}\delta_{ab}x_{ab}\), and by the paper’s congestion-adjusted effective-date measure.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that ECDA with dating-rate rankings produces the same recommendation matrix as a greedy algorithm that starts at zero and scans proposer–receiver pairs in descending order of \(\delta_{ij}\). For each pair it sets \(M_{ij}\) to the minimum of one, the proposer’s residual cognitive capacity, and the receiver’s residual exposure capacity divided by \(w_{ij}\). In the continuous mirror, scan type pairs in descending order of \(\delta_{ab}\), and allocate \(x_{ab}\) up to the smaller of the proposer type’s remaining capacity and the receiver type’s remaining exposure budget divided by \(w_{ab}\). If \(w_{ab}=0\), the receiver budget does not constrain that pair. The individual-pair upper bound of one drops out in the high-multiplicity limit: with positive mass in both types, the number of possible individual pairs grows with the market, while per-person capacities stay bounded.

For a finite type support, this is a sort-and-scan computation over \(|A||B|\) type pairs. That runtime is a consequence of the theorem and the paper’s stated greedy implementation; the theorem itself does not print a formal “in P” classification. The natural expectation for this mirror is tractability, or Class A.

A second, distinct mirror is Continuous Count-Capacity DA (C-DA). It uses the same type distributions, dating-rate rankings and proposer capacities, but receiver capacity \(q_b\) limits the number of recommendation links rather than their expected exposure. Its continuous constraint is \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le \rho q_b\nu_b\). Theorem 1, also proved in this paper, says that DA with dating-rate rankings returns the same recommendation matrix as scanning pairs in descending dating rate and matching a pair whenever both sides have residual capacity. The continuous version allocates type-pair recommendation mass in that order until either the proposer-type capacity or receiver-type count capacity is exhausted. This, too, is a tractable computation. It is a useful separate anchor because it mirrors the paper’s headcount-capacity mechanism, while C-ECDA mirrors its exposure-budget modification.

A plausible regime is a large regional dating platform’s daily active market: many eligible proposers and receivers, with substantially fewer recurring behavioral types, such as cohorts sharing eligibility, capacity and prediction profiles. A hiring platform with many applicants and vacancies in recurring skill and response categories is another possible setting. These stories fit the paper’s many-to-many recommendation problem: the mass represents people in each type, and the action remains allocation of recommendations between the two sides. The paper’s 8,000-proposer, 5,000-receiver simulation and its platform-scale motivation make large markets credible, though they do not establish that either sample has a small number of exact types.

That is the weak point. The type definition must preserve all behavior the mechanism uses. If pairwise predictions or rankings differ across individuals, they must be split into different types; then the support could approach the population size and the computational gain could disappear. Coarse bins would keep the support small but would approximate the paper’s inputs rather than exactly mirror them. The authors could plausibly recognize the exact-type version: they explicitly use dating-rate rankings and fractional exposure constraints. But the paper does not itself show that its production predictions fall into a small number of complete, interchangeable types.

The scope is correspondingly narrow: these mirrors cover the algorithmic results in Theorems 1 and 2. They do not turn the field experiment, the effective-date metric, or the paper’s empirical findings into complexity results. Nor do they claim a new optimization problem: the anchors compute specified mechanism outcomes. The paper has no named hardness anchor to carry over, so this case supports an algorithmic continuous mirror rather than a claim that discrete hardness dissolves. Follow-on questions include how to prove a finite-market-to-continuum correspondence with ties and fractional capacities, how to handle approximate types, and whether optimizing the exposure caps \(q_b\) for effective dates admits a tractable formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that both theorems already give explicit algorithms for the finite-user problem. The continuous mirrors are coherent, but the paper’s computational contribution does not appear to depend on population multiplicity: it sorts user pairs and scans them. Continuization would add value mainly if many users could be grouped into a small number of exact behavioral types.

For Theorem 2, a fixed finite set of proposer and receiver types gives a natural mass allocation \(x_{ab}\), with proposer capacity \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le c_a\mu_a\) and receiver exposure capacity \(\sum_a w_{ab}x_{ab}\le \rho q_b\nu_b\). Sorting type pairs by \(\delta_{ab}\) is a sensible continuous analogue of ECDA. But the paper’s input is a personalized matrix of predictions. An exact proposer type must preserve its interaction with every receiver type, and an exact receiver type must preserve its interaction with every proposer type. With pair-specific predictions, those complete profiles may leave almost everyone in a different type. Then \(\tau\) grows with the market, and aggregation gives no meaningful computational gain. Coarsening users into prediction bins may shrink \(\tau\), but it changes the rankings and can change which capacities bind; the paper supplies no approximation guarantee for that step.

Theorem 1 has the same limitation. Count-capacity DA can be aggregated over exact blocks, but the theorem already supplies the greedy computation on individual pairs. A fixed block market turns that into a sort over type pairs; it does not uncover a new computational bottleneck. If the more ambitious mirror instead uses a continuum of behavioral features and a pairwise score kernel, the type-pair order becomes an allocation problem over a continuum. That may be worth studying, but it requires additional structure and analysis beyond the theorem’s finite sorting argument. Tie-breaking and lifting aggregate mass back to named users would need explicit rules too; those are manageable modeling choices, but they underscore that the continuous result is not simply Theorem 1 or 2 with counts replaced by masses.

A stronger mirror might optimize the recommendation allocation or exposure caps for effective dates, rather than merely compute ECDA’s prescribed output. Under the paper’s Poisson approximation, the total effective-date contribution of receiver \(j\) is \(1-e^{-\mu_j}\), so an allocation model could optimize a concave welfare objective subject to capacity constraints. That is a more substantive computational question, and it could be relevant to the authors. It is also an extension beyond the two named results: the paper evaluates integrators and tunes a cap, but does not formulate allocation-wide optimization as its problem.

So the negative case is about payoff, not coherence. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 both admit sensible continuous mirrors, and large markets with repeated, genuinely interchangeable profiles are plausible. The paper does not establish that its personalized prediction setting has such a small exact support, or that the corresponding continuous computation would add much beyond its existing greedy algorithms. That makes these mirrors look thin on the evidence given, but I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists.
