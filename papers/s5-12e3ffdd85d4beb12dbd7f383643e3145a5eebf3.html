The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive observation is that apportionment already has population shares: for jurisdictions \(i\), one could set \(\mu_i=p_i/\sum_j p_j\), and ask for an integer seat allocation \(a\) with \(\sum_i a_i=h\). But that does not yield a ChoCo mirror. The paper already allows positive real populations \(p_i\); its decision objects are a finite set of jurisdictions, and its results characterize apportionment methods through axioms.

Theorem I says the Jefferson method is the unique method that is house-monotone, consistent, and satisfies lower quota. Theorem 2 says the method of smallest divisors is the unique method that is house-monotone, consistent, and satisfies upper quota. Both are proved in the paper, but neither asserts a computational result such as membership in P, hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. The lemmas give recursive characterizations, not complexity classifications.

So this paper contains no named computational result to use as an anchor. A continuous question about the apportionment axioms would continuize an axiomatic problem over finite jurisdictions, while a question treating residents as a continuous society would add agents and decisions absent from the paper. Neither supports a credible per-anchor computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The absence of a complexity theorem is real, but it rules out a literal continuous complexity classification of Theorem I or Theorem 2. It does not rule out a computational mirror of the apportionment problems those theorems characterize.

A natural population model takes each state as a type and its population share as its mass, \(\mu_i=p_i/\sum_j p_j\). Residents of a state are interchangeable for apportionment: the rule uses their aggregate population, not their identities. This is a finite-support society in precisely the high-multiplicity sense. The seats can remain integral; it is the population that becomes a distribution.

That gives a concrete computational question for each theorem. For Jefferson, given \(\mu\), a cost \(\gamma(i,j)\) for moving population mass from state \(i\) to state \(j\), and a state \(k\), find the minimum-cost mass transfer that gives \(k\) at least \(q\) seats. The corresponding question for the smallest-divisors method is whether its different allocation boundaries change the minimum. For a *fixed* target apportionment \(a\), Jefferson’s condition from Lemma 1 is \(\max_i \mu_i/(a_i+1)\le \min_{i:a_i>0}\mu_i/a_i\); cross-multiplication makes these linear constraints on the resulting masses. The full “at least \(q\) seats” question ranges over possible integer apportionments, so its computational complexity is a genuine question of its own. It measures how much of a society’s population must shift to change representation, rather than asking for a fractional seat allocation.

The paper does not solve that robustness problem, continuously or otherwise. Its axiomatic theorems supply the two allocation rules whose computational behavior under mass changes can be studied. Whether that problem turns out to have a simple algorithm is a question for the programme, not a reason to dismiss the mirror in advance.
