What the mirror covers

Theorem 4's asymptotic Bailey–Cavallo guarantee and Theorem 5's nonatomic equilibrium construction are the proposed anchors; neither supplies a continuous computational question distinct from the model already studied.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest foothold is a credible high multiplicity regime: a large road or packet network may carry many users drawn from a small number of origin–destination types. Mass would represent each type’s share of traffic, and routing decisions and congestion would be expressed as flows. That makes the population interpretation natural, but the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational mirror.

The closest result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for atomic multi-resource congestion games, if each resource’s congestion function is sub-exponential and Assumptions 1 and 2 hold, then Bailey–Cavallo redistribution returns a fraction of VCG revenue that tends to \(1\) as the number of agents tends to infinity. This supports the relevance of a large-population regime, but it is an asymptotic mechanism guarantee, not a complexity or algorithmic result.

Theorem 5, also proved here using the cited Beckmann result, says that in nonatomic routing, charging each resource \(r\) the congestion price \(p_r(m_r)=m_rg'_r(m_r)\) and giving each agent the equal rebate \(h(m)=\sum_r m_rp_r(m_r)\) makes the efficient congestion level a Nash equilibrium. This is the paper’s clearest population-continuous result—but a continuous mirror of it would restate a model the paper already studies, and the theorem is about equilibrium, not computation.

So there are no qualifying anchors for a prover-ready continuous computational problem, and I cannot honestly assign one an expected tractability class. The paper mentions computational complexity only in connection with separate prior work whose model it distinguishes from its own. The gap here is not whether a continuous population is sensible; it is that this paper does not establish a named computational result about that population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is a mismatch with ChoCo’s target. Theorem 4 proves an asymptotic revenue guarantee; Theorem 5 proves an equilibrium claim. Neither states a computational problem, fixes a finite input representation, or establishes a complexity bound or algorithm. The paper’s mention of computational work on congestion games is background, not a result of this paper. That is a strong reason not to treat either theorem as an existing computational anchor.

Theorem 4’s large-population setting is plausible: many users can share a small set of routing or valuation types. But its distinctive mechanism uses markets with one named agent removed, through \(R(v_{-i})\). In a mass model, removing an agent changes a type’s mass by \(1/n\); as that atom vanishes, the leave-one-out market becomes the original market. The resulting equal rebate is the nonatomic construction already given in Theorem 5. One could instead ask how to compute the finite-type VCG allocation and Bailey–Cavallo transfers from rational type masses. That is a coherent possible project, but it needs choices the theorem does not make—how valuations and costs are encoded, what output is required, and whether the target is exact computation or approximation. It would add an algorithmic problem to the paper’s model rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 5 is harder to dismiss: it already has a credible continuous population, with traffic divided among origin–destination types. But its proposed computational mirror would be to encode a network, demands, and congestion functions, then compute the efficient flow, prices, and rebate. That is a computational study of an already nonatomic routing model, not a continuization of a finite-population result. The core equilibrium computation also has substantial algorithmic literature: for example, work gives algorithms for computing Wardrop equilibria across flow demands and for approximate equilibria in combinatorial congestion games ([Klimm and Warode](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/1.9781611975482.56), [Nakamura et al.](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5596)). Those works do not settle the redistribution variant, so this is context rather than a novelty-collision argument; still, the rebate is specified from the resulting flow and prices, and the paper identifies no separate computational object for ChoCo to classify.

That makes the negative case convincing against the two stated anchors as *computational results*, but weak against the universal claim. A finite-type computational study of the mechanism could be well-posed and might be something the authors recognize as their problem. The paper’s lack of an encoding does not prevent one from supplying a natural encoding. I would not honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous computational question can come from this paper; the defensible claim is narrower: its two most natural anchors do not themselves supply one.
