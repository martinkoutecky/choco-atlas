What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue covers Theorem 1’s inclusion and proper-inclusion claims; Theorem 2 broadens the preference domains. Neither result provides a computational claim.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s own large-scale course-enrollment example: a university exchange with many students who share a complete type—initial allotment, marginal rankings over course-session categories, and category priority order. Mass would be the fraction of students of each type. An allocation could assign each student an integral bundle while conserving each category’s aggregate supply. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, though the paper does not analyze it continuously.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for lexicographically separable preferences, if a mechanism is strictly core stable, then for every problem its strict strong Nash equilibrium outcomes in the induced preference-revelation game are a subset of the strict core; moreover, for some problems that inclusion is proper. A continuous extension could ask whether the same relationship holds when agents are represented by type masses and coalitional deviations can involve measurable groups. That would be a new core-and-equilibrium theorem, though, not a continuous computational question established by this paper.

On the requested standard, the paper contains no named computational result: no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifies a problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise computationally. Theorem 2 extends Theorem 1 to domains with strict-core existence and minimal preference-domain richness; it too is a game-theoretic result. Corollary 1 is attributed to Takamiya (2009) and concerns implementation in single-type housing markets, not computational complexity. The paper describes MTTC, but its strict-core stability is attributed to Sikdar et al.; the present paper does not give a named complexity result for computing it.

So the possible population mirror is a reasonable research direction, but there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous problem per the brief. The weak point is decisive: the paper studies equilibrium implementation and core relations in finite markets, not computational complexity. I can’t honestly assign a tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness expectation to a computational mirror grounded in its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that Theorem 1 has no computational question to continuize. It relates two solution concepts: under strict core stability, strict strong Nash equilibrium outcomes lie in the strict core, and the inclusion can be proper. There is no algorithmic task, complexity bound, or approximation problem in that claim. Its continuum counterpart would ask whether an analogous relation holds for mass-based markets with measurable coalitions. That could be an interesting game-theoretic result, but it is outside ChoCo’s computational brief. Theorem 2 makes the same kind of claim on a broader preference domain; Corollary 1 is also about implementation, and is attributed to earlier work.

The course-enrollment example is plausible as a high-multiplicity setting, so it does not defeat this objection. Treating equivalent course slots as category supplies could make type masses meaningful. But then the proposed mirror still asks about core and equilibrium relations, not computational complexity. One could separately define an algorithmic task—for example, computing a core allocation or deciding whether a strict strong Nash equilibrium exists—but that would require specifying a new input, mechanism, and continuum deviation model. It would not be a continuous version of the paper’s Theorem 1.

There is a further modeling wrinkle: in the paper’s literal model, agents are endowed with distinct named objects, so preserving those identities can leave little or no repeated complete type. Aggregating equivalent slots can address that, but it is a reasonable redesign, not a reason to reject the enrollment regime outright.

So the strongest negative case is narrow but firm: none of the proponent’s anchors qualifies as an in-scope computational result, even under the better population model. The universal claim that *no worthwhile continuous computational problem exists for housing markets* would be too strong; the enrollment setting could motivate one. The paper itself, however, supplies no computational anchor for that research.
