The continuous mirror question

Given additive valuation \(v\) over goods \(\mathcal{M}\), find nonnegative rational masses \(q_S\) for \(S \subseteq \mathcal{M}\) satisfying \(\sum_S q_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\ni g}q_S=1/2\) for every good \(g\), minimizing \(\Psi(q)=\sum_S q_S\bigl(v(S)-v(\mathcal{M})/2\bigr)^2\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass continuum of identical agents with one valuation type \(v\); \(q_S\) is the mass receiving integral bundle \(S\), each good has per-capita supply \(1/2\), and the objective is the cross-sectional variance of bundle values.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 3.3 and its two-agent identical-valuation computation, including the difference-minimizing and MMS structure. It leaves the \(n \geq 3\) EFX and MMS results, the non-identical-valuation impossibility theorem, and the alternative variance-based objectives unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the high-multiplicity problem admit efficient approximation or fixed-parameter algorithms despite exact NP-hardness in the number of goods?
  • What precise encoding and rounding theorem relates rational finite-support \(q\) solutions to finite populations with \(N/2\) copies of each good?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s two-agent identical-valuation problem.

The anchor is Corollary 3.3 (NP-Hardness), proved in the paper. It states that, given an additive valuation \(v\), computing a distribution \(D\) over allocations of the goods to two agents with identical valuation \(v\), minimizing \(\Phi_{(v,v)}(D)\) among all ex-ante proportional distributions, is NP-hard. The paper derives this from its structural lemmas: every supported allocation of an optimum is difference-minimizing, every difference-minimizing allocation is MMS-fair, and computing such an MMS allocation is already hard.

I would mirror it as Continuum-Symmetric SoV Minimization.

The society is a unit-mass continuum \(I=[0,1]\) of otherwise indistinguishable agents, all having the same additive valuation \(v\) over \(M=\{1,\ldots,m\}\). Thus the type set has size \(\tau=1\), with \(\mu_v=1\), while the population is continuous. Each good \(g\) has supply \(1/2\) per unit population. This is the necessary high-multiplicity scaling of the original two-agent instance: with \(N\) agents, there are \(N/2\) copies of each good, while every individual still receives an integral subset of goods.

A population allocation is described by numbers \(q_S\), one for each \(S\subseteq M\), where \(q_S\) is the mass receiving the integral bundle \(S\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_S q_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\ni g}q_S=1/2\) for every good \(g\). The representative agent’s expected value is therefore \(\sum_S q_Sv(S)=v(M)/2\), exactly her proportional share. The continuous objective is the type-level variance \(\Psi(q)=\sum_S q_S\bigl(v(S)-v(M)/2\bigr)^2\), equivalently the variance of the bundle value of a uniformly sampled agent. A solution is a feasible mass allocation \(q\) minimizing \(\Psi(q)\), represented by its finite support and rational masses.

This is not continuity of the goods: each individual still receives an indivisible bundle. Only the population and the aggregate supply are continuous. The interpretation is natural for a large cohort of interchangeable recipients receiving scarce standardized vouchers or slots, with many copies of each good class and very few valuation types.

The mirror preserves the paper’s computational question exactly enough to be recognizable. Let \(d^\star=\min_{S\subseteq M}|v(S)-v(M)/2|\). Every bundle \(S\) is one side of a two-way partition, so every feasible \(q\) satisfies \(\Psi(q)\ge(d^\star)^2\). Conversely, if \(S^\star\) attains \(d^\star\), assigning mass \(1/2\) to \(S^\star\) and mass \(1/2\) to \(M\setminus S^\star\) is feasible and achieves \(\Psi(q)=(d^\star)^2\). Hence an optimal continuum solution identifies a minimum-difference partition, and therefore an MMS allocation for the corresponding two-agent instance. The continuum problem is consequently expected to be Class B: hardness transfers. The combinatorics live in the number and values of the goods, not in population multiplicity.

The authors should recognize this as their problem rather than a tractable surrogate: it retains additive valuations, indivisible bundles, ex-ante proportionality, variance minimization, and the same difference-minimizing/MMS structure. It merely replaces two named agents by a continuum of identical agents and replaces one copy of each good by high-multiplicity supply.

The weakest point is that the paper’s variance is phrased as lottery risk for a named agent, whereas the continuum formulation uses the common marginal distribution of an anonymous representative agent, equivalently the cross-sectional distribution of bundles. I think this is the correct high-multiplicity interpretation, but it should be stated explicitly. I would not claim that this single mirror covers the paper’s heterogeneous-valuation Theorem 5.1 or its EFX guarantees; those would require a separate definition of continuum EF1/MMS. The present mirror emphatically covers Corollary 3.3 and gives one precise, computationally meaningful continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that, as stated, it does not actually continuize the society. In Corollary 3.3 the agents already have identical valuations, so the type distribution remains \(\mu_v=1\). The variable \(q_S\) is simply a re-encoding of the paper’s existing lottery \(D\): it records the probability—or, equivalently, cross-sectional mass—of receiving bundle \(S\). The continuum is therefore attached to the allocation lottery, not to the population’s type structure.

Indeed, under the proposed supply constraints, the objective is just a rescaling of the paper’s objective. Since the complementary bundles have total value \(v(M)\),

\[ \Phi(D)=2\operatorname{Var}[v(A_1)] \]

and the proposed \(\Psi(q)\) is \(\operatorname{Var}[v(S)]\). The complement construction gives

\[ \min_q \Psi(q) = \left(\min_{S\subseteq M}\left|v(S)-\frac{v(M)}2\right|\right)^2. \]

Thus the proposed “continuous” problem is mathematically the same minimum-difference partition problem, with \(D\) renamed as a population distribution. On a strict reading of the programme, this is outcome-side randomization rather than a continuous society.

That objection is not ultimately strong enough, however. The natural repaired version is precisely the high-multiplicity model the programme endorses: take \(N/2\) copies of every good, let \(N\) identical agents each receive an integral bundle, and pass to the limit \(N\to\infty\). The resulting \(q_S\) is then genuinely a mass allocation over a continuous population, while goods remain indivisible for every individual. Its optimum is still exactly characterized by minimum-difference partitions, so the paper’s NP-hardness transfers.

Nor can the mirror be rejected because it has only one valuation type or because hardness persists; both are explicitly admissible in the programme. The objective has no problematic dependence on individual identity, and the continuum does not degenerate. Consequently, the only serious negative argument attacks the wording of the proposed construction, not its repaired high-multiplicity version. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor universally: Corollary 3.3 supports a worthwhile continuous mirror, and the negative case is weak.
