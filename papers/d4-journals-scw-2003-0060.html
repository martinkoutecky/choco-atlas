The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the required kind: none of its numbered results says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies computational complexity. Theorems 1, 2, and 5 characterize the constrained uniform rule by axioms; Lemma 4 proves continuity with respect to the *endowment*; Theorem 6 characterizes the constrained equal awards rule. Those are meaningful results, but they are not computational anchors. So I can’t honestly supply a continuous computational problem “per anchor” or name a lead anchor.

There is a plausible population regime in the paper’s EPA motivation: many firms, with relatively few distinct types \((p,c)\), where \(p\) is a firm’s preferred emissions level and \(c\) its permit constraint. A continuous society would give each type mass \(\mu_{p,c}\); an allocation would assign that type a per-firm permit amount \(x_{p,c}\), subject to \(\sum_{p,c}\mu_{p,c}x_{p,c}\le e\), with \(e\) the per-capita supply. That is a coherent high-multiplicity version of the allocation model, and the paper’s constrained uniform rule suggests a natural allocation to compute.

But the paper’s central claims concern which allocation rules satisfy fairness and incentive axioms—not the complexity of computing an allocation. Turning those claims into a continuum characterization would continuize an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s stated computational scope. A complexity study of computing the constrained uniform allocation could be posed, but it would not be anchored by a named computational result in this paper. The positive case is therefore limited: the application supports a sensible continuous-population model, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1, 2, and 5 characterize a rule by axioms; Theorem 6 characterizes a bankruptcy rule; Lemma 4 proves continuity with respect to the endowment. None poses a finite computational problem or classifies its complexity. Replacing the electorate with a distribution over types does not supply the missing algorithmic claim.

The EPA scenario is nevertheless a credible high-multiplicity regime. Let a firm’s type be its preferred emissions level \(p\) and constraint \(c\), and let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of type \(t\). With per-capita supply \(e\), the paper’s constrained uniform rule assigns \(x_t=\min\{p_t,c_t,\lambda\}\) when supply is scarce, where \(\lambda\) satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=e\); with excess supply, it assigns \(x_t=\min\{p_t,c_t\}\). Computing that allocation amounts to sorting the \(\tau\) thresholds and solving a weighted water-filling equation. This is a coherent continuous allocation model, but it is an implementation question for the rule the paper already defines, not a computational result among its findings.

A stronger proposed mirror—finding a rule with the paper’s fairness and incentive properties—would still ask an axiomatic characterization question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Adding an optimization objective, manipulation task, or extra permit constraints could create a computational problem, but would add a new question rather than continuize one of this paper’s results. The divisible commodity is outcome-space continuity; it does not make the population continuous, and it is not a novelty collision.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: this paper may motivate a sensible continuous-population model, but it offers no computational result for that model to mirror. I can’t establish that no worthwhile computational project could ever be built from it; the plausible firm-type regime is real. The weaker point is that such a project would need to be supplied by new problem design, not by the paper’s stated results.
