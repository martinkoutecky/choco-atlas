What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers direct rank aggregation under the squared positional-distance objective. It does not cover the sequential pair-selection strategy, its empirical selection rule, or the application results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational result to anchor a case on: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its hardness claim in §II-B is an unnumbered sentence citing [20] and [3]. It says rank aggregation is NP-hard, even with four input rankings, but does not clearly tie that hardness to this paper’s squared Euclidean objective. Algorithm 1, formulation (10), and the performance plots do not supply a numbered result of the required kind.

The strongest plausible mirror is still concrete. Imagine a large survey or crowdsourced panel ranking the same \(n\) options, with many respondents sharing each of \(\tau\) distinct rankings. Let each ranking type \(t\) have rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Given those types, find a permutation \(Y\) minimizing
\[ \sum_t \mu_t \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i^t-Y_i)^2. \]
This is a credible continuous-population version of the paper’s direct aggregation objective: the agents are respondents, the types are their complete rankings, and the output is the consensus ranking minimizing average squared positional error. Its likely classification is tractable: the objective reduces to assigning positions by the weighted average rank of each option, so it can be solved by sorting. That gives a useful high-multiplicity formulation, but it does not turn the paper’s cited hardness claim into a result about this objective.

This mirror covers direct rank aggregation, not the sequential pair-selection strategy or the paper’s three-ranking car-policy example. Its weakest point is the missing anchor: the paper’s own stated application has very few rankings, and the many-respondent scenario is an extrapolation. So I can make a plausible positive case for the underlying problem, but not the required anchor-based case for a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the anchor, not the population model. A large panel ranking a fixed catalogue is a credible high-multiplicity setting: respondents with the same ranking are a natural type. I would not claim that mass is meaningless or that the crowd scenario is implausible. Indeed, crowdsourced rank aggregation is an established setting. ([Caragiannis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.07460))

But that plausible model is not a computational result established by this paper. Its NP-hardness sentence cites other work without connecting the claim to its squared-Euclidean objective, and it gives no complexity theorem or algorithmic guarantee for the proposed method. As printed, the integer program assigns separate variables \(C_{ij}^{l}\) to each input ranking and constrains each ranking’s assignment separately, without linking them to one shared aggregate \(Y\). If those equations are repaired to express the intended common-permutation objective, then for population masses \(\mu_t\),
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\sum_i(r_i^t-y_i)^2 =K-2\sum_i\bar r_i y_i+\sum_i y_i^2, \qquad \bar r_i=\sum_t\mu_t r_i^t. \]
Since \(y\) is a permutation, the last term is constant. This identifies the direct weighted objective, but it does not supply the missing result from the paper. ([paper, problem statement and formulation](https://pure.port.ac.uk/ws/files/11012547/rankAggre_cameraReady.pdf))

The sequential procedure is a weaker bridge to population types. In the paper’s example, the three input rankings represent different criteria or stakeholder objectives—greenness, sales, and price—not repeated voters. In a voter-profile version, merging two types into their consensus ranking would replace their preference distribution with a synthetic type. Carrying the merged mass and its weighted rank information forward instead changes the procedure into a different summary method. The paper itself presents the selection rule as empirical and says formal selection rules and larger-scale computational evaluation remain future work. ([paper, application and conclusion](https://pure.port.ac.uk/ws/files/11012547/rankAggre_cameraReady.pdf))

So the crowd-profile question is coherent, but it is a new high-multiplicity rank-aggregation problem rather than a continuization of a named computational result here. That is the best case against this paper as an anchor. The universal claim remains weak: I cannot honestly rule out that the direct population model would be worthwhile to study on its own.
