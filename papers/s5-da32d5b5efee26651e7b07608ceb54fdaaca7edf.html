The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of agent types \(A\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to one, bounded valuations \(v_a\) over a finite item catalogue \(\Theta\), a horizon \(H\), and independent arrival laws \(Q^1,\ldots,Q^H\) with \(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{s=1}^H\lVert Q^s-\bar Q\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\delta\), consider a normalized unit-supply cohort arriving each period. Can a type-symmetric online PACE policy, allocating per-member amounts \(x_{a,s}\) with \(\sum_a\mu_a x_{a,s}\leq1\), achieve the analogues of Theorem 1’s per-type expected regret and envy rates \(\tilde O(\sqrt H+\sqrt{\delta}\,H)\), and mass-weighted mean-square utility and expenditure convergence rates \(\tilde O(\delta+1/H)\), relative to the mass-based expected-market and hindsight Eisenberg–Gale benchmarks? The hindsight benchmark maximizes \(\sum_a\mu_a\log U_a\), subject to \(U_a\leq\sum_s v_a(\theta_s)x_{a,s}\) and \(\sum_a\mu_a x_{a,s}\leq1\) for every period.

The model it lives in

A finite supported type set \(A\) carries masses \(\mu_a\), with each type specifying its valuations and all other relevant parameters. A unit-supply cohort arrives each period; the policy chooses allocations \(x_{a,s}\) per member of each type, subject to \(\sum_a\mu_a x_{a,s}\leq1\). The benchmark maximizes \(\sum_a\mu_a\log U_a\), and the mass-weighted dual has objective \(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{s=1}^H\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^s}[\max_a\{\beta_a v_a(\theta)\}]-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a\). A type-level PACE policy updates one pacing multiplier \(\beta_a\) per type and allocates each cohort according to the highest paced value.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s independent-corruption PACE guarantees: regret, envy, and convergence of average utilities and expenditures. It does not cover the general dual-averaging result or the ergodic and periodic input guarantees.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the weighted dual-averaging analysis establish the stated rates with explicit dependence on \(|A|\), valuation bounds, and \(\min_a\mu_a\)?
  • Which symmetric allocation or tie-breaking rule gives well-defined per-type utilities and envy in the mass model?
  • How closely does the mass policy approximate finite markets with integral assignments within each cohort?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass-PACE under independent corruption. The paper’s strongest anchor for it is Theorem 1 (Independent Case), proved in this paper. It says that for independent item arrivals whose average total-variation distance from their average distribution is at most \(\delta\), PACE has expected regret and envy \(\tilde O(\sqrt{t}+\sqrt{\delta}\,t)\). It also bounds the mean-square errors in average expenditures, average utilities relative to the expected-market optimum \(u^*\), and average utilities relative to the hindsight optimum \(u^\gamma\) by \(\tilde O(\delta+1/t)\). These are per-agent guarantees; the theorem takes the worst case over the stated input class. The proof combines this paper’s dual-averaging convergence result with its PACE-specific performance lemmas. The paper gives no named hardness or complexity-class result, so this algorithmic guarantee is my anchor.

Here is the population mirror. Let \(A=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be the supported agent types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to one. A type completely specifies an agent for this allocation problem: in particular, its valuation \(v_a(\theta)\) for every item category \(\theta\), and its budget or priority parameters. Restrict to equal incomes, as in the theorem’s main setting, and use a finite item catalogue \(\Theta\) with bounded rational valuations. The regime is a large online market with \(N\) agents and \(N\mu_a\) agents of each type, where \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\): for example, many compute-resource requesters in a small number of standardized contract tiers, with the same valuations over resource classes within each tier.

The supply must scale with that population. In the mass model, each arrival is a normalized cohort of one unit of supply; in a finite \(N\)-agent approximation, that represents a cohort of \(N\) ordinary units. A policy allocates mass \(y_{a,s}\) of the cohort to type \(a\), with \(\sum_a y_{a,s}\leq 1\). Each member of that type receives the type-average allocation \(x_{a,s}=y_{a,s}/\mu_a\). Individual cohort units can still be allocated integrally in the finite market; fractional \(x_{a,s}\) records the average across a type. This supply scaling is essential to keeping per-capita allocations meaningful as \(N\) grows.

Mass-PACE under independent corruption asks: given the type masses and valuations, a horizon \(H\), and independent arrival laws \(Q^1,\ldots,Q^H\) over \(\Theta\), find an online allocation policy that sees each arriving cohort’s category before allocating it, and that meets regret, envy, and convergence guarantees relative to the mass-based Eisenberg–Gale benchmark. The input class has \(\frac{1}{H}\sum_s\|Q^s-\bar Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\delta\), where \(\bar Q\) is the average arrival law. For a realized sequence, the benchmark maximizes \(\sum_a\mu_a\log U_a\), subject to \(U_a\leq\sum_s v_a(\theta_s)x_{a,s}\) and \(\sum_a\mu_a x_{a,s}\leq1\) for each arrival. Regret for a type is its hindsight benchmark utility minus its realized utility; envy is the greatest amount a member of that type would gain by receiving another type’s allocation instead. The corresponding expected-market benchmark uses \(\bar Q\).

A solution is an executable online policy, plus a proof of bounds for every type’s expected regret and envy and for mass-weighted mean-square utility error relative to both benchmarks. The target is the normalized analogue of Theorem 1’s rates, with dependence on \(\tau\), valuation bounds, and the minimum type mass made explicit. The natural policy maintains one pacing multiplier \(\beta_a\) per supported type, allocates the current cohort according to the highest \(\beta_a v_a(\theta_s)\), and updates multipliers using the mass-weighted log regularizer \(-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a\). Its dual objective has only \(\tau\) multiplier variables, \(\sum_{\theta}\bar Q(\theta)\max_a\{\beta_a v_a(\theta)\}-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a\). With a finite catalogue, evaluating the winning type takes \(O(\tau)\) work per update. I expect the mirror to be tractable for moderate support and suitably controlled minimum masses: it preserves the paper’s Nash-welfare allocation problem and its dual-averaging structure, while making repeated valuation profiles explicit.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem. Their agents already have valuation functions, their fair-allocation benchmark is Eisenberg–Gale, and PACE already acts through pacing multipliers. Grouping agents who are identical in valuations and all other parameters is precisely a high-multiplicity regime; the continuous object is the society’s mass over those types. The paper’s continuous item space does not already do this: its population of agents remains finite.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s PACE guarantees for independently corrupted item arrivals. I would not stretch it to claim that the paper’s general dual-averaging results, or its ergodic and periodic guarantees, have thereby all been mirrored.

The weakest point is that Theorem 1 does not itself establish these mass-based bounds. Its constants hide polynomial dependence on the finite number of agents, and arbitrary tie-breaking can leave identical agents with unequal realized utilities. A new proof must handle within-type symmetry and expose the dependence on small masses; it cannot simply replace \(n\) with \(\tau\) in the theorem. The supply-scaled cohort model is also a co-modification of the allocation setting. The case survives because the weighted population objective and the mass-weighted dual are not invented substitutes: they arise by aggregating repeated identical types in the finite market. The key further questions are whether the regret and envy rates survive that aggregation, how the bounds depend on \(\min_a\mu_a\), and how closely the mass policy approximates finite PACE when individual cohort units must be assigned integrally.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not the kind of computational result ChoCo is set up to continuize. It proves expected regret, envy, and convergence rates for PACE under stochastic input models. It does not classify the complexity of a problem or give an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm for one. So the proposed mirror would extend a result in online fair allocation and stochastic optimization, rather than chart the high-multiplicity complexity landscape described by the programme. That is a real scope objection, though it does not show the extension is uninteresting in its own field.

I cannot make a strong case that the *model* fails. With \(\tau\) repeated types, weighted Eisenberg–Gale welfare and the regularizer \(-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a\) give a coherent type-level formulation. Scaling supply with population is an allowed co-modification, and symmetric allocations can make per-type regret and envy meaningful. The paper’s continuous item space does not already provide this population model. A finite catalogue and masses bounded away from zero also avoid an obvious continuum degeneration.

The best version of the proponent’s mirror therefore survives: ask for PACE’s guarantees in a large market with repeated valuation types and population-scaled supply, exposing their dependence on \(\tau\) and the smallest type mass. The paper does not prove those bounds, and I see no fundamental obstacle that rules them out. The likely weighted dual-averaging extension may be a relatively direct one, but that is a priority argument, not a reason the question is ill-posed or worthless.

So my negative case is weak under the burden set here. I can argue that this is outside ChoCo’s central complexity agenda; I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
