The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in the paper. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes strategy-proof rules by equivalence with \(B_\mu\)-monotonicity and monotonic \(M_X\)-independence. Corollary 1 and Proposition 2 describe those rules using order filters. Proposition 3 identifies the co-majority rule for odd populations with the generalized Condorcet–Kemeny rule. These are results about strategy-proofness and aggregation, not complexity or running time.

The closest complexity statement is unnumbered footnote 18. It says complexity is not addressed in the paper, then cites Hudry (2012) for NP-completeness of computing median total preorders on arbitrary profiles when the population is suitably larger than the alternative set. Since that is neither a numbered result nor proved in this paper, it cannot serve as the requested anchor.

A plausible but unanchored mirror would be Continuous Kemeny Median: given a finite agenda \(A\), a support \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) total preorders on \(A\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_R\) summing to \(1\), return a total preorder \(Q\) minimizing \(\sum_{R\in\mathcal T}\mu_R\,\delta_C(Q,R)\), with a fixed tie-breaker for equal minima. Here a type is a reported total preorder, mass is the fraction of the population reporting it, and the output is the social preorder minimizing average Kemeny distance. A large electorate with many repeated rankings and relatively few distinct rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Proposition 3 is the nearest named result: for an odd-sized discrete electorate, it identifies co-majority with this generalized Kemeny median.

For empirical masses \(\mu_R=n_R/n\), the objective is the discrete profile’s total Kemeny distance divided by \(n\), so its minimizers agree. The natural expectation is therefore hardness transfer on that embedded subfamily, with the footnote’s cited result suggesting NP-hardness in its stated population regime. The weakness is that this turns the paper’s strategy-proof characterization into a computation problem the paper explicitly leaves aside. I would offer it as a credible continuous-population question, but not as a case satisfying the required named-anchor test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is a scope failure: this paper contains no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Proposition 2, and Proposition 3 characterize aggregation rules and strategyproofness. Proposition 3’s link between co-majority and the generalized Condorcet–Kemeny rule is the closest computational anchor, but it does not analyze computing that rule. Footnote 18 explicitly sets computational complexity aside and cites a discrete result from elsewhere.

The strategyproofness results also lose their central content in a nonatomic population. A single person’s report changes the distribution by zero, so unilateral strategyproofness is automatic for any rule defined on distributions. Giving a type positive mass and treating its report as a deviation instead models a coordinated bloc, which is a different incentive question from the one characterized here. That undercuts attempts to make Theorem 1 or Proposition 2 into continuous strategic-computation results.

The strongest improved candidate is nevertheless well posed: given rational masses \(\mu_R\) on total preorders, compute a \(Q\) minimizing \(\sum_R \mu_R\,\delta_C(Q,R)\), with a fixed tie-breaker. A population with many repeated rankings and few distinct rankings is plausible. For rational masses, clearing denominators turns this into an integer-weighted Kemeny instance. That does not defeat the mirror: high multiplicity supports its plausibility, and inherited hardness would still be informative. It does show, though, that this is a computational follow-on to a question the paper explicitly leaves aside, rather than a continuization of a computational result it proves.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror, but weak against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be extracted from it. The weighted Kemeny problem may be worth studying on its own; the paper’s axiomatic characterizations do not establish that it is a continuous mirror of their results.
