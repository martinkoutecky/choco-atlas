The case FOR (proponent)

Theorem 16 is its strongest general result: any ranking method that is natural, reducible by Condorcet tournaments, and satisfies the long tournament property must exhibit an inversion for some tournament—deleting the last-ranked player reverses the order of the remaining players. Corollaries 18, 21, and 28 establish inversion for Borda, Massey, and Colley; Propositions 32 and 35 characterize when it occurs on the paper’s special tournaments. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The claim that no continuous mirror exists overreaches. There is no numbered complexity theorem here, so this is not a strong anchor for a complexity classification. But Proposition 32 gives a specific, mass-sensitive result that has a natural continuous version.

In \(\mathcal Z_{n,k,\ell}\), replace the integer copies of \(\mathcal R_n^+\) and \(\mathcal S_n^-\) by positive rational masses \(a\) and \(b\). Equivalently, take a distribution over the match types in those two tournaments, assigning equal mass within each component. This describes repeated match outcomes across seasons. For Massey ratings, replacing counts by these weights gives the same linear system with real coefficients; normalizing the total mass does not change its ranking. The paper’s slope calculations then give \(\beta=-b/(b+an)<0\) before deleting \(P_n\), and \(\beta'=(a-b)/(a+b)\) afterward. Thus the remaining players’ order reverses exactly when \(a>b>0\), just as Proposition 32 says for integer multiplicities. The reversal holds on an open region of the mass space, so it does not disappear in the continuous model.

That yields a precise question over rational match masses: which distributions produce a strict reversal when the currently last player is removed? On the paper’s family, the answer is the threshold \(a>b\); for general supports, one can compute the Massey ratings before and after deletion by exact linear algebra. This is a narrow continuous mirror of the paper’s central phenomenon, not merely probability over discrete tournaments. Repeated match types are also a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s own setting, though treating match records as the population is the weakest part of the case.

The case remains limited: the paper proves structural properties, not complexity bounds, and the weighted version of Proposition 32 is likely easy. So this does not make the paper a strong ChoCo complexity case study. It does, however, defeat the universal claim that no sensible continuous mirror exists.
