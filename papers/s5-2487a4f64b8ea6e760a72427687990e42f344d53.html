The continuous mirror question

Given rational cohort masses \(\mu_i>0\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), a block adjacency matrix \(A\), update probabilities \(\rho_i\in(0,1)\), an initial rational belief-share vector \(x^0\in[0,1]^k\), and rational \(\varepsilon>0\), define \(q_i(x)=\frac{\sum_j A_{ij}\mu_jx_j}{\sum_j A_{ij}\mu_j}\) and update \(x_i^{t+1}=(1-\rho_i)x_i^t+\rho_i\) if \(q_i(x^t)>1/2\), \(x_i^{t+1}=(1-\rho_i)x_i^t\) if \(q_i(x^t)<1/2\), and \(x_i^{t+1}=x_i^t\) otherwise. Determine whether \(x^t\) converges to a fixed point and, if it does, return a rational \(\hat{x}\) within \(\varepsilon\) of its limit in \(\ell_\infty\) distance.

The model it lives in

The population is a continuum divided into \(k\) cohorts, with mass \(\mu_i\) per cohort; a type records its cohort's block-level connections and update probability. The state variable \(x_i\) is the fraction of cohort \(i\) that believes the statement. Each round, updating members adopt the strict majority belief among connected cohort mass, while ties preserve current beliefs. The task is to decide convergence and approximate the limiting belief shares when a limit exists.

What the mirror covers

The mirror addresses Theorem 4's convergence result in a dense block-network regime. It leaves Theorem 2's axiomatic properties and Theorem 3's existence of a converging update schedule unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(k\), can convergence be decided and the limit approximated efficiently when it exists?
  • Do fixed \(k\) or symmetric cohort interactions yield tractable convergence and limit-computation cases?
  • How does finite-population absorption time relate to convergence of the mass process?

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow: Theorem 4 is the paper’s strongest anchor for a population limit, though it is not a complexity classification. It states that, for every finite social network and every initial belief profile, random asynchronous evolution under the majority rule eventually reaches an equilibrium. The authors prove this here. Their proof uses the finite graph of possible belief profiles and the existence of a path to an equilibrium; it does not give a complexity bound for finding the equilibrium or for the convergence time. The paper contains no named result establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a comparable complexity class.

A concrete mirror is Block-Continuum Majority Convergence. Consider a large online community partitioned into \(k\) exposure cohorts. Members of cohort \(i\) have the same connections to other cohorts and the same update probability. Let \(\mu_i\) be cohort \(i\)’s share of the population, \(A_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether cohort \(i\) is connected to cohort \(j\), \(\rho_i\in(0,1)\) be its per-round probability of updating, and \(x_i^t\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of cohort \(i\) that believes the statement at round \(t\). Assume each cohort has positive mass among its connected cohorts, and define

\[ q_i(x^t)= \frac{\sum_j A_{ij}\mu_j x_j^t} {\sum_j A_{ij}\mu_j}. \]

The continuous random-asynchronous update is

\[ x_i^{t+1}= \begin{cases} (1-\rho_i)x_i^t+\rho_i,&q_i(x^t)>\tfrac12,\\ (1-\rho_i)x_i^t,&q_i(x^t)<\tfrac12,\\ x_i^t,&q_i(x^t)=\tfrac12. \end{cases} \]

Here, mass is the fraction of the population in a cohort, and the decision variable is the vector of belief shares \(x^t\). This models a dense block network: within a cohort, the population is large enough that updating a random subset produces deterministic mass changes in the continuum limit.

The problem to hand a prover is: given rational \(\mu_i\), \(A_{ij}\), \(\rho_i\), an initial rational vector \(x^0\), and an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), determine whether the resulting sequence converges to a fixed point and, if it does, return a rational vector within \(\varepsilon\) of its limit. I would provisionally expect the variable-\(k\) problem to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C): threshold interactions between cohorts can make the orbit’s long-term behavior difficult to analyze, while the finite theorem’s state-graph argument does not carry over to fractional mass states. Fixed \(k\) may have a tractable boundary. Further questions include whether symmetric cohort interactions guarantee convergence, and how finite-population absorption time relates to convergence in the continuum limit.

The regime is plausible when a very large community has many agents but relatively few recurring roles or exposure patterns. A type here records the cohort’s interaction pattern and update probability; belief is the changing state whose mass we track. The authors could recognize this as a mean-field version of their majority evolution problem, though it covers only dense block-structured networks, not their arbitrary graphs. The mirror covers Theorem 4 alone; it does not continuize Theorem 2’s axiomatic properties or Theorem 3’s existence of a converging update schedule.

The weakest point is also the main one: this is a dynamics and convergence question, not yet the computational complexity programme’s usual question about efficient algorithms or hardness. Moreover, finite-population convergence does not establish convergence of the limiting mass process: taking population size to infinity and taking time to infinity may not commute. That gap makes the mirror a real research question, but keeps the positive case modest.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch of subject. The paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2 checks axiomatic properties, Theorem 3 proves that a converging update schedule exists, and Theorem 4 proves almost-sure convergence. Theorem 4 is the proponent’s only anchor, but its conclusion is a property of the dynamics, not an algorithmic task. Asking for an algorithm to decide convergence and approximate a limit adds a new question; it does not mirror a complexity result in the paper.

There is a deeper modeling gap in the proposed cohort version. In the paper, an agent updates from the beliefs of particular neighbors. Cohort masses alone do not determine those neighborhoods or their members’ beliefs. The proposed \(q_i(x)\) works if the network is made dense and block-structured, so agents in a cohort respond to the same aggregate. That is a legitimate co-modification, but it is a restricted new network model. For sparse networks, preserving the local majority rule generally requires neighborhood or edge information beyond a finite list of type masses.

Even in the block model, the proposed recurrence captures a finite-time mean-field limit: random individual updates average into deterministic changes in belief shares. Theorem 4 concerns what happens over an unbounded time horizon in a finite-state stochastic process. Its proof relies on the finite transition graph and on every update subset having positive probability. Neither feature carries directly to the continuum. A continuous-time version with individual update clocks, such as \(\dot{x}_i=\rho_i(b_i(x)-x_i)\), may be a better analogue, but it is a newly specified dynamical system whose relation to finite-population absorption needs to be established.

That is the best negative case: the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and the proposed algorithmic problem depends on substantial choices about network structure and the limiting dynamics. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A dense block-network regime is plausible, and its convergence or limit-computation problem could be worth studying. The honest weakness is that this case argues against treating the paper as a strong anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme; it cannot rule out every sensible continuous-population question inspired by the paper.
