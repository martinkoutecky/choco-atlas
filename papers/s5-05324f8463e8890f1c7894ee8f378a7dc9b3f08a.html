The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s central gate: neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility claim about whether an SRF can satisfy specified principles across a domain of profiles. Theorem 2 is an axiomatic existence claim under stronger assumptions. Neither specifies an algorithmic input, an encoded action space, a cost, or an input length. The paper leaves the well-being transformations and appraisals highly general, and treats \(X\) as an abstract finite set. So there is no complexity problem here yet.

The best proposed mirror of Theorem 1 would give the society as a distribution over complete agent types and ask whether one can find an SRF satisfying PR and WP. But keeping the theorem’s universal quantifiers leaves an axiomatic existence question: replacing individual names with masses does not make it a computational one. Turning it into a finite-input search for a conflicting profile or an admissible relation could define a computational problem, but that would be a new problem, not the theorem’s mirror. It would also need finite encodings for the appraisals, transformations, group structure, and policy space, none of which the paper supplies.

The best mirror of Theorem 2 would use the paper’s construction to compute a social relation, or select a policy from it. Yet the construction takes the group evaluations as input and defines the output relation; the paper does not give a policy-selection objective or optimization task. Moreover, the axioms and construction depend on which complete types are present, not their population shares: duplicating an identical individual leaves the universal WP condition, group-appraisal intersections, and least-advantaged comparisons unchanged. A distributional version could therefore discard its mass values and retain its support. If a richer policy choice or resource allocation problem were added so that masses affected the answer, that would supply the missing computational content by changing the problem.

This does not rule out valuable future algorithms for policy design informed by well-being data. It does mean that neither named result supports a continuous computational mirror of this paper: any such project would have to introduce both a computational task and the structure that makes population mass matter. That is the honest limit of the negative case; the paper’s subject may inspire new computational work, but its results do not currently furnish the mirror.
