The continuous mirror question

Given a finite object set \(V\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\Omega\) of non-strict incomplete rankings \(a^t\), each ranking at least two objects, and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), find a complete ranking \(r\in\Omega_C\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\hat{\tau}_x(r,a^t)\).

The model it lives in

A population is represented by rational mass \(\mu_t\) on each supported ranking type \(a^t\in\Omega\). Choose a complete ranking \(r\in\Omega_C\), including ties, to maximize the mass-weighted scaled Kendall tau-extended agreement; equivalently, minimize mass-weighted normalized projected Kemeny–Snell distance.

The objection that survived

The mass-weighted objective collapses to the scaled combined ranking matrix, so the paper’s existing branch-and-bound and integer-programming machinery remains applicable; this limits novelty but not the mirror’s plausibility.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 2’s hardness for correlation-based NIRA and its link, via Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, to normalized projected Kemeny distance. It leaves the axiomatic results, sampling framework, and empirical comparisons outside the anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the problem parameterized by the number of objects \(n\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\)?
  • Do restrictions on ties or incomplete rankings yield useful approximation guarantees or tractable cases?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a population version of correlation-based NIRA, anchored on Corollary 2. As printed, Corollary 2 says that correlation-based non-strict incomplete ranking aggregation is NP-hard. The paper proves this by using Corollary 1’s equivalence between the correlation and distance formulations; the underlying NP-hardness of distance-based NIRA is cited from Morin et al. (2016).

Here is the continuous problem. Let \(V\) be a set of \(n\) objects. A type \(t\) is one of the paper’s non-strict incomplete rankings \(\av^t\in\Omega\): it specifies which objects the person ranks, their order, and any ties. Give each type a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(\tau\) be the number of types with positive mass. Require each type to rank at least two objects so that the paper’s scaled coefficient is defined. The decision is a complete ranking \(\rv\in\Omega_C\), including the possibility of ties. The Population-\(\hat{\tau}_x\) Consensus Ranking problem is to find
\[ \arg\max_{\rv\in\Omega_C}\ \sum_t \mu_t\,\hat{\tau}_x(\rv,\av^t). \]
A solution is any complete ranking attaining the maximum. Equivalently, by Theorem 1, it minimizes the mass-weighted normalized projected Kemeny–Snell distance. The question concerns agreement with a population; the consensus output remains the complete ranking the paper asks for.

A plausible regime is a large review or public-consultation platform asking people to rank the same slate of proposals, with ties and “not enough information” responses allowed. A shared rubric and repeated assignment patterns can produce many people with exactly the same ranking type: the same ranked subset, ties, and order. With \(N\) respondents and \(\tau\) observed patterns, \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). That is a meaningful high-multiplicity population: mass records each pattern’s share. If every respondent is given equal weight, the paper’s sum over judges becomes exactly this objective after replacing each type’s mass by its frequency divided by \(N\).

The hardness transfers directly. Given a finite NIRA profile \(\av^1,\ldots,\av^K\), assign each distinct ranking type mass equal to its frequency divided by \(K\). Then the continuous objective is \(1/K\) times the paper’s sum, so it has exactly the same optimal rankings. This conversion is polynomial in the explicit profile size and the masses have binary rational encodings. Replicating every respondent \(q\) times leaves the masses and optimization unchanged while making \(N/\tau\) arbitrarily large. The expected classification is Class B: the discrete hard instance embeds in the continuous problem, and the hard combinatorics can remain in the number of objects and possible consensus rankings even as the population grows.

This mirror covers Corollary 2’s correlation-based NIRA, specifically its \(\hat{\tau}_x\) version and its equivalence to the normalized projected distance. It preserves the paper’s incomplete input rankings, neutrality toward unranked objects, per-person normalization, and complete consensus ranking. All continuity is in the population distribution; the paper’s aggregation question remains intact. Corollary 1 and Theorem 1 support the link between the formulations, but Corollary 2 is the sole hardness anchor.

The mirror generates useful follow-up questions: how does the problem behave when parameterized by the number of objects \(n\), support size \(\tau\), or input bit length \(L\)? Are there useful approximation guarantees, or restrictions on incomplete rankings and ties that make the problem tractable? In particular, hardness after arbitrary replication shows \(N\gg\tau\) is compatible with hard instances; it does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is bounded independently of the problem size.

The weakest point is also the reason to expect Class B: this is a faithful high-multiplicity formulation, but its hardness transfer comes from preserving the discrete objective exactly. The cited hardness does not show that continuity creates a new difficulty, or that a small fixed set of types is hard. It does show that the authors’ own ranking-aggregation problem has a sensible population-mass version that retains its central computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about scientific payoff, not whether a mirror can be formulated. Corollary 2 is a numbered computational result, and its hardness claim is still an anchor even though it follows through the paper’s equivalence theorem. The proposed population model is faithful: in a large review platform, many judges can share a ranking type, and neither the input nor the consensus objective depends on their identities. The restriction to types ranking at least two objects handles the coefficient’s undefined denominator; the paper’s normalized distance also gives a natural convention for less informative types. I can’t make “no multiplicity,” “identity matters,” or “the continuum degenerates” stick.

The best objection is that mass is only a coefficient in this mirror. Let \(q_t\) be the number of objects ranked by type \(t\), and let \(a^t_{ij}\) be its ranking-matrix entries. Since the consensus ranking \(r\) is complete, the objective is
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\hat{\tau}_x(r,a^t) = \sum_{i,j} \left(\sum_t \frac{\mu_t a^t_{ij}}{q_t(q_t-1)}\right)r_{ij}. \]
Thus the population collapses to the scaled combined ranking matrix. The paper already derives that statistic, uses it in branch and bound, and gives an integer-programming formulation over the same discrete ranking space. Replacing counts by rational masses leaves that machinery and feasible set intact. It creates no mass-transfer decision, fractional ranking, or new pricing problem.

That makes this a thin continuation: the broad question is a weighted version of the paper’s existing consensus-ranking problem, and the distinctly new work would have to concern a narrower issue such as algorithms parameterized by support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\). Rational masses can encode large multiplicities succinctly, so that parameterized or encoding question may be worthwhile; it is a real computational question, not a mere notation change. But the paper does not itself supply a reason to expect a new continuous-optimization phenomenon from it.

This objection does not defeat the anchor under the stated standard. The high-multiplicity regime is plausible, and the population objective is a direct, author-recognizable mass version of NIRA. Existing high-multiplicity work would support that regime, not disqualify it, and a Class B outcome is allowed. So the honest negative case is weak: I can argue that this mirror is modest in novelty, but I cannot argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists here. Under the given gate, the Corollary 2 mirror survives.
