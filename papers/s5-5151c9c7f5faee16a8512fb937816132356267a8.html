The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that there is no computational result here to anchor a complexity mirror. Theorems 1–3 and 4 compare mechanisms by inclusion of the schools that are strategically accessible to each student; Proposition 2 identifies a prefix of students for whom constrained serial dictatorship is strategy-proof. These are results about mechanism properties, not the complexity of finding a manipulation or solving an optimization problem. The paper gives no complexity bound for any such task. The proponent is right on that narrow point.

The best possible rescue is a large district with a common priority order, a small set of preference classes, and capacities that grow in proportion to enrollment. Priority position can then be represented by a quantile \(u\in[0,1]\). Proposition 2 has a meaningful limit: the protected share is the sum of the capacity shares of the \(k\) smallest schools. One could also ask what fraction of students of each preference and priority type can profitably manipulate, comparing constrained GS with FPF or comparing list lengths. High multiplicity is quite plausible in that setting; the paper’s individual-level question has a natural population-level shadow.

But that shadow is a new statistic, not a continuous version of the paper’s computational result. The theorem comparisons quantify over all contexts and preference profiles. Measuring accessible students under one distribution changes the question: it does not follow from those universal comparisons, and the paper supplies no computational task or algorithmic obstacle for calculating it. With an atomless population, one student’s report does not change aggregate cutoffs; keeping unilateral manipulation meaningful requires tracking the student’s priority quantile and evaluating their report against fixed aggregate outcomes. Replacing an individual deviation with a positive-mass deviation makes aggregate outcomes respond, but changes strategic accessibility into a coalition question.

Exact priority position is also essential to who gets admitted. Grouping students only by preference erases that information; retaining it gives each student a distinct rank or a continuous priority coordinate. Either choice leaves no clear finite-support high-multiplicity computation of the kind the programme studies. This is a real weakness in the proposed mirror, though not proof that no useful continuum analysis could be built.

So the negative case is strong against an *anchored computational mirror*: none of the named results asserts computational complexity. It is weaker against the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists at all. The priority-quantile model makes a coherent continuous population question possible, but establishing its computational value would require inventing a new problem rather than continuizing a result in this paper.
