What the mirror covers

The proposed case covers Theorem 3.1’s committee construction and Theorem 4.1’s ranking construction; both already use the distributional population model. It leaves the paper’s PSC verification result and empirical evaluations outside these anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s locally stable committee problem. It is also a case where the paper already comes very close to the continuous model.

Take an LLM leaderboard with \(m\) models. A type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking of those models, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with that ranking. For a size-\(k\) committee \(W\), let \(q_\mu(a,W)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t w\ \forall w\in W}\mu_t\): the population share that prefers an excluded model \(a\) to every model in \(W\). The decision is to choose \(W\), and the natural objective is to minimize its stability ratio, \(k\max_{a\notin W}q_\mu(a,W)\). A \(\gamma\)-stable committee has ratio at most \(\gamma\).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large evaluation cohort for a fixed set of models, with users grouped by recurring task or use case and their resulting preference ranking. The type must include whatever context fixes those preferences; the paper itself suggests aggregating prompt and use-case factors into a latent type. In a deployment with many users but a manageable set of recurring preference types, population mass is a natural quantity: the question is what share of users is excluded from a useful lineup, not how many named users can be targeted individually. The assumption that preferences are stable within a type is the main modeling commitment.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.1 (the committee guarantee), proved in this paper, though it builds on earlier stable-lottery methods. It says that for any user distribution \(\mathcal D\) and committee size \(k\), the algorithm returns, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), a committee of size at most \(k\) that is \((16+O(\varepsilon))\)-approximately locally stable. It samples \(O(\operatorname{poly}(m,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta)))\) users and uses at most \(O\!\left(\frac{k}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{m}{\varepsilon\delta}\right)\) pairwise comparisons per user; for constant \(\varepsilon\), that is \(\widetilde O(k)\) comparisons per user. The corresponding continuous problem is: given \(m,k\) and a finite support of ranking types with binary-rational masses, output a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) whose stability ratio is at most \(16+O(\varepsilon)\), with failure probability at most \(\delta\). This is a direct question about representing the population by a committee, not about a fractional outcome.

A second anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. It says that if the committee-selection subroutine returns \(\gamma\)-approximately locally stable committees, the checkpoint construction returns a ranking whose every prefix is \(\frac{\lambda^2}{\lambda-1}\gamma\)-approximately locally stable when \(\lambda>1\) is an integer; as \(\lambda\searrow1\), its guarantee is \(O(\frac{1}{1-\lambda})\gamma\). The matching continuous problem is to output a ranking \(\pi\) minimizing \(\max_{1\le j<m} j\max_{a\notin W_j^\pi}q_\mu(a,W_j^\pi)\), where \(W_j^\pi\) is its top-\(j\) prefix. With \(\lambda=2\), the theorem multiplies the committee guarantee by four. Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1 therefore give a constant-factor approximate stable ranking as well as a committee.

Both mirrors look like Class A for approximate stability. For an explicit finite type list, the paper’s sampling and comparison procedure can be simulated by sampling types according to their rational masses and reading their rankings from the input. This supplies a randomized algorithmic route. A sharper bound in \(m,\tau,L\)—and a bit-complexity analysis for that explicit input representation—would be further questions; the theorem states oracle sample and query bounds, not that full accounting. Other natural questions are whether the approximation factor can be improved, how the guarantees change when the distribution is available only through pre-collected comparisons, and whether stronger representation notions such as PSC admit useful algorithms with this access.

The strongest point is that the population interpretation is faithful to the paper’s actual objective: its stability condition already bounds a *fraction* of users, and the authors explicitly work with a distribution over rankings. The weakest point is correspondingly serious: this paper already formulates its society as a distribution and proves results about population-level stability. These anchors cannot honestly be presented as discovering a new continuization, and the paper supplies no discrete hardness result whose difficulty might dissolve in a continuum. The defensible positive case is narrower: the paper’s sampling-oracle formulation motivates a distinct, support-explicit computational question in \(m,\tau,L\), and its theorems provide a strong algorithmic starting point. The committee mirror is the clearest instance of that case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is a direct collision: the paper has already made the population a distribution. Its model takes users from \(\mathcal D\), a distribution over complete rankings, and defines stability by the probability mass of users who prefer an excluded model to every committee member. For ranking types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), that is exactly \(q_\mu(a,W)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t W}\mu_t\). This is the continuous society in the programme’s sense, not a finite electorate awaiting continuization.

That defeats Theorem 3.1 as a *mirror*: its committee guarantee already holds for any user distribution \(\mathcal D\). It also defeats Theorem 4.1: the checkpoint construction takes that same distributional committee problem and transfers its guarantee to rankings. A plausible high-multiplicity story—many users in a large evaluation cohort sharing recurring preference types—fits the paper’s model exactly. Enriching types to include prompts or use cases does not create a distinct mirror either; the paper explicitly says such factors can be aggregated into latent types.

The proponent’s strongest refinement is to give the type masses as explicit binary rationals and ask for complexity in \(m,\tau,L\). That is a sensible way to study the paper’s problem under a different input-access model, but it does not supply the missing discrete-to-continuous step: the paper’s input is already \(\mathcal D\), and its algorithm samples from it. With an explicit finite support, one can sample types according to their masses and answer comparisons from their rankings, so the stated sample and query guarantees apply directly. Asking instead for exact optimization of the stability ratio would be a distinct computational question; it would not be a continuous mirror of either theorem.

This negative case is strong for the anchors raised, but it cannot establish that no related research is worthwhile. The paper does not provide a support-explicit bit-complexity classification, and exact optimization could merit study. The narrower conclusion is that these are extensions of a model already continuous at the population level, not continuizations of the paper’s results.
