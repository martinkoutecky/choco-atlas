The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s open-market equilibrium computation, in a high-volume market for interchangeable service providers. Take, for example, a large pool of standardized compute instances bidding for recurring jobs. A provider type \(t\) records all its persistent features that matter to the model: its cost law for jobs, idle cost, service duration, and entry or return conditions. The current realized job cost \(c\) is part of its decision state. Providers with the same \(t\) and \(c\) are indistinguishable. If there are \(N\) providers but only \(\tau\) such classes, mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction in each class; \(\tau\) stays small as \(N\) grows.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Fluid Open Procurement Equilibrium. An instance gives a finite set \(T\), initial provider masses \(\mu_t\), cost distributions \(F_t\), idle costs \(C_t\), a payment cap \(M\), entry and service-completion rules, and task demand \(q\) per period. Demand scales with the provider population: \(N\) providers face \(qN\) units of job workload. Each provider can take an integral job; the allocated workload and population are represented by mass. At each state \(x=(x_t)_{t\in T}\), providers observe their current cost and bid. The mechanism assigns workload \(q\) to the lowest bids and pays the winners the marginal losing bid, capped at \(M\), splitting ties proportionally. Assigned providers leave the available pool according to the service rule; idle providers incur their idle cost; new providers enter only when their expected net revenue is nonnegative.

The requested solution is a measurable bidding policy \(b_t(c,x)\), together with a consistent stationary mass flow and its average expense per unit of task workload. For an \(\varepsilon\)-solution, almost every positive-mass provider state must have no unilateral bid that improves expected net revenue by more than \(\varepsilon\); the entry and mass-flow conditions must also hold within \(\varepsilon\). This keeps the paper’s central computational question: finding self-interested providers’ equilibrium strategies in an open, changing market, so the manager can evaluate its expected expense. Scaling task supply with population is a co-modification forced by the population limit, not a move to continuize the outcome space as the goal.

My anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that Algorithm 2 terminates in finite time, and that if \(K\) is the equilibrium maximum number of participants, the resulting bid array stores the equilibrium bids to precision \(\rho\). In the paper, \(K\) ranges over the finite-agent open environment, bids are indexed by auction size and discrete task cost, and the algorithm builds on the paper’s revenue bounds and per-auction calculation. The continuous question above replaces counts by active mass and discrete cost points by cost distributions. It asks for the corresponding equilibrium policy and mass flow, not for an encoding used to compute them.

I expect the regular version of this problem to be tractable for approximation when \(T\) is fixed or small and the cost distributions have finite support or well-behaved, finitely represented densities. The paper’s own equilibrium divides costs into three regions: bid below the cap, bid at the cap, or wait for a later task. That threshold structure gives a plausible route from its finite cost equations to integral equations and a small number of boundary conditions. Theorem 4 supports the idea that the open-market equilibrium can be computed, but it does not establish polynomial time for the full continuous problem. Further questions include whether finite-market equilibria converge to the fluid \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium, whether atoms and ties affect existence or uniqueness, and how complexity grows with the number of provider types and cost-distribution breakpoints. This anchor concerns the equilibrium computation results; I am not claiming a mirror for the paper’s simulation comparison or its general MAS discussion.

The weakest point is fidelity to the single-task mechanism. A continuum batch auction uses a marginal losing bid and divides tied mass, while the paper’s reverse Vickrey auction awards one task to one agent and pays the lowest losing bid. The batch version is a natural high-multiplicity limit for recurring workloads, and it preserves the open entry, private costs, strategic bidding, future opportunities, and expense objective. But whether the authors would accept that limit as their problem’s continuous form depends on showing that its equilibrium incentives really arise from the scaled single-task model. The paper gives no hardness result to carry over or dissolve; the positive case rests on this plausible limit and the computational structure of Theorem 4.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a weak anchor for a continuous mirror. It proves finite termination and precision for Algorithm 2 when the maximum auction size \(K\) and the finite cost grid are given. It does not give a complexity classification for an input family. A continuous version would need a new input model and a representation for cost distributions and bidding policies; “compute the equilibrium” is a reasonable question, but the theorem does not itself supply a complexity result to continuize.

There is a more basic obstacle to a high-multiplicity limit with the paper’s fixed primitives. The paper derives that any \(K>2(M-c_{\min})/C-1\) guarantees negative expected revenue. Thus, for fixed \(M\), \(c_{\min}\), and positive \(C\), the open market’s equilibrium number of active agents is bounded independently of how many potential entrants exist. Normalizing an ever larger pool of potential providers therefore gives vanishing active mass. Keeping the original one-task-per-period mechanism does not produce a growing society of active agents.

Scaling task supply to \(qN\) jobs for \(N\) providers is the best repair, but it exposes a different problem. If those jobs are still sold through single-winner reverse Vickrey auctions, each provider faces a growing number of competitors. In the paper’s symmetric cost model, the gap between the lowest and second-lowest costs shrinks as the pool grows; with a positive mass at \(c_{\min}\), both are \(c_{\min}\) with probability approaching one. The continuation value in the paper’s bids matters, but it does not restore a substantial competitive margin over that value. With fixed positive idle cost \(C\), this gives no evident way to sustain positive mass under the paper’s rule that agents enter only when expected net revenue is positive. This is not a proof for every possible scaling, but it is a concrete failure of the natural one.

The proposed fluid auction avoids that problem by assigning workload to mass and paying a marginal losing bid, with ties split proportionally. That can be a sensible procurement model. But it changes the allocation and pricing process that drives Theorem 4: the paper computes bids across integer auction sizes in a stochastic open market, whereas the fluid problem computes a mass flow and a clearing price. A mean-field version built from many micro-auctions is a more faithful candidate, but with fixed primitives it runs into the bounded-\(K\) problem; rescuing it by scaling costs, idle charges, or entry incentives changes the economic regime. I would treat the fluid problem as a potentially worthwhile new model, not a continuous mirror of this result. The negative case is strongest against fidelity and the fixed-parameter limit; it cannot rule out the authors deciding that a scaled recurring-workload market is their problem.
