The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a budget \(1\le k\le |T|\), and an affinity table \(a_{tu}\in[0,1]\) promised to arise as expected normalized Kendall–Tau similarities under a joint distribution over rankings, choose exactly \(k\) representative types \(S\subseteq T\) maximizing \(\Psi_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_{s\in S}a_{ts}\). In the decision version, determine whether some \(S\) has \(\Psi_\mu(S)\ge B\), for a given rational threshold \(B\).

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution over complete preference and network-affinity types. Choose \(k\) representative types to maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t\max_{s\in S}a_{ts}\); assign each type’s mass to its most-similar selected representatives, splitting ties equally.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Proposition 5.1(b)’s max-sum representative-selection objective; it leaves the preference-spread models, aggregation-error guarantees, experiments, and the other thesis chapters aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact maximization remain NP-hard when the affinity table must be realizable by the chapter’s expected Kendall–Tau preference model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is in Chapter 5’s representative-selection problem. I would scope it to the thesis’s average-similarity objective, which is already a formal surrogate for choosing a small set of people whose reported preferences represent the wider population.

My anchor is Proposition 5.1(b), proved here. It says that, given a set of nodes \(N\), a similarity function \(c\), a budget \(k\), and a threshold \(\omega\), it is NP-hard to decide whether there is a set \(M\) of \(k\) nodes with \(\psi(M)\ge\omega\), where \(\psi(M)=\sum_{i\in N}c(M,i)\) and \(c(M,i)=\max_{j\in M}c(j,i)\). The proof reduces from Dominating Set: it assigns similarity \(0.9\) to adjacent vertices and \(0.6\) to nonadjacent vertices, so a size-\(k\) dominating set exists exactly when the threshold \(k+0.9(|N|-k)\) can be reached.

A plausible regime is a large recurring customer or public-opinion panel: millions of people, but far fewer preference and social-network strata. For example, a type could include a complete ranking of the alternatives and a social-network block with a fixed homophily profile. Members of the same type must be interchangeable for this problem: they have the same ranking and the same expected similarity to every other type. The mass \(\mu_t\) of a type is its fraction of the population. This is a scenario-specific high-multiplicity assumption, rather than a claim that every social network has few types.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, Mass-Weighted Representative Selection. An instance has finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a symmetric rational affinity table \(a_{tu}\in[0,1]\) with \(a_{tt}=1\), a representative budget \(1\le k\le |T|\), and a threshold \(B\). The affinity \(a_{tu}=1-d_{tu}\) is the expected normalized preference similarity of types \(t\) and \(u\), in the sense used by the thesis. Choose exactly \(k\) representative types \(S\subseteq T\) to maximize
\[ \Psi_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_{s\in S}a_{ts}. \]
The decision version asks whether some such \(S\) satisfies \(\Psi_\mu(S)\ge B\). A solution also gives represented masses \(x_{ts}\): type \(t\)’s mass is divided equally among its most-similar selected types, so \(x_{ts}=\mu_t/|\arg\max_{r\in S}a_{tr}|\) for a maximizing \(s\), and zero otherwise. Each selected representative’s reported ranking then receives weight \(\sum_t x_{ts}\), the continuous counterpart of the thesis’s weighted representative profile.

For exact optimization, I expect hardness to transfer (Class B) when the support size \(\tau=|T|\) is unbounded. Set \(\mu_t=1/n\) for each of the \(n\) types in the anchor reduction and use the same affinity table; then \(\Psi_\mu(S)=\psi(M)/n\), with threshold \(B=[k+0.9(n-k)]/n\). Each type can have an arbitrarily large number of identical members while keeping the same mass, so this hardness persists in a high-multiplicity population. The combinatorics live in the number of distinct types, not in the number of people. There is still useful algorithmic structure: the weighted sum of per-type maximum affinities is monotone submodular, so greedy selection gives a \(1-1/e\) approximation in polynomial time in \(k\), \(\tau\), and the affinity-table encoding length. Exact enumeration is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\).

This mirror covers the max-sum representativeness subproblem used by the thesis’s Greedy-sum method. It does not claim to continuize the full preference-spread model or guarantee the actual aggregate-preference error for every voting rule. The rankings stay discrete; the population distribution and the represented weights are what become continuous.

My weakest point is the affinity model. Proposition 5.1’s reduction uses an arbitrary similarity table, and it may not be realizable by the thesis’s particular network and preference-generation models. Also, exchangeable preference-and-network types may be a poor fit when named network positions matter. A narrower hardness theorem for affinities generated by the paper’s own models would therefore be an important next question. The mirror remains credible for the chapter’s abstracted sum-of-similarities problem because that is precisely the objective its reduction and representative-selection algorithm study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 5.1(b) does not establish hardness for the preference-derived similarity model in the proposed mirror. Its reduction assigns similarity \(0.9\) to graph edges and \(0.6\) to nonedges. Equivalently, the distances are \(0.1\) and \(0.4\). On an induced path \(u\!-\!v\!-\!w\), that gives \(d(u,w)=0.4>d(u,v)+d(v,w)=0.2\). But expected Kendall–Tau distance must satisfy the triangle inequality, since Kendall–Tau distance does and expectation preserves it. So the reduction’s affinity tables need not be realizable by the thesis’s preference model. The thesis proves hardness for an abstract similarity-table problem, not for its preference-based version.

The proponent’s mass-weighted formulation is a sensible repair: use types with rational masses, metric affinities derived from preferences, and select representative types to maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t\max_{s\in S}a_{ts}\). It also reflects the chapter’s weighted representative profiles. But that repair is a new computational question; the cited proposition does not settle its complexity. In a real network, making type membership complete enough to preserve representative quality may require each type to share not just a ranking, but its relevant social position and affinity profile. Many observed networks would then have almost no repeated types. Coarser blocks would make the continuum tractable to describe, but would discard network distinctions the thesis uses, including degree centrality.

That is the strongest negative case: the anchor’s reduction misses the natural preference geometry, and the faithful type model may have little multiplicity on the networks studied. It does not defeat the better mirror, though. A large panel with repeated ranking and network strata is plausible, and the authors’ use of affinity-based representative selection makes the mass-weighted question recognizable as their problem. I can’t honestly argue that no such scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. The case against is weak at that point: Proposition 5.1(b) needs a valid preference-geometry reduction, but Chapter 5 still appears to offer a credible continuous question.
