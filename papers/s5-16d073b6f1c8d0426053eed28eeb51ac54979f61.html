The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one. The paper has a natural continuous-population analogue, but it has no qualifying computational anchor: it proves no theorem stating membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic formulation. Its named results are structural and axiomatic. Thus, under the programme’s strict anchor rule, no fully compliant positive case exists. Still, the underlying aggregation problem is a credible mirror.

The natural regime is a large population of agents holding attitudes over a fixed finite agenda \(X\). A type is a complete rational attitude function on \(X\): for judgment aggregation, a truth-functionally coherent map \(a_t:X\to\{0,1\}\); for probability aggregation, a coherent map \(a_t:X\to[0,1]\); for preference aggregation, a complete strict ranking, equivalently encoded by its binary ranking propositions. A society is a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types. Thus \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with exactly the same attitudes on every proposition. A plausible regime is a large electorate, expert panel, or distributed fact-checking system with \(N\gg\tau\): many agents but relatively few recurring information or ideological profiles.

My lead anchor would be Theorem 1, proved in this paper, although its proof invokes earlier results by the authors. Under the paper’s assumptions—\(n\ge2\), a finite agenda \(X\) closed under negation, at least one contingent proposition, and a minimal inconsistent subset of at least three propositions—an aggregation rule satisfies universal domain, collective rationality, independence, and implication preservation if and only if it is linear:

\[ A(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_iA_i(p), \qquad w_i\ge0, \qquad \sum_iw_i=1. \]

For subjective probabilities, every choice of weights is valid. For binary judgments, only the dictatorial choices are valid: one weight is \(1\) and all others are \(0\). The paper further derives the analogous dictatorial conclusion for preferences by encoding pairwise rankings as binary propositions.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Linear Opinion Pooling. An instance consists of a finite agenda \(X\), a finite list of rational probability-attitude types \(a_1,\dots,a_\tau\), and rational population masses \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\). The task is to compute the collective attitude

\[ A_\mu(p)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t a_t(p) \]

for every \(p\in X\), and to output the resulting coherent probability assignment. The solution is the vector \(A_\mu\), represented with rational arithmetic.

This is a genuine population continuization: the society, not the propositions or outcomes, is continuous. If \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), the instance is exactly the high-multiplicity representation of a finite profile with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\). Theorem 1 predicts Class A behaviour: the computation takes polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(|X|\), and the encoding length. The interesting extension is to permit type-dependent weights \(w_t\), or to ask whether a desired collective probability assignment can be generated by a mass-based linear rule.

The binary case makes the mirror more revealing. A continuous judgment population would retain binary individual and collective attitudes, while replacing the named profile by \(\mu\). The finite theorem says that the four axioms force dictatorship. But an atomless mass distribution contains no distinguished individual whose attitude can serve as dictator. Consequently, an anonymous mass-only version of the theorem should have no feasible rule on a sufficiently rich agenda, unless the model explicitly permits a designated representative or a positive-mass atom. That would be a useful continuum boundary: probability pooling survives as a tractable mass operation, while judgment aggregation exposes the incompatibility between atomless anonymity and dictatorship.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in this paper with a self-contained proof. It considers valuation functions \(f:L\to V\) satisfying three conditions: logical entailment cannot decrease value; adjoining a proposition of minimal value to a disjunction does not change its value; and the valuation class has enough separating valuations to realize arbitrary values below strictly stronger propositions. If \(X\) is path-connected through conditional entailments, then every aggregation rule satisfying universal domain, collective rationality, independence, and consensus preservation is systematic. That is, there is one propositionwise decision criterion \(d:V^n\to V\) such that

\[ A(p)=d(A_1(p),\dots,A_n(p)) \]

for every proposition \(p\).

Its continuous mirror would be Continuous Systematic Rationality Checking. An instance consists of a path-connected finite agenda \(X\), a finite-support rational type distribution \(\mu\), and a computable local criterion \(d_\infty\) acting on finite-support distributions over \(V\). For each proposition,

\[ \nu_{\mu,p}=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{a_t(p)}, \qquad A_\mu(p)=d_\infty(\nu_{\mu,p}). \]

The task is to compute \(A_\mu\) and decide whether it extends to an admissible valuation function; if so, output such an extension. This is exactly the high-multiplicity form of the systematic rule forced by Theorem 2. With explicit agendas and efficiently evaluable \(d_\infty\), it is plausibly Class A. If the underlying logic is succinct, rationality checking can inherit hardness from satisfiability, giving a Class B phenomenon whose combinatorics live in the agenda rather than in population multiplicity.

The authors would recognise both mirrors: the type is precisely their complete attitude function, the mass vector is a normalized profile, and the collective object and axioms are unchanged. The weak point is substantial: the paper itself does not study computation, so these are new computational questions inspired by Theorems 1 and 2, not continuizations of computational results proved there. A strict referee should therefore record this as “natural continuous mirror, but no qualifying computational anchor,” rather than as a strong ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has nothing computational to continuize. The proponent’s concession is fatal under ChoCo’s anchor rule: Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are axiomatic classification results. The paper gives no computational problem, input encoding, objective, algorithm, complexity bound, hardness theorem, approximation guarantee, or parameterized result. The literature mentioned in the introduction is background, not a result proved here. Thus neither theorem can serve as a qualifying computational anchor.

Theorem 1 does support a coherent high-multiplicity interpretation. Large populations with repeated attitude profiles are entirely plausible, and I would not object to that regime. But the proposed “Continuous Linear Opinion Pooling” is only the evaluation of an already specified formula:

\[ A_\mu(p)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t a_t(p). \]

With explicit support, this takes \(O(\tau |X|)\) arithmetic operations. It introduces neither a combinatorial optimization problem nor a population-dependent computational phenomenon. Theorem 1 classifies aggregation rules globally over every profile; replacing the profile by its empirical distribution does not create a new instance problem corresponding to that classification. It merely supplies a compressed way of evaluating one selected rule.

The stronger versions do not repair this. The theorem allows arbitrary individual weights \(w_i\), whereas a distribution over attitude types records neither individual labels nor their weights. Two finite profiles can have the same type distribution but different designated dictators, and therefore different outputs. To preserve the theorem exactly, one must retain identity or attach weights to individuals; then the model is no longer a mass-only society. To discard identity, one must impose anonymity or type-level dictatorship, neither of which is among the theorem’s assumptions. The resulting impossibility for an atomless population would be a new axiomatic observation, not a computational result about a continuous society. The preference implication has the same defect: its dictatorial conclusion is identity-sensitive.

Theorem 2 is even less suitable as an anchor. Its conclusion is that a rule has a common propositionwise criterion \(d:V^n\to V\). A continuum has no canonical replacement for this criterion. Passing to a distribution of values implicitly imposes anonymity and symmetry, because the distribution forgets the order and identity of the \(n\) inputs. Those assumptions are not present in the theorem. If one retains identity, the population distribution is insufficient; if one forgets identity, one is studying a different class of rules.

The proposed \(d_\infty\) also comes from outside the paper. The paper does not specify how a decision criterion should act on probability measures over \(V\), how such a criterion is encoded, or how it is computed. With an explicitly supplied and efficiently evaluable \(d_\infty\), computing

\[ A_\mu(p)=d_\infty\!\left(\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{a_t(p)}\right) \]

is again merely proposition-by-proposition evaluation. If rationality checking or extension to a valuation function is hard, that hardness comes from the logic, agenda, or valuation semantics—not from continuizing the population. The same logical difficulty can already be present for a finite profile.

One can certainly invent worthwhile new problems: minimum-mass modification to restore collective rationality, bribery over attitude types, learning a systematic rule, or robustness of a collective valuation under mass perturbations. But those introduce the objective, costs, and algorithmic structure that the paper lacks. They would be new ChoCo problems inspired by the paper, not continuous mirrors of its named results. Calling them mirrors would hide the fact that the computational contribution has been supplied entirely by the later modeller.

This is not the mistaken objection that the paper is “already continuous”: continuous probability values, fuzzy truth values, and ranking functions do not continuize the population. Nor is existing high-multiplicity work a collision; it would support the plausibility of the regime. The narrower and stronger point is that, despite a sensible population interpretation, the paper’s actual contributions are axiomatic. Its natural mass versions are either trivial evaluations, identity-incomplete reformulations, or new optimization problems with no anchor in the paper.

So the honest verdict is: plausible high-multiplicity setting, but no worthwhile qualifying continuous mirror of this paper for the programme. The negative case is strong under ChoCo’s computational-anchor requirement, though not a claim that no later computational research could ever be built from the paper.
