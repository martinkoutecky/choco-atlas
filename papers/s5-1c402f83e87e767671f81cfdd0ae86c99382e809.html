The continuous mirror question

Given a finite cost-type set \(T\subseteq[0,1]\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), benefit \(v\), and quota \(\alpha<v\), consider a unit-mass society with mass \(\mu_t\) of members whose cost is \(t\). Assume the distribution satisfies the discrete counterpart of the paper's monotone-hazard condition. A mechanism maps reported types to binary activity recommendations, which are enforceable; there are no transfers, success requires active mass at least \(\alpha\), and truthful reporting must satisfy interim incentive compatibility and individual rationality. Find a mechanism maximizing expected per-capita utility \(v\Pr[\mathrm{success}]-\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t t\,a(t)\), where \(a(t)\) is a type-\(t\) member's activation probability.

The model it lives in

In the fixed-share case, select a report-independent subset of active mass \(\alpha\), proportionally within each type. Then \(a(t)=\alpha\), success is certain, and expected per-capita utility is \(v-\alpha\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t t\); each type's interim utility is \(v-\alpha t\ge0\).

The objection that survived

The finite lottery operates on sampled finite profiles, while the mirror fixes type masses and selects an exact mass of members; finite-sample composition fluctuations therefore fall outside its coverage.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2's fixed-share strong-organization benchmark with \(\alpha<v\); leaves the unorganized equilibria, VBO and general honest-and-obedient mechanisms, the \(n^{2/3}\) boundary, and endogenous organization outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the report-independent lottery remain optimal for every finite-support cost distribution without the discrete monotone-hazard condition?
  • What formal finite-to-continuum dictionary preserves interim incentive compatibility and individual rationality for exact-quota lotteries?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a positive case around one result, but it is narrow: Theorem 2, proved in this paper, gives a constructive optimum for the strongly organized benchmark. It says that if \(F\) satisfies MHRA and \(m_n\prec n\), then for all sufficiently large \(n\), the optimal mechanism subject to incentive compatibility and interim individual rationality selects exactly \(m_n\) members uniformly at random and succeeds with probability tending to \(1\). If \(m_n=\alpha n\) with \(\alpha<v\), that same lottery is optimal for every \(n\) and succeeds with probability \(1\). This is an explicit optimal mechanism, though the paper does not claim a complexity class such as P.

My lead mirror is Continuous Strong-Organization Threshold Provision. Imagine a large union or community group with many members but only \(\tau\) standardized participation-cost types—for example, members with the same shift or travel constraints—where \(N\gg\tau\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over costs \(T=\{c_1,\ldots,c_\tau\}\subseteq[0,1]\); each member receives benefit \(v\) if the group succeeds, and success requires active mass at least \(\alpha\). Given \((T,\mu,v,\alpha)\), find a randomized direct mechanism that maximizes expected per-capita surplus
\[ v\,\Pr[\text{active mass}\ge\alpha]-\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t t\,a(t), \]
where \(a(t)\) is the probability a type-\(t\) member is activated. The mechanism must be truthful and interim individually rational, with no transfers; as in Theorem 2, it may enforce its recommendations. A solution is the optimal mechanism and its value.

For a finite-support version satisfying the corresponding monotone-hazard condition and \(\alpha<v\), I expect Class A: the continuous counterpart of Theorem 2 is to activate exactly \(\alpha\) mass by a lottery independent of reported cost. Then \(a(t)=\alpha\), \(p(t)=1\), and each type’s interim utility is \(v-\alpha t\ge0\). The paper’s result is good evidence for this tractable case. Further questions are whether the exact optimum survives without the hazard condition, and what changes when obedience is required.

The regime is plausible for large organizations with repeated, standardized member categories, and the type is complete for this model because members with the same cost face the same benefit, quota, and mechanism. The mirror covers only the paper’s Section 3.2 benchmark. Its weakest point is also substantial: the paper’s main contribution concerns honest and obedient organizations. In a truly nonatomic population, one member has zero pivotal impact, so obedience can change sharply; Theorem 2 does not resolve that problem. I would not present it as a mirror of the paper’s headline HO results, or as a complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the wrong kind of anchor for ChoCo: it characterizes an optimal Bayesian mechanism, but states no complexity result for a computational problem. It gives no input encoding, running time, hardness claim, or algorithmic obstacle. The proposed mirror adds “find the optimal mechanism,” but in the regime it identifies, the mechanism is already explicit: activate each member with probability \(\alpha\), independently of reported cost. Its utility is \(v-\alpha t\). That describes an economic mechanism-design result, not a complexity landscape.

There is also a real change of model in replacing the paper’s \(n\) independent private costs with a fixed continuous society \(\mu\). The paper’s mechanism operates over random finite profiles; the continuum has fixed type masses and no sampling fluctuation. For the paper’s \(m_n\prec n\) case, the required share \(\alpha_n=m_n/n\) tends to zero, so the limiting quota is zero. The fixed-share case \(\alpha<v\) has a meaningful analogue, but its lottery is immediate and supplies no pricing or other computational problem for the programme to chart.

The more faithful candidate would continuize the paper’s honest-and-obedient organizations. There the atomless limit removes the very incentive the paper studies: one member’s action changes active mass by zero, so it cannot change the probability of success. A member with positive cost therefore has no reason to obey a recommendation to participate. For a positive quota, nontrivial obedient participation collapses; when the quota share tends to zero, the requirement itself vanishes. The paper’s pivotal probabilities, the \(n^{2/3}\) boundary, and the slow finite-\(n\) success tail all depend on binomial fluctuations that this limit removes. Retaining pivotality requires finite agents with positive influence, which gives up the continuous population.

A large group with standardized cost categories is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, so implausibility is not the objection. The negative case rests on scope: the one proposed anchor is not a computational result, while the paper’s central honest-and-obedient question degenerates in an atomless population. This case is not airtight if any tractable mechanism-design optimization counts as sufficient for ChoCo; its weakness is that Theorem 2’s lottery remains a sensible continuous model, even though it offers little computational content.
