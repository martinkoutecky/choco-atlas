The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper has a natural continuous-population analogue, but it has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. None of its numbered results classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally hard. Its theorems establish properties of a finite-state dynamical system. So I cannot present a fully compliant complexity mirror based on this paper.

The closest support is Theorem 5.2, proved here. For \(m=2\), it fixes two persistent nodes at opposite rankings \(\rho=(a_1\succ a_2)\) and \(\rho^\dagger=(a_2\succ a_1)\), and gives two free nodes \(i,j\) whose influence weights are \(1-\varepsilon\) on each other and \(\varepsilon\) on their respective persistent source, with \(0<\varepsilon<\tfrac12\). Under synchronous unbounded updates, the initial pair \((\rho,\rho^\dagger)\) alternates with \((\rho^\dagger,\rho)\), staying at least \((1-2\varepsilon)/2\) from the score-tie hyperplane. Under bounded updates, it instead alternates with \((\eta,\eta)\), where \(\eta=(a_1\sim a_2)\), and stays at least \(\min\{\varepsilon,1-2\varepsilon\}/2\) from that hyperplane. This is a structural existence theorem, not a computational classification.

A plausible population regime is a large deliberative organization with four cohorts: two free cohorts and two persistent camps. Each cohort member has the same influence profile as the other members of that cohort; there may be millions of people but only four network roles. A type is a role together with a current weak preference ordering. Its mass is the fraction of the population in that role and preference state. This keeps the population continuous while retaining the paper’s discrete preference space, Borda targets, persistent camps, and synchronous updates.

That gives a precise candidate computational question, Continuous Borda Cohort 2-Cycle. An instance consists of alternatives, rational role masses \(\alpha_r>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational row-stochastic influence kernel \(K\) over roles, fixed preference distributions for persistent roles, a deterministic shortest-path tie-breaking rule, and a rational margin \(\delta>0\). A society is a mass distribution \(\mu_{r,\omega}\) with \(\sum_\omega\mu_{r,\omega}=\alpha_r\). Role \(r\)’s aggregate score is
\[ S_r(\mu)=\sum_s K_{rs}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\frac{\mu_{s,\omega}}{\alpha_s}b(\omega). \]
Each free role moves its mass from \(\omega\) to the bounded-step successor toward \(T(S_r(\mu))\); persistent roles stay fixed. The question is whether there are two distinct rational societies \(\mu^0,\mu^1\) that map to each other and whose role scores at both states stay at least \(\delta\) from every tie hyperplane. A solution is the pair of mass distributions, with the update and margin conditions satisfied. Theorem 5.2 supplies a yes-instance: replace each of its four nodes with a positive-mass cohort and use the same role-level influence weights.

For that exact four-role, two-alternative family, the orbit is explicit and tractable to verify. I would leave the complexity of the general problem open: Theorem 5.2 gives no basis for expecting either an efficient general algorithm or hardness. It also does not establish the computational result the programme seeks; that would be a new question about the continuous model. Further questions include whether such cycles can be found or ruled out with a small number of roles, how the answer depends on the number of alternatives and support size, and whether the discrete network problem embeds into the role model.

The weakest point is that the paper’s contribution is dynamical and graph-structural. A cohort model requires a regular network regime where many agents share influence profiles; arbitrary individual networks may require nearly one role per agent and lose the high-multiplicity advantage. The mirror preserves the paper’s forced-oscillation question in a convincing large-population setting, but it does not turn the paper’s existing results into computational social choice results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 5.2 is not a computational result to mirror. It constructs a period-\(2\) orbit for a particular four-node network and proves a score margin along that orbit. The other named results likewise concern existence, periodicity, or robustness of trajectories; none classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. So the proposed “Continuous Borda Cohort 2-Cycle” is a new computational question inspired by the theorem, not a continuous version of a computational question the paper asks. That leaves this paper without the kind of anchor the programme is meant to chart.

The four-role example itself adds little computational content: each role has a single preference at each point of the orbit, so replacing each node by a cohort preserves the same finite-state construction. The cohort masses can change while the two-state orbit remains the same. To get a less degenerate continuous question, one can allow each role to contain a distribution of preferences and ask whether the resulting mass dynamics has a robust cycle. That is a coherent model, but it introduces heterogeneous role distributions and an orbit-existence problem absent from Theorem 5.2. It is a research direction suggested by the paper, rather than a result this paper already gives a computational mirror for.

The best version of that direction is still plausible: use a finite set of influence roles, give each role a fixed positive population mass, and represent its members’ preferences by a distribution over weak orders. Members of a role must share the same influence profile, including how their states affect other roles. In a large organization or platform with repeated communities and similar exposure patterns, that could describe a genuine high-multiplicity regime. The population can remain non-atomic in the limit, and score margins need not vanish. I cannot honestly rule out that someone could formulate a worthwhile complexity problem on this model.

The limitation is that this regime depends on strong network symmetry. For a general social graph, an agent’s influence position is part of their complete type; if those positions are mostly unique, the number of types grows with the population and the high-multiplicity compression disappears. The paper’s four-node construction does not establish that large repeated-role networks are central to its intended applications. A stronger population model could still use them, but its contribution would be a complexity theory for role-based network dynamics, not a computational analysis already latent in the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strongest on the fit to this programme’s anchor standard, and weak as a universal claim. The proposed cycle problem is a legitimate population-continuous computational question, and the theorem provides a clean example for it. But the paper supplies no named computational result to convert, and the exact example’s cohort lift preserves a small graph gadget rather than exposing a population-scale computational issue. That makes this a poor fit for the programme’s core agenda; it does not justify claiming that no plausible scenario could make a continuous mirror worthwhile.
