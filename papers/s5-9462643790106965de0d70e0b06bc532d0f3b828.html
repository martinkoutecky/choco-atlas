The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the required kind. Theorem 1 characterizes rules satisfying a list of axioms; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize when a particular rule satisfies consistency and independence of unallocated objects; Corollary 1 gives a related condition. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. They are not valid anchors for this brief.

There is a plausible population regime behind the subject: for example, a large allocation of identical objects among residents drawn from a small number of preference and valuation types. Mass could represent each type’s population share, and the aggregate assignment could record how much mass receives each integral quantity and pays each price. That is a reasonable high-multiplicity model of object allocation. But the paper studies rules over finite named agents, and its results concern axiomatic properties of those rules. Turning them into computational questions about a continuous population would be a new research direction, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a ChoCo mirror starts with the paper’s strongest candidates: Theorem 1 characterizes rules satisfying axioms; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize when a specified sequential dictatorship rule satisfies consistency and independence of unallocated objects; Corollary 1 gives a related priority condition. None poses a computational problem or makes a complexity claim. A continuous restatement of these results would therefore add no computational landscape to the paper.

The most promising extension would be to give each preference type positive mass and scale the object supply with population, then ask whether the characterization of sequential dictatorship survives. But the paper’s priority order is over named agents, and an agent’s position in that order affects their bundle. A distribution over preferences alone loses that information. Add priority position to the type, and in a large population those positions are typically all distinct: the type space no longer has the small support that makes high multiplicity useful. Compress agents into finitely many priority classes instead, and the mechanism becomes a new type-level rule.

That change also alters a central axiom. In an atomless population, one person has measure zero, so changing their reported preference cannot change an aggregate allocation. Individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Replacing it with incentives for a positive-mass cohort to misreport is a different property, and characterizing rules under it would be a new axiomatic project, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 1. With a fixed finite object supply, the problem is worse: only measure-zero agents can receive objects. Scaling supply per capita avoids that degeneration, but it does not restore individual priorities or nonvacuous individual incentives.

One could still ask how to compute allocations under a chosen continuum rule, or optimize some new objective over such rules. Those may be worthwhile questions in their own right. The paper gives no computational result to anchor them, though, and its priority and incentive structure means that making them well-posed requires substantial new modeling choices. The negative case is strongest within ChoCo’s remit; it does not establish that no adjacent research project could be useful.
