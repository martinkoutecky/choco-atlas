The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of strict rankings over \(m\) objects, rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and feasible rational quota shares \(\ell_j,u_j\), compute exactly the type-conditioned lottery \(p_{tj}\) produced by unit-demand MPS. Let \(x_{tj}=\mu_t p_{tj}\) and \(y_j=\sum_{t\in T}x_{tj}\). Starting from \(x=0\), each type consumes its most-preferred available object at aggregate rate \(\mu_t\); availability is governed by \(\sum_j x_{tj}\le\mu_t\), \(y_j\le u_j\), and \(\sum_j\max\{\ell_j,y_j\}\le1\). Return the endpoint probabilities \(p_{tj}=x_{tj}/\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

A finite distribution over ranking types with mass \(\mu_t\) per type and quota shares \(\ell_j,u_j\). The decision variables are aggregate assignment masses \(x_{tj}\); MPS event dynamics determine them, and the output is the type lottery \(p_{tj}=x_{tj}/\mu_t\). There is no optimization objective: the task is to compute the mechanism's endpoint.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 4 and the unit-demand MPS computation in Algorithm 1. It leaves the general-demand algorithm, the strategic properties under positive-mass group deviations, and construction of an implementation lottery outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a bit-complexity proof for exact event-time arithmetic in the mass algorithm, parameterized by \(m,\tau,L\).

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is the unit-demand, type-compressed MPS problem. The paper has no numbered theorem that classifies a problem as being in P or NP-hard. Its best qualifying anchor is the computationally used formulation in Proposition 4, proved here in Appendix A.1: for unit demand, the lower contour set \(lcs(\Delta D)\) consists exactly of the nonnegative partial allocations satisfying \(\sum_j\mu_{ij}\le 1\) for every agent \(i\), \(\mu_j\le c_j\) for every object \(j\), and \(\sum_j\max\{m_j,\mu_j\}\le N\), where \(\mu_j=\sum_i\mu_{ij}\). The paper uses this characterization to derive its finite-step MPS algorithm. Its claim that the unit-demand algorithm is polynomial-time appears in the text around Algorithm 1, not as a numbered complexity theorem.

A plausible regime is a large school district assigning a cohort of students to schools or programs. Each student gets one school; some schools have minimum viable enrollment as well as a capacity. Many students share the same strict ranking, so the number of ranking types \(\tau\) is much smaller than the number of students. A type is a complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of the cohort with that ranking. Normalize the lower and upper enrollment bounds to population shares \(\ell_j\) and \(u_j\). They scale with cohort size, as the programme permits when continuization requires per-capita supplies. Students still receive indivisible assignments; the continuum describes the population, while each type’s output is its members’ lottery over schools.

Here is the precise mirror I would give a prover: Continuous MPS computation. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of strict rankings over \(m\) objects, rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and feasible rational lower and upper quota shares \(\ell_j,u_j\). Compute the exact type-conditioned lottery \(p_{tj}\) produced by the mass version of MPS. Its aggregate assigned mass is \(x_{tj}=\mu_t p_{tj}\), with \(y_j=\sum_t x_{tj}\). During the eating process, the type-level version of Proposition 4’s partial-allocation conditions is \(\sum_j x_{tj}\le\mu_t\), \(y_j\le u_j\), and \(\sum_j\max\{\ell_j,y_j\}\le1\). Each type eats its most-preferred available object at unit speed per person, so its aggregate consumption rate is \(\mu_t\); availability is determined by those inequalities. A valid answer is the exact endpoint lottery, with each type’s probabilities summing to \(1\) and the aggregate allocation implementable while meeting every quota.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The paper’s MPS procedure has at most \(2m+2\) event steps. For a type-compressed instance, the rate at which an object is eaten is just the sum of the masses of types currently eating it. Those rates, and the event times calculated from them, are rational. This gives a direct exact algorithm with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). It is the same mechanism computation with population counts replaced by type masses, not a relaxation that removes the minimums or changes the agents’ assignments. When masses and quotas are rational, scaling by a common denominator also gives a finite market with matching proportions, so the bridge to high multiplicity is concrete.

This mirror covers the paper’s unit-demand characterization and MPS construction. It does not cover the general-demand case, for which the paper leaves an algorithmic description open, or the separate task of producing a finite-support implementation lottery. It also raises a strategic question: the paper’s weak strategyproofness result concerns an individual deviation, whereas a continuum model needs to specify whether the deviator is negligible or a positive-mass type group.

The weak point is that this is not a hardness-dissolution example. The paper already gives a polynomial-time algorithm for the finite unit-demand case, so the continuous version mainly offers type-based compression and a natural large-market formulation. Proposition 4 is a formulation anchor, not a numbered P-result, and the paper supplies no named hardness anchor. That limits the mirror’s value for the programme’s complexity trichotomy, but the population-continuous problem itself is a faithful and plausible one for the authors to recognize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious computational anchor is Proposition 4 together with Algorithm 1. The paper gives no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time or hard, but it does characterize the unit-demand lower contour set and use that characterization to compute MPS in at most \(2m+2\) steps. A type-compressed continuous version is easy to state: give each ranking type a rational mass, scale the minimum and capacity quotas to population shares, and compute the lottery for each type.

The strongest case against that mirror is that it introduces almost no new computation. For rational masses and quota shares, choose a common denominator \(D\), replace each mass \(\mu_t\) by \(D\mu_t\), and scale each quota share by \(D\). This yields a finite instance with the same MPS event times: each type’s aggregate eating rate, and every quota, has simply been multiplied by \(D\). Conversely, grouping the finite instance’s agents by ranking recovers the mass description. The grouped algorithm needs only \(O(\tau m)\) output entries and the paper’s \(O(m)\) event steps. The continuous computation is therefore an exact compression of the paper’s already polynomial unit-demand computation.

That makes this a weak addition to a programme aimed at discovering where continuization changes the computational landscape. There is no hardness to dissolve, no pricing problem newly exposed, and no difficult optimization question left open for this case. The incentive result also offers little extra value in an atomless model: an individual deviation has zero effect on the population distribution, while a positive-mass group deviation is a different question from the one Theorem 1 proves. The paper leaves general demand without an algorithmic description, but that does not rescue the unit-demand anchor or supply a stronger one.

Still, these points make a case for low priority, not for rejecting the mirror. A large school cohort with many repeated rankings is a convincing high-multiplicity regime; type mass is meaningful, and quota shares are a natural co-modification. The fact that the finite algorithm transfers directly supplies the answer to a well-posed continuous question. Under the programme’s stated standard, that answer does not make the question worthless.

So the honest negative case is weak: Proposition 4’s mass mirror survives. The best argument against it is that it may be too direct and already settled to deserve much programme time. I cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
