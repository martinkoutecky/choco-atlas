The case FOR (proponent)

This is a strong *setting* for a continuous-population study, but the paper has no named result that classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. Its equilibrium-seeking procedure is Algorithm 1, an unnumbered numerical method; the paper gives no complexity or convergence guarantee for it.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved here: under Assumption 1 (continuity of the state-transition function in the social state) and Assumption 2 (karma is preserved in expectation across the population), for every average karma \(\bar{k}\in\mathbb{N}\), there exists a stationary Nash equilibrium \((d,\pi)\) with \(d\in D^{\bar{k}}\). This establishes existence, not the complexity of finding an equilibrium, so it is not a qualifying computational anchor under the requested standard.

The most credible follow-up question would be Stationary Equilibrium Computation for Karma Populations. In a natural regime, the agents are a large pool of ride-hailing users repeatedly matched in pairs, with relatively few behavioral types. A type specifies the agent’s urgency transition process and discount factor; mass \(g_\tau\) is the fraction of the population of type \(\tau\). The state distribution \(d_\tau[u,k]\) gives the fraction of that type at urgency \(u\) and karma \(k\). Each agent chooses a randomized bid policy \(\pi_\tau[b\mid u,k]\), with \(0\le b\le k\), to maximize expected discounted reward: zero when served and \(-u\) when yielding. Given rational type proportions, urgency transitions, discounts, a karma payment rule, and average karma \(\bar{k}\), the problem is to find a stationary distribution and policies satisfying both population stationarity and each type’s best-response conditions.

That is recognizably the authors’ problem, and Theorem 1 guarantees that a solution exists under its stated assumptions. But the paper already makes the population continuous and formulates this dynamic population game. So this is a computational follow-up to an existing continuous model, not a new continuous mirror of a finite-population problem. Also, karma ranges over \(\mathbb{N}\), leaving the exact problem with infinitely many state variables; the paper does not provide a finite representation or an error guarantee that would settle its complexity. A capped-karma version could give a finite-state variant, but it would modify the model.

My positive case is therefore limited: the scenario and continuous population model are convincing, and equilibrium computation is a natural open computational question. The weak point is decisive for the requested anchor-based argument: the paper supplies no named complexity result to mirror, and its main model is already continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named result offered as an anchor is Theorem 1: for each average karma \(\bar{k}\), a stationary Nash equilibrium exists under the two stated assumptions. That is an existence theorem, not a complexity result. Algorithm 1 and the simulations show that the authors compute equilibria numerically, but they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no named computational result here for a continuous mirror to reproduce.

The proposed equilibrium-computation question is the strongest version of the anchor, and the population regime behind it is entirely credible: a large pool of anonymous users, grouped by urgency process and discount factor, with rational type masses. But Section 3 already models precisely that continuum population, through \(g_\tau\), \(d_\tau[u,k]\), and policies \(\pi_\tau[b\mid u,k]\). Theorem 1 is proved in that model, and Algorithm 1 seeks equilibria in it. Recasting the question as “compute an equilibrium of the continuous population game” leaves the population unchanged. It is a possible complexity project on the paper’s existing model, not a new mirror made by continuization.

A finite-\(N\) version, followed by a limit or approximation result, is the best alternative framing. It starts from a finite population and asks how its equilibria relate to the mass model. That could be useful, but it is a finite-to-continuum convergence question; the paper has already supplied the continuum model and an existence result for it. Capping karma could make computation more manageable, but that would refine the algorithmic study of the same continuous game, not create a distinct population mirror.

The negative case is strong against the proposed anchor as a *continuization*, but weaker against the broader claim that equilibrium computation is worthwhile research. An approximate-equilibrium complexity problem could be well posed and recognized by the authors. I cannot honestly argue that it is unworthy; the narrower point is that its value would come from analyzing a model that is already continuous, not from continuizing this paper.
