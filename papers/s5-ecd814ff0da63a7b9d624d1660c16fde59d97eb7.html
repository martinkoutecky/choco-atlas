The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case has a clear limit: this paper has no numbered computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. Theorem 1, proved here, says WF is utilitarian-optimal, envy-free, and group-strategyproof. Theorem 2, proved here, says QP is utilitarian-optimal, envy-free, and strategyproof. Proposition 9, proved here, gives profiles where QP’s egalitarian overlap welfare is \(n/2\) times WF’s; Theorem 3, also proved here, bounds WF’s welfare by twice QP’s for every profile. These are mechanism-property and welfare results, not complexity classifications. The paper says in unnumbered prose that both mechanisms can be implemented in polynomial time, but that does not supply the required named anchor.

There is nevertheless a plausible population-continuous question in the authors’ setting. Imagine many cities with repeated resource needs, drawn from a small set of target-distribution types. Give type \(t\) mass \(\mu_t\), ideal distribution \(p_t\in\Delta(M)\), and per-capita allocation \(x_t\in\Delta(M)\); let resource supplies be \(s_j\), with \(\sum_j s_j=1\), and require \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=s_j\) for each resource \(j\). One could extend WF and QP to this population and ask whether the factor-two welfare guarantee of Theorem 3, or the separation exhibited in Proposition 9, persists. The regime is credible—the number of cities can be large while the number of distinct need profiles is small—and the paper itself motivates allocation across cities.

That would continuize the population, not merely its already-fractional outcomes. But it would extend welfare guarantees, not mirror a named computational result. So under the requested anchor rule, I cannot present it as a compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no numbered computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 establish mechanism properties; Proposition 9 and Theorem 3 compare welfare. The proposed continuous questions ask whether those welfare claims survive a population limit. They may be sensible questions, but they are about welfare guarantees, not the computational landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

There is a credible population model here. For example, represent many cities by types \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), target distribution \(p_t\), and per-capita resource supplies \(s_j\). This is a genuine population continuization; the fact that assignments are already fractional does not count against it. But computing the mechanisms in this model appears to reduce directly to their existing constructions: weighted water filling solves \(\sum_t \mu_t\min\{p_{tj},\theta_j\}=s_j\), while the QP uses \(\sum_t\mu_t\lVert x_t\rVert_2^2\) over the corresponding type-level constraints. That gives a formulation with \(m\tau\) variables, rather than a new complexity problem. The paper already says both mechanisms are polynomial-time computable.

Theorem 3’s factor-two comparison could have a legitimate type-mass extension. That is the best version of the proposed mirror, but it remains a welfare theorem. The \(\Omega(n)\) separation in Proposition 9 is less directly a population-limit result: its witness combines a rare type of mass \(1/n\) with a growing number of objects. With a fixed set of resource types and positive limiting type masses, that particular separation does not carry over as stated. A fixed-mass variant could still be worth studying, but it would not supply a computational anchor. Strategyproofness also needs care in an atomless model, where a single agent has zero mass; coalition deviations by positive-mass groups would be a different formulation.

So I would reject these as worthwhile ChoCo mirrors: the proposed extensions are coherent, but they do not open the programme’s computational questions, and the natural computation seems to be a straightforward type-compressed implementation. I cannot honestly defend the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario. A population-level welfare study may be worthwhile on its own; the negative case is strong only against treating it as the kind of mirror this programme seeks.
