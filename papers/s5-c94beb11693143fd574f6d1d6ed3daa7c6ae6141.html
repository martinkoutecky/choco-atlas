The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational income distribution \(\mu\) with a unique mass median \(q\), mean income \(\bar y\), \(q<\bar y\), and required per-capita revenue \(\rho\le\bar y\), compute a TAL parameter \(\theta^*\in[0,1]\) such that no other \(\theta\) gives strictly higher post-tax income to mass greater than \(1/2\). For each \(\theta\), define the TAL tax schedule by the paper’s capped or leveled form, choosing its balancing constant so that \(\int R^\theta(y)\,d\mu(y)=\rho\).

The model it lives in

Income levels are types with mass \(\mu_y\); aggregate income and required revenue are measured per unit mass. A TAL parameter \(\theta\) determines each type’s tax, the schedule balances to revenue \(\rho\), and voters compare methods by post-tax income.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is tractable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classified by computational complexity.

The tempting anchors are Theorem 1 (no majority-rule equilibrium among all piece-wise linear tax methods), Theorem 2 (an equilibrium exists within the TAL family), and Theorem 3 (a formula identifying that equilibrium). Theorem 4 gives the corresponding political equilibrium. These are equilibrium existence and characterization results, not computational results; treating them as complexity anchors would stretch the requested standard.

A population mirror is imaginable: take income levels as types, let \(\mu_y\) be each type’s population share, and choose a tax schedule whose taxes integrate to a fixed per-capita revenue. But that would create a new computational question rather than mirror a named computational result in this paper. Also, the paper’s piece-wise linear schedules and TAL family make the policy space continuous; its modeled population remains finite. The paper even mentions continuum-agent taxation models, but as neighboring literature, not as the model analyzed here.

So the honest positive case stops short of a prover-ready continuous computational problem. The paper may support a continuous-population analogue of its equilibrium analysis, but it supplies no qualifying anchor for the requested complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right to stop at the absence of a computational anchor. Theorem 1 proves that a majority-rule equilibrium need not exist; Theorem 2 proves existence within the TAL family; Theorems 3 and 4 characterize the equilibrium by the median taxpayer, first for direct voting and then for party competition. These are equilibrium results, not complexity classifications. Changing the population to a distribution of income types cannot change what those results ask.

There is a natural continuous population model here. Let income be the type, let \(\mu\) give each income type its population share, and express required revenue per capita. Majority preference can then be defined by the measure of voters who prefer each tax method. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; neither the anonymity of income types nor the move from a finite median to a mass median is a fundamental objection.

But that model still does not supply a computational result to mirror. One could ask for an algorithm that finds a TAL equilibrium from a finite-support rational distribution; the paper’s median characterization points directly to that task. Or one could ask whether a proposed schedule has a majority-improving alternative. The latter would need a specified representation of schedules and a bounded or otherwise encoded policy space. Those could be legitimate new problems, but they add computational questions the paper does not pose. Theorem 1’s impossibility proof is not itself a complexity result about finding a defeating schedule.

The political-equilibrium result and the revenue-targeting corollaries do not provide a stronger anchor: they characterize equilibria and which policies can be supported, rather than classify the computation of an operation. Adding strategic lobbying, endogenous revenue choice, or a search objective might produce worthwhile computational research, but it would be a new model, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result here.

So the negative case is strong within ChoCo’s stated scope: this paper offers no computational result for a continuous-population mirror to carry forward. It does not establish the broader claim that no worthwhile computational project could be built from tax voting; that claim would go beyond what the paper and its results support.
