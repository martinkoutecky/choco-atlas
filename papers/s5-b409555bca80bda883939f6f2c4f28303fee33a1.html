What the mirror covers

Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 and the convex-decomposition example in Section 5 concern structural properties of rules; none supplies a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper has a natural *population* to continuize: residents with single-peaked rankings choosing the location of a public facility on a tree-shaped road or rail network. A type is a complete admissible ranking; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents of type \(t\). A credible high-multiplicity regime has many residents and relatively few repeated rankings. The rule still chooses a lottery over locations. That lottery is part of the paper’s model; the continuization would be the distribution of residents, not the outcome.

The paper’s strongest foothold is Theorem 4.1, proved here. It says that on a rich single-peaked domain, an RSCF is unanimous and strategy-proof if and only if it is tree-monotonic. In the paper’s characterization, tree-monotonicity is expressed through lotteries indexed by assignments of agents to leaves: assigning everyone to the same leaf puts probability one on that leaf; changing one agent’s assigned leaf makes probability weakly increase along the path toward the new leaf and leaves probabilities off that path unchanged. Theorem 3.1, also proved here, establishes the related uncompromisingness result: changing one agent’s reported peak cannot change probabilities off the path between the old and new peaks. These are structural characterization results, not computational ones.

That distinction sets the limit on the positive case. The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: it states no algorithmic upper bound, hardness result, or parameterized-complexity result. Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 characterize mechanisms; they do not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no eligible anchor, no lead anchor, and no honest way to supply a continuous problem *per anchor* under the requested criteria.

There is also a genuine modeling question: a single agent has zero mass in a continuum, so an ordinary unilateral report change leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. To retain strategic content, a mirror would need to say how an agent’s report affects the population distribution—perhaps through a limit of finite anonymous populations, or through a carefully defined infinitesimal incentive condition. Treating a positive-mass cohort’s coordinated report change as ordinary strategy-proofness would change the paper’s individual incentive condition.

So the positive case is real but limited: the public-facility scenario makes a continuous population plausible, and the paper’s tree structure gives a strong mathematical basis for exploring such a model. It does not, however, supply the computational anchor that ChoCo asks analysts to mirror. The weakest point is that any precise continuation would have to introduce new computational and incentive questions rather than continuize a computational result already in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own strongest candidates, Theorems 4.1 and 3.1, are not computational results. Theorem 4.1 characterizes unanimous, strategy-proof rules; Theorem 3.1 proves an implication between axioms and a structural property. Neither gives an algorithm, complexity bound, or computational problem whose complexity could be mirrored. That is the strongest objection: this paper offers no eligible computational anchor.

The public-facility setting does make a continuous population plausible. Residents can share rankings over a finite tree, and their society can be represented by rational masses on preference types. So “there is no meaningful multiplicity” would be a poor argument. But the best version of this mirror would still need to change what the theorems ask. Theorem 4.1 would become a characterization of rules on mass distributions; Theorem 3.1 would ask how outcomes respond when mass moves between peak types.

That raises a basic incentive mismatch. In an atomless population, one resident has zero mass, so changing their report leaves the distribution unchanged. To make incentives bite, one could require that no positive-mass cohort gain by changing its report, or impose a directional condition under small mass transfers. Either is a coherent extension, but neither is the paper’s unilateral strategy-proofness condition. It asks about coalitions or about the limiting response to perturbations. Keeping finite agents preserves the original incentive condition, but then the proposed continuous object is no longer the population limit.

Aggregation also narrows the result. The paper does not assume anonymity: agents are labeled, and its tree-monotonic lotteries are indexed by assignments from those agents to leaves. A mass distribution over rankings would describe an anonymous version. That may be a sensible model, but it is a restriction, not a faithful continuization of the full characterization.

Even after making those choices, the result would remain axiomatic rather than computational. The paper specifies no objective for choosing among the many unanimous, strategy-proof rules. A question such as “compute the rule’s outcome” needs a particular rule and an encoding; “find the best rule” needs a new welfare objective. Those additions could produce a worthwhile computational project, but the computational question would come from them, not from either theorem. Likewise, continuing the paper’s convex-decomposition question would be a structural question about rules, not a computational mirror.

The weakness in this negative case is clear: the paper’s setting is unusually amenable to a continuous-population model, and a new computational mechanism-design problem could be built around it. But the case against a mirror of this paper is strong. The natural continuization either loses individual incentives or changes them, and neither theorem supplies a computational task to carry across.
