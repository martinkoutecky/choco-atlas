The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of terminal types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), positive fairness weights \(w_t\), and a succinct source model providing the fractional coalition-cost function \(b_\mu(y)\) for \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\), find the unique \(q\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t q_t^2/w_t\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t q_t=b_\mu(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t y_tq_t\le b_\mu(y)\) for every fractional coalition mass vector \(y\).

The model it lives in

A weighted minimum-norm allocation over a continuous source-rate core: types carry masses \(\mu_t\), \(q_t\) is the per-terminal rate for type \(t\), and \(b_\mu(y)\) bounds the rate available to a coalition with mass vector \(y\). The computational bottleneck is separation over fractional coalition masses for a succinctly represented source model.

The objection that survived

The proposed source model is not specified beyond supplying \(b_\mu\), and the opponent argues that ordinary fixed-alphabet exchangeable sources may yield a linear normalized coalition function and a singleton core; the proponent does not answer that collapse argument.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the fractional egalitarian rate-allocation problem and its optimal-rate core; it leaves the Shapley-value approximation, distributed decomposition results, and complete network-coding construction aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which succinct type-symmetric source representations admit efficient exact separation over fractional coalition masses?
  • Do natural high-multiplicity source families produce a nontrivial continuous core, and what is the bit complexity of optimizing over it?
  • How does the continuous optimum relate to finite high-multiplicity instances through rounding or packet splitting?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a tractable mirror of the paper’s fractional egalitarian rate-allocation result. There is no named hardness result in the paper, and no numbered theorem that literally classifies the problem as being in P. The best named computational anchor is Theorem 10, proved here: for CCDE, the steepest descent algorithm generates a sequence converging to the minimizer of
\[ \min\left\{\sum_{i\in V}\frac{r_i^2}{w_i}: r_V\in R^*(V)\cap \mathbb{Q}^{|V|}_K\right\}, \qquad K=|P^*|-1, \]
where \(R^*(V)\) is the minimum-sum-rate optimal region, \(P^*\) is the fundamental partition, and \(\mathbb{Q}_K\) is the rate grid used for packet splitting. The theorem establishes convergence; the surrounding Section V-C gives the bound \(O(KL(V)|V|\operatorname{SFM}(|V|))\), with \(L(V)\) the stated \(\ell_1\)-size of the core. That is a real algorithmic anchor, though the complexity bound is not itself stated in Theorem 10.

My lead mirror is Type-Continuum Egalitarian Omniscience. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of terminal types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational fairness weights \(w_t\), and a type-symmetric source model. A type records everything the allocation problem uses: a terminal’s source-statistical role, communication conditions, and fairness weight. The source model supplies \(b_\mu(y)\), the analogue of the paper’s \(\hat f_{R^*}(X)=H(X\mid U)\): the maximum coding-rate share available to a coalition containing mass \(y_t\) of each type \(t\), for \(y\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\). It also supplies the minimum total rate \(R_\mu^*=b_\mu(\mu)\).

The decision variable is \(q_t\), the rate assigned per terminal of type \(t\). The feasible optimal-rate core is
\[ \mathcal C_\mu= \left\{q\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{T}: \sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t=R_\mu^*,\ \sum_{t\in T}y_tq_t\le b_\mu(y)\ \text{for every }y\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t] \right\}. \]
The question is to return the unique vector \(q\in\mathcal C_\mu\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\frac{\mu_t q_t^2}{w_t}, \]
together with a feasibility and optimality certificate. This asks for a fair allocation of the minimum communication rate across the society; it does not ask to encode an LP or another formulation.

A plausible regime is a large sensor or mobile-client deployment with many terminals but only a small number \(\tau=|T|\) of repeated source-observation and communication roles. For example, thousands of sensors might fall into a few exchangeable observation and radio-quality profiles. Each profile needs to be complete for the problem: terminals in it must be indistinguishable under the source model and the allocation costs. The continuum mass is the fraction of the deployment in each profile; the rate \(q_t\) is per terminal. Here \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\) stays modest.

This is recognizably the authors’ rate-allocation problem. It preserves omniscience, the minimum total coding rate, the optimal-rate core, and the paper’s weighted fairness objective. Replacing individual terminals by type masses and allowing fractional coalition mass is a co-modification needed to express a continuum population. It also fits the paper’s interest in wireless sensor networks and fractional rates. The paper’s “asymptotic model” alone is not a population continuum: its asymptotic limit concerns observation-sequence length, while the user set remains finite.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, subject to a substantive separation question. With finite support and an efficient exact separator for the core inequalities—minimizing \(b_\mu(y)-\sum_t y_tq_t\) over the fractional-mass box—the objective is strictly convex and the feasible set is convex, putting the problem within continuous optimization. The useful research question is whether the source’s submodularity and continuous Dilworth-truncation structure provide that separator efficiently from a succinct source model. If they do, the natural complexity parameters are \(\tau\), the rational input bit length \(L\), and the cost of the source oracle, rather than the number of terminals \(N\). The paper’s finite-population runtime does not by itself prove that this continuous separator exists.

This case covers the egalitarian rate-allocation result and its optimal-region structure. It does not claim to mirror the Shapley-value approximation, a complete network-coding scheme, or every result in the paper. It generates further questions: which source representations admit polynomial-time separation; how the exact continuous optimum relates to the finite high-multiplicity problem; and whether rounding or packet-splitting gives an additive approximation with bounds in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

The weakest point is the type-symmetric source model. Real deployments may have idiosyncratic source dependencies or communication costs, causing the number of complete types to grow with \(N\); then the compact-support advantage disappears. Also, Theorem 10 concerns a finite-user rate grid, and its stated complexity involves \(L(V)\), so it does not settle bit-complexity for the type-continuum problem. Still, the paper’s focus on fractional rate allocations makes this a more faithful mirror than one that changes the underlying task to divisible outcomes or a continuously varying source sequence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is a real computational anchor: it proves convergence of an algorithm for a fractional egalitarian rate allocation, and Section V-C gives its running-time bound. I cannot dismiss the mirror on the ground that the paper has no algorithmic result. The strongest case against is that the proposed type model does not yet say how a continuous population of terminals generates the source constraints the algorithm needs.

In communication for omniscience, a terminal’s type must capture its dependence on every relevant coalition, not just its marginal source statistics, radio quality, and fairness weight. For a generic source, terminals have different dependence neighborhoods, so complete types may be nearly unique and \(\tau\) may grow with the population. That defeats the fixed-support advantage. The proposed function \(b_\mu(y)\) could supply the missing information, but without a source model and access to that function it is only a placeholder for the hard part of the problem.

Requiring repeated types gives a cleaner repair: specify a growing family of source models that is exactly exchangeable within each type, and derive \(b_\mu\) from that family. Yet in the ordinary fixed-alphabet exchangeable setting, the extensive part of entropy tends to be linear in the number of observations; global shared information and other finite-size effects disappear under per-capita normalization. If the resulting profile is \(b_\mu(y)=\sum_t a_t y_t\), the core constraints force \(q_t\le a_t\), while the total-rate equality forces \(q_t=a_t\) for every type of positive mass. The egalitarian optimization then has no freedom left to resolve. A rich nonlinear profile would require persistent extensive interactions. Those often require observations whose size grows with the population, or relational structure that type labels alone do not preserve. That becomes a substantially new source model, with its own representation and separation problem.

These points defeat the proposed formulation as stated, but they do not rule out the better mirror. A carefully specified relational source ensemble could have a nontrivial continuous core and an interesting separation problem. The paper’s focus on fractional rates and wireless sensors makes that scenario plausible, and the authors could reasonably recognize it as their problem. I know of no basis here for claiming that the continuous computational question is already answered. So the negative case is that natural finite-alphabet models may collapse while richer models require substantial new modeling; it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists.
