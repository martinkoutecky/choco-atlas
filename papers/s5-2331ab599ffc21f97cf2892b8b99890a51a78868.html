The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one focused case: the paper’s approximate market equilibrium problem has a direct continuous population mirror.

Let a type \(t\) specify both a per-person endowment vector \(e_t\) and a linear utility vector \(v_t\). Those must both be part of the type: two traders with different endowments can have different budgets at the same prices. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The supply of good \(j\) is \(A_j=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tj}\), so supplies scale with population mass.

A plausible regime is a large exchange for standardized resource entitlements, such as water or energy credits. Many households or firms belong to the same contract or technology class, giving them the same endowment bundle and linear per-unit valuations. The population might number in the hundreds of thousands or millions, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct classes is in the dozens or hundreds. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that all markets have few types.

My lead problem is Continuum-Type \(\varepsilon\)-Approximate Market Equilibrium. Given \(m\) goods, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-person endowments \(e_{tj}\), positive rational utilities \(v_{tj}\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), find positive prices \(p_j\) and aggregate allocations \(z_{tj}\ge 0\). A solution must clear every good,
\[ \sum_t z_{tj}=A_j, \]
and, for each type \(t\), keep its total spending within the paper’s budget tolerance,
\[ \sum_j p_jz_{tj}\le (1+\varepsilon)\mu_t\sum_j p_je_{tj}. \]
Writing \(\alpha_t=\max_j v_{tj}/p_j\), every good allocated to that type must be approximately as attractive per unit of money as its best available good:
\[ z_{tj}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_t p_j\le (1+\varepsilon)v_{tj}. \]
Each member of type \(t\) receives \(z_{tj}/\mu_t\) units of good \(j\). Thus \(z\) describes allocations to a mass of identical traders, not a divisible population of individually different people.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: algorithm main2 terminates in
\[ \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(nm^2+mn^2)\log(v_{\max}/v_{\min})\right) \]
steps. Its output guarantee is supported by Lemma 12, also proved here: the algorithm returns exact market clearing, the paper’s \((1+\varepsilon)\) budget bound, and aggregate equality between total spending and total endowment value. The approximate utility-per-price condition follows from the algorithm’s maintained complementary-slackness condition in Lemma 3.

The mirror preserves the paper’s market and asks for the same prices, market clearing, budgets, and utility-maximizing assignments. The aggregation is exact: replace every trader in a type by one aggregate trader with endowment \(\mu_t e_t\) and utility vector \(v_t\). Since goods are divisible and members of a type are identical, any aggregate allocation can be shared equally among them. Applying Theorem 2 to these \(\tau\) aggregate traders gives the corresponding step bound with \(n\) replaced by \(\tau\):
\[ \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\tau m^2+m\tau^2)\log(v_{\max}/v_{\min})\right). \]
The theorem states a step bound; a full binary bit-complexity analysis would need to account explicitly for the encoding length of the rational input.

I expect this problem to be tractable. It is a type-compressed version of the paper’s own problem, and the auction algorithm already works with the aggregate trader’s budget and allocation. The continuous population is the central change; goods were already divisible in the paper. I would limit the mirror to the positive-utility approximate equilibrium computed by main2, with the paper’s perturbation discussion for zero utilities treated separately.

The weakest point is that the paper supplies an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result: its discrete approximate problem is already polynomial-time solvable. This mirror therefore illustrates support-size compression and an algorithm for a continuous population, but it does not show discrete hardness disappearing. That limits its value as a hardness-to-tractability case study; it does not undermine the model correspondence. A natural next question is whether exact equilibrium, rather than this approximate guarantee, admits an algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational input length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror is already an instance of the paper’s market model in different coordinates. For each supported type \(t\), make one trader with utility vector \(v_t\) and endowment \(a_{tj}=\mu_t e_{tj}\). Since goods are divisible and budgets and utilities are linear, this is exact: aggregate identical traders’ endowments and allocations, or split the representative trader’s allocation equally among the type’s members. Market clearing, budget feasibility, and utility maximization correspond in both directions.

That covers the proponent’s anchor. Theorem 2 applies to those \(\tau\) traders, and Lemma 12 gives the same guarantees, with \(n=\tau\). The proposed large cohorts make the encoding especially natural; adding distinctions to the complete type merely increases \(\tau\). So the paper’s existing algorithm already accepts the continuous society’s aggregate input. The mirror adds no distinct computational model or pricing problem to classify.

The suggested exact-equilibrium question has the same aggregation: it becomes exact equilibrium for a finite market with \(\tau\) representative traders. That may be a worthwhile market-equilibrium problem, but it is not a new question created by continuizing the population. An infinite type space could escape this encoding, but it would require a new input representation and would no longer be the finite-support rational society anchored by this paper.

This negative case depends on treating the paper’s trader model as already covering the continuous aggregate market, even though the authors do not formulate it as a population distribution. If “already done continuously” requires an explicit atomless-population formulation, the objection weakens substantially. The proposed standardized-cohort mirror is plausible, and the case against it is therefore weak under that stricter reading.
