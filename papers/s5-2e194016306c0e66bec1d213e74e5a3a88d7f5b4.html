The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive kernel is a plausible high-multiplicity hiring regime: large annual cohorts of Ph.D. applicants and positions, with many applicants sharing complete preference types and many universities sharing hiring-priority types, so the number of agents greatly exceeds the number of types.

An unanchored research question would give those types rational masses \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), let a planner choose \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), and have each applicant type retain the top \(\alpha\) fraction of university mass in its ranking. Run deferred acceptance on the resulting mass flows, then minimize total mass-weighted match inefficiency, including a specified penalty for unmatched mass. The population is the continuous object; the type-level rankings, per-capita supplies, truncation decision, and matching objective preserve the paper’s central trade-off. This looks like a plausible extension of the authors’ problem, but it is a newly formulated computational question.

Under the required anchor rule, though, the paper has no qualifying named computational result. Its closest formal result is Theorem 1, proved here: if \(p^\star\) minimizes expected aggregate graduate inefficiency under simultaneous top-\(p\) truncation, then \(p^\star\) cannot exceed \(\tilde p\), the symmetric equilibrium truncation level discussed by Coles and Shorrer (2013). That is a comparative claim about truncation levels, not an algorithmic or complexity result. The simulation findings are not named complexity claims either. So there is no valid per-anchor mirror or A/B/C forecast to offer; the mass-matching question is only a research lead.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is also the narrowest: there is no named computational result here to anchor a complexity mirror. Theorem 1 compares the optimal truncation level \(p^\star\) with the symmetric equilibrium level \(\tilde p\); it says nothing about an algorithm or its complexity. The paper’s central findings come from simulations under a particular preference generator, with selected correlation and inefficiency parameters. So the proponent is right that this theorem is not a qualifying anchor.

That does not defeat the better mirror they propose. Give applicant and university types complete preference descriptions, rational masses, and per-capita capacities; run a type-level deferred-acceptance process under a truncation policy; then compute mass-weighted inefficiency. Individual identity need not matter if people of a type are interchangeable, and the paper’s rank-based objective can be aggregated over type flows. One can retain the paper’s top-\(p\) policy for finite type lists or define a cutoff by cumulative mass when the counterpart population is continuous. Choosing the cutoff to optimize aggregate inefficiency is a computational question about a continuous society, even though the paper itself did not ask it in those terms.

The best remaining objection is that this model is substantially cleaner than the paper’s setting. Its simulated preferences come from individual score vectors, so exact complete types could be numerous; the type-level version replaces that heterogeneity with shared rankings and makes the matching dynamics deterministic. But that is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, not a fundamental mismatch. Nor does a potentially simple algorithm for finite support disqualify the question: a tractable answer is still an answer the programme could chart.

So the negative case succeeds against the claim that this paper already contains a computational theorem to mirror. It does not support the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed type-level planner problem survives; my case against it is weak.
