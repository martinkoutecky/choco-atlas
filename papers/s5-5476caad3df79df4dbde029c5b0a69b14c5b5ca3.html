The continuous mirror question

Given rational group masses \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2=1\), a finite-support rational distribution \(\nu\) over utility-preference pairs \((u,\sigma)\), a bias factor \(\beta\in(0,1]\), and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_\ell\) with \(\kappa=\sum_\ell\kappa_\ell\le1\), compute the stable mass allocation \(x_{g,u,\sigma,\ell}\). Group \(g\)'s type mass is \(\lambda_g\nu_{u,\sigma}\), its observed utility is \(u\) for \(g=1\) and \(\beta u\) for \(g=2\), and its capacity at institution \(\ell\) is \(\lambda_g\kappa_\ell\); process types by observed utility, using a shared tie rule and splitting mass at capacity boundaries. Compute the utility ratio against the maximum true utility obtainable from total mass \(\kappa\), and the ratios of the groups’ per-capita selection rates and first-choice rates.

The model it lives in

A finite rational mass distribution over complete types \((g,u,\hat u,\sigma)\), with group-conditional masses given by the same distribution over \((u,\sigma)\). The decision variables \(x_{g,u,\sigma,\ell}\) allocate type mass to institutions subject to group-specific per-capita capacities \(\lambda_g\kappa_\ell\); the objective measures selected true utility against the best total mass-\(\kappa\) selection. Shared normalized types and proportional capacities yield \(R=P=U=1\).

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper in Section 7.3. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or parameterized-hard; this is an algorithmic performance theorem, not a complexity-class theorem. It is still a credible anchor because it proves guarantees for an explicit allocation algorithm.

In the paper’s finite model, candidates’ utilities and preference lists are drawn independently from common distributions, group 2’s observed utility is \(\hat u=\beta u\), and group 1’s is \(\hat u=u\). Theorem 4.2 says: if each group has at least \(\eta_1 n\) candidates, total capacity \(K\ge\eta_2n\), and every institution has capacity \(k_\ell\ge\eta_3K\), then Algorithm 3, \(A_{\text{inst-wise}}\), guarantees
\[ P(A_{\text{inst-wise}})\ge 1-O\!\left(\frac{p\sqrt{\log K}}{\eta_1\eta_3\sqrt K}\right),\qquad U(A_{\text{inst-wise}})\ge 1-O\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{\eta_2n}}\right),\qquad R(A_{\text{inst-wise}})=1. \]
Here \(R\) compares the groups’ selection rates, \(P\) compares their rates of receiving a first choice, and \(U\) is the expected selected true utility divided by the true utility of the top \(K\) candidates. The algorithm runs the stable assignment procedure separately for each group, giving group \(g\) a fraction \(|G_g|/n\) of every institution’s capacity. The theorem applies for arbitrary common utility and preference distributions under the paper’s i.i.d. model; the guarantee does not require knowing \(\beta\).

A concrete mirror is Continuum Institution-Wise Stable Selection. Its instance is a finite rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(g,u,\hat u,\sigma)\), where \(g\in\{1,2\}\), \(u\) is true utility, \(\hat u\) is the observed score, and \(\sigma\) is a ranking of the \(p\) institutions. Let \(\lambda_g\) be group \(g\)’s mass, with \(\mu_g=\lambda_g\nu\) for a common conditional distribution \(\nu\) over utility and preferences. Institution \(\ell\) has per-capita capacity \(\kappa_\ell\), with \(\kappa=\sum_\ell\kappa_\ell\le1\). The decision is a mass allocation \(x_{t\ell}\): how much of each type is assigned to each institution. It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t\ell}=\lambda_g\kappa_\ell,\qquad \sum_\ell x_{t\ell}\le\mu_t. \]
Require the allocation to be stable within each group under the common priority order given by observed utility: process types from highest priority to lowest, assigning their mass to their most-preferred institution with remaining group capacity, splitting mass when a capacity boundary cuts through a type. A fixed tie rule completes the definition. The question is to compute this allocation and its utility and fairness ratios, with utility measured against the maximum true utility obtainable by selecting mass \(\kappa\), and group fairness measured by their per-capita selection and first-choice rates.

This is a faithful high-multiplicity regime for a large annual admissions cohort: many applicants share an exact score, group label, and preference ranking, so \(n\) can be in the hundreds of thousands while the supported type count \(\tau\) is much smaller. That is a scenario, not a claim about the support of the paper’s JEE data. The group-wise capacity \(\lambda_g\kappa_\ell\) is a necessary co-modification: seats, like the population, are measured as mass.

The mirror is tractable (Class A). Sort the \(\tau\) types by observed utility and apply the stated greedy assignment, scanning each ranking as needed; this takes \(O(\tau\log\tau+\tau p)\) arithmetic operations. This running-time observation is for the finite-support mirror, not a bound stated in Theorem 4.2. In the continuous model the theorem’s conclusions sharpen to exact identities: \(R=P=U=1\). Each group has the same normalized type distribution and the same normalized capacities. Since \(\beta>0\) preserves utility order within each group, both groups select the same top \(\kappa\) fraction of \(\nu\) and make the same normalized assignments. Their first-choice rates therefore agree, and the combined selected mass is the globally optimal mass \(\kappa\) by true utility.

The mirror covers Theorem 4.2’s institution-wise algorithm and its utility and fairness guarantees. It does not cover Theorem 4.1’s unconstrained bias bounds, the empirical comparisons, or the paper’s future case where institutions have different utilities for candidates. Natural next questions include what guarantees survive when groups have different preference distributions, how utility and preference fairness trade off as institution-wise quotas are relaxed, and what happens to stability when institutions no longer share a common priority order.

My weakest point is that this is close to a law-of-large-numbers limit of the paper’s own model: Theorem 4.2 already gives finite-population bounds that converge to these guarantees. The mirror is a distinct mass-allocation input, not a new hardness problem, and the finite-support running-time result is simple. Its positive case rests on the authors already making institution-wise proportional capacity central to their question; a referee looking specifically for a new complexity landscape could reasonably judge that contribution modest.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor does not quite match ChoCo’s computational target. Theorem 4.2 bounds the expected fairness and utility of a specified algorithm on finite random populations. It does not classify the complexity of an allocation problem or analyze an algorithm’s running time. The paper already gives the allocation procedure: sort candidates by observed utility, then assign each to their highest available preference. That is a performance theorem about this procedure, not a complexity result waiting to be continuized.

The strongest mass version is coherent. Give each group the same conditional distribution over true utility and preferences, scale its institution capacities by its population mass, and split mass at priority cutoffs. But under those assumptions the groups have identical normalized allocations: \(\beta>0\) preserves each group’s utility order, and both groups receive the same proportional capacities. Thus \(R=P=U=1\) follows from the construction. Computing the allocation from finite support amounts to sorting the \(\tau\) types and scanning their preference lists. That is a useful implementation description, but it does not uncover a new computational problem or a complexity landscape.

The reason is that the continuum version removes the part of Theorem 4.2 that needs proof: fluctuations from sampling finite, independently drawn groups. The theorem’s error bounds quantify those fluctuations as the population grows. In a mass model with exactly matching group distributions, those fluctuations are absent. If the mirror retains finite-sample uncertainty, it is back to the paper’s stochastic finite-population analysis; if it allows different group distributions, it becomes a broader performance question that the theorem does not pose. Neither route yields a new computational task anchored by this result.

I would not argue that the population regime is implausible. Large admissions cohorts can have many applicants per complete type, and proportional capacity is a natural co-modification. Nor does existing high-multiplicity work count against the mirror. The negative case is narrower: this theorem’s contribution is a finite-sample performance guarantee, while its best continuous counterpart is principally a deterministic limit of that guarantee. The paper does not supply a named complexity result for that counterpart.

So the case against this anchor is meaningful but not airtight. A fluid-limit formulation could still interest the authors, especially as a clean account of what their finite bounds converge to. I am less convinced that it merits ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme: the mirror either inherits the paper’s sampling question or makes the allocation’s normalized behavior exact by assumption, without opening a distinct algorithmic question.
