The case FOR (proponent)

On the paper’s own terms, there is no qualifying anchor. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. Its closest computational statement, in Section 2, is unnumbered and explicitly cited from elsewhere: for every \(p>0\), deciding whether a size-\(k\) committee has \(p\)-HUV score at least a given threshold is NP-hard. The paper also says that \(0\)-HUV is solved by selecting the \(k\) resources with greatest total utility, and that the greedy algorithm achieves a \(1-1/e\) approximation, citing standard submodular-optimization work. None of these is a result proved and numbered in this paper. Thus, strictly speaking, the paper supplies no admissible anchor and cannot support the requested positive case under the stated rules.

If unnumbered computational claims are admitted, the strongest mirror is the following.

Lead candidate: Continuous \(p\)-HUV Winner Determination. An instance consists of a finite resource set \(R\), committee size \(k\), a fixed \(p>0\), and voter types \(t\in[\tau]\). Type \(t\) has a complete utility vector \(u_t:R\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), and mass \(\mu_t\ge0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a committee \(S\subseteq R\) of size \(k\), sort its utilities for type \(t\) as

\[ u_{t,(1)}(S)\ge\cdots\ge u_{t,(k)}(S). \]

Define

\[ \operatorname{HUV}_{p,\mu}(S) = \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t \sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{-p}u_{t,(j)}(S). \]

The task is either to output a size-\(k\) committee maximizing this score, or, in decision form, determine whether some committee has score at least a rational threshold \(B\).

This is exactly the paper’s underlying HUV committee problem with repeated users aggregated by type. Mass is the fraction of the local user population with the same complete utility profile; the action remains selecting resources, and the objective is the population-average HUV score. If one wants to retain the paper’s TF-IDF construction, the utility vectors can be generated from approval types by replacing approval counts with approval masses; the common factor corresponding to the total population is irrelevant to the winning committee.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a streaming or catalogue platform with millions of users but a few dozen or few hundred recurring audience profiles—say, standardized cohorts with the same relevant approval and utility patterns over the local catalogue. This is stronger than claiming that the MovieLens users themselves have few exact types, which is doubtful. The authors should nevertheless recognize the mirror: their Section 2 utility election and HUV objective are unchanged, and aggregating identical users merely divides the finite objective by the population size.

The expected classification is mixed. For \(p=0\), the continuous problem is plainly tractable: compute each resource’s aggregate utility and choose the \(k\) largest. For \(p>0\), hardness is likely to transfer in the unrestricted formulation. A discrete election is recovered by giving each voter type mass \(1/n\), so the NP-hard HUV winner problem embeds directly. The combinatorics live in selecting resources and committees, not in population multiplicity. However, this does not establish hardness when \(\tau\ll n\); aggregation might make some restricted high-multiplicity regimes tractable. That is precisely the worthwhile follow-up question.

The main further questions are whether \(p\)-HUV winner determination is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\) or \(k\), whether the \(1-1/e\) greedy guarantee survives verbatim for mass profiles, and whether the cited NP-hardness reductions can be compressed to few distinct types.

The weakest point is decisive: this is a good continuous analogue of the paper’s underlying optimization problem, but not a mirror anchored in a numbered result of this paper. The paper is primarily a prototype and empirical study; its computational claims are background citations rather than its own formal complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own first paragraph supplies the decisive objection. This paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its NP-hardness statement for \(p\)-HUV is an unnumbered background claim cited from earlier work; the greedy guarantee is likewise imported from standard submodular optimization. The paper’s actual contributions are a prototype, experiments, visualizations, and heuristic comparisons. Thus there is no computational result of this paper to mirror.

Even granting the unnumbered claim, the proposed mirror is less substantial than it appears. If \(n_t\) users share a complete utility vector \(u_t\), then for any committee \(S\),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_p(u_i(S)) = n\sum_t \frac{n_t}{n}\lambda_p(u_t(S)). \]

Replacing repeated users by masses therefore performs exact row compression and normalization. The paper already uses users only through an additive objective; it never tracks their identities, imposes individual constraints, or assigns them different outcomes. The committee remains the same integral size-\(k\) subset of resources. Nothing in the population axis becomes a new continuous optimization object.

The best plausible scenario—millions of users drawn from a small number of standardized taste profiles—is perfectly coherent, but it creates a weighted \(p\)-HUV winner-determination problem, not a computational mirror of a result proved in this paper. For the actual TF–IDF construction one would also need global type masses and query-conditioned local masses, after which the HUV computation still sees only a finite weighted utility matrix. A genuinely continuous distribution of taste vectors would require an additional representation or integration model, making it a new recommender problem rather than a population-only continuization.

This conclusion does not rely on what the continuous answer would be, on hardness transferring, or on high-multiplicity work being prior art. The issue is that the paper never studies a population-sensitive computational problem in the first place. A \(\tau\)-parameterized study of weighted HUV could be worthwhile independently, but it is not a qualifying mirror of this paper.

The universal modelling claim is not literally airtight: a platform with repeated audience personas gives a sensible high-multiplicity version. The negative case is nevertheless decisive under the stated anchor rule, and strong enough substantively to reject this paper as a worthwhile target for the continuization programme.
