The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-backed positive case under your criteria: this paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification.

The closest candidates are Theorem 5.7, which proves that the paper’s \(\lambda\)-rule is Condorcet consistent, monotone, and \(2\)-NM\(_{11}\) for every tournament size; Theorem 4.4, which proves those first two properties and \(3.5\)-multiplicative pairwise non-manipulability for its other rule; and Theorem A.5, which proves top-cycle consistency, monotonicity, and \(5\)-multiplicative pairwise non-manipulability. These are constructive axiomatic guarantees, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The paper’s quadratic implementation claim appears in unnumbered prose, so it does not qualify as an anchor either.

There is a possible limit-object intuition: in a large league, team types could represent recurring team archetypes, mass could be their share of the league, and a tournament could be represented by a type-level match-outcome kernel. A rule would output a probability density over types. But the resulting question—whether such a rule satisfies continuum versions of fairness and manipulation axioms—is an axiomatic rule-design problem, not the computational question the programme is seeking. In an atomless model, changing one team’s match changes the kernel on a measure-zero set, making the paper’s pairwise manipulation condition vacuous. Giving a whole type positive-mass power to manipulate would change the problem.

So the strongest honest positive observation is that the rules’ score formulas suggest a possible continuum extension. It does not yield a valid continuous computational problem anchored in this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that none of the three named results is a computational result. Theorem 5.7 gives an axiomatic guarantee for the \(\lambda\)-rule; Theorem 4.4 gives one for the geometric-score rule; Theorem A.5 gives one for the top-cycle rule. Each says what a rule guarantees on every finite tournament, not how hard it is to compute an answer to a problem. The paper even says its proposed rules run in quadratic time. Its main open question—whether a rule can satisfy \(2\)-NM\(_1\)—is a question about existence of a rule, so a continuous version would be axiomatic rule design, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The strongest population-based alternative is to model many voters by a distribution over preference types and derive a majority tournament from that distribution. But the paper takes the tournament itself as input: its rules discard the voter profile, and the manipulations concern teams changing the outcome of their match. A continuous voter electorate therefore supplies context for the tournament without continuizing the object these results study.

One could instead make teams a continuum and represent match outcomes by a kernel, then ask for continuous versions of all three theorems. That fails as a computational mirror across Theorems 5.7, 4.4, and A.5 for the same reason: changing one pairwise match affects a null set in an atomless model, so the pairwise manipulation condition becomes unobservable if inputs identify kernels up to null sets. Giving positive mass to a type and letting it change matches on behalf of its members restores a nonzero effect, but changes a two-team manipulation into a bloc manipulation. And if the finite-step kernel is encoded by \(\tau\) team types, the rules’ score calculations reduce to sums over that encoding; the paper offers no hard computational task for the continuous formulation to expose.

The negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with this programme, not as proof that no interesting continuous tournament theory can be developed. A graphon-based axiomatic study might be worthwhile on its own. The material here gives little reason to count it as a continuous computational mirror of these results.
