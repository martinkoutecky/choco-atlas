What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror targets continuous fair-map optimization, but does not cover Theorem 1’s bound on the number of plans represented by the sample tree; it also leaves the paper’s ensemble experiments and cited hardness results without a mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has one numbered theorem: Theorem 1, proved here, bounds the number \(P(B,k)\) of distinct \(k\)-district plans admitted by a sample tree of constant width \(w\): for split sizes in \([2,z]\),
\[ w^{(k-1)/(z-1)} \le P(B,k) \le w^{k-1}. \]
That is a combinatorial counting result about the authors’ generated ensemble, not a complexity classification of the underlying districting problem. The paper’s statement that optimal political districting is NP-hard for useful objectives is unnumbered and cites other work. So, under the requested anchor rule, there is no qualifying named computational result to anchor a mirror on. I would not present Theorem 1 as a hardness or tractability result.

The strongest mirror I would still propose is continuous fair districting. Let a state be a polygonal region \(\Omega\), divided in the input into \(\tau\) polygonal cells with rational population densities and partisan-affiliation data. Residents are a nonatomic population distributed over those cells: a type records location and all electoral attributes used by the model, and its mass is the fraction of the statewide population there. In a setting with millions of residents but hundreds or thousands of such cells, \(\tau\) is much smaller than the number of people.

Given \(k\), a population tolerance \(\epsilon\), a compactness limit \(K\), and the paper’s affiliation model, ask for a partition of \(\Omega\) into \(k\) connected polygonal districts \(D_1,\ldots,D_k\). Require each district to have mass between \((1-\epsilon)/k\) and \((1+\epsilon)/k\), and satisfy the chosen compactness limit. The objective is to minimize
\[ \left|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} q(D_i)-h(\hat{\nu})\right|, \]
where \(q(D_i)\) is the model’s probability that district \(D_i\) elects the party, \(\hat{\nu}\) is the statewide vote share, and \(h\) is the desired vote-to-seat mapping (for the paper’s efficiency-gap choice, \(h(\hat{\nu})=2\hat{\nu}-\tfrac12\)). A decision version asks whether a feasible plan with objective at most \(B\) exists. A finite encoding can require districts to have at most \(q\) rational-coordinate vertices.

This targets the paper’s underlying question—choosing a population-balanced, contiguous map to optimize expected electoral fairness—and leaves the number of districts finite. My forecast is hardness likely transfers: contiguity and geographic partitioning appear to carry the hard combinatorics, rather than the number of individual residents. That is a conjecture for this continuous problem, though; a reduction must show that allowing boundaries to split cells preserves the relevant hardness.

The authors would likely recognize the fairness objective and district constraints as their problem. The weak point is the spatial relaxation: their method assigns whole geographic blocks, while this mirror lets district boundaries divide cells. That may be a sensible continuous-map model, but it changes a consequential feature of their implementation. This candidate covers fairness-optimized political districting; it does not mirror Theorem 1’s sample-tree counting claim or the paper’s ensemble-generation results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proponent has no named computational result from this paper to continuize. Theorem 1 counts the plans admitted by the authors’ sample tree; it says nothing about the complexity of finding fair district plans. The NP-hardness claim is in the introduction and cites other work. That makes the proposed decision problem a reasonable extension of the paper’s subject, but not a mirror of a computational result the paper establishes.

The proponent’s fairness model is the strongest candidate anyway. A faithful finite-block version would treat each geographic block as a type, give it mass \(p_i/P\), and retain its location and partisan data. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and individual voter identity is not needed. But with whole blocks assigned to districts, the population mass only normalizes coefficients: the computational object remains the same graph-partition problem the paper already studies. The sample-tree theorem still counts combinations of blocks. If blocks may be split, the question acquires a continuous geometric decision space; bounding polygon vertices makes it finite, but the complexity then depends on that geometric encoding as well as on population mass. This is a substantial modeling choice, not a consequence the paper’s theorem addresses.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror outright. The paper explicitly allows different atomic geographic units, and a state with many residents represented by a smaller set of spatial cells is a plausible continuous-population instance. A mass-splittable version of its fairness optimization is recognizable as the same underlying districting question. The supplied paper gives no continuous treatment that already answers it, and vanishing single-voter effects are beside the point.

So the negative case is weak on the universal claim. It can deny the proponent a paper-level complexity theorem to mirror and question whether normalizing tract populations creates a distinct computational problem. It cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror of the fair-districting optimization itself.
