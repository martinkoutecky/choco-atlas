The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item categories, finite supports \(T_X,T_{SM}\) of XOS and single-minded types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and private type valuations \(v_t\), find an \(\alpha\)-approximation to \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_x\sum_{t\in T_X\cup T_{SM}}\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), subject to \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\); characterize communication complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), especially whether exact optimization is polynomial or the Theorem 1.4 approximation threshold remains hard for \(\alpha=1/(1-(1-1/|T_X|)^{|T_X|})+0.001\), with \(|T_X|\ge2\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support continuous market of complete bidder types, each with rational population mass and a type representative holding its valuation. The decision assigns mass \(x_{t,S}\) to each type's integral bundle \(S\), subject to type-mass and per-capita item-supply constraints, maximizing total welfare.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.4's \(\mathrm{XOS}\cup\mathrm{SM}\) welfare result; it leaves the subadditive results, other separation results, and truthful-mechanism motivation unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass configuration LP be solved exactly with communication and bit complexity polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) using the paper's valuation-query model?
  • For markets formed by replicating each type and item category \(K\) times, what is the approximation rate between the integral finite market and its mass-allocation limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is Theorem 1.4, proved in this paper. It says that with \(n\ge2\) XOS bidders and \(c\) single-minded bidders, any \(\bigl(1/(1-(1-1/n)^n)+0.001\bigr)\)-approximation to welfare maximization for \(\XOSSM\) requires \(2^{\Omega(\min\{c,\sqrt m\})}\) communication. This is a communication lower bound for finding an allocation, not a claim of NP-hardness. The paper proves it here, building on its strong inapproximability results.

A concrete mirror is XOS–Single-Minded Mass Welfare. The instance has \(m\) discrete item categories and a finite support \(T=T_X\sqcup T_{SM}\) of complete bidder types. Each type \(t\) has a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a valuation \(v_t\): XOS for \(t\in T_X\), single-minded for \(t\in T_{SM}\). Each item category \(j\) has a rational supply \(b_j\), measured per unit of population. The type representatives privately know their valuations, as bidders do in the paper’s communication model.

A solution is a sparse list of pairs \((t,S)\) and masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), where every member of mass assigned to \((t,S)\) receives the whole bundle \(S\subseteq M\). The constraints are \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for each type \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\) for every item \(j\). Its welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). The task \(\textsc{XOS-SM-MassWelfare}_\alpha\) is to output a feasible solution whose expected welfare is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha\), measuring communication among the type representatives. A basic feasible solution needs at most \(m+|T|\) positive bundle variables, so it has a polynomial-size output representation.

The high-multiplicity regime is concrete: take \(K\) copies of each type and \(K\) copies of every item category in a pooled, repeated procurement or advertising market. With \(r\) XOS types and \(c\) single-minded types, the population has \(N=K(r+c)\) agents and support size \(\tau=r+c\); each type has mass \(1/(r+c)\), as does the supply of each item category. As \(K\) grows, many indistinguishable agents share each complete valuation type. For the theorem’s scale, one can take \(r=2\) and \(c=\Theta(\sqrt m)\). The bundles remain integral; only the fraction of each type assigned to each bundle is continuous. Scaling supplies with the population is part of this mirror.

For this model, I expect the theorem’s hardness to dissolve: the mass problem should be tractable, plausibly exactly solvable with polynomial communication in \(m,\tau,L\), under the paper’s demand-query convention and polynomial-bit rational inputs. Its welfare LP has exponentially many bundle variables, but pricing a bundle for type \(t\) amounts to a demand query maximizing \(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\). The single-minded types answer directly; XOS types use the demand-query machinery the paper already invokes for its configuration LP. The continuous allocation can divide a type cohort among bundles, so it does not need the rounding step that converts fractional choices into one bundle per named bidder. The continuous question is whether that LP can be solved with the stated communication bound and how its solution behaves as a finite replicated market approaches it.

The authors should recognize this as their welfare problem: it keeps their item-disjoint allocation constraint, welfare objective, and XOS and single-minded valuation classes. It changes the allocation variable to match a population with repeated complete types. This case covers Theorem 1.4’s \(\XOSSM\) welfare-maximization result. It does not purport to mirror the paper’s truthful-mechanism motivation or its subadditive results.

The weak point is that Theorem 1.4’s lower bound depends on private inputs and integral allocations, while the mirror has one valuation representative per type and allows type mass to split across integral bundles. Also, the hard family has \(c\) distinct single-minded types, so \(\tau\) grows with \(m\); the regime has many agents per type, but not fixed support. Those are substantive differences, and they are exactly why hardness transfer should not be assumed. They do not make the mirror artificial: deciding whether the hard allocation barrier disappears under repeated identical types is a meaningful complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t think the negative case defeats the sole anchor. Theorem 1.4 is a named communication lower bound for welfare maximization, and there is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for it: repeat each complete valuation type many times and scale item supplies with population. The fact that the single-minded bidders have distinct demands makes support grow with \(c\), but it does not erase multiplicity within each cohort. So “no multiplicity” is not a sound objection here.

The strongest concern is that the lower bound’s hard information sits in those distinct private valuations, while repetition adds no new information. With one-bundle-per-bidder allocations, clones do not remove that communication obstacle. The proposed mass model changes the problem by letting each type’s population divide among integral bundles. That makes the configuration LP an actual allocation model rather than a relaxation, and its pricing step uses the demand-query machinery already central to the paper. This makes the mirror vulnerable to the charge that its tractability comes from fractionalizing allocation and scaling supply, rather than from a population limit alone.

That concern is limited. The programme explicitly allows type-level fractional averages when they arise from a continuous population, and this model still asks the authors’ welfare question. I cannot use the allocation change or the growing support to rule it out. The honest negative verdict is weak: the mirror looks worthwhile, though its contribution may be a clean high-multiplicity formulation of configuration-LP welfare rather than a new source of complexity structure.
