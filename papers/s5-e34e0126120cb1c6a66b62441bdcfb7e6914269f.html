The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), candidate groups with diversity bounds, reference committees \(W_P\) with representation bounds, and a finite support \(T^+\) of voter types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies a complete ranking and its voter-population memberships, decide whether there is a size-\(k\) committee \(W'\) satisfying all bounds and \(U_P(W')=U_Q(W')\) for every pair of populations \(P,Q\). For each nonempty population \(P\subseteq T^+\), define \(\bar b_P(c)=\sum_{t\in P}\mu_t b_t(c)/\sum_{t\in P}\mu_t\), where \(b_t(c)\) is candidate \(c\)’s Borda score in type \(t\), and \(U_P(W')=\sum_{c\in W'}\bar b_P(c)\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete voter types carrying rankings and population memberships; type masses determine each population’s per-capita Borda utility, while the selected committee remains an indivisible size-\(k\) subset of candidates.

The objection that survived

After the type averages are computed, UEC is a finite committee feasibility problem over a population-by-candidate utility matrix; the distribution contributes an aggregation step rather than a richer continuous optimization structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover global UEC feasibility from Theorem 3 and intermediate-relaxation FEC feasibility from Theorem 2. They leave WEC, the other fair-allocation variants, and the empirical findings without a continuous analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can Theorem 3’s subset-sum reduction be made valid for the paper’s fixed Borda scores, and what is the complexity of the continuous UEC problem as a function of \(m\), support size \(\tau\), population count, and mass bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a continuous-population version of the paper’s global utility-fair committee problem. The committee stays indivisible; the population becomes a distribution. That keeps the question close to the paper while putting voter mass, rather than named voters, into the input.

A plausible regime is a large public consultation or election over a manageable candidate slate: perhaps millions of voters but only hundreds or thousands of recurring combinations of complete ranking and demographic or regional memberships. Each type records everything the model uses, including its ranking and which overlapping voter populations it belongs to. A type’s mass is its fraction of the electorate. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime when ballots and population profiles recur; it would be much less credible for a small committee of individually distinct voters.

For a precise mirror, let the input include candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a finite set of supported types \(T^+\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), candidate groups and their diversity bounds, and voter populations \(\mathcal P\). Each population \(P\) is a subset of types, with positive mass \(\mu(P)=\sum_{t\in P}\mu_t\). For type \(t\), let \(b_t(c)\) be candidate \(c\)’s Borda score in that type’s ranking. Define the population’s per-capita score for a candidate by
\[ \bar b_P(c)=\frac{1}{\mu(P)}\sum_{t\in P}\mu_t b_t(c), \]
and its utility from committee \(W'\) by \(U_P(W')=\sum_{c\in W'}\bar b_P(c)\). This agrees with the paper’s population-level Borda utility when everyone in a population has the same ranking, and extends it naturally to heterogeneous populations.

My lead question is Global UEC\(_\infty\). As in the paper, the instance also supplies a feasible DiRe committee \(W_0\) and each population’s reference committee \(W_P\), used for the representation constraints. The question is whether there is a size-\(k\) committee \(W'\) satisfying every diversity constraint \(|G\cap W'|\ge l^D_G\), every representation constraint \(|W_P\cap W'|\ge l^R_P\), and exact utility equality \(U_P(W')=U_Q(W')\) for every pair \(P,Q\in\mathcal P\). A solution is an ordinary committee, not a fractional selection. The instance is encoded by the \(\tau=|T^+|\) supported types and their binary-rational masses, along with the candidate and constraint data; relevant parameters include \(m,\tau\), the number of populations, and mass bit length \(L\).

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper’s Appendix D.2. It states that, given a DiRe committee, deciding whether a UEC committee \(W'\) exists that meets the diversity and representation constraints is NP-hard, even when all those lower bounds are zero. The continuous problem asks the same underlying feasibility question, with each population’s Borda utility computed from its mass-weighted type distribution. I expect this mirror to be Class B: the apparent reduction’s combinatorics are in choosing candidates to balance utilities, rather than in the number of voters. A discrete profile also embeds as rational type masses. This is the most direct mirror of the paper’s utility-fairness contribution.

There is a second, independently useful hardness mirror: FEC\(_{x,\infty}\). For each population, order candidates by \(\bar b_P(c)\), breaking ties by a fixed priority order. Given an integer \(x\), ask whether a size-\(k\) committee meets the DiRe constraints and contains at least one of each population’s top \(x+1\) candidates. Its anchor is Theorem 2, proved here in Appendix D.1: for \(x\in[1,k-3]\), deciding whether an FEC up to \(x\) exists is NP-hard, even when the diversity and representation lower bounds are zero. The paper reduces from vertex cover on uniform hypergraphs; the committee’s candidate choices meet the populations’ top-candidate sets. That suggests Class B as well, since the hard structure lies in the candidate slate and the overlaps among those sets.

These mirrors cover the paper’s global UEC feasibility result and its intermediate-relaxation FEC hardness result. They do not claim to continuize the empirical findings, WEC, or every proposed fair-allocation variant. They also leave useful follow-up questions: does UEC remain hard with fixed type support or a fixed number of populations? What changes if exact equality is relaxed to a tolerance? Can the minimum achievable utility disparity be approximated or parameterized by committee size?

The weakest point is the UEC hardness anchor’s proof. Appendix D.2 appears to encode arbitrary subset-sum integers as a population score vector, although the model fixes Borda scores. The stated Theorem 3 is the right named result to mirror, but that reduction needs reconciliation with the paper’s Borda utility definition before its hardness classification is secure. There is also a modeling choice in extending utility to heterogeneous populations: per-capita mean Borda scores are natural for a continuum and fit the paper’s examples, but a referee may ask the authors to affirm that this is their intended aggregate utility. Neither issue makes the population-continuous question artificial; both make the exact theorem-to-mirror bridge the part that needs the closest scrutiny.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does have named computational results, and there are plausible large-electorate settings for it. So the strongest negative case is not that the population cannot sensibly be made continuous. It is that the two proposed anchors become group-level committee problems whose connection to population mass is thin.

That case is strongest against Theorem 2’s FEC mirror. FEC up to \(x\) asks whether the committee contains one of each group’s top \(x+1\) candidates. Once those candidate sets are known, voter masses do no work: a group with a tiny positive share has the same force as a large one. Letting the sets come from a mass-weighted ranking does not change that; the decision still depends only on the resulting sets. A more mass-sensitive rule—for example, requiring a specified fraction of each group’s mass to rank a selected candidate highly—would make mass matter, but it would replace the paper’s all-or-nothing FEC condition and its theorem with a different fairness criterion. FEC is therefore a poor anchor for a continuous-population programme.

Theorem 3’s UEC mirror is harder to dismiss. The proposed extension gives each population a per-capita score vector,
\[ \bar b_P(c)=\frac{1}{\mu(P)}\sum_{t\in P}\mu_t b_t(c), \]
then asks for a committee whose utilities are equal. This is a sensible model for a large electorate with recurring ranking and demographic types. But after the averages are computed, the distribution has been compressed into a finite population-by-candidate utility matrix. UEC does not transfer voter mass, and the equality tests use committee membership and those matrix entries. A direct input of the averages describes the same decision problem; the continuum contributes a way to encode and aggregate the valuations, rather than a new continuous optimization structure. That is a real negative for this programme’s fit, though not a proof that the mirror lacks value: high-multiplicity preprocessing and complexity in \(\tau\) could still matter.

There is also a specific reason to be cautious about treating Theorem 3 as a secure anchor. Appendix D.2 appears to encode arbitrary subset-sum integers as a scoring vector, although Borda scores are fixed by rank. That reduction does not establish the stated hardness as written. This weakens the theorem-to-mirror argument, but it does not make UEC an unworthy question: the continuous feasibility problem remains well-posed even if the paper’s hardness proof needs repair.

So the negative case can make FEC look like a poor mirror and can argue that UEC mostly repackages group valuation data. It cannot honestly rule out the better UEC version in a large electorate with repeated ranking-and-demographic types. Since one such mirror is enough, the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is weak; UEC remains a credible question for the programme.
