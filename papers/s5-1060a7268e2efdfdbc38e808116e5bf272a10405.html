What the mirror covers

No continuous population mirror is anchored by a computational result. The paper’s PI classifications and simulations of violation frequencies under specified probability models remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, this paper has no named computational complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror on. Lemma 6 says that single-voter positive involvement implies coalitional positive involvement. Proposition 7 classifies the listed voting methods: Baldwin, Beat Path, Bucklin, Coombs, Copeland, Ranked Pairs, Strict Nanson, Top Cycle, and the Gillies Uncovered Set violate positive involvement; positional scoring rules, Instant Runoff, and Split Cycle satisfy it. The violations are illustrated in the appendix, while the satisfaction claims cite earlier work. Neither result classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The simulation findings are reported in figures, not as numbered complexity results. The paper’s mention that computing Ranked Pairs winners is NP-complete cites earlier work; it is not a numbered result of this paper.

There is still a plausible continuous research question suggested by Proposition 7, though it is unanchored under your rules. For Coombs, let \(T\) be the set of complete rankings of \(m\) candidates, and let \(\mu\) give each ranking’s fraction of a large electorate. Given \(x\in\mathrm{Coombs}(\mu)\), ask for the infimum \(\alpha\geq 0\) such that adding mass \(\alpha\) of some type \(t\) with \(x\) ranked first makes \(x\) lose under weighted Coombs on
\[ \mu_{t,\alpha}=\frac{\mu+\alpha\delta_t}{1+\alpha}. \]
The output is the threshold and a ranking \(t\) that attains it, or approaches it if the infimum is not attained; return \(\infty\) if no such addition exists. Here types are complete rankings, and mass is population share. A plausible regime is a large membership electorate with many people but relatively few distinct rankings.

This would measure the coalition share needed to trigger a Coombs positive-involvement failure. It fits the paper’s attention to coalitions and their size, and its Coombs result supplies a reason to study that failure. I would expect fixed-\(m\) versions to be manageable, but the paper gives no basis for predicting the variable-\(m\) complexity. The weakness is that this threshold measures worst-case vulnerability, while the authors study how often violations occur under specified probability models. They might accept it as a related measure, but it cannot serve as a result-anchored case that the continuous question is their computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 7 is an axiomatic classification: it says which rules satisfy positive involvement. Lemma 6 is a logical implication. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem. The simulation findings are estimates, and the NP-completeness claim about computing Ranked Pairs winners is cited prior work, not a result of this paper. So the paper offers no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror on.

The proposed Coombs threshold is well-defined: given a weighted society \(\mu\) and a winner \(x\), find the least \(\alpha\) and a ranking \(t\) such that adding mass \(\alpha\delta_t\) makes \(x\) lose. But that changes the paper’s question. The paper measures how often violations occur under specified probability models, including for coalitions of specified sizes, and compares those rates across rules. A worst-case threshold replaces those probabilities with an adversarial choice. It may be a useful winner-robustness problem, but the paper’s Coombs classification does not establish it as the authors’ computational problem.

A closer extension would put the paper’s probability experiment over continuous societies: draw a measure \(\mu\), add coalition mass \(\alpha\) of a first-place ranking, and estimate the probability of a Coombs reversal. For independent ballot models, the society typically concentrates on a fixed measure, and single-voter additions have vanishing mass; positive coalition shares can still matter. Models with random limiting measures could preserve more of the original variation. These are plausible follow-ups, but they require a new computational question about probabilities over measures. The paper neither formulates that problem nor gives a complexity result for it.

I would not claim that no such follow-up could be worthwhile. The negative case is weaker against new continuous research inspired by the paper than against a continuous mirror of its results. Its strongest point is that the apparent Coombs anchor is not computational, and the proposed threshold changes the measured quantity. Calling either one a result-anchored mirror would go beyond what this paper establishes.
