The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for type based unsupervised label aggregation. The paper’s natural continuous population is its workers: imagine a large annotation operation with many annotators but a small number \(\tau\) of stable response profiles. A type groups annotators with the same task access, assignment pattern, and Dawid–Skene confusion matrix; \(\mu_t\) is type \(t\)’s share of the workforce. The regime would be, for example, a very large pool drawn from a few dozen recurring worker profiles. The paper’s small benchmark datasets do not themselves establish that regime.

One precise candidate is Fractional Dawid–Skene Aggregation. Given \(M\) items, \(K\) labels, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), assignment fractions \(a_{tj}\), and observed label fractions \(q_{tj\ell}\), set \(w_{tj\ell}=\mu_t a_{tj}q_{tj\ell}\). Let \(\theta_{t,k\ell}\) be the probability that type \(t\) reports label \(\ell\) when the true label is \(k\). With a uniform prior on each item’s true label, ask for confusion matrices maximizing the fractional-count marginal likelihood
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{M}\log\!\left( \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \prod_{t\in T}\prod_{\ell=1}^{K} \theta_{t,k\ell}^{\,w_{tj\ell}} \right), \]
and return, for each item, its maximum-posterior label. This keeps the paper’s unsupervised aggregation problem and its DS worker model, while replacing individual workers’ counts with rational type masses. My tentative expectation is Class C: jointly fitting latent item labels and worker response profiles appears capable of retaining hard combinatorics even when annotator mass is continuous. That is a conjecture for this stated problem, not a result established by the paper.

The required anchors are absent. The supplied text has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition giving a computational classification; its results are empirical comparisons of MV, DS, and GLAD accuracy. So there is no honest theorem number or in-paper complexity result to restate, and Fractional Dawid–Skene Aggregation is a plausible extension of the paper’s computational object, not a mirror anchored in one of its formal results.

The mirror would cover categorical label aggregation, especially the paper’s use of Dawid–Skene. It would not mirror the reported accuracy gains from adding LLM labels: those are empirical findings, not complexity results. The weak point is that the paper neither formulates nor studies global optimization of the DS likelihood. A referee could accept this as a continuous version of the aggregation problem the authors evaluate, but not as a result of theirs whose complexity changes under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no named computational result here to continuize. The paper reports benchmark construction, accuracy comparisons, and experiments applying MV, DS, and GLAD. It gives no theorem or other formal result about the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent acknowledges this: Fractional Dawid–Skene Aggregation is a proposed new problem, and its Class C label is a conjecture, not a mirror of a result in the paper.

The best version of that proposal is coherent: a large annotation workforce could have a small number of stable response profiles, with workers in each profile represented by a mass. But running aggregation on type-level label fractions mostly replaces worker-indexed counts with fractional counts. MV becomes weighted voting; DS or GLAD would need a specified continuous-data variant. The paper neither studies global likelihood optimization for DS nor claims that the aggregation routines have an interesting complexity boundary. To make the proposal computationally ambitious, one must add a new optimization task; the resulting difficulty would come from latent labels and model fitting, not from a population-size bottleneck the paper identified.

A closer mirror of the paper’s main empirical finding would ask whether changing the masses of crowd and LLM types improves aggregate accuracy. That is a reasonable population-level statistical question, but it is not a computational result from this paper. It would need a model for accuracy or new experiments; any allocation optimization would also need costs and constraints the paper does not provide.

So the proponent’s candidate does not supply an anchor that survives scrutiny as a *mirror of a paper result*. The negative case is weaker as a universal claim: a high-volume annotation service with a few recurring worker profiles is plausible, and continuous label aggregation could be useful in its own right. But that plausibility does not give this paper a worthwhile complexity mirror on the evidence presented.
