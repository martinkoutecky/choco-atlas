What the mirror covers

No result has a qualifying computational mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes equilibrium, and the experimental findings report treatment comparisons without computational guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has a strong *population-continuum plausibility signal*: its theoretical model already assumes voters form a continuum uniformly distributed over \([0,1]\), and vote shares are masses. In a large-election regime, voters with the same ideal point could be types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction holding that ideal point. The parties’ entry and platform choices would remain the decisions, and the outcome would remain their equilibrium. That is close to the paper’s subject, but it would generalize its assumed uniform electorate rather than continuize a discrete population the paper studies.

I do not find a qualifying computational anchor. The nearest named result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper: it establishes a unique equilibrium and threshold \(\hat n\); for \(n\le\hat n\), all three parties enter with platforms \((\frac12-\frac1{n+1},\frac12,\frac12+\frac1{n+1})\), while for \(n>\hat n\), only \(L\) and \(R\) enter, at \((\frac12-\frac1{2n},\frac12+\frac1{2n})\). That is an equilibrium characterization for an already-continuum electorate, not a named result placing an underlying problem in a complexity class or giving an algorithmic classification. The experimental hypotheses and findings do not supply such anchors either.

So the honest positive case is that a mass-based electorate would be natural to these authors. But there is no named computational result here on which to base the required per-anchor continuous problem, and presenting Proposition 1 as an in-\(\mathrm P\) result would overstate what it proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The only named formal result, Proposition 1, characterizes a subgame-perfect equilibrium and its threshold \(\hat n\). It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result. Turning it into “compute the equilibrium” would recast an already derived characterization as an algorithmic task; the paper supplies no discrete computational problem whose continuous version that task would be.

The population side is already continuous in the theory: voters are uniformly distributed over \([0,1]\), vote sincerely, and their behavior enters through vote shares. The strategic actors are three distinct parties with different ideal policies and separate entry and platform decisions. Grouping those parties into types would erase the roles the model studies. In the experiment, too, each election has only two or three candidate participants; the paper poses no computational question about them.

The best stronger proposal is to replace the uniform electorate with a distribution over voter types and study equilibrium computation. That could be a coherent follow-on, but it changes the paper’s electorate assumption and introduces a new computational question. It does not continue a discrete computational result from this paper into a continuous society; the paper’s theoretical society is continuous already.

So the negative case is strong against a *continuous mirror of this paper*. It is weaker against the value of studying equilibrium computation in richer electorate models in general. That neighboring direction may be worthwhile, but the paper does not establish it as a ChoCo mirror.
