What the mirror covers

The proposed allocation uses Proposition 2.1’s weighted-welfare representation and the paper’s redistribution and public-service applications; Proposition 4.1 and the paper’s other named results receive no computational counterpart.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an application-level mirror, but this paper has no named computational result to anchor it to. Its named results are axiomatic: Proposition 2.1, proved here, characterizes social preferences by a weighted sum of utilities attached to different rights; Proposition 4.1, also proved here, decomposes an individual’s utility by the rights attached to their actions. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. They cannot serve as the required anchors.

A plausible adjacent problem is Rights-Weighted Public-Service Allocation\(_\infty\). Take a finite set \(T\) of resident types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type completely specifies its rights profile, eligibility, resource use, and utility \(u_{tj}(a)\) under each right \(j\) for each bundle \(a\) in a finite menu of integral service bundles. The planner chooses masses \(y_{ta}\geq 0\), where \(y_{ta}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(a\), subject to \(\sum_a y_{ta}=\mu_t\) and stated linear resource and eligibility constraints. Given rights weights \(\theta_j\), the goal is to return a feasible allocation maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_a y_{ta}\sum_j\theta_j u_{tj}(a). \]
Each resident receives an integral bundle; fractional quantities occur only at the type-mass level. This is plausible for a large public-service system with many residents but a comparatively small number of relevant rights and eligibility profiles. The paper itself points to redistribution and services such as education and health as applications. With the finite menu and linear constraints specified above, the problem is an LP and hence tractable.

That is a coherent continuous-population question, and Proposition 2.1 supplies a natural objective for it. But the LP setup and its tractability are additions: the paper neither formulates this optimization problem nor studies its complexity. The main weakness is therefore decisive for the requested case: this candidate extends an axiomatic theory into computation; it does not mirror a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive for the requested kind of mirror: this paper proves no computational result. Proposition 2.1 characterizes social preferences as weighted sums; Proposition 4.1 decomposes utility across actions. Neither defines an algorithmic problem, an input, or a complexity question. For a society with type masses, the first proposition gives the direct aggregate \(W(\mu)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_j\theta_j U_{tj}\). Computing that value is just evaluation of the representation. To ask for an optimum, one must supply a feasible set and a choice problem that the proposition does not specify.

The proposed Rights-Weighted Public-Service Allocation\(_\infty\) supplies those missing pieces: a finite bundle menu, linear resource constraints, eligibility constraints, and a planner’s optimization objective. That is a coherent allocation model, but its LP and tractability come from those added choices. A stronger application-level version could extend the paper’s redistribution example to a population with rights profiles and concave utilities, then maximize weighted welfare subject to a resource constraint. That may be a sensible project, especially since the authors mention redistribution and public services. Yet it is a new computational model built around the paper’s welfare representation, not a continuous counterpart to a computational result in the paper. The basic separable version is standard welfare optimization; adding combinatorial constraints or interactions to make the complexity question richer adds structure the paper leaves open.

Section 3 offers another tempting route: assign actions to types and aggregate the resulting rights-weighted utilities. But the paper supplies no feasible-action model or tractable representation of the joint effects of actions. Under separability, an analyst can make the resulting optimization a routine LP; with richer interactions, its complexity depends on the added payoff and feasibility structure. Proposition 4.1 itself is a decomposition identity, not a result about computing an optimal action profile.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about *this paper*: its axiomatic results do not anchor a ChoCo complexity study, and the proposed allocation LP is an adjacent model rather than a mirror. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile project could grow from the paper. The redistribution and public-service examples give an application-level route the authors might recognize. What is missing is evidence that the resulting computational question belongs to the paper’s contribution, rather than being a promising new project that borrows its objective.
