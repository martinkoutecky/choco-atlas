The proposed type-mass problem is coherent and recognizable as a related coalition-formation extension, but an infinitesimal departure cannot remove a type from a coalition's support, unlike a finite player's departure. That changes the strategic question rather than carrying Proposition 6 to a continuous population, so the anchor fails the continuous-analogue test.

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror I would pursue is Type-Mass Nash-Stable Coalition Formation. Its anchor is Proposition 6, proved in this paper. It says that a partition \(\pi\) of a Boolean hedonic game \((N,\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_n)\) is Nash stable exactly when
\[ \pi \models \bigwedge_{i\in N}\bigl((\hat{\exists}_i\gamma_i)\to\gamma_i\bigr). \]
Here \(\hat{\exists}_i\gamma_i\) captures whether some feasible unilateral move by \(i\)—to another existing coalition or to being alone—would make \(i\)’s goal true. Thus the condition says that if a player has a beneficial move, their current coalition must already satisfy their goal. The paper notes that this characterization can be used with SAT solvers to compute Nash-stable partitions; the quantified formula can be exponential in size. This is an encoding anchor under the paper’s computational method, not a complexity classification: the paper establishes no P or hardness result for the search problem.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a dichotomous goal formula \(\gamma_t\) for each type \(t\). The formula uses variables \(q_u\), where \(q_u\) means that a positive mass of *other* agents of type \(u\) is in the same team as the focal agent. A coalition with resident-type support \(S\subseteq T\) therefore gives a type-\(t\) agent satisfaction value \(\gamma_t(S)\).

A candidate mass partition is a family \(F\) of nonempty supports and rational masses \(x_{t,S}\). For each \(S\in F\), \(x_{t,S}>0\) exactly for \(t\in S\); for every type \(t\), \(\sum_{S\in F}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\). This assigns all type mass to teams, while allowing a type’s mass to be split across teams. The question is: does there exist such a partition with no profitable unilateral deviation by any positive-mass type? In the atomless model, one agent’s move does not change a team’s mass support. So Nash stability requires that whenever type \(t\) is unsatisfied in an occupied support \(S\), it is unsatisfied in every available destination support \(R\in F\) and when alone, represented by \(R=\varnothing\). A solution is the supports and mass allocation satisfying these conditions, or a certificate that none exists. Coalitions with the same support can be merged without changing preferences or deviation options, so at most \(2^{|T|}-1\) supports are needed.

A plausible regime is a very large workforce organized into sizeable project teams, with many employees but a short list of roles or skill profiles. Members of a type share the same complete dichotomous rule about team composition; for example, they may be satisfied exactly when their team includes a designer and excludes a compliance role. Mass records the fraction of the workforce assigned to each team profile. Here the number of people can be enormous while \(\tau=|T|\) remains small. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their Nash-stability problem in a clone-invariant, high-multiplicity setting: coalition formation and unilateral moves remain central, while the population and preferences are represented by type masses and type-level goals.

My expected direction is tractable in the few-types regime, at least fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\). Enumerate possible families \(F\) of supports, check the stability condition by evaluating the formulas, and use linear feasibility to test whether the masses can realize \(F\). Positivity can be tested by requiring each active \(x_{t,S}\ge\varepsilon\) and asking whether a feasible allocation exists with \(\varepsilon>0\). This gives a finite algorithm with a large function of \(\tau\) and polynomial dependence on the rational input and formula lengths. The open question is whether that can be improved to polynomial time in unbounded \(\tau\). Other natural follow-ups are how stable mass partitions relate to stable partitions in large finite cloned populations, and whether the result survives goals based on quantitative mass thresholds rather than type presence.

The weak point is the type-level model. The paper’s formulas can distinguish named individuals; this mirror instead treats agents with the same goal formula as interchangeable and records only whether a type has positive mass in a team. It also makes a single deviator infinitesimal, so the move cannot change the team’s mass profile. Those are material changes for some instances, though natural ones for macroscopic teams. I would present this as a narrow, credible mirror of Proposition 6, not as a continuous version of every Boolean hedonic game or as a complexity result already proved by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against this anchor is that Proposition 6 does not establish a complexity result. It characterizes Nash stability by a formula, and the paper explains how to use that characterization with a SAT solver. It gives no running time or complexity classification for finding a stable partition; the conclusion names that classification as future work. The proponent’s “encoding anchor” is therefore the paper’s best computational foothold, but it is a logical characterization rather than one of the computational results the programme is designed to mirror.

The proposed mass model is coherent enough to analyze, but its Boolean goals make the masses do very little. Satisfaction depends on whether a type has positive mass in a coalition. Once a type’s total mass is positive, it can be split among any finite number of supports in arbitrarily small amounts. Thus the rational mass values do not affect the stability test: the problem reduces to choosing a family of type-support sets and checking which types would want to move between them. The proposed enumeration captures that finite combinatorial problem, with a double-exponential dependence on \(\tau\). It offers no evident role for continuous optimization.

The underlying mismatch is the atomless limit. In the finite game, a player can be the last member of their type in a coalition, so leaving can change whether that type is present. In the proposed continuum, a single player’s departure never changes a positive-mass support. Because preferences are Boolean membership tests, even an arbitrarily small positive mass counts the same as a large one. A continuum model can retain this rule, but it describes a support-based equilibrium with non-pivotal individuals; it does not preserve the pivotal membership changes that can drive Nash stability in the finite game. Giving formulas quantitative mass thresholds would restore sensitivity to mass, but it would change the preference domain.

A large workforce with a short list of interchangeable roles is a plausible setting for the modified model, and the paper’s authors could recognize its coalition-formation question. That is the strongest part of the case for. The case against is weaker on that ground: the proposed model is well-posed, and studying its complexity could still be worthwhile. Its weakness as a *continuous mirror* is that the source paper leaves the relevant complexity questions open, while the proposed limit discards the mass magnitudes and changes the effect of individual deviations.
