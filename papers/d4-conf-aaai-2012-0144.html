What the mirror covers

A population reformulation could revisit the worst-case claims in Theorems 1 and 2, but the paper provides no computational result to carry over; Theorem 5 and the finite-profile Monte Carlo tables also lack a continuous computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind this paper: a large allocation platform whose users fall into a small number \(\tau\) of complete valuation types. A continuous model could give each type mass \(\mu_t\), choose allocation rates by type, and assign per-capita transfers while measuring welfare and fairness. That is a possible research direction, but I can’t honestly anchor it in a named computational result from this paper.

The closest candidates are Theorems 1 and 2, both proved here. Theorem 1 says that for \(n\ge2\), any smoothly connected cake-cutting type space admitting linear-satiation slopes \(0\) and \(n-1\) has worst-case welfare rate \(0\) and disproportionality rate \(1\) under every dominant-strategy-efficient, ex-post individually rational, no-deficit mechanism. Theorem 2 gives the same worst-case rates for assignment with \(n\) agents and \(n\) items, when each agent’s value space is smoothly connected and admits values \(0\) and \(x\) for each item, for some \(x>0\). These are mechanism-performance impossibility results, not claims about computational complexity. Theorem 5’s comparison of redistribution and VCG in single-item assignment is likewise not a complexity result.

The paper’s Monte Carlo evaluation samples finite type profiles; its divisible cake is continuity in the outcome, not in the population. A continuous-population mechanism problem might be sensible, but it would be a new question without a qualifying named anchor here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 prove worst-case welfare and proportionality impossibilities for classes of mechanisms; Theorem 5 gives a fairness guarantee for the redistribution mechanism in single-item assignment. None states a complexity bound, hardness result, or algorithmic optimization problem. The paper’s computation consists of Monte Carlo evaluation of finite profiles. Under ChoCo’s computational scope, that is the decisive gap.

The best mirror of Theorems 1 and 2 would represent a large society by masses of valuation types, assign bundles and per-capita transfers by reported type, and extend the welfare and fairness rates using population averages. That is a coherent mechanism-design model. I would not dismiss it by saying individual incentives vanish: a representative agent can still choose among report-contingent bundles and payments, so dominant-strategy constraints can remain meaningful. But asking whether the impossibility theorems extend is still a mechanism-theoretic question. To get a computational question, one must choose a finite representation of mechanisms and ask, for example, how to compute or optimize their rates. The paper supplies neither that representation nor that task. Such a project could be worthwhile, but its algorithmic problem would be newly introduced rather than mirrored from these theorems.

Theorem 2’s assignment setting makes the modeling change especially visible. With a fixed supply of indivisible items and a nonatomic population, the allocation to a particular recipient is a zero-mass event that a type distribution does not record. Replicating items or scaling supply with population would preserve positive per-capita supply, but would create a large-market assignment model rather than the theorem’s \(n\)-agent, \(n\)-item instance. That repaired model could support its own computational study; it does not provide a computational claim from Theorem 2 to carry over.

Theorem 5 is an even poorer computational anchor. Its single-item guarantee concerns which named agents receive the item and how their payments compare. In a type-mass description, a single winner has zero population mass, so the usual aggregate allocation loses the identity that the result tracks. A lottery over individual recipients or a supply that grows with the population can repair that representation, with further choices about allocation and fairness metrics. Either repair changes the object under study, and neither turns Theorem 5 into a complexity result. The average-case tables do not fill the gap: they average performance over distributions of finite profiles; they do not analyze computational complexity on a continuous society.

So the strongest honest negative case is that this is a poor ChoCo result-anchored candidate, not that every continuous population study inspired by it would be worthless. A type-mass study of redistribution, welfare, and fairness could be sensible and perhaps recognizable to the authors. The paper gives no computational anchor for it, though, and I cannot honestly claim that no scenario could produce worthwhile follow-on work.
