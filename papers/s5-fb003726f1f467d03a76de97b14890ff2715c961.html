The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is the paper’s discussion of Bayesian Truth Serum. Theorem 7.8, attributed to Prelec [22], says that under the common-prior, symmetric-prior, conditional-independence, and infinite-group assumptions, BTS is detail-free and truthful, truth-telling maximizes expected average information score across equilibria, and BTS is focal for \(\alpha>1\). This paper reconstructs parts (ii) and (iii) using mutual information. That is already an infinite-population model, so a “mirror” of it would mostly restate the existing setting.

It does not, however, provide an eligible computational anchor. The numbered results concern incentive properties, information inequalities, and impossibility for mechanisms; none asserts a complexity or algorithmic result about an underlying problem. Theorem 6.3 is proved here, but its continuum is the number of questions, not the population. Proposition 8.8 is an impossibility result about equilibrium and payments, not computational complexity.

So I can’t honestly state a continuous problem per anchor: this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor one to. Its infinite-agent BTS setting is the nearest positive lead, but it already continuizes the population and does not study the computational questions ChoCo targets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is right, though “BTS already has infinitely many agents” is not itself a valid collision: the paper’s infinite-population analysis is an incentive result, not a computational treatment. The sharper objection is that none of the cited results gives ChoCo a computational problem to continuize. Theorem 7.8 establishes incentive and welfare properties; Theorem 6.3 establishes properties of the multi-question mechanism; Proposition 8.8 is an impossibility result about equilibria and payments. The other central theorems likewise concern information inequalities or mechanism properties, not complexity or algorithms.

The best population mirror for BTS would take an exchangeable crowd with signal-generating types of masses \(\mu_t\), then ask for an efficient way to synthesize or optimize a truthful payment rule. That is a sensible population model, but it does not mirror Theorem 7.8: the theorem already assumes an infinite group, and it does not pose a mechanism-optimization problem. Allowing heterogeneous signal models makes the population richer, but also changes the theorem’s assumptions. Optimizing over a specified mechanism family could be worthwhile in its own right; the paper supplies neither that objective nor a computational result to anchor it.

Theorem 6.3 offers the strongest alternative. One could imagine a large crowd of workers grouped by signal and effort type, with each group assigned mass. But its guarantee is stated agent by agent and holds across other agents’ strategies. Aggregating identical workers into masses does not create a new algorithmic task in that result. An optimization over budgets or mechanism parameters would be an added question, not a continuous version of the theorem.

Proposition 8.8 fares no better as an anchor. Its content is a symmetry argument over priors, signal relabelings, and indistinguishable scenarios. A continuum of agent types could be added, but the proposition does not define an instance-wise computation over their masses. The same applies to the paper’s information-theoretic framework: its probability distributions are mathematical inputs to incentive claims, not instances of a stated complexity problem.

So none of the proponent’s anchors survives as a ChoCo computational mirror. The infinite-agent BTS model is useful context, but I would not call it a novelty collision: it does not answer a computational question. The negative case is strong for this paper’s fit to the programme. The universal claim is less secure: one could attach a new algorithmic mechanism-synthesis problem to almost any model. But that possibility is not a mirror established by this paper, and its merit would need to be argued separately.
