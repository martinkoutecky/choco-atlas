The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 3.1, proved here, is a finite sample concentration bound for an empirical matching statistic around its conditional expectation. Corollary 3.1 gives a convergence rate; Corollaries 3.2–3.4 establish consistency results; Lemma 3.3 bounds how many students’ assignments can change when one student’s type changes. These are statistical results, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally tractable. The paper therefore supplies no named result on which to base a continuous complexity mirror.

A plausible adjacent problem would be: given finitely many colleges, rational per capita capacities, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) of complete student types—including each type’s preferences and college priorities—find a stable allocation \(x_{tj}\) of type mass to colleges, with each type’s mass assigned and no blocking type–college pair. A plausible regime is a large centralized school assignment with many students but relatively few complete types. That is a genuine continuous-population problem, and the paper’s school-priority setting makes the regime credible.

But that would be a new computational study of stable allocation, not a mirror of one of this paper’s named results. The authors explicitly study finite, identity-based markets and set aside a continuum-limit market; their estimand is an empirical statistic under an unknown stable matching mechanism. The cited continuum-market literature is relevant background, but it does not provide this paper with a computational anchor. So the positive case has a plausible setting and model, but no anchor-specific question or honest A/B/C prediction to submit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a paper-specific mirror starts with the missing anchor: this paper proves no computational complexity result. Theorem 3.1 is a finite-sample concentration bound; Corollaries 3.1–3.4 give statistical convergence and consistency results. None specifies an algorithmic problem whose running time, approximation, or hardness could be mirrored.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 3.1: ask how to compute matching probabilities or their concentration bounds from a distribution over student types. But the theorem’s object is the fluctuation of a finite market’s empirical statistic around a finite-market conditional expectation. A continuous allocation would turn those statistics into deterministic masses or integrals. Keeping the theorem’s sampling fluctuation requires retaining a finite random population. And the paper supplies no encoded matching mechanism or report map for an algorithm to take as input; those are deliberately allowed to be unknown. Adding such an input would define a new computational problem.

Lemma 3.3 offers a second possible anchor: measure how assignments respond when one student’s type changes. Its bound concerns a single person. With bounded \(k\), the affected fraction is at most \(O(k/n)\), which vanishes in the population limit. Replacing the person with a positive mass of students could yield a meaningful sensitivity question, but that changes the perturbation and calls for a new analysis. The paper gives no computational objective for it.

The proposed stable-allocation problem is the most credible adjacent project. A large school market with many students of shared types and per-capita capacities is a sensible continuous-population setting. The cited continuum-market literature supports that regime; its existence is evidence in favour of the model, not a novelty collision. Still, computing a stable mass allocation is a different object from the paper’s concentration and estimation results. With a small set of complete types and common priorities, it may reduce to a finite type-level deferred-acceptance calculation. If complete types retain the paper’s student-specific match qualities and college priorities, the support may grow with the market, undermining the moderate-support premise.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: none of its named results yields an anchor-specific computational question, and the best proposed mirror shifts the research target to continuum stable allocation. The universal claim is weaker. The allocation problem could still be worthwhile to study in its own right, and this paper alone cannot establish that no such formulation would interest its authors.
