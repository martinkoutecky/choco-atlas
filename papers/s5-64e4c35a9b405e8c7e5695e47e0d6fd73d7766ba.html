What the mirror covers

The proposed type-distribution model could extend the paper’s axiomatic characterizations, including Theorems 1–4 and Propositions 1–3, but it covers no computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous population model nearby. In a large redistribution system with many people sharing a modest number of exact income–need pairs, take each type to be \(t=(y_t,z_t)\), with population share \(\mu_t\). An allocation gives each person of type \(t\) an amount \(x_t\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=\sum_t\mu_t y_t\). The paper’s proportional rule then becomes \(x_t=z_tY_\mu/Z_\mu\), where \(Y_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t y_t\) and \(Z_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t z_t>0\). This is a faithful population-level version of its allocation model.

But the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that a rule satisfies core, no advantageous transfer, stability, and dummy if and only if it is laissez-faire or proportional redistribution. The other numbered theorems and propositions also characterize allocation rules axiomatically; none establishes a complexity classification or an algorithm for a computational problem. The paper’s continuity axiom concerns how a rule responds to changes in finite profiles—it does not make the population continuous.

One could ask for a continuous-population version of Theorem 1: characterize allocation rules on distributions over income–need types that satisfy mass analogues of those axioms. That is a coherent nearby question, and the regime above makes high multiplicity plausible. But it would continue the paper’s axiomatic programme, not mirror a computational result from it. Since there is no eligible anchor, I cannot honestly state a per-anchor computational problem or predict whether its hardness dissolves. The absence of a computational anchor is the decisive weakness of the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed population model is coherent: a large redistribution system could have a finite support of income–need types, with mass \(\mu_t\) assigned to each type. That establishes a plausible high-multiplicity regime. It does not, however, supply a computational result from this paper to mirror.

Theorem 1 and the other numbered results characterize allocation rules by axioms. Their objects are mappings from profiles to allocations; their conclusions do not give an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational classification. A continuous-population version of Theorem 1 would still be an axiomatic characterization, now over distributions of types. It could be worthwhile work in its own field, but it would not chart the computational landscape ChoCo is intended to study.

One might add an algorithmic question, such as computing an allocation from the mass profile. For the rules displayed here, that is direct arithmetic over the support. More ambitious questions—such as deciding whether an arbitrary rule satisfies the axioms—would need a finite representation of the rule that the paper does not provide. Optimization questions would require adding objectives or constraints absent from the model. Those may be sensible new projects, but they are not mirrors of a result in this paper.

So the strongest negative case is narrow but firm: there is no eligible computational anchor here. Real-valued incomes and needs, and the paper’s continuity axiom, do not make the population continuous. I would not claim that no useful computational redistribution problem could ever be built around these ideas; the paper gives no basis for that universal claim. Its proposed continuous mirror is plausible as an axiomatic project, but it falls outside this programme’s computational brief.
