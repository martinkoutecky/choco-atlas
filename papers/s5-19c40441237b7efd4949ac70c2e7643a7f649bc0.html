The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant types \(A\), slot types \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\ge 0\), acceptable edges \(E\subseteq A\times B\), and nonnegative rational costs \(\gamma_{ab}\), choose \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with \(x_{ab}=0\) off \(E\), \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le \mu_a\), and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le \nu_b\). Require that whenever \(s_b:=\sum_a x_{ab}>0\), every \(a\) with \((a,b)\in E\) is fully matched, \(\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}=\mu_a\). First maximize \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), then minimize \(\sum_{a,b}\gamma_{ab}x_{ab}\) among maximizers.

The model it lives in

A capacitated high-multiplicity envy-free assignment with applicant types \(a\), slot types \(b\), masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\), acceptability graph \(E\), mass-assignment variables \(x_{ab}\), and per-unit social costs \(\gamma_{ab}\); occupied slot types force every adjacent applicant type to be completely matched.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s central graph problem, not of its cake-cutting applications.

Take finitely many applicant types \(A\) and slot types \(B\). An applicant type \(a\) is specified by its acceptable slot types \(N(a)\) and, for the weighted version, a social assignment cost \(\gamma(a,b)\). Let \(\mu_a\) be the mass of applicants of type \(a\), and let \(\nu_b\) be the mass of available slots of type \(b\). A mass assignment is \(x_{ab}\geq 0\), with \(x_{ab}=0\) for unacceptable pairs, \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\), and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\).

The assignment is envy-free if no unmatched applicant mass is adjacent to an occupied slot type. Equivalently, whenever \(s_b:=\sum_a x_{ab}>0\), every applicant type \(a\in N(b)\) must be completely matched: \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a\). Thus homogeneous types are matched all-or-nothing, exactly as the paper’s definition forces them to be. Individuals still receive integral slots; \(x\) is only the aggregate assignment of a large population.

I would call the lead problem \(\mathrm{MC\text{-}EFM}_\infty\):

Given rational \((\mu,\nu)\), an acceptability graph, and nonnegative rational costs \(\gamma\), first maximize the matched applicant mass \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\) over envy-free assignments, and then minimize \(\sum_{a,b}\gamma(a,b)x_{ab}\) among those of maximum matched mass. The output is the mass assignment \(x\), or its value.

This is a natural regime for school-seat, public-housing, or hospital-slot assignment: millions of applicants but relatively few eligibility/preference/cost profiles, together with many slots of each category. If \(N\) is the population scale, one has \(N\mu_a\) applicants of type \(a\) and \(N\nu_b\) slots of type \(b\), with \(|A|+|B|\ll N\). The type description includes every feature relevant to the problem, so idiosyncratic costs simply create separate types rather than being discarded.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1.6, proved in this paper. It states that, for a finite bipartite graph \(G=(X\discup Y,E)\), a maximum-cardinality envy-free matching can be found in \(O(m\sqrt{\min(|X|,|Y|)})\) time. It further states that, given nonnegative edge costs, a minimum-cost envy-free matching among those of maximum cardinality can be found in \(O(m\min(|X|,|Y|)+\min(|X|,|Y|)^2\log\min(|X|,|Y|))\) time.

The continuous question above is recognisably the same problem under a high-multiplicity encoding. For rational masses, expand each type into the corresponding number of identical applicants or slots. The paper’s unique EFM partition is invariant under permutations of identical copies, so a type cannot be split between the “good” and “bad” sides. This suggests a compressed version of the paper’s alternating-sequence algorithm: compute a capacitated maximum flow in the network \(s\to a\to b\to t\), use residual reachability to obtain the continuous EFM partition, and then solve a minimum-cost flow problem on the surviving block.

I therefore expect \(\mathrm{MC\text{-}EFM}_\infty\) to be Class A: solvable exactly in time polynomial in \(|A|+|B|\), \(|E|\), and the input bit-length of \(\mu,\nu,\gamma\). The continuous version does not merely allow fractional matching as a shortcut. It preserves the paper’s distinctive envy constraint, while replacing a huge blow-up of identical vertices by capacities. The paper’s structural theorem is precisely what makes that compression plausible.

A secondary, genuine extension is Theorem 6.1(a), also proved here: for every integer \(r\geq1\), there is a polynomial-time algorithm that finds a maximum-cardinality envy-free \(r\)-star matching. Its continuous mirror, \(\mathrm{StarEFM}_{\infty}(r)\), uses the same applicant and slot types, but every selected applicant unit must receive \(r\) distinct acceptable slots, while every unselected type must remain nonadjacent to all occupied slot types. The objective is to maximize the mass of selected applicant types. Formally, this can be represented by \(r\) labelled copies of every applicant type, each of mass \(\mu_a\), matched to slot mass, with all \(r\) copies of a type required to be active together.

For fixed \(r\), this should also be Class A: the paper’s clone construction becomes a capacitated flow construction on \(r|A|\) agent types. If \(r\) is binary-encoded and allowed to be enormous, dependence polynomial in \(\log r\) would be a separate question.

The authors would, I think, recognise these as their problems. Their motivating interpretation already has people and houses, and the continuous model simply studies a large cohort of indistinguishable people and a proportionally large supply of indistinguishable houses. The finite paper is not already a collision: its cake is continuous in the resource space, while its population and matching graph remain finite. Here the population is the object being continuized.

The main weakness is that Theorem 1.6 is already polynomial on an explicitly listed graph. The genuinely new claim is therefore the compressed, bit-complexity version, which the paper does not prove. A referee could also reject the mirror if the intended applications require every house or applicant to be intrinsically unique. I think the standardized school-seat or housing regime answers that objection, but it is a scenario-dependent argument rather than a universal property of fair division.

The paper contains no numbered NP-hardness theorem suitable for anchoring this case. Its NP-hardness statement for partial one-to-many envy-free matching in Section 6.2 is an external consequence of cited results and is followed by an open question, so I would not misrepresent it as an anchor here. The scope of my positive case is therefore deliberately narrow: Theorem 1.6, and the fixed-\(r\) extension in Theorem 6.1(a).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that the paper’s central object is not really a population model. It is a structural problem on a finite incidence graph. Vertex identities matter only through adjacency and edge cost, and duplicating identical vertices does not change the EFM partition; it merely scales the number of matched vertices.

This becomes especially clear in the proposed mass formulation. If \(s_b=\sum_a x_{ab}>0\), envy-freeness requires

\[ \sum_{b'}x_{ab'}=\mu_a \qquad\text{for every }a\in N(b). \]

Consequently, every applicant type that receives positive mass must be completely matched. Each row of \(x\) therefore has total either \(0\) or \(\mu_a\); the population is not divisible at the level at which the paper’s envy condition operates. The only genuinely fractional choice is how an already-selected type is routed among acceptable slot types. If one permits partial matching of a type, one has changed envy-freeness rather than continuized it.

Thus the proposed “continuous” problem is, at best, a capacitated version of the same graph problem. Its natural solution is residual reachability in a capacitated matching network followed by min-cost flow. The distribution \(\mu\) contributes capacities, but not a new population-level phenomenon, a new notion of social choice, or a configuration-LP question. If types are sufficiently heterogeneous that this capacity compression disappears, then the number of types is essentially the number of agents and the high-multiplicity premise has disappeared.

The school-seat or housing story does not fully repair this. It is a plausible application of capacitated matching, but it supplies the semantics externally; the paper itself gives no reason to privilege repeated eligibility/cost profiles over the individualized preferences and priorities that normally define these markets. The proponent is right that idiosyncratic costs may legitimately be incorporated into the type, so this is not a formal objection. It does, however, leave a very narrow mirror: a homogeneous cohort-assignment problem whose continuous version is a standard capacity encoding of Theorem 1.6.

Theorem 6.1(a) adds no independent rescue. For fixed \(r\), its mirror has exactly the same all-or-nothing type activation, with \(r\) layers of capacity. It is a routine lifting of the first construction. If \(r\) is part of the input, the literal clone construction is not polynomial in \(\log r\), but that is a defect of that implementation, not a reason the mirror is worthwhile.

This is the best negative argument, but it is not decisive. The paper does contain named computational results, and the applicant/slot interpretation gives a genuinely sensible high-multiplicity regime. The all-or-nothing condition is not an illegitimate modelling choice; it is exactly what individual envy-freeness becomes for identical agents. Since high-multiplicity work is supporting evidence rather than a collision, I cannot honestly claim that Theorem 1.6 has no worthwhile continuous mirror. The negative case is therefore weak precisely where the proponent is strongest: the capacitated assignment interpretation is natural, even if its mathematics is comparatively routine.
