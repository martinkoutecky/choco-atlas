The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a plausible research lead, but it does not satisfy the paper-specific anchor requirement. I find no numbered result here that establishes a computational complexity classification such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or FPT. The nearest relevant result is Theorem 1.1, Mean-field Quantitative Arrow’s Theorem, \(q=3\), proved in this paper: for \(\beta<3/4\) and \(\epsilon>0\), under the \(n\)-voter mean-field Gibbs model, there is a \(\delta(\epsilon,\beta)>0\), independent of \(n\), such that every IIA constitution either is \(\epsilon\)-close to a dictator or a rule with a fixed top or bottom candidate, or produces an intransitive outcome with probability at least \(\delta\). This is a probabilistic structural theorem, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s unnumbered Remark 2.13 does mention NP-hardness of computing the inversion-tilt normalizer, via weighted FAST, but that is not an eligible numbered anchor and does not concern a continuous population.

There is nevertheless a natural high-multiplicity setting behind Theorem 1.1. Consider large three-candidate electorates in which each person’s complete type is one of the six rankings, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters of ranking type \(t\). Then \(n\) can be large while the support has at most six types. The mean-field interaction depends on aggregate pairwise margins, making this a credible population-mass model. The paper also shows that, for \(\beta<3/4\), the scaled aggregate margins converge to a Gaussian \(Z_\beta\sim N(0,\Sigma_\beta)\) (Lemma 3.10). That limit suggests the following research question, though it is not a qualifying anchor.

Call it the Mean-field Anonymous Arrow Gap. Let \(Z_\beta=(Z_1,Z_2,Z_3)\) have the Gaussian law from Lemma 3.10. A mass-based anonymous IIA constitution is a triple \(\Phi=(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)\) of measurable maps \(\phi_i:\mathbb R\to\{\pm1\}\), one for each pairwise margin. Let \(\mathcal C\) be the class of rules with two fixed, opposite pairwise outcomes, corresponding to a fixed top or bottom candidate, and let \(d_\beta\) be disagreement probability under \(Z_\beta\). Define
\[ \Gamma_\beta(\epsilon)= \inf_{\Phi:\,d_\beta(\Phi,\mathcal C)\ge\epsilon} \Pr\!\left[\phi_1(Z_1)=\phi_2(Z_2)=\phi_3(Z_3)\right]. \]
The equality event is a cycle in the paper’s orientation convention. The problem is to compute or characterize \(\Gamma_\beta(\epsilon)\), with an approximately minimizing rule as a witness. A closely related decision version takes rational \(\beta<3/4\), \(\epsilon\), and \(\delta\), and asks whether there is such a rule at distance at least \(\epsilon\) whose paradox probability is below \(\delta\).

The expected phenomenon is a positive gap for fixed \(\epsilon,\beta\), as Theorem 1.1 suggests. For the optimization problem’s computational class, I would only make a tentative Class A guess: there are three margins and a fixed-dimensional Gaussian law, so a finite-threshold reduction might make the problem tractable. Proving such a reduction is an open question, not a consequence of the paper. Further questions include whether optimal rules can be represented by finitely many thresholds and what happens at the critical value \(\beta=3/4\).

The scope would be narrow: the high-temperature, three-candidate mean-field result, restricted to anonymous rules that depend on population margins. It would not cover the paper’s nonanonymous constitutions, its larger-\(q\) results, or the matching model. The weakest point is also decisive for this assessment: the paper studies probabilistic and analytic properties of finite-agent constitutions; it does not classify the complexity of the mass-based aggregation problem above. Moreover, dictators have no direct representation as functions of a nonatomic population state. So this is a credible continuous-population research direction, but presenting it as a computational mirror anchored by this paper would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1.1 is a probabilistic structural guarantee about all IIA constitutions under a finite-agent random model; Theorems 1.2–1.5 give asymptotic probabilities and related guarantees. None classifies an algorithmic problem. Remark 2.13 mentions NP-hardness of a normalizer, but it is unnumbered and concerns a finite-permutation quantity. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, a new computational question inspired by these results cannot supply the missing anchor.

The proposed Mean-field Anonymous Arrow Gap is the best attempt to create one, but it changes the object. In the high-temperature regime, the empirical distribution over the six rankings converges to the uniform distribution. The Gaussian in Lemma 3.10 describes fluctuations around that distribution at scale \(\sqrt n\); it is not itself a population distribution over voter types. An exact continuum society therefore has a single, zero-margin state, so its deterministic mass-based rule has no paradox probability to optimize. Retaining the paper’s nontrivial probability requires carrying along the fluctuation scale and its random law. That may support an interesting analytic question, but it makes finite-population noise—not computation over continuous societies—the object being studied.

There is a further gap between the proposed \(\Gamma_\beta(\epsilon)\) and a complexity result. Its variables are arbitrary measurable functions, with no finite encoding or input-size measure. Restricting them to thresholds might make the optimization finite-dimensional, but showing that this restriction preserves the optimum is a new theorem, and it would change the class of constitutions under study. The original theorem’s dictator exception also refers to dependence on a named voter, which an atomless population does not represent. Replacing that exception with a mass-level one may be reasonable, but again it defines a new analytic problem rather than a computational mirror of Theorem 1.1.

I would reject this as a ChoCo mirror, while conceding that the Gaussian optimization could be worthwhile in probability or aggregation theory. The universal claim that no continuous-population scenario could ever produce a useful computational problem is much harder to defend: one could add a finite representation for rules and ask for algorithms over rational masses. But that would be a new problem whose computational content comes from the added representation, not from a computational result in this paper.
