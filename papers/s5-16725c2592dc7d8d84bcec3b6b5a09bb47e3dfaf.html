The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(G\) of \(q\) standardized indivisible item classes with rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\), and a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) complete agent types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), output variables \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) for \(S\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=b_g\), such that \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S))\ge \mathrm{OPT}_\infty/(4+\varepsilon)\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(q\), input length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), independently of the common denominator \(N\) after clearing rational masses and supplies. Welfare is zero if positive mass receives a zero-valued bundle.

The model it lives in

A standardized repeated-inventory fair-division model with finite valuation types, population masses \(\mu\), per-capita supplies \(b\), and mass variables \(x_{t,S}\) distributing agents over integral bundles \(S\); the objective is population Nash welfare and the target is a denominator-independent approximation.

The objection that survived

The item-class and saturation assumptions make this a restricted repeated-inventory variant rather than a uniform high-multiplicity lift of every arbitrary named-good instance; this limits direct algorithmic transfer but does not defeat the explicitly scoped mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2 through high-multiplicity NSW and support-wise \(\tfrac12\)-EFX; it leaves the internal matching lemmas, local-optimality lemmas, later remarks, and related-work hardness claims untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper's \((4+\varepsilon)\) guarantee be implemented in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(q\), input length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), without dependence on \(N\)?
  • What is the complexity of separating the configuration formulation, in particular pricing configurations by maximizing \(\log v_t(S)-p(S)\)?
  • Can the support-wise continuous \(\tfrac12\)-EFX condition be enforced or approximated efficiently despite its nonconvex dependence on the support?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Nash social welfare problem. The paper is not itself a continuous-population paper: its agents are named and its goods are indivisible. That makes the following a genuine mirror rather than a collision.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper. It states that, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), asymmetric NSW with monotone submodular valuations has a deterministic polynomial-time \((n w_{\max}+2+\varepsilon)\mathrm e\)-approximation; in the symmetric case it gives a \((4+\varepsilon)\)-approximation. The number of arithmetic operations and value-oracle calls is polynomial in \(n\), \(m\), and \(1/\varepsilon\).

Call the mirror Continuous High-Multiplicity Submodular NSW.

An instance has a finite set \(G\) of \(q\) item classes, with rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\in[0,1]\). Thus a population of size \(N\) has \(Nb_g\) physical copies of item class \(g\), after clearing denominators. It also has finitely many complete agent types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type includes its monotone submodular valuation oracle \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), its entitlement density \(\rho_t>0\), and every other parameter relevant to allocation. Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_t\rho_t=1\). The symmetric case is \(\rho_t=1\) for every type.

The decision variable is \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\). It must satisfy \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=b_g\) for each item class \(g\). Thus every unit of population receives a whole bundle, and every item copy is allocated; only the distribution of integral bundles across a large repeated population is fractional.

The objective is the population Nash welfare

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) = \exp\!\left(\sum_{t,S}\rho_t x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right), \]

with value \(0\) if positive mass receives a zero-valued bundle. The problem asks for a finite-support feasible \(x\) whose welfare is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty/(4+\varepsilon)\) in the symmetric case, or at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty/(\mathrm e(\rho_{\max}+2+\varepsilon))\) in the asymmetric case, in time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), \(q\), the input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), independently of the common population denominator \(N\).

This is not merely divisible-goods NSW. If \(\mu_t\), \(b_g\), and \(x_{t,S}\) are rational, clearing denominators gives a finite allocation of \(N\) named agents to integral bundles and \(Nb_g\) physical item copies. Conversely, every such high-multiplicity finite allocation induces an \(x\). The bridge is exact.

The regime is plausible for large-scale allocation of standardized inventories: millions of households, applicants, or beneficiaries; perhaps \(q=20\)–\(100\) item classes; and a much smaller number of repeated valuation and entitlement types. For example, households may have coverage-style submodular preferences over standard packages, with the same eligibility and entitlement parameters within a type. The relevant comparison is \(N\gg\tau,q\), not merely “fair division is continuous.” If every household has an idiosyncratic valuation or every item is unique, this mirror should not be claimed.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. It preserves the NSW objective, submodular valuation model, integral bundles, complete allocation, and asymmetric entitlements. Their matching/local-search/rematching architecture also suggests a route: initial item-to-population matching becomes a transportation problem; local exchanges move mass between type–bundle configurations; and the rematching stage becomes a flow or configuration-allocation problem. The natural continuous formulation is an exponential-column LP, with configurations \(S\) as columns, so the paper’s matching and pricing perspective becomes directly relevant.

I would provisionally expect this approximation problem to be Class A. The finite NP-hardness discussion in the paper is not a named theorem and is driven by small-agent, item-specific instances; it does not automatically survive when repeated item classes and rational population masses allow a type to be split across many integral bundles. The substantive open questions are whether the paper’s \(4+\varepsilon\) analysis compresses to type level, whether arbitrary submodular value oracles permit the required pricing or local search, and how much dependence on \(\tau\) and \(q\) is necessary. Exact optimization could still be hard, even if constant-factor approximation is tractable.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Corollary 1.2, also proved here. It states that, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm that returns a complete \(\tfrac12\)-EFX allocation that is an \((8+\varepsilon)\)-approximation to symmetric NSW under submodular valuations; its arithmetic operations and value-oracle calls are polynomial in \(n\), \(m\), and \(1/\varepsilon\).

The corresponding mirror is Continuous Complete \(\tfrac12\)-EFX NSW. It uses the same instance and variables \(x\), but requires the following population-level fairness condition: for every represented type \(t\), every bundle \(S\) with \(x_{t,S}>0\), every represented type \(u\), every bundle \(R\) with \(x_{u,R}>0\), and every \(g\in R\),

\[ v_t(S)\ge \tfrac12\,v_t(R\setminus\{g\}). \]

The question is to output a complete feasible \(x\) satisfying this condition and

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) \ge \frac{\mathrm{OPT}_\infty}{8+\varepsilon}, \]

where \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\) is the unconstrained continuous NSW optimum. This is exactly the finite \(\tfrac12\)-EFX condition lifted from named agents to all positive-mass bundles: every member of every represented type must be protected against every other bundle in the support.

I would also initially expect this problem to be Class A, because the paper’s fairness proof already uses bundle trimming, alternating paths, matching, and envy-cycle operations—all structures that have natural type-level or mass-level analogues. But the universal support condition is a genuine possible Class C boundary: a continuous population may use several bundles per type, and deciding whether a sparse support simultaneously satisfies all EFX inequalities could itself become difficult. That is a useful question rather than a defect in the mirror.

The scope of this case is deliberately limited to Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2. I would not use the paper’s informal statements about NP-hardness of exact NSW as hardness anchors: they occur in related-work prose and cite other papers, rather than appearing as numbered computational results proved or stated here.

The weakest point is that Theorem 1.1’s polynomial algorithm is polynomial in the number of named agents, not obviously in the number of types. Its local-search proof may require a nontrivial mass-preserving reformulation, and arbitrary submodular pricing may be hard. So this is a credible continuous research problem, not a theorem that follows mechanically from the paper. The case nevertheless survives because the mirror preserves the paper’s central object—fair allocation of indivisible bundles under submodular preferences—while replacing only repeated named agents by population mass, with an exact finite/high-multiplicity correspondence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed mirrors silently change the resource model, and the paper’s polynomial algorithms do not survive the compression.

For Theorem 1.1, the proposed \(q\) item classes are not merely a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s \(m\) indivisible items. The theorem allows an arbitrary submodular oracle on named goods. Replacing physical goods by classes assumes that all copies are interchangeable and that an agent receives at most one copy of each class. Neither assumption is present in the theorem. Even a copy-symmetric additive valuation may value two copies of one class, whereas the proposed bundle \(S\subseteq G\) forbids that. Clearing denominators therefore recovers a different capacitated, unit-demand-per-class problem, not the original NSW instance.

Keeping the physical copies would not rescue the claimed compression: an arbitrary valuation oracle on \(Nq\) goods cannot be represented using only \(q\) class parameters. Imposing symmetry or saturation on the copies produces a new valuation model. Likewise, with a fixed inventory, \(N\to\infty\) makes NSW zero once there are more agents than goods, so the meaningful limit requires per-capita supply scaling. That scaling is defensible, but it confirms that the proposed object is a new repeated-inventory configuration problem rather than a population-only continuation of Theorem 1.1.

The paper’s algorithm also gives no compressed algorithm for that new problem. Its matching and local-search phases operate on named agents and individual goods. The natural formulation is instead the configuration LP

\[ \max \sum_{t,S}\rho_t x_{t,S}\log v_t(S), \]

with exponentially many bundle configurations. Its pricing problem is essentially

\[ \max_{S\subseteq G}\left(\log v_t(S)-p(S)\right), \]

which is nontrivial, and can inherit the hardness of optimizing submodular valuations against prices. This may be a worthwhile Class A or Class C research problem, but it is not a consequence of the paper’s theorem. The proponent’s exact finite/high-multiplicity correspondence supports the model’s legitimacy; it does not establish that the paper’s result has been continuized algorithmically.

Corollary 1.2 is even less amenable to the proposed compression. The paper’s \(\tfrac12\)-EFX guarantee is pointwise: every individual bundle must be compared with every other individual bundle. Averaging EFX over a type would not preserve the result. For example, half of a type could receive a bundle of value \(0\) and half a bundle of value \(1\), while the average satisfies the relaxed inequality even though the zero-valued agents violate EFX.

The faithful alternative is the proponent’s support-wise condition. But then the problem is no longer a straightforward mass analogue of the paper’s proof. EFX depends on the support \(\{S:x_{t,S}>0\}\), so feasibility is a union of faces of the configuration polytope and is generally nonconvex. The envy-cycle proof uses a graph whose vertices are named agents and whose updates remove individual items; replacing it by type masses does not preserve that structure. Moreover, “finite support” is not a compactness result here: with \(q\) item classes there are \(2^q\) possible bundles, so a finite support may still be exponentially large.

Thus I can defeat the claims that the algorithms lift naturally and that the proposed formulations are exact mirrors of the paper. I cannot honestly defeat the repaired high-multiplicity NSW model itself: repeated agent types, scaled supplies, integral bundles per individual, and mass distributed across configurations form a coherent and recognisably worthwhile problem. The \(\tfrac12\)-EFX version is a weaker, more speculative extension, but its support-wise formulation is also legitimate. The universal negative therefore does not hold; the strongest honest verdict is that the proponent overstated the directness and algorithmic payoff of both mirrors, especially the EFX one.
