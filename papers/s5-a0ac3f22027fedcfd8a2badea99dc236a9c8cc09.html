The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(C\), a finite set of complete student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita capacities \(q_c\), and an added-capacity budget \(b\), choose \(a_c\geq 0\) and assignment masses \(x_{tc}\geq 0\) (including \(x_{t,\emptyset}\)) minimizing \(\sum_{t,c} r_{tc}x_{tc}\), subject to \(\sum_c x_{tc}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tc}\leq q_c+a_c\), and \(\sum_c a_c\leq b\). For each type \(t\) and school \(c\), require \(\min\{L_{tc},A_{tc}\}=0\), where \(L_{tc}=\sum_{d:\,c\succ_t d}x_{td}\) and \(A_{tc}=q_c+a_c-\sum_u x_{uc}+\sum_{u:\,t\succ_c u}x_{uc}\). Determine the optimal capacity vector and stable assignment, and investigate algorithms parameterized by \(m\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic admissions cohort with finitely many complete preference and priority types, each carrying mass \(\mu_t\). Choose per-capita capacity additions \(a_c\) and type-to-school assignment masses \(x_{tc}\) to minimize the rank and unassigned penalty, subject to the budget, capacity, and mass-stability conditions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns capacity planning and stability separation over type masses. It leaves aside the paper’s cited hardness and inapproximability results, mechanism properties, and Chilean computational findings.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can mass stability be enforced or separated efficiently with complexity depending on the number of types and input bit length?
  • How does the optimum compare with the discrete high-multiplicity problem when type masses are rational proportions?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Type-Mass Capacity Planning. The paper’s numbered computational anchor is Theorem 3, proved in the paper: given a candidate fractional matching and an integer capacity expansion, its algorithm finds, for each school in the relevant set, a least-valued generalized comb and detects a violated comb if the candidate is unstable. The running time is \(O(mn\bar q)\), where \(n\) is the number of students, \(m\) the number of schools, and \(\bar q=\max_c(q_c+t_c^\star)\). This is a polynomial-time result for the stability-separation problem used in their cutting-plane method; it does not establish polynomial time for the full capacity-planning problem. [Theorem 3 in the authors’ manuscript](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.00734)

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance has schools \(C\), a finite set of complete student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita capacities \(q_c\), and an additional-seat budget \(b\). A type specifies a student’s complete school ranking, rank cost including the penalty for being unassigned, and priority category at each school. Each school has a strict priority order over types; students of the same type share that priority category. Choose added capacities \(a_c\geq 0\) with \(\sum_c a_c\leq b\), and assignment masses \(x_{tc}\geq 0\), including \(x_{t,\emptyset}\), with \(\sum_c x_{tc}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tc}\leq q_c+a_c\).

Require stability in the mass sense. For type \(t\) and school \(c\), let \(L_{tc}=\sum_{d:\,c\succ_t d}x_{td}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned below \(c\), and let \(A_{tc}=q_c+a_c-\sum_u x_{uc}+\sum_{u:\,t\succ_c u}x_{uc}\), the vacancy plus the mass at \(c\) with lower priority than \(t\). Stability requires \(\min\{L_{tc},A_{tc}\}=0\) for every \(t,c\): no positive mass wants a school that has room for them or holds lower-priority students. The goal is to minimize \(\sum_{t,c}r_{tc}x_{tc}\), the population’s average rank-and-unassigned penalty. A solution is the optimal capacity vector and stable type-to-school mass assignment; the atomless population can be partitioned so each individual attends one school.

The regime is a large centralized admissions cohort with repeated preference lists and priority categories: for example, many thousands of students but tens or hundreds of distinct complete profiles. The paper reports a Chilean system serving more than half a million students annually, and discusses priority groups and lotteries, so capacity planning at population scale is already central to its setting. [The authors’ manuscript](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.00734) The mirror preserves the joint choice of where to add capacity, stable assignment in the expanded market, and the rank-based objective. Fractional capacities are the natural per-capita version of seats, while fractional \(x_{tc}\) records how much of a type goes to each school.

I would initially bet on Class A, tractability through a continuous optimization formulation and a separation method whose size depends on \(m,\tau,L\), rather than on the number of individuals. Theorem 3 gives a real foothold: the paper already computes with fractional matching variables and exploits the structure of stability cuts. The continuous version asks whether that structure survives when both mass and capacity are divisible. Further questions are whether the stability constraints admit a tractable separation oracle, how the optimum compares with the discrete high-multiplicity instance, and how to represent lottery scores without making every student a distinct type.

The soft spot is the priority data. In a regime with person-specific lottery numbers that affect school rankings, complete types may be nearly unique and \(\tau\) may approach \(n\). This mirror fits most cleanly where priorities are shared by category, with ties within a type; that is a real co-modification of the paper’s strict individual priorities, even though it preserves its central matching-and-capacity question. Also, the paper’s NP-hardness claim is cited to Bobbio et al. (2022), rather than given as a numbered hardness theorem here, so I do not use it as an anchor or claim its hardness transfers. The scope of this case is the capacity-planning optimization and its stability-separation computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 3’s separation algorithm. It is a genuine computational result, but its input is an assignment indexed by named students and a fixed vector of integer seat expansions. Its \(O(mn\bar q)\) running time is polynomial in the expanded market, not in the support size \(\tau\) and bit length \(L\) of a high-multiplicity input. Replacing rational masses by that many individual copies can make \(n\) and \(\bar q\) grow with the mass denominator. So Theorem 3 offers no compressed-population algorithm to carry over.

There is also a modeling fork. In the paper’s Chilean application, actual lottery numbers help determine strict school priorities. A complete type that preserves those priorities must include the lottery score. With a continuum of scores, the finite type-mass model in the proponent’s proposal no longer applies; keeping the realized scores gives support that grows with the cohort. Omitting the scores and ordering only priority categories instead treats students as tied. The proposed \(A_{tc}\) stability condition then describes a weak, fractional assignment, not the student-optimal matching under the paper’s realized strict priorities. Restoring the scores in a continuum would require a new model and input representation, and Theorem 3’s discrete combs do not supply its separation method.

The strongest repair is to plan capacities against a distribution of lottery scores before those scores are drawn. That is a coherent large-market question, but it changes the timing and asks for an expected or distribution-level policy rather than capacity planning for the realized cohort. It might still be useful to the authors: the paper itself discusses making decisions without lottery information. I therefore cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror. The strongest case against is narrower: the cited theorem does not anchor the proposed finite-type continuous problem, and a faithful lottery-aware version needs new computational foundations. The negative case is weakest against that ex-ante quantile model.
