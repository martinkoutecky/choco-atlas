The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results establish stochastic limits, welfare formulas, and fairness properties; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The closest bridge is Theorem 6.2, proved here: if, for each rank \(s\), the fraction of agents up to queue position \(\theta\) receiving rank \(s\) converges in probability to \(\int_0^\theta q_s(\phi)\,d\phi\), with \(\sum_s q_s(\theta)=1\), and the positional scores satisfy \(\sigma_n(s)\to\lambda_s\), then normalized welfare converges in probability to
\[ \sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\lambda_s\int_0^\theta q_s(\phi)\,d\phi. \]
That is an asymptotic welfare theorem, not a computational-complexity result, so it cannot serve as the requested anchor.

The strongest positive connection is a fluid-limit model of the paper’s welfare analysis. Give each agent a preference ranking \(\pi\) and a normalized position \(\theta\) in the tie-breaking order. In a continuum version of the Boston or serial-dictatorship process, mass follows the same round-by-round choices, with earlier priority mass resolving conflicts over available items. The objective is the mass assigned each preference rank, or its aggregate score \(W=\sum_s\lambda_s q_s\). Theorem 6.2 supplies the welfare functional, while the paper’s other limit results derive the relevant rank distributions for its mechanisms. This is a faithful analytical mirror of what the authors study, but the paper already derives those limits; it does not pose a new algorithmic question about them.

There is also a regime problem. The paper has \(n\) agents and \(n\) distinct items, so the ranking type space grows with \(n\). Under impartial culture, there is no evident regime with many agents but relatively few distinct complete rankings. Keeping a fixed finite set of ranking types would require changing the growing inventory into categories with per-capita supplies, which moves toward multi-unit assignment. That may be a plausible related model, but this paper’s results do not anchor its computational complexity.

So the strongest honest positive case is an analytical continuum interpretation of the welfare limits, not a continuous computational mirror of a named complexity result. The weakest point is decisive under the requested standard: there is no qualifying anchor, and the paper already performs the limiting analysis that the closest mirror would ask for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This paper has no computational result to mirror. Its theorems establish stochastic limits, welfare formulas, and fairness properties; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result in the programme’s sense. The closest anchor, Theorem 6.2, takes limiting rank distributions \(q_s\) as given and derives a welfare limit from them. It does not pose an input-dependent computation.

The best direct mirror is a fluid version of the paper’s Boston and serial-dictatorship processes. But for the impartial-culture setting, the paper already derives the limiting rank distributions and uses them to calculate welfare and order bias. Theorem 6.2 packages that analysis; a continuous process reproducing it would restate an analytic limit, not open a computational question.

A stronger variant could take a distribution over a fixed set of ranking types, give item categories per-capita supplies, and track priority position so the model can still express order bias. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity problem, and the co-modification of supplies is legitimate. But it changes the paper’s setting of \(n\) agents, \(n\) distinct items, and uniformly random rankings over a type space growing with \(n\). More importantly, any computational question about evaluating or optimizing that variant would be a new problem: the paper supplies no complexity result to anchor it. The same is true of adding manipulation or mechanism choice.

So the negative case is strong for this paper’s results: its direct continuous counterpart is already analytic, while computational variants require a new problem rather than continuizing a named result. The limit of the case is that one cannot rule out a worthwhile computational project inspired by these mechanisms. Whether such a project belongs to the authors’ problem would need separate evidence.
