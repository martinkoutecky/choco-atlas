The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying anchor: the paper has no numbered result about computational complexity—no hardness classification, algorithmic running time, or computational formulation it solves. Its numbered results concern mechanism existence and equilibrium behavior.

The closest positive anchors are Theorem 3.1, proved here: if source identifiability fails, no mechanism using the reports and model specification can make truthful reporting a strict Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Theorem 3.2, also proved here using peer-prediction machinery, says that under source identifiability and Assumption 1, a truthful strategy profile is a strict equilibrium for a suitable feasible signal distribution, and every less-informative equilibrium has lower total expected payoff than a truthful one. Lemma 4.1 adds that a positive probability of an unconditionally honest observer makes the truthful equilibrium unique. These are substantial results, but none is a computational-complexity result of the kind the requested anchor rule requires. Proposition 5.3’s convex-hull characterization is likewise a geometric identifiability result, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible population extension behind them. In a large DePIN sensor network, let a type specify everything relevant to an observer—location or hardware, signal channel, report options, incentives, and honesty status—and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s share of observers. The source remains a distinguished agent; the mechanism samples observers by mass, and strategies are indexed by type and signal. One could ask whether a prior-free mechanism makes truthful reporting a strict equilibrium, and whether positive mass of potentially honest observers yields uniqueness. That is a credible continuization of the paper’s observer population, rather than of its outcomes. But its question is still about equilibrium implementation, not computational complexity, and it is a proposed extension rather than a mirror of a qualifying named result.

So the strongest honest case is a plausible research direction, not an anchor-backed ChoCo case. Its soft spot is decisive under the requested standard: the paper does not chart the computational landscape that the continuization programme is meant to study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has effectively conceded the decisive point: none of the named anchors is a computational result. Theorem 3.1 is an impossibility about truthful equilibrium, Theorem 3.2 is an equilibrium-existence result, and Lemma 4.1 is an equilibrium-uniqueness result. Proposition 5.3 gives a geometric identifiability condition. None studies worst-case complexity or an algorithm for a computational problem. So the proposed observer-population extension does not supply the kind of result this programme is set up to mirror.

The best version of that extension would put a mass distribution over observer types, where a type includes everything relevant about an observer: location or hardware, signal channel, incentives, and honesty status. It could ask whether a mass-based mechanism elicits reports truthfully, or whether positive mass of honest observers gives uniqueness. That is a coherent population model, especially for a large sensor network. But the paper’s general model lets the source choose a joint distribution over the \(n\) observers’ signals. A distribution over individual types alone does not capture those cross-observer dependencies. Preserving them requires additional joint structure; imposing independent or exchangeable signals to avoid that complication narrows the model. Either way, the new model needs its own input representation and computational question.

The individual anchors fare no better as continuization targets. For Theorem 3.1, source identifiability is a property of the feasible distribution sets \(L_x\), not a result about the number of observers. For Theorem 3.2, the proposed type-based question concerns whether the equilibrium guarantee extends to a different population model; the paper’s mechanism already gives the existence result, with no computational challenge identified. For Lemma 4.1, replacing a positive chance of meeting an honest observer with a positive mass of honest types is a natural restatement, but still an equilibrium claim rather than a complexity result. And for Proposition 5.3, sensor multiplicities do not change the convex hull of their locations; the mass formulation supplies no computational landscape to chart.

One could define fresh computational tasks—for example, deciding source identifiability from a specified encoding of type-dependent signal laws. The paper supplies neither that encoding nor a complexity result for it. Such work might be worthwhile, but it would be a new agenda built on the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of one of its named results. The negative case is strongest on that boundary. It is weaker as a universal claim: large observer networks are plausible high-multiplicity settings, and their mass-based incentive properties could merit study even if this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor.
