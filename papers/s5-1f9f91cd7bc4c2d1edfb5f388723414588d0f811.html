The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) and a finite list of complete ranking types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether there is an order \((c_1,\ldots,c_m)\) such that, for each consecutive pair \((a,b)\) among the first two candidates, both directional first-two ranking masses \(p_{ab}\) and \(p_{ba}\) are positive, and every later candidate has at least two connected predecessors.

The model it lives in

The society is a finite-support distribution over complete rankings. Construct a graph on candidates with edge \(ab\) exactly when \(p_{ab}>0\) and \(p_{ba}>0\), where \(p_{ab}\) is the mass of rankings placing \(a\) first and \(b\) second; decide whether the graph has a linked order.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s linked-domain recognition result. It leaves aside the paper’s brief observation about adapting the algorithm to \(\beta\)-domains and does not address the strategy-proofness theorem that motivates linked domains.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is continuous linked-domain recognition. It keeps the paper’s question intact while describing the electorate by type shares.

An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a list of \(\tau\) ranking types \(t\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The type is a voter’s complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. For an ordered pair \((a,b)\), let \(p_{ab}\) be the total mass of types ranking \(a\) first and \(b\) second. Call \(a,b\) connected exactly when \(p_{ab}>0\) and \(p_{ba}>0\). The problem asks whether there is an order \((c_1,\ldots,c_m)\) in which \(c_1,c_2\) are connected and each later candidate has at least two connected predecessors. A linked order is a certificate for YES; NO means no such order exists.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: determining whether an election is linked can be done in polynomial time. The paper builds the graph whose vertices are candidates and whose edges are connected pairs, then tests possible connected first pairs. For each pair, it greedily adds any remaining candidate with at least two neighbors already placed. If it gets stuck, no linked order can extend that starting pair: the first candidate outside the greedy prefix in any proposed linked order would need two neighbors inside the prefix, contradicting the reason the algorithm got stuck. If any trial reaches all candidates, its order certifies YES. For the continuous mirror, the graph is built from the positive directional masses \(p_{ab}\); scanning the \(\tau\) types and running the trials takes time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length.

The natural regime is a large electorate voting on a fixed or modest-sized candidate slate, with many voters sharing a relatively small number of complete rankings: \(n\gg\tau\). A ranking type is a complete description for this problem, and its mass is the population quantity the recognition test uses. The paper’s authors should recognize this as their question: positive mass in each direction preserves precisely the paper’s “there are two votes” condition. Every finite election maps to rational type shares with the same connectedness graph; conversely, any rational type-share list can be scaled to integer vote counts. The continuous problem is therefore a compressed, high-multiplicity version of the same recognition problem.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. It covers Theorem 1’s recognition result only; the paper provides no named hardness result to anchor another mirror. It does not claim that continuity dissolves hardness, since the discrete problem is already in P. Its value is a faithful population-level formulation and an algorithm stated in support size and bit length.

A natural follow-up would ask for *robust* linkedness: for a threshold \(\epsilon>0\), is there a linked order whose required pairs each have at least \(\epsilon\) mass in both directions? That is a new quantitative question, not Theorem 1’s mirror.

The weakest point is that the exact mirror depends only on whether each directional mass is zero. A tiny positive group counts just as much as a large one, so the mass values otherwise play no role. This makes the mirror honest and recognizable, but gives it little of the analytic or algorithmic payoff that motivates ChoCo’s harder examples.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational result, so I can’t defeat the anchor by claiming the paper has nothing computational to mirror. Nor is there a good case that high multiplicity is implausible: a large electorate with many voters sharing rankings is a sensible regime, and the mirror preserves the paper’s test exactly for rational vote shares.

The strongest objection is narrower. Linkedness depends on the *support* of the electorate, not on its mass distribution. Aggregate voters by their first-two-candidate type and write \(p_{ab}\) for the mass ranking \(a\) first and \(b\) second. The mirror’s graph has edge \(ab\) exactly when \(p_{ab}>0\) and \(p_{ba}>0\). Once those zero-versus-positive facts are known, every other feature of the continuous society is irrelevant. Reweighting types by arbitrarily large or small amounts leaves the recognition question unchanged. This is not a reason to reject the formal mirror, but it limits its claim to population continuization: the theorem’s predicate is about witnesses in the support, rather than quantities of population.

That support dependence also makes the predicate unstable as a limit property. A society can be linked because two reciprocal witness types each have mass \(\varepsilon\), yet converge, as \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero, to a society with neither witness. The exact rational-share model remains well-defined, but it treats every positive mass as decisive, however small. Requiring a minimum mass would address that instability, but it changes the question. For example, one could maximize the minimum reciprocal mass required by a linked order. That is a plausible weighted robustness problem; it is not the recognition theorem in this paper, whose linked-domain premise is existence of witnesses, not prevalence. The paper gives no result connecting such a thresholded condition to its strategy-proofness motivation.

So the best negative case is about priority, not impossibility: the exact mirror is faithful, but its continuous masses contribute only their support, and the more population-sensitive replacement changes the paper’s condition. I don’t think that is enough to sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proponent’s anchor survives; the honest reservation is that its payoff for ChoCo may be modest.
