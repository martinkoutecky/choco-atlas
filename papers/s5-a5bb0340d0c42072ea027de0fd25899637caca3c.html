The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bundle set \(B\), voter or student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita course supplies \(q_j\), and integral bundles for each individual, let \(\Omega\) be the feasible mass plans \(x_{t,b}\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}a_j(b)x_{t,b}\le q_j\). Given a sampler for a fair prior \(F\) over \(\Omega\), a mechanism returning \(A\in\Omega\) with \(W(A)\ge\lambda\max_{x\in\Omega}W(x)\), and \(\alpha,\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), construct a sampler for a lottery \(Q_\varepsilon\) over plans such that \(\operatorname{TV}(Q_\varepsilon,F)\le\alpha\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W(Q_\varepsilon)]\ge\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha\), where \(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha=\sup_{Q:\operatorname{TV}(Q,F)\le\alpha}\mathbb{E}[W(Q)]\). Theorem 3.1's algorithm uses \(s=\left\lceil 8((1-\alpha)\varepsilon^2)^{-1}\log(2/\varepsilon)\right\rceil\) samples from \(F\), in addition to the supplied welfare mechanism.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity course-allocation market with a large population represented by \(\tau\) types of rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision variable \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(b\); per-capita capacities constrain the feasible plans \(\Omega\), and welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}u_t(b)\). A supplied fair-prior sampler gives a lottery over these plans, and the paper's algorithm produces a new lottery close in total variation while retaining its welfare guarantee. The population is the continuous object; the lottery remains the paper's ex-ante fairness mechanism.

What the mirror covers

Covers the generic interpolation guarantees of Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 for lotteries over feasible type-level allocation plans; it leaves implementation of the fair-prior sampler and welfare mechanism, and the paper's reviewer-matching and sortition experiments, unanalyzed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a concrete anonymous fair-prior policy, can its sampler and a \(\lambda\)-approximate welfare mechanism be implemented in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the bundle data, and input bit length \(L\)?
  • How does restricting plans to the finite-population grid \(x_{t,b}\in N^{-1}\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) affect the optimum and interpolation guarantees as population size \(N\) grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is a large course-allocation market. Think of many students competing for seats in a fixed catalogue of courses. A student’s type records their complete preferences and utilities over course bundles, plus eligibility or other parameters the allocation problem uses. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of students of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime of interest has a large student population \(N\) but a much smaller support of types \(\tau\); course capacities scale with \(N\), so \(q_j\) is the per-student supply of course \(j\).

For a finite set \(B\) of allowed bundles, an allocation plan is a vector \(x=(x_{t,b})\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\). It satisfies \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and the course-capacity constraints \(\sum_{t,b}a_j(b)x_{t,b}\le q_j\), where each person’s bundle \(b\) is still integral. Thus fractional quantities describe how the population is divided among bundles; they do not give an individual a fractional course. Let \(\Omega\) be the feasible plans and \(W(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}u_t(b)\) be per-capita welfare.

Take as input a sampler for a fair prior \(F\) over \(\Omega\), a welfare mechanism returning a plan \(A\) with \(W(A)\ge\lambda\max_{x\in\Omega}W(x)\), and \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Fairness is the paper’s own ex-ante notion: the output lottery \(Q\) over plans must have \(\operatorname{TV}(Q,F)\le\alpha\). Define \(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha=\sup_{Q:\operatorname{TV}(Q,F)\le\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[W(X)]\). For lotteries on this continuous plan space, total variation is \(\sup_E|Q(E)-F(E)|\). The fair prior could be an anonymous randomized allocation policy supplied with the instance, as in the paper’s sampling model.

There are no named hardness or complexity-class results in the paper. The strongest anchors are its two proved algorithmic guarantees.

Theorem 3.1, proved here, says that for any FWI instance, Algorithm \(\emix\) outputs an \(\alpha\)-fair distribution with welfare at least \(\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha\), using samples from the fair prior. The theorem states \(O(1/\varepsilon^2)\) samples; Algorithm 3’s displayed sample count is \(\left\lceil 8((1-\alpha)\varepsilon^2)^{-1}\log(2/\varepsilon)\right\rceil\). The corresponding continuous problem, Continuum-FWI-\(\varepsilon\), is: given the type masses, supplies, utilities, fair-prior sampler and \(\lambda\)-approximation mechanism just specified, produce a sampler for \(Q_\varepsilon\) such that \(\operatorname{TV}(Q_\varepsilon,F)\le\alpha\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W(Q_\varepsilon)]\ge\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha\). This is my lead anchor.

Theorem 4.1, also proved here, says that \(\smix\), which outputs \(A\) with probability \(\alpha\) and otherwise draws once from \(F\), is \(\alpha\)-fair and achieves welfare at least \(\min\{\lambda,\alpha\lambda+(1-\alpha)^2\}\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha\). Its mirror, Continuum-Simple-Mix, asks for a sampler for \(Q=\alpha\delta_A+(1-\alpha)F\) with those same fairness and welfare guarantees, using the given mechanism and one fair-prior draw. This is a useful second anchor: it captures the paper’s zero-overhead interpolation result as well as its stronger sampling-based guarantee.

These are Class A questions for interpolation, conditional on the supplied sampler and welfare mechanism being efficient. In the course model above, maximizing additive welfare over \(\Omega\) is a linear program when bundles are listed, so the welfare mechanism can be exact. The paper’s results then give the interpolation guarantee around that mechanism; their runtime does not include the cost of implementing the fair-prior sampler. The discrete dictionary is also direct: a finite instance with \(N\mu_t\) students of each type and \(Nq_j\) seats corresponds to a grid-restricted set of plans, whereas the continuous plans permit arbitrary mass splits.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem because it keeps their central object intact: a fair lottery over feasible allocations, adjusted toward welfare under a total-variation constraint. Their framework explicitly treats the fair mechanism as a sampler and the welfare mechanism as a black box, so a continuum of type masses fits its formal assumptions unusually well. The primary continuization is the society: the input records \(\tau\) masses rather than \(N\) named students. Describing allocations by type-level mass is the co-modification that makes those agents allocable while keeping each person’s bundle integral. The lottery over outcomes is retained because it is the paper’s fairness concept, not offered as the continuization itself.

The mirror covers the generic FWI framework and Theorems 3.1 and 4.1. It does not claim a continuous complexity analysis of the paper’s reviewer-matching or sortition experiments. Follow-on questions include whether a particular anonymous fair-prior mechanism has an efficient sampler in the \((\tau,L)\) representation; how accurately discrete finite-population mechanisms approximate that sampler; and whether the guarantees extend when welfare or feasibility constraints make the base optimization problem hard.

My weakest point is that the paper’s theorems are deliberately indifferent to the population model. Their interpolation algorithms can be applied to many solution spaces, so this mirror yields no new population-dependent complexity result by itself. A referee might ask whether the continuous market is just a new application of an already generic theorem. I think it is still a sensible mirror: the continuous type-mass allocation model is a concrete high-multiplicity problem the authors could recognize, and their result supplies a meaningful algorithmic answer for its interpolation layer. But the contribution would stand or fall on analyzing the continuous market’s own sampler and allocation mechanisms, not on presenting Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 as new results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that the paper’s computational results have no population input. Its algorithms receive a solution space, a welfare function, a sampler for the fair prior, and a welfare mechanism. The prior is a distribution over whole allocation outcomes; it is not a distribution over voter or student types. The paper’s theorems are genuine algorithmic results, so I would not use the “no named result” objection. The issue is that they analyze a generic wrapper, not computation on a population.

For Theorem 3.1, the proposed course-allocation model can make the feasible plans depend on type masses and per-capita supplies. But the theorem treats the fair-prior sampler and welfare mechanism as black boxes. Its sample bound does not say how to construct either one, how their running times depend on \(\tau\) or the input bit length, or how the continuous allocation problem is represented. If those mechanisms are supplied efficiently, the interpolation guarantee follows just as it does for a finite roster. If the interesting question is how to compute them for the mass-based allocation model, that is a new high-multiplicity allocation problem, not a computational result mirrored by Theorem 3.1.

Theorem 4.1 is an even thinner anchor: it mixes the given mechanism’s output with one draw from the prior, \(Q=\alpha\delta_A+(1-\alpha)F\). The fairness and welfare argument applies to any outcome space and never uses a population statistic. Replacing named students with type masses leaves the operation and its guarantee unchanged. Theorem 3.1 has a nontrivial sampling analysis, but its population dependence is still entirely behind the supplied oracles.

The course-allocation example is the best case for the proponent: a large cohort with repeated preference types and scaled course capacities is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. That makes the proposed continuous allocation model coherent. It does not give these two theorems a population-dependent computational question to answer. The paper’s reviewer-matching and sortition experiments offer less support: they rely on named reviewers, papers, or candidate representatives, with identity-sensitive assignments and little type compression in the described data. A larger course market avoids those particular problems, but it does not change the black-box structure of the anchors.

So I would argue against counting either theorem as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror: when the base mechanisms are available, the paper’s generic guarantee carries over verbatim; when computing those mechanisms is the hard part, the relevant result must come from elsewhere. This is a limited negative case, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. If the programme considers a direct application of an already generic interpolation theorem to a well-motivated high-multiplicity allocation model worthwhile, the course-allocation anchor remains plausible.
