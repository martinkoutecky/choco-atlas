The continuous mirror question

Given a room size \(s\), a finite support \(T\) of complete types \(t\) (color and weak order over fractions \(j/s\)), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether there is a feasible assignment \(x_{t,j}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T_R}x_{t,j}=(j/s)q_j\), and \(\sum_{t\in T_B}x_{t,j}=((s-j)/s)q_j\) for some \(q_j\ge0\). For an incumbent \(x\), a challenger is any \(z_{t,j,\ell}\ge0\) with \(\sum_\ell z_{t,j,\ell}=x_{t,j}\) and, for some \(q'_\ell\ge0\), \(\sum_{t\in T_R,j}z_{t,j,\ell}=(\ell/s)q'_\ell\) and \(\sum_{t\in T_B,j}z_{t,j,\ell}=((s-\ell)/s)q'_\ell\). Is there a feasible \(x\) for which every feasible challenger has \(\sum_{t,j,\ell}\delta_t(j,\ell)z_{t,j,\ell}\le0\), where \(\delta_t(j,\ell)\) is \(1\) if type \(t\) strictly prefers \(\ell/s\) to \(j/s\), \(-1\) if it strictly prefers \(j/s\), and \(0\) if indifferent?

The model it lives in

An atomless population of unit mass with rational masses \(\mu_t\) of complete color-and-preference types. A feasible assignment distributes each type’s mass across the discrete room fractions \(j/s\), respecting the red and blue composition balances. A challenger reallocates that same mass, and its popularity margin is the mass strictly preferring its new fraction minus the mass strictly preferring the incumbent fraction.

The objection that survived

The proposed polynomial algorithm is not derived: dualizing the challenger LP puts incumbent masses \(x\) in the dual objective, so choosing \(x\) and the dual certificate creates bilinear terms. This leaves the complexity forecast open but does not invalidate the population-level question.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 28, proved in this paper: deciding whether a roommate diversity game has a popular outcome is co-NP-hard, even when preferences are trichotomous. In full, the input is a finite game with rooms of a common size and agents who rank the possible red fractions in one of three tiers; the question is whether some partition into rooms is never beaten by another partition in a head-to-head comparison of strict preferences. “Popular” means that no alternative makes more agents strictly better off than worse off. The paper proves the hardness by reduction from X3C.

I would mirror it with Atomless Roommate-Diversity Popularity, \( \mathrm{RDP}_\infty \). An instance gives a room size \(s\), finitely many supported types \(T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) specifies an agent’s color, red or blue, and their complete weak order over the possible red fractions \(j/s\), for \(j\in\{0,\ldots,s\}\). Interpret \(\mu_t\) as the fraction of a large population with that complete description. A plausible regime is a large university repeatedly forming fixed-size project teams: many students share a color and the same preference over the local/international mix, so the population is large while \(\tau=|T|\) is modest.

The rooms still have exactly \(s\) members, and agents still care about the fraction of red agents in their room. Only the population becomes continuous: a type’s mass can be divided among different room fractions. This is a deterministic population assignment, not a lottery over partitions.

Write \(x_{t,j}\) for the mass of type \(t\) assigned to rooms with exactly \(j\) red members, and \(q_j\) for the total population mass in those rooms. Feasibility is precisely
\[ \sum_{j=0}^{s}x_{t,j}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T_R}x_{t,j}=\frac{j}{s}q_j,\qquad \sum_{t\in T_B}x_{t,j}=\frac{s-j}{s}q_j, \]
with all variables nonnegative. These balances preserve the room size and its discrete set of possible red fractions.

The question is: does there exist a feasible \(x\) such that no other feasible assignment wins a head-to-head comparison by mass? More explicitly, for a proposed assignment \(x\), let \(z_{t,j,\ell}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents who receive fraction \(j/s\) in \(x\) and \(\ell/s\) in a challenger. The challenger must satisfy \(\sum_{\ell}z_{t,j,\ell}=x_{t,j}\); for each \(\ell\), its red and blue masses must be \((\ell/s)q'_\ell\) and \(((s-\ell)/s)q'_\ell\), respectively, for some \(q'_\ell\ge0\). Let \(\delta_t(j,\ell)\) be \(1\) if type \(t\) prefers the challenger’s fraction, \(-1\) if it prefers the incumbent’s, and \(0\) if indifferent. The incumbent is popular exactly when the maximum of \(\sum_{t,j,\ell}\delta_t(j,\ell)z_{t,j,\ell}\) over all such challengers is at most \(0\). A yes-instance can return the witnessing \(x\); a no-instance has no such assignment.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). For a fixed \(x\), the most threatening challenger is an LP with \(O(\tau s^2)\) transition variables. Its dual has variables for each incumbent type/fraction pair and each challenger red fraction/color balance. Jointly choosing \(x\) and a feasible dual certificate with objective at most \(0\) gives one finite LP for the existence question, again with \(O(\tau s^2)\) size. Thus the finite paper’s co-NP-hardness need not transfer: the continuous model allows a type’s mass to split across room fractions, and the universal “no challenger beats this assignment” condition becomes an LP dual certificate. The expected running time is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(s\), and the input bit length \(L\).

I believe the authors and a referee could recognize this as their problem: it keeps their room size, fraction-based preferences, and head-to-head popularity criterion, translating counts into mass. The weak point is that the finite paper compares partitions of named people, while this mirror lets an atomless cohort split across assignments. I think that is the intended high-multiplicity change here; moreover, popularity comparisons still concern the same agents across the two assignments. The transition variables \(z\) encode that identity-level comparison, and atomlessness makes every feasible coupling realizable.

This mirror covers Theorem 28’s pure popular-outcome existence question, not the paper’s strict-popularity or mixed-popularity results. It also raises a useful bridge question: which finite replications of a type distribution admit a popular partition when the continuous LP has a solution, and how large must the replication be? Strict popularity and mixed popularity have their own continuous versions, but I would treat them as separate questions rather than stretch this anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 28 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and I can challenge its tractability forecast. For a fixed incumbent mass assignment \(x\), checking whether a challenger beats it is an LP. But finding an \(x\) that no challenger beats asks for
\[ \exists x\in X(\mu)\quad \max_{z\in Z(x)} \Delta(z)\le 0. \]
Dualizing the inner LP gives an objective containing products between incumbent masses and dual variables. The proposed “one finite LP” therefore does not follow; it needs a separate argument or algorithm. The mirror may be harder than the proponent predicts.

That does not dispose of the mirror. A stronger formulation compares assignments of the same atomless population and quantifies over every feasible way agents can move between their incumbent and challenger room fractions. With complete types and mass as the unit, those comparisons can be represented by the coupling variables \(z\). This is a coherent population-level question. Large cohorts repeatedly forming teams, with members sharing the modeled color and fraction preferences, are a credible high-multiplicity regime. The reduction’s use of many distinct preference profiles does not rule out that regime.

The paper itself studies finite populations; its mass-based question is not already answered by that treatment. Nor does the fact that room composition remains discrete defeat the mirror: the population is what becomes continuous. So the case against has a real weakness: I can show that the claimed LP solution is unproved, but I cannot show that the authors’ Theorem 28 has no worthwhile continuous counterpart. On the material given, that anchor survives.
