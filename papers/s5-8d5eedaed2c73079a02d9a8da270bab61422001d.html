What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers possible winners given known and outstanding ballots; it leaves the paper’s branch-and-bound performance results, empirical evaluation, and estimates of the minimum missing ballots needed to win without a qualifying anchor.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its closest numbered computational claim is Algorithm 1: given a proposed partial elimination order and a tentative profile of bound and unbound ballots, verify decides whether the order can be realized by assigning unbound ballots, and the paper claims runtime \(O(|C|^2m)\). Algorithm 2 uses that check to enumerate possible elimination orders, with a stated bound of \(O(|C|^2|C|!m)\) after memoization. These are algorithm claims, not results in the form your anchor rule requires. So I can make a plausible case for a mirror, but not an anchor-compliant one.

The natural mirror is Continuous Possible-Winner RCV. An instance has candidates \(C\), a finite domain \(T\) of valid ballot signatures, rational known masses \(b_t\) for \(t\in T\), and rational missing mass \(u\), with \(\sum_{t\in T} b_t+u=1\). Fix a tie-breaking rule and the paper’s ballot-exhaustion semantics. A type is a ballot signature; mass is the fraction of the electorate holding that signature. The decision variable is a completion \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{T}\), where \(\sum_t x_t=u\). For a target candidate \(c\), ask whether there exists such an \(x\) for which \(c\) wins IRV on masses \(b+x\). A yes-instance has a rational completion \(x\) as its solution. The all-winners version returns every possible winner, with a witness completion for each.

This is a population continuization of the paper’s central question: which winners remain possible given known and outstanding ballots? It does not make the outcome fractional or add probabilities. A plausible regime is a large public RCV election, perhaps with hundreds of thousands of voters and far fewer distinct ballot signatures. Those signatures make natural types, and the missing absentee ballots become an unknown portion of the population. The paper’s NYC examples make that regime credible, though it does not report the number \(\tau\) of distinct signatures, so the size advantage is a scenario assumption rather than a measured result.

For a fixed elimination order, the continuous question has a promising tractable shape: each ballot type’s contribution at each round is determined by that order, so feasibility can be expressed with linear constraints in the completion masses. With an explicitly listed type domain, that suggests an LP. The full possible-winner question ranges over elimination orders, however; the paper’s own enumeration bound is factorial in \(|C|\). My tentative expectation is that the full problem is Class B, because the remaining combinatorics concern the candidates and their elimination orders. That is a conjectural direction, not a hardness result from this paper: allowing fractional completions may prevent a direct embedding of the discrete problem.

The soft spot is substantial. The paper assigns individual missing ballots, while this mirror can split missing mass fractionally among rankings. That can create possible winners that no assignment of the original indivisible ballots permits. The authors might accept the mass-based question as a natural high-multiplicity version of their problem, but the paper’s algorithmic claims do not automatically establish results for it. The core follow-up is to determine exactly when discrete and continuous possible-winner sets agree, and how the answer depends on \(|C|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the proponent has not anchored the mirror to a qualifying named result. The paper’s numbered contributions are algorithms, not a theorem or lemma establishing a complexity classification; its running-time bounds appear in the algorithm discussion. That makes the proposed continuous question a new problem suggested by the paper, rather than a continuization of one of its stated complexity results.

But this does not defeat the mirror itself. Grouping ballots by signature and treating known ballots and outstanding ballots as masses is a coherent high-multiplicity version of the paper’s central question. Large public RCV elections are a plausible regime, and a richer model can include ballot styles and validity constraints in the voter types. For a fixed elimination order, the completion constraints can be expressed linearly in those masses. The fact that fractional completions might admit winners unavailable to a finite set of indivisible ballots is a substantive difference the mirror would study, not a fatal mismatch under this programme.

So the negative case is weak where it matters most: the paper may lack a qualifying theorem anchor, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed possible-winner problem survives as a sensible research question.
