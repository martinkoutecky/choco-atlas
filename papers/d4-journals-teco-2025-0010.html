The continuous mirror question

Given one agent type, a finite set \(J\) of repeatable item classes, rational per-capita supply \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{J}\), binary weights \(w\in\{0,1\}^{J}\), and an explicitly represented finite downward-closed family \(\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{J}\) of integral feasible configurations, define \(v(B)=\sum_{j\in J}w_jB_j\) for \(B\in\mathcal{F}\) and \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)=\max\{z:\exists y_B\ge0,\ \sum_B y_B=1,\ \sum_B y_BB_j\le q_j,\ y_B>0\Rightarrow v(B)\ge z\}\). Compute, or decide the existence of, a finite-support mass allocation \(x_B\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_B=1\), \(\sum_Bx_BB_j\le q_j\), and \(x_B>0\Rightarrow v(B)\ge(2/3+\varepsilon)\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\).

The model it lives in

A one-type high-multiplicity indivisible-goods market with integral bundle configurations \(B\), per-capita supplies \(q\), mass variables \(x_B\), and hereditary feasibility \(\mathcal{F}\); the objective is to compute an approximate \(\operatorname{HMMS}\) allocation.

The objection that survived

The finite theorem's named-item constraints have no canonical replication semantics: choosing repeatable classes and fractional configuration packing can remove the integral partition obstruction that powered the reduction.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the computational allocation results in Theorems 4.1 and 4.6, with natural extensions to budget and interval-scheduling applications; it leaves most existence and nonexistence bounds unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the explicit configuration-LP formulation make the \((2/3+\varepsilon)\) hardness disappear for the continuous version?
  • For succinct or oracle representations of \(\mathcal{F}\), what is the complexity of pricing and separation?
  • Can the lone-divider argument behind Theorem 4.6 be lifted to finitely many agent types and masses without dependence on the replicated population size?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a genuine continuous mirror here, and the paper’s strongest foothold is unusually clean: its main hardness theorem already holds when all agents are identical. That is almost the ideal high-multiplicity regime.

The mirror I would use is a high-multiplicity market for indivisible bundles. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete agent types, with mass distribution \(\mu\), and let \(J\) be item classes with per-capita supplies \(q_j\). A finite population of size \(N\) would have \(Nq_j\) copies of item class \(j\). An individual still receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate allocation is fractional. If \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving integral bundle \(B\), feasibility means \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}b_j\le q_j\).

For a hereditary system \(\mathcal F_t\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\), define \(v_t(B)\) as the maximum weight of an \(\mathcal F_t\)-feasible subbundle of \(B\). The correct high-multiplicity MMS benchmark is

\[ \operatorname{HMMS}_t(q)=\max\left\{z:\exists y_B\ge0,\ \sum_B y_B=1,\ \sum_B y_Bb_j\le q_j,\ y_B>0\Rightarrow v_t(B)\ge z\right\}. \]

This is the limiting MMS of a type when both the agent population and the item inventory are scaled up. The continuous fairness requirement is that \(x_{t,B}>0\) only when \(v_t(B)\ge\alpha\operatorname{HMMS}_t(q)\). Thus every unit of population receives an indivisible bundle; mass merely records how many agents receive each bundle.

The natural regime is a large population with few complete types: for example, many households with identical valuations and identical compatibility constraints, or many students with one of a small number of course-demand and budget profiles. In the lead case below, there is only one type, \(\tau=1\), so the high-multiplicity assumption is as strong as possible.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, though explicitly based on Theorem 4 of Li and Deng [35]. It states:

For every \(\varepsilon>0\), computing a \((2/3+\varepsilon)\)-approximate MMS allocation, if one exists, is strongly NP-hard under hereditary set-system valuations, even when all agents are identical, all marginal gains are binary, and the independent sets of the hereditary system are explicitly supplied as input.

The proof reduces from strongly NP-hard \(3\)-PARTITION. There are \(n\) identical agents and \(3n\) items. A set is independent precisely when it has at most three items whose associated numbers sum to at most \(T\), and every item has value \(1\). The MMS is \(3\) exactly when the \(3n\) items can be partitioned into \(n\) valid triples; otherwise it is at most \(2\). Because values are integral, a \((2/3+\varepsilon)\)-approximation must deliver value \(3\), thereby detecting the \(3\)-PARTITION solution.

The corresponding continuous question is:

Continuous Hereditary MMS\(_{2/3+\varepsilon}\). Given one agent type, per-capita item supplies \(q\), binary item values, and an explicitly represented hereditary system \(\mathcal F\), compute a finite-support mass allocation \(x_B\) such that \(\sum_Bx_B=1\), \(\sum_Bx_Bb_j\le q_j\), and every used bundle satisfies \(v(B)\ge(2/3+\varepsilon)\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\).

This is not a disguised fractional-item problem. Each \(B\) is integral. It is the high-multiplicity version of computing an MMS partition: a distribution over integral bundles replaces a partition of one finite catalogue.

The paper’s \(3\)-PARTITION construction illustrates why this is an interesting mirror rather than a restatement. Set \(q_j=1/n\) for each of the \(3n\) item classes. Asking whether \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\ge3\) becomes asking whether the uniform supply vector admits a fractional packing of valid triples. In the explicit reduction, that is a linear feasibility problem over the valid triples, whereas the original problem asks for an integral partition. Thus I expect this particular continuous version to be Class A: the finite-population hardness dissolves into a configuration LP. With a succinct hereditary-system oracle rather than an explicit list, however, pricing may itself be hard, potentially producing a continuum-specific Class C problem.

This is a persuasive mirror because the hard finite instance already has \(\tau=1\). The paper cannot object that we homogenized away meaningful preference differences: its theorem does that itself. The new question asks exactly whether the computational difficulty comes from finite item-partition integrality and population multiplicity, or survives the high-multiplicity limit.

A second, algorithmic anchor is Theorem 4.6, proved in this paper. It states that, given hereditary set-system valuations and approximate valuation oracles with error \(0\le\eta<1\), one can find in polynomial time a

\[ \frac{1-\eta}{1+\frac32(1-\eta)} \]

-approximate MMS allocation, provided that \(\eta\le1-1/\delta\) for some polynomially bounded \(\delta\), and either \(\eta\le1/3\) or independence of two-item bundles can be tested in polynomial time.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Oracle-MMS\((\eta)\). Given finitely many agent types \(T\), their masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q\), common hereditary feasibility structure, and approximate valuation oracles returning an independent subbundle worth at least \(1-\eta\) times the queried bundle’s value, output a finite-support mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) satisfying the item-capacity constraints and

\[ v_t(B)\ge \frac{1-\eta}{1+\frac32(1-\eta)} \operatorname{HMMS}_t(q) \]

for every bundle assigned positive mass to type \(t\).

I expect this version to be Class A, at least for fixed \(\eta<1\) and polynomially accessible bundle generation. The finite matching step in the paper should become a flow or mass-matching step, while the bundle-generation part becomes configuration-LP pricing. The natural technical question is whether the lone-divider argument can be lifted from finitely many agents to type masses without reintroducing dependence on the total population size.

The scope is deliberately limited. I am mirroring the paper’s MMS allocation and approximation problems, especially Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.6. I am not claiming that every existence bound, interval-scheduling corollary, or asymmetric-system impossibility result automatically has a useful continuum form.

The weakest point is the MMS benchmark itself. Ordinary MMS is indexed by a finite number \(n\) of agents and a finite item set, so replacing it by \(\operatorname{HMMS}_t(q)\) is a substantive asymptotic choice. If the authors insist that the original problem only makes sense with one copy of each named item, then no continuum population can receive positive bundles. But with per-capita inventory—which is precisely the co-modification forced by continuizing the population—this becomes a well-defined high-multiplicity problem while preserving integral bundles and hereditary constraints. That seems a defensible, recognizable mirror rather than a tractable problem obtained by discarding the paper’s combinatorial core.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirrors become coherent only by changing the paper’s object on the item side, not by continuizing its population.

Theorem 4.1’s identical agents do not by themselves constitute a high-multiplicity regime. The reduction’s substance lies in one finite catalogue of named items and an arbitrary hereditary family \(\mathcal F\) over that catalogue. If the item set \(M\) is held fixed while the number of agents tends to infinity, then

\[ \operatorname{MMS}_i\le \frac{v_i(M)}{N}\longrightarrow 0. \]

The fairness problem therefore degenerates. To avoid that, the proponent replicates every item class and sets \(q_j=1/n\). But the paper gives no canonical way to replicate an arbitrary hereditary system. Copies could be substitutes, in which case value does not scale; independent copies, in which case the original constraints disappear; or one could impose a new “at most one copy per class” rule, in which case one has deliberately selected a fractional configuration or hypergraph-packing model.

That last choice is mathematically respectable, but it is not the high-multiplicity shadow of the theorem. It changes the one-copy partition problem into a fractional cover problem. In the \(3\)-PARTITION construction, the continuous question becomes whether the valid triples admit a fractional perfect matching. The original theorem says nothing about that object. The proposed \(\operatorname{HMMS}\) is itself defined by optimizing over distributions of bundles, so the finite allocation obstruction has already been replaced by its configuration-LP relaxation. This could be useful research, but its interest comes from inventing a repeatable-commodity model, not from continuizing the paper’s hereditary-item problem.

The distinction matters because per-capita supplies are not forced by population continuization here. In the programme’s voting examples, the population is the object being replaced by proportions. In this paper, the scarce named items are the combinatorial object. Scaling them with the population is an additional market assumption. It may be plausible for particular applications such as course seats or budgeted goods, but then the relevant hereditary systems are structured families such as cardinality or knapsack constraints, not the arbitrary systems responsible for Theorem 4.1.

Theorem 4.6 fares worse as an anchor. Its algorithm is not merely a finite matching theorem waiting to become a flow problem. It depends on a finite set of agents, removal of whole bundles, a lone divider, and repeated reductions of an agent-specific MMS estimate by \(n/(n+1)\). The valuation oracle supplies pointwise information about a queried bundle; it does not supply the pricing or separation oracle needed to optimize over all integral bundle configurations under aggregate capacities. For general hereditary systems, that missing pricing problem is precisely where set packing and maximum-independent-subset difficulties reappear.

Thus a continuous version faces an unavoidable choice. With only the paper’s valuation oracle, the configuration-LP problem is not algorithmically specified or efficiently separable. With an added pricing oracle, tractability has been assumed. With a specially replicated item model, the result becomes a new fractional fair-allocation problem rather than a mirror of the finite theorem. The suggested “replace matching by mass matching” skips this central issue.

This is not an airtight universal impossibility claim. If the authors explicitly accept repeatable item classes and the fractional HMMS benchmark, the proponent has identified a legitimate and potentially valuable Class A problem. But that concession is also the weakness of the affirmative case: both anchors survive only after introducing a new supply model and, for the algorithmic anchor, new oracle structure. On the paper’s own terms, Theorems 4.1 and 4.6 concern one-shot allocation of a finite, identity-sensitive item catalogue; that object has no canonical, nondegenerate continuous-population mirror.
