The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no named computational result. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for \(n\geq 3\), every mechanism that is ex-post efficient and envy-free fails even weak strategyproofness on the uniform preference domain. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that for \(n\geq 4\), every mechanism that is ordinally efficient and treats equals equally fails strategyproofness. Both are axiomatic impossibility results, not claims about computational complexity. The paper’s remark that checking ordinal efficiency is easy is attributed to earlier work; it is not a numbered computational result proved here.

There is a plausible *axiomatic* mirror. Consider a unit mass of scheduling jobs, grouped into a small support of types \(t\), where each type is a weak ranking—equivalently, indifference intervals—over a common time horizon \([0,1]\). Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population share, and let \(q_t\) be the probability measure over time slots assigned to a job reporting type \(t\). Require \(\sum_t \mu_t q_t(A)\leq |A|\) for every measurable set of slots \(A\), and \(\int q_t=1\). A direct mechanism could then be tested for the paper’s efficiency, fairness, and incentive properties. A large cohort of similar jobs with common deadline patterns gives this scenario a credible high-multiplicity interpretation.

But that candidate asks whether an axiomatic incompatibility persists; it does not chart computational complexity. The assignments in the paper are already fractional, while its population remains finite. So this is not a qualifying ChoCo computational mirror, and I would not assign it a tractability or hardness class based on these theorems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 says that ex-post efficiency and envy-freeness are incompatible with weak strategyproofness for \(n\geq 3\); Theorem 2 gives a different incompatibility involving ordinal efficiency, equal treatment of equals, and strategyproofness for \(n\geq 4\). Both are statements about what mechanisms can satisfy, not about the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The remark that checking ordinal efficiency is easy is cited background, not a computational result established here.

The proposed job-cohort model is plausible: many jobs can share a deadline pattern, and a type-level assignment can be represented by measures \(q_t\) with capacity constraints such as \(\sum_t \mu_t q_t(A)\leq |A|\). That gives a sensible continuous assignment model. But asking whether the corresponding fairness, efficiency, and incentive axioms can coexist is still an axiomatic question. Strategyproofness compares outcomes across reports and profiles, so a single rational distribution \(\mu\) is not enough to specify the problem. To turn this into an algorithmic task, one would need to choose a representation for the mechanism and constrain the profile domain. Those choices could define useful research, but they would add a new computational problem rather than mirror Theorem 1.

The same issue applies to Theorem 2. Its eight profiles constrain one mechanism across a web of possible reports. A continuous version could ask whether a mechanism on a simplex of type distributions satisfies the corresponding conditions. That may be an interesting mechanism-design question, but it is still a global existence claim about a function over profiles, not an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithmic problem. Defining per-type allocations and individual report incentives carefully may preserve meaningful incentives in the continuum; it does not supply the missing computational task.

The finite population is not itself a convincing objection: a large cohort of similar jobs is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and fractional assignments do not make the population continuous. Those points support the model’s plausibility, but they do not make the paper’s main results computational. The best algorithmic salvage is the background question of checking ordinal efficiency after compressing agents by type. The paper reports that this check is already easy in the finite setting; a type-level flow or LP formulation would be a natural extension, but it is peripheral to the two main theorems and does not appear to open the complexity landscape this programme targets.

So I would reject these theorems as continuous *computational* mirrors, while accepting that their axiomatic continuous counterparts could interest a different research programme. This case is strongest for “no worthwhile mirror of this paper’s main results.” The broader claim that no re-modeling of assignment could ever motivate a worthwhile continuous computational problem is harder to defend: a researcher could add a concrete algorithmic task, but that would be a new question rather than one anchored in these results.
