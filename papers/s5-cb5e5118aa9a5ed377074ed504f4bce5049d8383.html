The continuous mirror question

Given public rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(\tau\) bidder cohorts, public item-type supplies \(q_e\) per unit population, and item eligibility sets \(I_e\subseteq T\) of size at most \(k\), each cohort representative privately holds its cohort’s XOS valuation \(v_t\). What is the communication complexity, as a function of \(\tau\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length, of outputting an optimal mass allocation \(x_{t,S}\), where each unit of cohort mass receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq E_t\), \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t:e\in E_t}\sum_{S\ni e}x_{t,S}\le q_e\)?

The model it lives in

A private-input market with a continuum of agents grouped into \(\tau\) homogeneous cohorts. Each cohort has mass \(\mu_t\); item types have per-capita supply \(q_e\) and are eligible for at most \(k\) cohorts. Cohort representatives communicate their XOS valuations, and the protocol outputs a mass allocation plan maximizing aggregate welfare while assigning each unit of mass a whole bundle.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 27’s XOS welfare lower bound in \(k\)-hypergraphs. It leaves the paper’s other valuation classes, graph results, and truthful-mechanism results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the configuration LP with XOS demand separation yield polynomial communication in \(\tau\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length for this cohort protocol?
  • How does allowing cohort mass to split among whole bundles change the communication lower bound of Theorem 27?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is for Theorem 27, the paper’s XOS lower bound for \(k\)-hypergraphs. It states that any protocol achieving an approximation factor better than
\[ \alpha_k=\frac{1}{1-(1-1/k)^k} \]
by any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) requires exponential communication. The lower bound also applies to randomized and nondeterministic protocols. This is an author result in Section 5.2, obtained by adapting the technique of [20]; the supplied version defers the proof details to the full version. It is a communication-complexity lower bound, not an NP-hardness result for algorithm running time.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: XOS Hypergraph Market Welfare. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of bidder types, with \(|T|=\tau\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Each type completely specifies eligibility and an XOS valuation \(v_t\). There is a finite set \(E\) of item types; each \(e\in E\) has a rational supply \(q_e\) per unit of population and is eligible for the types in a set \(I_e\subseteq T\) of size at most \(k\). Every person assigned to type \(t\) receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq E_t\), where \(E_t=\{e:t\in I_e\}\). The question is to find an allocation plan maximizing aggregate welfare. A plan is nonnegative mass \(x_{t,S}\) assigned to each type and bundle, subject to
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq E_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t:e\in E_t}\sum_{S\ni e}x_{t,S}\leq q_e. \]
Its welfare is \(\sum_t\sum_{S\subseteq E_t}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). A solution is an optimal value and a plan attaining it.

This makes population mass the continuous object: \(x_{t,S}\) says how much of a type receives a whole bundle. It does not give fractional bundles to individuals. The supplies scale with the population, as they must in a high-volume market; a discrete replication with \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type and \(Nq_e\) copies of each item type is the corresponding high-multiplicity instance when those quantities are integral. Types share the complete description relevant to allocation, including valuation and eligibility.

I expect this problem to be Class A: tractable in a standard exact demand-oracle model, or with explicitly listed XOS clauses. Its configuration LP has a variable for each type-bundle pair and exponentially many variables in general. The dual pricing question for type \(t\) is to maximize \(v_t(S)-\sum_{e\in S}p_e\), which an XOS demand oracle answers. With an explicit clause list, it can be computed by maximizing over clauses. Thus ellipsoid and separation machinery can optimize the continuous problem in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|E|\), input bit length, and oracle cost. A basic feasible plan has polynomially many positive type-bundle entries.

The paper’s Section 5 makes this a plausible mirror of its welfare question: it uses the configuration LP and rounding to study XOS welfare on hypergraphs. The continuous plan gives that LP a direct population interpretation. This is the narrow claim: it mirrors the welfare-maximization problem underlying Theorem 27, not the theorem’s communication protocol model, and not the paper’s truthful-mechanism results. Further questions include how quickly the optimum of replicated finite markets approaches the continuous optimum, and whether any analogue of the communication lower bound remains when valuation information is held privately by type cohorts.

The weakest point is that Theorem 27’s obstruction concerns communication among bidders with private valuations. The continuous problem gives the optimizer a type distribution and valuation access, so it changes the information model as well as removing individual-level population granularity. The theorem itself therefore does not imply that continuous welfare optimization is hard, or that its barrier dissolves. Still, the mirror preserves the paper’s XOS valuations, hypergraph eligibility, integral bundles, and aggregate social-welfare objective; the authors could reasonably recognize it as a continuous version of the optimization problem their protocols approximate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 27. Its stated resource is communication: bidders privately hold XOS valuations, and a protocol must approximate welfare using those distributed inputs. The proposed mirror asks a central optimizer, given the type distribution and access to demand oracles, to compute an allocation. That is a real computational problem, but its tractability follows from the configuration LP and XOS pricing machinery already used in Section 5. It gives no continuous counterpart to the communication lower bound. Adding private reporting by type cohorts would restore a communication question, though then population masses do not determine its communication cost; the valuation encoding and the number of distinct types do.

There is also a structural cost to the proposed high multiplicity regime. In the paper, each item is eligible for at most \(k\) named bidders. In the mirror, an item type with supply \(q_e\) is eligible for every member of up to \(k\) bidder cohorts. A single copy can therefore have a continuum of possible recipients. If each copy instead remains attached to particular people, those individual neighborhoods must be recorded in the types; keeping the graph’s local incidence structure then offers no obvious bounded-support population model. The market over bidder types may be sensible, especially with per-capita supplies, but it changes the paper’s graph restriction along with its population model.

Finally, the continuous allocation plan is precisely a population interpretation of a fractional configuration LP: mass is divided among integral bundles, while each person still receives a whole bundle. That is coherent and may be useful for high-multiplicity markets. Its pricing problem, however, is the same demand-oracle problem as the paper’s LP, and the continuous optimum is the relaxation already driving the paper’s welfare protocols. The proposed result would mainly make that relaxation literal; it would not chart where Theorem 27’s communication barrier goes.

This is the strongest case against the anchor: the easy continuous question changes the information model and weakens the graph incidence model, while the version preserving private information has no clear population-based communication measure. Still, the negative case is limited. The type-market welfare problem is well-posed, retains XOS valuations and aggregate welfare, and the authors could recognize it as a population version of their allocation setting. Under the programme’s stated standard, I cannot rule it out as worthwhile; I can only argue that it mirrors the welfare optimization behind Theorem 27 more convincingly than it mirrors the theorem itself.
