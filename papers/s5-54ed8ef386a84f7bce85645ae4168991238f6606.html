Theorem 10

The continuous mirror question

Given finite good classes \(G\), valuation types \(T\), rational population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), nonnegative additive weights \(w_{tg}\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), find nonnegative masses \(y_{t,B}\) for integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\subseteq G}y_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}y_{t,B}\le q_g\), and \(p_g=q_g-\sum_{t,B:g\in B}y_{t,B}\), with \(\sum_g p_g<1\). Writing \(v_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}w_{tg}\), require for every supported \(y_{t,B}>0\), every supported \(y_{u,B'}>0\), and every \(g\in B'\), \((1+\varepsilon)v_t(B)\ge v_t(B'\setminus\{g\})\), and require \((1+\varepsilon)v_t(B)\ge\sum_g w_{tg}p_g\) for every supported type-bundle pair.

The model it lives in

A repeated indivisible-goods market with complete valuation types \(T\) and population mass \(\mu\); each individual receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), while type mass may split across bundles. The variables are configuration masses \(y_{t,B}\), the residual charity is per-capita stock \(p_g\), and the objective is to find a feasible approximate-EFX allocation.

The objection that survived

The mass-split support formulation has no finite envy graph and may yield nonconvex, support-dependent EFX constraints, so Theorem 10's \(\Delta\)-based termination and query bound do not provide a continuous algorithm.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10's approximate EFX-with-bounded-charity guarantee after specializing to additive high-multiplicity types; it leaves the exact general-valuation results, the MMS and GMMS guarantees, and the Nash-social-welfare guarantee unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the support-dependent EFX constraints be reduced to a polynomial-size separation or column-generation problem in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input encoding length?
  • Can an exact or approximate mass-transfer analogue of the envy-cycle updates yield a polynomial-time algorithm without expanding the population?
  • Do finite additive fair-division hardness results survive under high multiplicity, or is any remaining hardness continuum-specific?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity fair-division mirror of the paper’s approximate EFX-with-charity result. It is a credible Class A candidate, although the charity normalization is the soft spot.

The anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It states that, for normalized and monotone valuation functions and any \(\varepsilon>0\), one can use \(\poly(n,m,1/\varepsilon,\log V)\) value queries to find an allocation \(X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) and an unallocated pool \(P\) such that

\[ (1+\varepsilon)v_i(X_i)\ge v_i(X_j\setminus\{g\}) \]

for every pair of agents \(i,j\) and every \(g\in X_j\),

\[ (1+\varepsilon)v_i(X_i)\ge v_i(P) \]

for every agent \(i\), and

\[ |P|<n. \]

Thus the paper proves an FPTAS-style value-query result for approximate EFX with bounded charity. This is the strongest computational anchor available: the paper contains no named NP-hardness theorem of its own.

I would formulate the continuous problem as follows.

There is a finite set \(T\) of valuation types, with \(|T|=\tau\), and a rational population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Type \(t\) has an additive valuation

\[ v_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}w_{tg} \]

over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) of \(m\) indivisible good classes. Good class \(g\) has per-capita supply \(q_g\). Thus an \(N\)-agent realization contains approximately \(Nq_g\) copies of \(g\), while each individual still receives an integral bundle.

The decision variable is \(y_{t,B}\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(B\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}y_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,B:g\in B}y_{t,B}\le q_g \]

for every good \(g\). Let

\[ p_g=q_g-\sum_{t,B:g\in B}y_{t,B} \]

be the unallocated per-capita stock of \(g\). The continuous problem is:

Continuous Approximate EFX with Bounded Charity. Given \((T,\mu,G,q,(w_{tg}),\varepsilon)\), find a finite-support mass allocation \(y\) such that:

\[ > \sum_g p_g<1, > \]

every type-\(t\) bundle in the support is approximately EFX against every supported bundle,

\[ > (1+\varepsilon)v_t(B)\ge v_t(B'\setminus\{g\}) > \]

for all \(t\), all \(B\) assigned to type \(t\), all supported \(B'\), and all \(g\in B'\), and no type values the charity stock more than its own bundle:

\[ > (1+\varepsilon)v_t(B)\ge \sum_g w_{tg}p_g. > \]

The question is to compute such a \(y\), not to fractionally assign a course or good to an individual. The variables are fractional only because a positive mass of indistinguishable agents may be split across different integral bundles. The condition \(\sum_g p_g<1\) is the population-scaled form of “fewer than \(n\) goods go to charity.”

The regime is quite plausible: a large university allocating repeated course seats, a public employer assigning training packages, or a housing authority allocating repeated apartment types. There may be millions of agents but only a few dozen or hundred recurring valuation types. A type contains the complete information relevant to the problem—preferences and item values—so this is genuine high multiplicity rather than an assumption that named individuals happen to share prices.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The central object remains indivisible-bundle allocation; the fairness condition is still EFX, specifically “up to any good”; and the charity guarantee remains a per-capita version of their bounded-charity statement. What changes is only the representation of a repeated population and the forced scaling of repeated good supplies. Existing high-multiplicity fair-division work would support, rather than collide with, this formulation.

I would expect the additive continuous problem to be tractable, probably through a configuration LP or related column-generation formulation. The configuration variables are the masses \(y_{t,B}\); additive valuations make the relevant pricing problem much simpler than arbitrary valuation-oracle pricing. The finite algorithm in Theorem 10 also suggests that envy-cycle elimination and charity exchange can be lifted to mass transfers between type/bundle configurations. Exact EFX, sparse-support bounds, and polynomial dependence on the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(q\) would be natural questions.

This mirror covers only Theorem 10’s approximate EFX-with-bounded-charity result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s MMS or GMMS theorems, nor does it claim that the paper already proves the continuous algorithm.

The weakest point is that the finite algorithm cannot simply be run on \(N\) cloned agents: \(N\) may be exponentially large in the binary description of the masses. A genuine continuous algorithm must compress the allocation directly, and the charity-value normalization for non-additive valuations needs a careful definition. Consequently, the positive claim should be modest: the paper supplies a strong and recognizable algorithmic anchor for an additive high-multiplicity mirror, with tractability as a credible conjecture—not an already established theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 10’s guarantee is intrinsically tied to a finite set of indivisible goods. If the population alone is made nonatomic while \(M\) remains finite, only measure-zero agents can receive goods. Almost everyone receives the empty bundle, and EFX-with-charity becomes vacuous or trivial. Thus the literal continuum is not a meaningful mirror.

The proponent’s repair—replicating goods by classes with per-capita supplies \(q_g\)—is the only serious alternative. But it changes the problem substantially. The finite theorem allocates each good to a named agent and uses an envy graph over those agents. In the repaired model, a valuation type may be split across many integral bundles. EFX must then be imposed against every bundle in the support, not against a type-level average. Consequently there is no envy graph on types, and the paper’s progress argument no longer transfers: arbitrarily small mass transfers produce arbitrarily small welfare improvements, so the \(\Delta\)-based termination argument disappears.

Forbidding type-splitting would restore a finite envy graph, but would also impose an artificial restriction absent from the high-multiplicity interpretation. Allowing splitting yields instead a new, nonconvex configuration-selection problem: the variables \(y_{t,B}\) determine which bundle constraints are active, and the natural configuration LP does not directly encode EFX. This is not merely a routine continuous version of Theorem 10.

That is the best case against the anchor. It is not decisive. Per-capita supplies and mass assignments are precisely the sort of co-modification the programme permits, and additive valuations make fractional charity meaningful. The resulting problem remains a recognizable high-multiplicity fair-division question, even if the paper supplies no algorithm for it. I therefore cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists; Theorem 10 survives as a credible anchor, though its tractability is much less immediate than the positive case suggests.
