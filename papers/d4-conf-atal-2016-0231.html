What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers only Theorem 7’s quadratic, complete-interaction recency dynamics; it leaves the other equilibrium results, bounded-rationality simulations, and human-trial findings aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper: no numbered result says that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The numbered results concern equilibrium existence and convergence; Proposition 1 cites Rosen, while Theorem 7 and the other later convergence results are developed in the paper. The simulations and user study do not add a named complexity result.

The closest positive case is a continuous analogue of Theorem 7. It proves that, for the recency update rule with \(N\ge 4\), the compositions \(G_k=F_k\circ\cdots\circ F_0\) converge uniformly to a constant function whose value is the limit of the fixed points of \(F_k\). In the quadratic, complete-interaction setting, that limit is the mean of the users’ preferred privacy levels. The authors prove this result using the contraction argument and a theorem cited as [15].

A plausible large-scale scenario would be a large organization deciding a privacy setting for shared content, with many stakeholders but relatively few behavioral types. A type would specify a stakeholder’s preferred privacy level and relevant utility parameters; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s share of the stakeholders. Each type chooses a privacy level \(x_t^{(k)}\in[0,x_0]\). With per-capita peer pressure, the quadratic recency update is \(x_t^{(k)}=(a_t+\rho(k)\bar{x}_{k-1})/(1+\rho(k))\), where \(a_t\) is the type’s preferred level and \(\bar{x}_{k-1}=\sum_t\mu_t x_t^{(k-1)}\). The natural continuous question is to compute the limiting shared privacy policy; under the paper’s increasing, unbounded peer pressure, it is \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t\). That is a recognizable population mirror of the convergence result, and its type structure could be sensible when stakeholder count greatly exceeds the number of behavioral profiles.

But that candidate asks for a mean-field convergence result, not a computational-complexity result. Its answer is a weighted mean, and the paper does not study the complexity of computing it or any comparably named computational problem. Under the programme’s stated scope, I can offer this as an adjacent analytic extension, but not as a valid anchored continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper as a ChoCo candidate is that it supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is an equilibrium-existence result; Propositions 2 and 9, Corollaries 3 and 8, and Theorem 7 concern convergence; Lemma 4 and Corollary 5 establish contraction and uniqueness. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or analyzes an algorithm’s running time.

Theorem 7 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and its proposed population scenario is coherent. With complete mixing, type masses, and per-capita peer pressure, the recency update has a natural mean-field form and converges toward the mass-weighted mean comfort level. That is a plausible analytic continuization. Its limitation is not that the answer is simple or unchanged; those would be poor objections. It is that the paper proves a property of a real-valued dynamical system, not a computational result. The model leaves the pressure schedule and utility functions broadly specified, without an encoding or computational task from which to ask a complexity question.

One could make a computational question by choosing a pressure schedule and finite representations, then asking how many rounds suffice for \(\epsilon\)-consensus, or how to compute an outcome on a specified typed network. But those choices define a new algorithmic problem. They are not complexity questions established by the paper’s named results, and the paper gives no representation assumptions or rate bounds to anchor them. The type-level mean-field version therefore looks like a worthwhile direction for analytic opinion dynamics, but it does not supply the computational mirror this programme is seeking.

I would not green the paper for ChoCo. The case is strongest on that scope mismatch; I cannot honestly claim that no scenario could ever make a related computational problem worthwhile.
