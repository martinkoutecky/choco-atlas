The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq\{0,1\}^d\) of valuation types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) for \(d\) divisible resource pools, choose \(y_{tj}\ge 0\) subject to \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t y_{tj}\le q_j\). Define \(u_t=\sum_{j=1}^d a_{tj}y_{tj}\), where \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^d\) is type \(t\)'s valuation vector. Maximize \(W=\sum_t\mu_tu_t\), then among its maximizers minimize \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t^2\); return an allocation matrix \(y\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population described by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over binary-additive valuation types, with divisible resource supplies \(q\) per capita and type-level per-person allocations \(y\). Maximize aggregate welfare \(W=\sum_t\mu_tu_t\), then minimize the population's squared-utility integral \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t^2\); this is a rational convex quadratic program with size polynomial in \(d\), \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

The objection that survived

An exact rational finite replication shows the model is a weighted compression of finite DIV instances, so the mirror may add limited algorithmic novelty.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the DIV, binary-additive, max-USW/SPD allocation task and its algorithmic stable-allocation result in Theorem 3. It leaves IND, IND-SUB, the layer-structure results, and Theorem 4's individual-profile uniqueness result outside this specific question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a mass-transfer stability characterization analogous to Theorem 3 characterize all minimizers of \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t^2\) among welfare-maximizing allocations?
  • What finite-replication or rounding guarantee relates the mass optimum to allocations for large finite populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for the paper’s divisible-items setting. I find no numbered hardness result to anchor on: the paper’s \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard counting claim in §3.3 is in prose, not a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. For divisible items and binary additive valuations, among allocations with maximum utilitarian welfare, the optima under any symmetric strongly Pigou–Dalton criterion are exactly the stable allocations. Stability means there is no path of feasible reallocations transferring a positive amount \(\Delta\) from an agent \(u\) to an agent \(v\) while leaving \(u\)’s resulting utility at least \(v\)’s: \(h_u-\Delta\ge h_v+\Delta\). The theorem also says a stable allocation can be found in polynomial time. Its proof uses a fair multi-flow result cited as [18]; §4.1 also gives the paper’s combinatorial algorithm.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large platform dividing bandwidth or compute capacity among users. Users fall into a small number \(\tau\) of cohorts, distinguished by which of \(d\) resource pools they value. Each cohort has a binary additive valuation vector, and each pool has a supply that scales with population. For a population of size \(N\), there may be millions of users but only a modest \(\tau\). This treats the population as the continuous object; the divisible resources are the substrate of the paper’s DIV scenario, with per-capita supply as the needed co-modification.

I would pose Continuous-Population SPD Allocation as follows. The input is a support \(\mathcal T\subseteq\{0,1\}^d\) of \(\tau\) valuation types, positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) for the \(d\) resource pools. Choose a nonnegative per-person allocation \(y_{tj}\) for each type and pool, subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{tj}\le q_j\). Type \(t\)’s utility is \(u_t=\sum_j a_{tj}y_{tj}\), where \(a_t\) is its valuation vector. The task is to maximize \(W=\sum_t\mu_tu_t\), then, among welfare maximizers, minimize \(\Psi=\sum_t\mu_tu_t^2\). Return the allocation matrix \(y\). Here \(\Psi\) is the population integral of squared utility; at fixed welfare it is equivalent to the paper’s congestion criterion, since \(\binom{u}{2}\) differs from \(u^2/2\) by a linear term. Allocating the same \(y_t\) to every member of a type is without loss for this objective: averaging their allocations preserves resource use and welfare, and convexity cannot increase \(\Psi\).

I expect this specified mirror to be Class A, tractable: it has \(O(d\tau)\) allocation variables, a linear welfare-maximization stage, and a convex quadratic optimization over the welfare-optimal face, with rational input. Its running time should be polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). This covers Theorem 3’s divisible-items, binary-additive, max-USW/SPD slice, not the paper’s indivisible-item or binary-submodular results. It also raises two useful follow-up questions: whether the stable-transfer characterization extends to continuous utility distributions for a broader class of SPD criteria, and what rounding guarantee relates a mass optimum to finite replicated populations.

The weakest point is the regime assumption: resource supply must grow with population, and the paper does not itself posit replicated per-capita pools. The mirror also selects the divisible-items slice rather than the paper’s more combinatorial indivisible case. Those limits are real, but a large service population sharing access patterns and per-capita capacity is a plausible instance regime, and the selected slice is explicitly one of the paper’s central scenarios.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real computational anchor: it characterizes the optima and gives a polynomial-time algorithm for the divisible, binary-additive case. The proposed cohort model is also credible. Users can be grouped by valuation vector, and per-capita resource supply keeps utility from vanishing as the population grows. I can’t defeat it on grounds of individual identity or an ill-posed continuum.

The strongest negative case is about how much new computational content the mirror adds. Let \(x_{tj}=\mu_t y_{tj}\) be the total amount of resource \(j\) allocated to type \(t\). Then \(\sum_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), and the proposed squared-utility objective becomes
\[ \sum_t \frac{\left(\sum_j a_{tj}x_{tj}\right)^2}{\mu_t}. \]
That is a weighted convex allocation problem with only \(O(d\tau)\) variables. For rational masses and supplies, it also has a direct finite replication: choose a common denominator \(N\), use \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type and \(Nq_j\) units of each resource, then aggregate the divisible allocation by type. Averaging allocations within a type preserves resource use and welfare, while convexity does not increase the objective. So this mirror is an exact weighted compression of the paper’s DIV setting, and its computation uses standard finite-dimensional optimization rather than a new pricing or structural problem.

A stronger attempt to mirror the indivisible setting would replicate items as the population grows to preserve per-capita supply. But aggregate allocations then become fractional across members of each cohort and lead back to the same weighted flow model. Keeping the supply fixed instead risks making per-capita utility vanish. Neither route gives me a stronger objection than the incremental-value concern.

That concern may be too weak for the programme’s stated gate. A formal high-multiplicity dictionary, especially with complexity in \(\tau\) and input bit length, could itself be useful; existing high-multiplicity work supports the regime rather than colliding with it. My negative case is therefore narrow: this anchor looks like a straightforward compression of a divisible allocation problem the paper already solves. I cannot honestly claim that it establishes there is no worthwhile continuous mirror.
