The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_i\) for \(\tau\) ordered voter types and a finite rational polyhedral description of a monotone complete coalition rule \(g\) on \(Q=\prod_i[0,\mu_i]\), find the infimum \(\alpha\ge1\) over ordered nonnegative per-mass weights \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_\tau\ge0\) satisfying \(w\cdot x\ge1\) for every winning allocation \(x\) and \(w\cdot x\le\alpha\) for every allocation in the closure of the losing region; report if no finite feasible weights exist.

The model it lives in

A continuum of members divided into \(\tau\) ordered voting types with masses \(\mu_i\); a coalition is represented by the mass vector \(x\in Q\) that joins it. The rule \(g\) labels allocations winning or losing, and the decision variables are per-mass weights \(w_i\) and the threshold \(\alpha\), with the objective of minimizing \(\alpha\).

The objection that survived

The paper does not show that every proposed polyhedral rule \(g\) arises as a scaled limit of finite complete simple games, so convergence of their critical thresholds and certificates remains open.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 5's fixed-game critical threshold \(\mu(\chi)\); it leaves the extremal values \(c_S(n)\) and \(c_C(n)\), threshold spectra, and cost-of-stability results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which sequences of finite complete simple games does the continuous rule arise as a scaled limit, and do their critical thresholds converge to the continuous value?
  • Can the continuous problem be solved in polynomial time in the type count, polyhedral description size, and encoding length, including the boundary cases?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Lemma 5, proved in this paper. For a complete simple game \(\chi\) with \(t\) equivalence classes of voters, it gives \(\mu(\chi)\) as the optimum of an LP: minimize \(\alpha\), subject to \(\sum_i a_iw_i\ge1\) for every shift-minimal winning vector \(a\), \(\sum_i a_iw_i\le\alpha\) for every shift-maximal losing vector, \(\alpha\ge1\), and \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_t\ge0\). The paper does not establish a named P or hardness result for this problem; the computational anchor is its exact LP characterization, which it then uses in its computations.

Here is a continuous version that preserves the quantity the authors study. Consider a large federation in which members have identical voting rights and coalition effects within each of \(\tau\) ordered classes. A type is one such class; a society has mass \(\mu_i\) of type \(i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A coalition is a measurable subset of members, summarized by its type masses \(x\in Q=\prod_i[0,\mu_i]\). Its pass/fail rule is a complete simple game \(g:Q\to\{0,1\}\): it is monotone under adding supporters and under replacing lower-class supporters with higher-class ones. In a plausible regime, the number of members is very large while \(\tau\) remains small. Individual membership in a coalition is still yes or no; \(x_i\) is the fraction of a large type class that joins.

I would ask the authors to study \(\textsc{Cont-Critical-Threshold}\). Its input is the rational masses \(\mu_i\) and a finite rational polyhedral description of the winning and losing regions of \(g\), including their boundary convention. Its output is an optimal \(\alpha\) and weights \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_\tau\ge0\) minimizing \(\alpha\ge1\) subject to \(w\cdot x\ge1\) for every winning allocation \(x\), and \(w\cdot x\le\alpha\) for every losing allocation \(x\). Taking the closure of the losing region in the second constraint makes the margin exact even at boundaries. The weights are influence per unit mass; rescaling them to fix the winning threshold does not change the critical value.

This is a direct extension of Lemma 5’s underlying question: how well can an additive weight system separate winning from losing coalitions? It does not replace that question with a new outcome space or an axiomatic one. For an explicit polyhedral input, finding a violating allocation amounts to optimizing a linear function over the listed regions. That gives a plausible Class A route through separation and the ellipsoid method, with running time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of polyhedral regions, and their encoding length. The paper’s integer vectors become continuous allocation vectors, so the boundary pricing problem is new rather than settled by the lemma.

The mirror covers the fixed-game critical threshold \(\mu(\chi)\), not the paper’s extremal functions \(c_S(n)\), spectra, or cost-of-stability results. Its weakest point is that the paper’s games are finite Boolean functions on coalitions, and its equivalence classes still have integer counts. Allowing every fractional class mass changes the coalition domain; the polyhedral description is also an added input restriction. A referee could reasonably ask whether this is their problem or a new limit model. The case for acceptance is that it keeps the paper’s central separation objective and gives its equivalence classes a natural high-multiplicity interpretation. The most useful follow-up would be to determine when the continuous threshold is the limit of thresholds for scaled finite games, and whether the finite antichain LP and its certificates survive that limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 5 is the strongest anchor, but it is an LP characterization, not a computational complexity result. It gives the critical threshold of a *fixed finite game* from its shift-minimal winning and shift-maximal losing vectors. The paper proves no complexity bound or hardness result for computing that value; it explicitly leaves that complexity question open. The integer programs and solver timings in Section 5 concern a different task: maximizing the threshold over all games of a given size. So the proposed mirror does not continue a computational result established by the paper.

The natural rescue is to ask for the complexity of the threshold problem in a continuous game. But with the proponent’s explicit rational polyhedral input, the infinite constraints have standard LP separation: for each winning or losing region, optimize the candidate weights over that polyhedron. The resulting problem is ordinary rational linear programming, with size governed by the supplied regions. That may be a clean extension, but it exposes no new complexity landscape. If the game is instead given succinctly enough to make separation hard, the choice of encoding supplies the difficulty; it can encode the finite coalition problem directly, so the hardness would not show that continuizing the population changed anything.

A fixed-\(\tau\) sequence of complete games, with large voter classes, is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. I would not reject it on the ground that voters are individuated. But a limit version of the paper’s extremal quantities would need to choose which game sequences converge and how boundary coalitions are treated. Asking for that limit’s geometry could be worthwhile, but it is a new asymptotic or extremal project, not a continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper.

That is the strongest negative case I can make: the proposed anchor supports a plausible continuous extension, but not the claimed computational contribution. It does not prove that no worthwhile limit project exists; the fixed-type regime is the case where this objection is weakest.
