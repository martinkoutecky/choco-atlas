Why no mirror

The paper's results concern individually strategic agents, and the proposed constituency model replaces them with finitely many strategic bloc representatives. Treating residents as an atomless population makes an individual's report unable to affect the outcome, changing the incentive problem behind both theorems. Neither proposed anchor therefore yields a continuous-population question that preserves the paper's problem.

What the mirror covers

No result survives as a continuous-population mirror; the AMD construction and global-optimality proof remain results for finite strategic agents.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I see is a weighted social-decision problem for large constituencies. Think of a city choosing among a finite set of public projects. Each constituency is a strategic agent representing a large population share \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Its per-capita valuation for outcome \(j\) is \(v_{tj}\), known to lie in a public interval \([L_{tj},U_{tj}]\). The constituency’s total valuation is \(\mu_t v_{tj}\); its transfer is collected from or distributed to its members per capita. The city may have millions of residents but only \(\tau\) distinct constituency types, with \(\tau\ll n\). The outcome set stays finite.

This gives a concrete continuous problem in the paper’s setting: choose a deterministic, strategy-proof, individually rational, non-deficit mechanism to minimize worst-case efficiency loss. For a reported profile \(v\), welfare from outcome \(j\) is \(W(v,j)=\sum_t\mu_t v_{tj}\). A mechanism chooses \(j(v)\) and total transfers \(p_t(v)\); truth-telling must be optimal for each constituency, \(\mu_t v_{t,j(v)}-p_t(v)\ge0\), and \(\sum_t p_t(v)\ge0\). Its loss is \(\max_v[\max_j W(v,j)-W(v,j(v))]\). The instance consists of the outcomes, rational masses, valuation bounds, and the usual feasibility assumptions from the paper. A solution is a mechanism minimizing that loss, or an optimality certificate.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that, under the paper’s assumptions, the optimal shifted Groves mechanism has the lowest worst-case efficiency loss among *all deterministic* mechanisms that are strategy-proof, individually rational, and non-deficit. Its continuous mirror is the global mechanism problem just stated, with the constituencies as the strategic agents. In the shifted Groves candidate, choose outcome \(j^*\in\arg\max_j\{W(v,j)-q_j\}\), for outcome shifts \(q_j\), and charge constituency \(t\)
\[ p_t(v)=\max_h\left\{-q_h+\mu_t L_{th}+\sum_{s\ne t}\mu_s v_{sh}\right\} +q_{j^*}-\sum_{s\ne t}\mu_s v_{s j^*}. \]
The question is whether this candidate is globally optimal for the continuous instance. I expect Class A: setting \(y_{tj}=\mu_t v_{tj}\) turns each constituency into a finite weighted player with box bounds \([\mu_tL_{tj},\mu_tU_{tj}]\), which is exactly the paper’s finite mechanism-design model. Applying Theorem 4 after that change of units gives a direct route to the result. The relevant dimensions are outcomes \(k\), support \(\tau\), and input bit length.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It says that the paper’s AMD process produces a unique, feasible, onto shifted Groves mechanism with minimum worst-case efficiency loss among all shifted Groves mechanisms. Its mirror is the restricted problem: given the same weighted constituency instance, find the optimal vector \(q\), or report that no feasible mechanism exists. The solution is the shift vector and its induced mechanism. The weighted change \(y_{tj}=\mu_t v_{tj}\) again preserves the model, so I expect Class A here too. The paper presents an explicit AMD procedure, though it does not state a formal complexity-class theorem; polynomial-time solvability in \(k,\tau\), and bit length is the natural conclusion from the finite-dimensional arithmetic, not a quoted claim of the paper.

These anchors are related but do different work: Theorem 3 gives the constructive optimization within shifted Groves, while Theorem 4 supplies the stronger guarantee against every deterministic mechanism. Both are theorems in the paper, not results cited from elsewhere. The paper offers no NP-hardness anchor, so this is an algorithmic case rather than a claim that hardness dissolves.

The weakest point is the choice of strategic unit. The paper treats each agent as an individual; this mirror treats a constituency or delegated group as one agent, with its mass scaling its welfare and payment. That is plausible for public decisions made through district representatives, unions, or other organized stakeholders, and it keeps the paper’s finite outcomes, private bounded valuations, incentive requirement, participation constraint, and budget constraint. But if the authors or referee require strategy-proofness for every resident separately, the weighted constituency model may be judged a nearby extension rather than their problem’s continuous form. I would not claim this settles the individual-agent, atomless limit. It does give a credible population-continuous version for a real institutional regime.

The mirror covers the paper’s main mechanism-design results, Theorems 3 and 4, under the stated domain and feasibility assumptions. It raises useful follow-up questions about individual-level incentives in a large-population limit, expected rather than worst-case efficiency loss, and valuation domains with interdependence or non-box constraints.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central mismatch is the unit of agency. The paper’s \(n\) counts independently strategic agents: each can change a report, affect the mechanism, and receive an individual payment. A continuous distribution over people does not preserve those incentives.

For Theorem 4, the proposed constituency model keeps one strategic actor per constituency. Replacing \(v_{tj}\) by \(\mu_t v_{tj}\), and scaling the bounds and payments accordingly, gives an instance of the paper’s finite-agent model. That is a coherent model of delegates or organized blocs, but each bloc can make a collective report on behalf of all its members. Its mass weights that actor’s welfare; it does not make the underlying population continuous in the relevant strategic sense.

The stronger version would keep residents as individual agents and let their reports form a population distribution. In an atomless limit, one resident’s report does not change that distribution or the chosen outcome. Strategy-proofness then loses its force: the resident cannot affect the outcome, and a truthful mechanism cannot make their payment depend on their own report either. Individual rationality and non-deficit can still matter, but the paper’s tension between incentives, payments, and welfare is no longer the same problem. For example, if residents have the same public valuation bounds and an outcome has a negative lower-bound utility, an individual-independent payment cannot make that outcome individually rational for everyone while also collecting nonnegative total payments. The outcome may simply be ruled out, rather than made feasible for sufficiently favorable reports as in the finite-agent mechanism.

Theorem 3 has the same problem. Its AMD process finds shifts for the finite set of strategic agents. In the constituency model, applying it after the change of units is the finite theorem on weighted agents. In the atomless model, the pivotal-report structure that motivates those shifts disappears. The paper does describe a constructive AMD procedure, but it gives no complexity analysis in the parameters ChoCo would track, and the two natural translations do not yield a new population-complexity question: one preserves the finite strategic actors, while the other removes their individual influence.

Keeping a large but finite population does not resolve the mismatch. Each person still has a \(1/n\)-scale influence, and exact strategy-proofness can depend on those individual pivotal cases. Passing to the limit erases them; retaining them keeps the finite population that the programme is meant to continuize. A study of how mechanisms behave as \(n\) grows could be worthwhile, but it would be an asymptotic or approximate-incentive project, not a direct continuous mirror of these theorems.

I would not call the negative case airtight. Large organizations can plausibly act as single strategic principals, and then Theorems 3 and 4 provide a natural benchmark for a weighted version. But that setting has a finite set of strategic blocs whose welfare weights happen to be masses. For individual citizens—the more natural high-multiplicity reading—the continuum either changes the incentive problem or removes its pivotal structure. That is why I would reject these anchors as strong ChoCo mirrors, while recognizing the bloc model as a plausible nearby extension.
