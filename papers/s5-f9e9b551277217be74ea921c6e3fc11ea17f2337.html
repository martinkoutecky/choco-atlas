The continuous mirror question

Given finite need types \(Q\), integral bundles \(B\), rational utilities \(u(q,b)\), resource use, transitions \(P(q'\mid q)\), a rational initial distribution \(\mu_0\) over \((q,z)\), per-capita supplies, horizon \(H\), discount factor \(\gamma_p<1\), and \(\epsilon>0\), choose feasible mass flows \(x_t(q,z,b)\). Each person receives an integral bundle and updates memory to \(z'=\gamma_p z+u(q,b)\); need types then transition according to \(P\). Find a plan within \(\epsilon\) of the maximum of \(\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}[(1-\lambda)\bar u_t+\lambda(W(\mu_{t+1})-W(\mu_t))]\), where \(\bar u_t\) is mean utility and \(W(\mu)=\operatorname{ess\,inf}_{(q,z)\sim\mu}z\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete agent types \((q,z)\), with mass flows assigning integral bundles and per-capita resource constraints. The memory update is \(z'=\gamma_p z+u(q,b)\); the objective combines mean utility with changes in the population’s essential-infimum discounted memory.

The objection that survived

The Rawlsian welfare \(W(\mu)=\operatorname{ess\,inf} z\) can be determined by an arbitrarily small positive mass, so a fixed-grid approximation may fail to preserve the objective without a separate argument.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers discounted-recall allocation planning for a high-multiplicity population. It does not directly mirror the PPO learning experiments, the perfect-recall results, the averaged-memory results, or the LTI stability theorem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fixed-resolution quantization of \(z\), combined with mass-flow optimization, admit an \(\epsilon\)-optimality guarantee and a runtime polynomial in \(H\), the type and bundle sets, grid size, and input bit length?
  • How can essential-infimum welfare be approximated when arbitrarily small positive masses determine the minimum?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow. The paper has no named theorem showing that an allocation or learning problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another standard complexity class. Its closest computational anchor is Corollary 1 (Horizon-independent discretization under past discounting), proved here from Theorem 4. It says that, when \(\gamma_p\in[0,1)\) and each utility lies in \([0,u_{\max}]\), a uniform grid of width \(\Delta\) needs at most \(\left\lceil u_{\max}/((1-\gamma_p)\Delta)\right\rceil\) bins per agent’s discounted-memory coordinate, independent of the horizon. Across \(n\) coordinates, the grid has at most that number raised to \(n\) cells. The underlying memory stays in a bounded interval; with constant maximum utility its bound is approached as time grows. This is a state-representation result, not a classification of an allocation problem’s complexity.

A continuous-population problem suggested by that result is High-Multiplicity Discounted-Fair Allocation Planning. Consider a recurring public-service allocator with millions of clients, such as a city assigning appointment slots. Clients fall into a modest number of classes with the same need profile, bundle utilities, and transition law. Each client receives an integral bundle, while the number of available slots scales with the population. Thus mass represents a fraction of clients, and splitting a type’s mass between bundles means serving different members of that type. Time remains discrete.

An instance gives a finite set \(Q\) of need types, a finite menu \(B\) of integral bundles, rational utilities \(u(q,b)\in[0,u_{\max}]\), bundle resource use \(r_j(b)\), and need-state transitions \(P(q' \mid q)\). It also gives a finite-support rational initial society \(\mu_0\) over \((q,z)\), where \(z\) is a client’s past-discounted utility, a per-capita supply \(\rho_j\) of each resource, a horizon \(H\), and a discount factor \(\gamma_p<1\). The complete type is \((q,z)\): clients with different need dynamics or fairness memories are distinct types.

At time \(t\), choose mass flows \(x_t(q,z,b)\geq0\), with \(\sum_b x_t(q,z,b)=\mu_t(q,z)\) and \(\sum_{q,z,b}x_t(q,z,b)r_j(b)\leq\rho_j\). A client assigned bundle \(b\) moves to memory \(\gamma_p z+u(q,b)\); their next need type follows \(P\). With Rawlsian welfare \(W(\mu)=\operatorname{ess\,inf}_{(q,z)\sim\mu}z\), let \(\bar u_t=\sum_{q,z,b}x_t(q,z,b)u(q,b)\). The question is: given rational \(\epsilon>0\), find a feasible mass-allocation plan whose value is within \(\epsilon\) of optimum for
\[ \sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\left[(1-\lambda)\bar u_t+\lambda\bigl(W(\mu_{t+1})-W(\mu_t)\bigr)\right]. \]
The plan assigns each client an integral bundle; only the population shares are fractional.

I would treat the additive-approximation version as a Class A candidate. The corollary supplies a reason to try a fixed-resolution memory representation: at fixed \(\Delta\), the number of memory bins does not grow with \(H\). With finitely many need classes, a binned society can be represented by masses over need-class and memory-bin pairs. The finite-horizon allocation can then be posed as a mass-flow optimization; with Rawlsian terminal welfare, one can test candidate minimum-memory thresholds and solve the resulting flow problems. This is a plausible route to running time polynomial in \(H\), the need and bundle sets, the grid size, and the input bit length. Proving that the resulting plan is \(\epsilon\)-optimal for the exact-memory problem is part of the proposed question, not a result established by the paper.

The regime is credible for the authors’ underlying problem: a large service population, repeated allocations, a small vocabulary of standardized needs, and shared resource constraints. They would likely recognize this as a high-multiplicity form of their dynamic fair allocator. The model co-scales supply with population and lets each person retain an integral bundle, while the decision variables describe fractions of types served. It continuizes the population; it does not make time or individual outcomes continuous.

The main soft spot is also why this is only a qualified yes. Corollary 1 bounds the number of bins per individual coordinate; it does not prove that optimizing over the population histogram is efficient. Exact memory values can still generate many distinct types over time, and the bound alone gives neither an approximation guarantee nor a learning algorithm. The proposed mirror therefore rests on a plausible continuous-optimization question the paper makes visible, not on a complexity classification the authors already proved. It covers past-discounted fairness in long-horizon allocation; it does not mirror the PPO sample-complexity question or claim that the ten-agent experiments are themselves a high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that Corollary 1 establishes a representation bound, not an algorithmic result for the allocation problem. For fixed grid width \(\Delta\), it bounds the number of bins for each agent’s memory coordinate independently of the horizon. The joint grid still has size \(K^n\), and the corollary gives no guarantee that replacing exact memories with bins preserves the value of a plan or the behavior of a learning algorithm. It gives a reason to investigate discretization, not a complexity classification to carry over.

That gap matters in the proposed high-multiplicity model. The exact type includes both need and discounted memory, \(z'=\gamma_p z+u(q,b)\). Even with finitely many needs and bundles, allocation histories can produce exponentially many distinct memory values over \(H\) steps. A fixed grid makes the model finite, but its approximation to the planning objective still needs proof. For the proposed Rawlsian objective, the essential infimum can also be determined by an arbitrarily small positive mass, so approximation methods need to preserve the lowest-memory cohorts. Switching to a smoother welfare measure may help, but would require a separate analysis.

The stronger version of the mirror is nevertheless coherent: public-service cohorts with shared need classes, per-capita supplies, integral bundles for each person, and mass flows over need and memory types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocator, and the programme explicitly permits the population-scale supply and fractional type-level decisions it requires. One could ask whether a discretized occupancy model gives an \(\epsilon\)-accurate plan. Nothing in the paper proves that it does; equally, the missing proof does not make the question unworthy.

So the negative case can reject the claim that Corollary 1 already supports a tractability result for continuous-population planning. It cannot honestly rule out the planning mirror itself. The universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists is weak here: the proposed cohort model survives as a plausible research question, though its connection to the paper’s proved result is less direct than the proponent suggests.
