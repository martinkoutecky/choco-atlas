What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror; Theorems 1–3 are existence and implementability results without a computational claim.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. The closest is Theorem 1, proved here: in every finite minimally fair game, the max-min fair payoff can be implemented as a super-strong mediated equilibrium relative to \(\Omega_{\text{full}},\Psi_{\text{basic}}\). Theorems 2 and 3, also proved here, give related existence claims for symmetric games or for strong rather than super-strong equilibrium. These are game-theoretic existence results; the paper does not classify a problem as tractable, hard, or complete, or give a complexity result for computing a mediator.

One could propose a population mirror: agents with identical payoff-relevant game roles form types, their masses replace player counts, and a mediator chooses actions for delegated mass; the question would be whether a max-min fair payoff is implementable against deviations by measurable coalitions. But that is a new continuum-game question, not a continuous version anchored by a computational result in this paper. Its existing distributions concern randomized strategies over outcomes in a finite-player game, not a continuous population. Under the requested standard, there is no defensible anchored mirror to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Propositions 1 and 2 compare equilibrium concepts under different information structures; Theorems 1–3 establish that certain payoffs can be implemented as mediated equilibria. The paper gives no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity claim. Theorem 1’s proof constructs a mediator as an existence witness, but does not ask how efficiently to find one. On a strict reading of ChoCo’s remit, there is no computational result here to anchor a mirror.

The best proposed rescue is a population of identical users routing traffic through a congestion game: represent route use by mass, and ask how to compute the max-min fair payoff and a mediator policy that sustains it against deviations by positive-mass coalitions. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper itself names congestion games. It is not defeated by saying that many agents are implausible or that the result might change in the limit.

The difficulty is that this rescue adds the computational task the paper does not contain. “Minimally fair” alone does not make a game high-multiplicity: it permits payoffs to depend on player identities and the full action profile. A type model must preserve each agent’s complete payoff-relevant role, so the broad theorem cannot simply be compressed into a distribution over types. Restricting to anonymous congestion games makes mass a natural representation, but then the mirror needs new choices about how to define fairness over types, which measurable coalitions count as deviations, and what information the mediator observes. Those choices can be made sensibly; making them is part of formulating a new continuum contract-synthesis problem, not carrying over a computational problem stated in this paper.

Theorems 2 and 3 do not supply separate computational anchors. One gives an existence result for a symmetric optimal payoff under a relaxed information condition; the other gives one for max-min fairness with strong rather than super-strong equilibrium. Their continuum versions would face the same gap between existence and computation, and the same need to specify a population model and mediator policy problem.

So the proponent is right that a worthwhile research question can be built from the paper, especially around anonymous congestion games. The negative case is strongest—and defensible—when it says that this would be a new computational programme inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper proves. It cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no useful mirror exists in any scenario; the congestion-game population remains its clearest weakness.
