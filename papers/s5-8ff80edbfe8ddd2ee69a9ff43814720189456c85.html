The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive setting I can imagine is a large auction market—say, repeated ad placements—with many bidders sharing the same per-unit valuation and eligibility conditions. A type would be that complete bidder description, and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of bidders of type \(t\). A tagged bidder of type \(t\) faces the rest of the market’s type distribution; the mechanism assigns that bidder an allocation and payment. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but it does not give this paper a qualifying computational anchor.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, whose characterization is attributed to Archer and Tardos, Nisan, and Roughgarden and is partly reproved here: incentive compatibility implies that allocation is monotone in the agent’s bid; monotonicity suffices for an incentive-compatible payment rule; and any two such payment rules differ by a constant. Theorem 13 gives the single-parameter characterization in payment-formula terms, and Theorem 14, proved here, extends it to a multidimensional setting. These are mathematical characterizations of incentive compatibility and revenue equivalence. None asserts a computational complexity result or computes an algorithm for an underlying optimization problem.

So I cannot honestly name a continuous computational problem *per anchor* under the brief’s rule: this paper has no named computational result to anchor one to. The auction-market scenario is a possible population continuization, but turning it into a complexity question would add a new problem rather than mirror a result of this paper. The paper’s continuous values and possibly fractional allocations concern the value and outcome spaces; they do not make the population continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to anchor one to. Theorem 1 characterizes incentive compatibility; Theorem 13 expresses that characterization as a payment formula; Theorem 14 extends it to vector-valued allocations. None states a complexity bound, an algorithm, or a hardness result. The paper’s contribution is a mathematical proof of uniqueness and revenue-equivalence claims, not a computational problem.

The proposed ad-market setting is plausible: bidders could be grouped by valuation, eligibility, and other relevant attributes, with a tagged bidder considered against a distribution of other types. So I would not argue that multiplicity is inherently nonsensical here. But that setting does not turn any of the three theorems into a computational result. Preserving dominant-strategy incentives means considering each bidder’s deviations against the reports of others; an aggregate distribution can describe the market, but the paper gives no task for computing over that distribution. Theorem 14’s vector dimension concerns allocation parameters, not population size.

One could add a task such as computing payments from a finitely encoded allocation function, or maximizing revenue over a type distribution. The first requires choosing a function representation the paper does not study; the second introduces an optimization problem absent from its results. Either might be worthwhile research, but neither is a continuous mirror of a named result here.

The negative case is therefore strongest within ChoCo’s stated remit: a continuous auction market is credible, but this paper offers no computational question whose population-continuous version the programme could chart. The limitation is that this does not rule out a valuable new algorithmic problem motivated by the paper’s setting; it rules out calling that problem a mirror of this paper.
