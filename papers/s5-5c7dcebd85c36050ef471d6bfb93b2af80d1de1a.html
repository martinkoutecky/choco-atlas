The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric compatibility graph on \(T\), and a strict ranking of compatible partner types for each type, consider an atomless population with mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\). Each agent can be paired with one compatible agent or left unmatched, with being unmatched ranked last. Decide whether there exists a measurable matching \(M\) such that for every feasible measurable matching \(N\), the mass of agents who prefer their partner’s type under \(N\) to their status under \(M\) is no greater than the mass who prefer their status under \(M\) to their partner’s type under \(N\).

The model it lives in

A finite typed roommates market with rational masses \(\mu_t\), symmetric compatibility, and strict rankings over compatible partner types. Use nonnegative assignment masses \(x_{tt'}\), where \(x_{tt'}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents assigned to type \(t'\); require \(x_{tt'}=x_{t't}\) for \(t\ne t'\), \(\sum_{t'}x_{tt'}\le\mu_t\), and zero mass on incompatible pairs. The diagonal \(x_{tt}\) counts type-\(t\) agents paired within their type. Decide whether a feasible flow and its atomless realization is immune to every feasible challenger matching under mass-based majority votes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I would defend is Atomless Typed Popular Roommates. It keeps the paper’s central question—whether a matching can withstand every majority challenge—and puts the population, rather than each named participant, at the center.

A type \(t\) records an agent’s complete profile for this problem: which other profiles they can match with and their strict ranking of those profiles. An instance gives a finite set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric compatibility graph on \(T\) (self-compatibility is allowed), and each type’s ranking of its compatible types. Imagine the society as atomless sets \(\Omega_t\) of mass \(\mu_t\). Agents of type \(t\) can match with any compatible type and are indifferent between individuals of the same type; being unmatched, \(\bot\), is worst.

A feasible matching \(M\) is a measurable pairing of agents: each matched agent has exactly one compatible partner, and unmatched agents receive \(\bot\). For any two matchings, count votes by mass:
\[ V(N,M)=\lambda\{a:N(a)\succ_{\operatorname{type}(a)}M(a)\}. \]
Here an agent compares the types of their partners, or \(\bot\), under their type’s ranking. A matching is popular if \(V(N,M)\le V(M,N)\) for every feasible \(N\). The decision problem asks whether a popular matching exists; a solution is such a measurable matching, equivalently its feasible type-to-type matched masses together with a realization. Since the population is atomless, fractional type-level masses can be realized by matching whole agents; no individual receives a fractional roommate. The input is finite and rational, with natural parameters \(\tau=|T|\), the type-graph size, and the mass bit length \(L\).

This is plausible in large housing or residential-cohort markets: many applicants share a small set of compatibility and preference profiles, and allocation is made across profiles. The number of people \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). It is the roommates problem in a genuine repeated-type regime, with the paper’s majority comparison retained as a comparison of mass. It does co-modify the model: agents rank profiles and are interchangeable within a profile, rather than strictly ranking named individuals. That is exactly what makes type a complete description and the population high-multiplicity.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: Popular Matching is NP-complete. In full, its input is an arbitrary graph whose vertices have strict rankings of their neighbors; the decision question is whether there exists a matching \(M\) such that no other matching is preferred by more vertices than prefer \(M\). The theorem’s hardness is proved here, by reduction from Partitioned Vertex Cover; Lemma 3.1, establishing that source problem’s NP-hardness, is also proved here from the classic 3-SAT reduction. I rely on Theorem 1, not Lemma 3.1, as the mirror anchor. The mirror covers the paper’s existence decision problem, not its discussion of maximum-sized popular matchings.

My provisional expectation is hardness transfers (Class B) when the number of types is allowed to grow: the paper’s reduction encodes choices and edge-coverage constraints in graph and preference structure, so its combinatorics appear to live in the compatibility graph and rankings, not in the number of copies of each agent. The concrete research question is whether one can encode those constraints in the atomless typed model and prove NP-hardness in \(\tau\) and \(L\). That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1: replacing a single vertex by atomless mass allows a gadget’s mass to split, so the discrete reduction does not automatically survive. Useful follow-ups are whether hardness persists with \(\tau\ll N\), whether fixed \(\tau\) admits an efficient algorithm, and whether a finite type-flow characterization gives an exact algorithm.

My weakest point is that the paper’s arbitrary graph and strict rankings are person-specific. A profile-based compatibility graph changes those details, and atomless agents can split across matches. The mirror is strongest as a well-posed question about the homogeneous-profile regime; the claimed hardness transfer is precisely the part that still needs proof.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent chose the right anchor: Theorem 1 is a numbered NP-completeness result for a computational decision problem, so “there is no result to mirror” cannot carry the negative case.

The strongest objection is about what makes that theorem hard. Its reduction uses a graph whose vertices have particular neighborhoods and strict rankings of particular neighbors. To preserve those instances exactly, a type must include that whole profile; generally that gives one type per vertex, so the reduction supplies no large population per type. Grouping vertices into repeated profiles instead makes compatibility and preferences uniform across each type class. That changes the reduction’s setting: its exact-one and exact-two gadget choices can split across atomless mass. Replicating the original vertices does not fix this; it preserves their distinct profiles and leaves the number of types growing with the instance. So the proposed model has a valid existence question, but the paper’s hardness result gives no evidence that its gadgets survive there. The claimed transfer is a research conjecture, not a mirror of the reduction.

A stronger anti case is that the proposed model replaces strict rankings of named neighbors with rankings of profile classes, allowing indifference among same-type partners. In roommates applications, compatibility and preference are often relational and individual: donor compatibility, officer pairing, or who can share spectrum with whom. A small set of interchangeable profiles is much less clearly the natural regime for those examples. A repeated-profile residential cohort is conceivable, but it needs to be established as a convincing instance regime, not inferred from the paper’s applications. Even then, a new reduction or structural algorithm would be needed to show that this model has a substantial complexity landscape.

There is close adjacent work: Kavitha studies finite roommates instances where an integral matching is challenged by fractional matchings, and proves NP-hardness for deciding whether a “truly popular” matching exists. That is not the proposed atomless typed-population problem—the candidate remains an integral matching, and the population is not the continuous object—but it means the obvious fractional challenger model already has a computational literature nearby. [Popular Roommates in Simply Exponential Time](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol150-fsttcs2019/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2019.20/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2019.20.pdf)

This defeats the proponent’s hardness-transfer story and makes the application case less secure than their sketch suggests. It does not defeat the mirror itself: the type-based, atomless existence question is coherent, and neither the altered preference model nor the adjacent fractional literature shows that it is unworthy. I can make a case against treating this paper as a strong ChoCo candidate, but I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
