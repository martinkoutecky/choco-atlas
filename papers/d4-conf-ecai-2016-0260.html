What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1's ACCU truthfulness claim; it leaves the cooperative scheduling and contributor-selection procedures and the simulation results outside the anchored mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a large residential prosumer cooperative or virtual power plant: many households share standardized tariffs, equipment, shiftable-load profiles, and forecast-error models. Let each type be a complete operational profile and let \(\mu_t\) be its population share. A plausible regime has many more members than types. The paper’s 7,376-prosumer simulation suggests the scale, though it does not establish that those members form a small number of exact types.

There is an important limit on the anchors: the paper gives no named result about computational complexity—no tractability or hardness classification. Its closest formal hook is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that under ACCU reward sharing, participants are incentivized to make accurate statements. The mechanism scores a participant using normalized CRPS between the reported error distribution and the realized error, then uses that score to determine their share of the cooperative’s balance increase. This is a mechanism-design theorem, not a complexity result, so it does not satisfy a strict complexity-anchor requirement.

The most credible continuous question suggested by that theorem is Nonatomic ACCU truthfulness and reward allocation. An instance consists of a finite type support \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s true distribution \(F_t\) of action errors, a fixed realized shifting schedule, and its resulting positive balance increase \(B\). Each infinitesimal member reports a predictive distribution \(P_t\). Write \(a(P,e)=1-\mathrm{CRPS}(P,e)\), and let \(\bar a_t(P_t)=\mathbb E_{e\sim F_t}[a(P_t,e)]\). The question is to find each member’s reward-maximizing report and the resulting per-member reward density. Under the nonatomic assumption, one member’s report does not change the aggregate denominator. The density is \(B\bar a_t(P_t)/\sum_u\mu_u\bar a_u(P_u)\); truthful reports \(P_t=F_t\) maximize expected score, and the densities sum to \(B\). For finite-support normal error models, this is tractable by evaluating the score for each type. It is a direct mirror of the ACCU incentive question, though it offers no discrete hardness to dissolve.

That mirror has a real caveat: strict propriety guarantees truthful reporting in expected score under the true error distribution. It does not, by itself, establish that a forecast maximizes the score for every fixed realized error. The continuous question should therefore use the expected-incentive formulation; a referee could reasonably ask whether this is the theorem the authors intended to prove. The other limitation is computational: the paper’s theorem supplies no algorithmic complexity result, so this supports a continuous mechanism-design question more strongly than it supports ChoCo’s complexity programme. My case covers the ACCU reward-sharing result, not the paper’s full scheduling and contributor-selection scheme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the sole anchor is not a computational result. Theorem 1 is an incentive claim about COOPcoin rewards; it gives no complexity bound or algorithm for a computational problem. Also, its “scoring rule” is a proper forecast score, not a voting rule over candidates. That makes it a weak fit for ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The proposed nonatomic question is coherent, but it changes the theorem’s formulation. The paper scores a realized error \(\epsilon_i\) and claims truthfulness using strict propriety. Strict propriety supports truthful reporting when scores are evaluated in expectation under the agent’s true error distribution. The mirror therefore adds a stochastic reporting problem with distributions \(F_t\), then asks for expected-score-maximizing reports. In the stated fixed-schedule model, this is an elicitation question; the population masses normalize reward densities, but do not create a search or allocation problem. With the proposed finite-support normal model, the calculation is per type. If arbitrary distributions make it computationally difficult, the difficulty comes from how those distributions are represented, not from continuizing the population.

A stronger version could include mass-based contributor selection and dispatch: types would encode load profiles, shifting costs, capacities, and forecast behavior, and the cooperative would choose actions subject to aggregate constraints. That is a plausible continuous computational problem. But it moves beyond Theorem 1’s accuracy claim to the paper’s scheduling and selection procedures. The paper does not state a named complexity result for those procedures either. This version may be a useful new ChoCo question, but it does not rescue the proposed theorem as a computational anchor.

This negative case does not establish that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. Standardized households or prosumers could form a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s focus on aggregate demand makes mass a natural modeling choice. My case defeats the asserted anchor as a ChoCo complexity result; it is weak against the broader claim that the paper supports no worthwhile continuous mirror.
