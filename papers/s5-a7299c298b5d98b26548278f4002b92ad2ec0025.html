Its numbered results concern mechanism properties: Proposition 1 shows that direct TTC is neither incentive compatible nor individually rational; Theorem 1 proves that no mechanism can be both incentive compatible and Pareto optimal in the stated setting; and Theorems 2–4 establish individual rationality, incentive compatibility, and Pareto optimality for TTCI. None establishes a complexity classification or an algorithmic running-time result.

So there is no anchor here from which I can honestly formulate the requested continuous complexity problem or predict whether its hardness transfers, dissolves, or is continuum-specific. Theorem 1 is a named impossibility result, but it is a mechanism-design result, not a computational-complexity result.

A possible research direction would be a large barter platform with repeated cohorts of users sharing preference rankings over good categories and similar patterns of social contacts. One could model type masses and exchanges between types. But the paper’s key ingredients—who can invite whom, ancestor and descendant relations, and trades along cycles—depend on individual network structure. Compressing those individuals into types may change the problem, so this would need a carefully specified network model before it could count as a credible mirror. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case raises no positive mirror anchor: it names Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–4 only to argue that none is a computational result. On the ChoCo brief, that is the strongest case against a paper-anchored mirror. The proposition and theorems concern incentive compatibility, individual rationality, Pareto optimality, and their incompatibility. TTCI’s pseudocode is an algorithm, but the paper gives no named result about its running time or the complexity of a computational problem. There is therefore no complexity result here to continuize.

The best rescue would be a large barter platform with repeated cohorts: agents share rankings over a small set of good classes, endowment classes, and roles in a finite network of contact classes. Given rational masses for those complete types, one could ask how to compute a TTCI-like allocation of mass along feasible trading cycles. That is a plausible population model, and the network’s individual identities alone do not rule it out.

But it would be a new model and a new computational question, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. Making invitation incentives meaningful also takes care: a single agent has zero mass, while a deviation by a whole type is a group deviation, unlike the paper’s individual IC condition. Pareto optimality could be restated for mass allocations, but that remains an axiomatic result rather than the computational question ChoCo targets.

So the negative case is strong under the programme’s paper-anchoring criterion, but weak as the literal universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile work. The paper supports a possible continuous barter project; it does not, on its own, supply the computational anchor for one.
