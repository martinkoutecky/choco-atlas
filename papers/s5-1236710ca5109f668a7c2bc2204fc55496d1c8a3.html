The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over follower ideal positions, with unique median \(a\), and finitely many proxies with rational peaks \(q_1,\ldots,q_K\), let each proxy report a position in \(\mathbb{R}\), let each follower type delegate its mass to the nearest reported proxy, and let \(W(s)\) be the reported position of the proxy nearest \(a\), with fixed tie-breaking. Starting from truthful reports \(s=q\), decide whether there are a proxy \(j\) and a report \(r\in\mathbb{R}\) such that \(\lvert W(q_{-j},r)-q_j\rvert < \lvert W(q)-q_j\rvert\), and return a witnessing pair if one exists.

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over follower-position types carries the population mass; a finite set of individually strategic proxies have peaks \(q_j\) and report positions \(s_j\). Followers delegate to their nearest proxy, and the winner is the reported position of the proxy nearest the population median.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow. There is no eligible computational anchor in the supplied paper text. Its theorem statements concern strategyproofness, proxy manipulation, bounds on outcomes, convergence, and regret; none classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The supplied text also omits theorem numbers, so I can’t cite a numbered complexity result without inventing one.

The closest hook is the Section 3.2 theorem, proved in the paper: in the truthful state, some proxy has a profitable manipulation if and only if no proxy is located at the true median and there are proxies on both sides of it. That is a useful structural result, but it is not a computational-complexity claim, so it cannot serve as the requested anchor.

A plausible follow-on is Continuous Median-Proxy Manipulation. An instance gives a rational probability measure \(\mu\) of follower ideal positions, with finite support of size \(\tau\) and a unique median \(a\); \(K\) named proxies with rational peaks \(q_j\); and a fixed tie-breaking rule. Followers delegate to their nearest reported proxy. In the large-population limit, define the outcome \(W(s)\) as the reported position of the proxy nearest \(a\), matching the paper’s nearest-proxy characterization. Starting from truthful reports \(s_j=q_j\), ask whether there are a proxy \(j\) and report \(r\in\mathbb R\) such that
\[ |W(q_{-j},r)-q_j|<|W(q)-q_j|. \]
A solution is either “none exists” or a witnessing proxy and report.

The motivating regime is a large civic platform or poll with many followers but relatively few distinct, standardized ideal positions, alongside a small set of active proxies: \(n\gg \tau+K\). It is plausible that the authors would recognize this as their truthful-state manipulation question with the follower population represented by rational type masses. The paper’s characterization suggests a Class A answer: compute the median and check whether proxies bracket it with none at it, in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(K\), and the rational input length. That is an extrapolation from their theorem, not a result they claim.

This candidate covers only the truthful-state proxy-manipulation characterization, not the dynamics or partial-information results. Its weak point is substantial: it leaves the strategic proxies finite and individual while continuizing the followers, and the resulting computation largely makes an existing structural characterization explicit. So it is a sensible population model and a precise follow-on question, but a weak ChoCo example: the paper itself poses no computational problem whose discrete hardness might dissolve.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper offers no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Its theorems concern strategyproofness, profitable proxy deviations, and convergence; none classifies a computational problem by complexity or gives an algorithm. The missing theorem numbers are incidental. The content is the issue.

The closest anchor is the truthful-state characterization: a proxy can manipulate exactly when no proxy sits at the true median and proxies lie on both sides. The proposed continuous decision problem turns that strategic characterization into an algorithmic query. A stronger version could use a large population of followers grouped into identical position types, with a small finite set of strategic proxies. That is a plausible regime, but it is a new computational framing of a game-theoretic result, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

There is also a structural limit to improving that mirror. Keeping proxies finite preserves the paper’s unilateral strategic actors, but leaves its strategic core finite; making proxies nonatomic risks removing the effect of any one proxy’s deviation. The hybrid model avoids that problem and may be a sensible follow-up, but it changes the population model around the game rather than continuizing the game’s central actors.

That is the best negative case, and it is not airtight. A large follower population with a few proxies is credible, and the authors might well recognize the manipulation query as their problem. I would argue that this paper is a weak fit for ChoCo’s complexity programme because it supplies no computational anchor—not that no worthwhile continuous question can be asked about proxy voting.
