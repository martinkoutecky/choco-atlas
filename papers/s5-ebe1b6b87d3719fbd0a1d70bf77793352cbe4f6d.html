What the mirror covers

The proposed question extracts per-profile HWU supportability from Proposition 3; it does not cover the axiomatic characterizations in Propositions 1 and 2 or the sufficient condition in Proposition 4.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow and rests on Proposition 3 (proved here). It says that a social choice functional satisfies the non-existence property exactly when it satisfies Harsanyi’s weighted utilitarianism: at each evaluation profile, there is a probability distribution over individuals whose weighted utilities make every chosen alternative a maximizer. Equivalently, there is no pair of lotteries—one over the chosen alternatives and one over all alternatives—such that every individual strictly prefers the second lottery in expected utility. The paper’s proof writes the weight-existence condition as a nonnegative linear feasibility system and uses Farkas’ lemma.

The paper gives no named complexity classification. I would count Proposition 3 only under the prompt’s allowance for a result whose proof computes with an LP formulation. The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Continuous HWU Supportability:

An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(X\), a finite support \(T\) of utility-function types \(u_t:X\to\mathbb{Q}\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a nonempty proposed choice set \(S\subseteq X\). Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of a large population with evaluation type \(t\). Decide whether there are nonnegative per-capita weights \(a_t\) such that
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t u_t(x)\ge \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t u_t(y) \quad\text{for every }x\in S,\ y\in X. \]
A solution is such a weight vector. This asks whether \(S\) can be supported as a subset of the weighted-utilitarian winners. Its LP has \(|T|\) variables and at most \(|S||X|\) winner constraints, so I expect it to be tractable in \(m=|X|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length. That algorithmic expectation follows from the formulation; Proposition 3 itself does not claim a runtime.

The regime is a large population choosing among a small set of public plans, where many people share the same complete cardinal evaluation vector—for example, residents using a common evaluation scale and falling into a modest number of response types. The mass is the share of residents in each type, and the type-level weight \(a_t\) preserves the paper’s profile-dependent Harsanyi weighting. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s voter interpretation.

This mirror covers Proposition 3’s per-profile HWU supportability, not the paper’s axiomatic characterizations of utilitarianism or its sufficient condition for exact HWU. Natural follow-up questions include exact supportability, where \(S\) must equal the full winner set, and variants that bound how much type weights may depart from population shares.

The weak point is substantial: Proposition 3 characterizes a property of an arbitrary choice functional; it does not pose this supportability test as a computational problem or analyze its complexity. I am extracting the test from its LP/Farkas proof. Also, unrestricted weights can offset the masses \(\mu_t\), so in this version the answer depends mainly on which types are present, not their population shares. The mirror is a credible population interpretation of the proposition, but its computational contribution is an extension the authors might not recognize as the paper’s own problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is the only plausible anchor, but it is an axiomatic equivalence, not a computational result. It characterizes when a choice functional satisfies Harsanyi’s weighted utilitarianism; it gives no input problem or algorithm. “Given a proposed choice set \(S\), find weights that support it” is a reasonable inverse question, but the paper does not pose it. It takes a functional \(F\) over a domain of profiles as its object, not a single observed output \(S\). The Farkas argument establishes the characterization; it does not turn supportability into the paper’s computational problem.

The proposed continuous version also discards the population masses. For every positive \(\mu_t\), set \(q_t=\mu_t a_t\). Then
\[ \sum_t \mu_t a_t=1,\qquad \sum_t \mu_t a_t u_t(x)\ge\sum_t \mu_t a_t u_t(y) \]
becomes
\[ \sum_t q_t=1,\qquad \sum_t q_t u_t(x)\ge\sum_t q_t u_t(y). \]
Every probability vector \(q\) is attainable for every positive mass vector \(\mu\). So the supportability answer depends only on which utility types are present, not their shares or the bit length of those shares. In the paper’s own condition, identical individuals likewise supply duplicate utility constraints; their multiplicity does not affect whether suitable Harsanyi weights exist. The proposed task reduces to finite convex feasibility over utility vectors, whether those vectors describe a population or just a list of distinct evaluations.

The natural repairs change the question. Requiring weights to track population shares reduces Harsanyi’s profile-dependent weighting to ordinary utilitarian aggregation, rather than mirroring Proposition 3. Capping or penalizing per-capita reweighting could make the population composition matter, but that constraint is absent from the paper and changes its central property. The proponent’s exact-supportability follow-up has the same mass cancellation: requiring \(S\) to be the full winner set adds tie and strict-gap constraints, not a role for population shares.

The resident-types scenario is plausible, and a population-based supportability problem could be worth studying as a new inverse problem. But the proposed formulation gives the population no computational work to do: its masses can be removed by a change of variables. Propositions 1 and 2 are also axiomatic characterizations, and Proposition 4 is a sufficient condition; none supplies another computational anchor. The negative case is strongest on this boundary between the paper’s results and the extracted task: a broader continuous model might be useful, but it would be an extension rather than a continuous mirror of a computational question in this paper.
