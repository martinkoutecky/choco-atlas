The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper: its numbered results characterize admissions choices and policy effects, but none establishes that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So it has no anchor of the kind the programme normally asks for. The closest result for a positive case is Theorem 2, proved in this paper, though it is a structural optimization result rather than a complexity result.

Theorem 2 says: fix the slots and their types, assume every tier has enough applicants to fill its tier slots and compete for the merit slots (Assumption 1), and assume tier 1 has no greater score density than any other tier at any score (Assumption 2). Among tier-blind precedence orders, the one that maximizes tier 1’s assignments puts all merit slots before all tier slots; the one that minimizes them puts all tier slots before all merit slots.

A corresponding continuous optimization problem would take as input a population with tier-specific score distributions, the numbers of merit and tier-reserved seats, and the score range. A type is an applicant’s tier and score; mass is the fraction of applicants of that type. The decision is an ordering of the slots. Process slots in that order: a merit slot takes the highest-scoring available unit of mass, and a tier slot takes the highest-scoring available unit of mass from its tier. Among orders that treat tiers symmetrically, find an order maximizing the mass of tier 1 applicants admitted, and return that order and the resulting allocation. In an exact finite-type encoding, scores could be discrete with fractional splitting at ties; the paper’s theorem instead assumes atomless score densities.

The regime is credible for an oversubscribed exam school serving a large district: many applicants compete for a comparatively small number of seats, while the number of socioeconomic tiers is fixed and small. Mass represents shares of the applicant pool, and the seats scale with that pool. The paper itself studies precisely this kind of aggregate, including four tiers and a continuum of scores. Its focus on a single school’s choice function also makes the proposed question faithful to the theorem; it would not cover the full deferred-acceptance assignment across ten schools.

Under Theorem 2’s assumptions, this optimization is tractable in the strongest possible sense: the theorem identifies the optimal order directly. Further questions include what happens when tier 1 is not pointwise underrepresented, and how difficult it is to optimize over tier-blind orders for arbitrary rational score distributions. Those would be computational extensions, not results established by this paper.

The positive case is that this is plainly a sensible population-level problem: the authors already reason about admissions in masses and score distributions, and they explicitly optimize access across precedence rules. The weakness is also decisive for a *continuization mirror*: the proposed model is already the paper’s continuum model. Calling it a mirror would restate existing work, not turn a discrete problem into a continuous one. So Theorem 2 supports the plausibility of studying this problem computationally, but it does not supply a qualifying complexity anchor or a new continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only serious anchor, and it is a weak one for a continuization mirror: the paper already makes the population continuous. Its applicants are described by tier-specific score distributions, admissions and outcomes are measured in mass, and the result optimizes over slot precedences. The proposed mirror would use the same population, inputs, policy choice, and objective.

Under the theorem’s assumptions, the best tier-blind precedence for tier 1 is already identified: process all merit slots before all tier slots. Recasting the distributions as rational masses over tier-score types would not create a new continuous question; it would encode the same one. Nor does the paper offer a discrete complexity result whose high-multiplicity relaxation might reveal new algorithmic structure. Its numbered results are characterizations and comparative statics, not complexity classifications.

There is a stronger follow-up one could propose: given arbitrary rational score distributions, find the best tier-blind precedence when the density condition in Assumption 2 fails. A discrete high-multiplicity version could represent applicants by tier and score, with ties split by mass. That is a coherent computational problem, and the paper’s result does not settle it. But it extends the paper’s continuous policy-optimization model; it does not turn one of its discrete computational results into a continuous mirror. Studying the full deferred-acceptance process would be another extension, since the paper’s theorems concern a single school’s choice function.

So I would argue against treating this paper as a continuization target: it starts in the continuum and already solves its central policy-optimization question under the stated distributional condition. The negative case is not airtight, though. The unrestricted precedence-optimization problem could be worthwhile within ChoCo; what is weak is the claim that it constitutes a continuous mirror of this paper.
