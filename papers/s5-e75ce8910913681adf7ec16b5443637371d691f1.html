The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many unweighted interval request types \(j\in\mathcal{J}\), with rational intervals and rational per-worker supplies \(q_j\), compute the maximum accepted request mass in a unit-mass population of interchangeable workers. Each worker receives a whole nonoverlapping bundle \(S\); choose a distribution \(x_S\) with \(\sum_S x_S=1\), \(\sum_{S:j\in S}x_S\le q_j\), and EF1 across every pair of bundles in the support. Compare this optimum with the unconstrained optimum under the same supplies.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of interchangeable workers and finite interval request types. Each type \(j\) has rational interval data, unit value, and per-worker supply \(q_j\); decision variables \(x_S\) distribute workers among whole, feasible schedules \(S\). Enforce the supply constraints and EF1 across supported bundles, then maximize \(\sum_S x_S|S|\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the offline unweighted EF1 objective and its price-of-fairness question; it leaves the weighted offline results and online competitive-ratio results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP be optimized in time polynomial in support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\), using prescribed-cardinality interval scheduling for pricing?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s offline scheduling problem, with machines interpreted as workers in a large, interchangeable workforce. Theorem 3 is my lead anchor: it is a proved lower bound on the price of fairness, rather than a computational-hardness classification. The paper gives no NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result.

Take a unit-mass population of identical workers. A finite set \(\mathcal J\) of request types specifies rational start and end times and weights; type \(t\) has supply \(q_t\), measured as requests per worker. Each worker receives a whole set \(S\subseteq\mathcal J\) of nonoverlapping requests. A solution is a distribution \(x_S\geq0\) over feasible sets, with
\[ \sum_S x_S=1,\qquad \sum_{S:\,t\in S}x_S\leq q_t. \]
Thus \(x_S\) is the fraction of workers receiving bundle \(S\); every worker still receives an integral schedule. With common valuations \(u(S)=\sum_{t\in S}w_t\), EF1 requires that for every two bundles \(S,S'\) in the support of \(x\), some \(t\in S'\) satisfies \(u(S)\geq u(S'\setminus\{t\})\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_S x_Su(S)\), and the price of fairness compares that optimum with the same mass-allocation problem without EF1.

The regime is a large platform with many interchangeable workers and standardized, repeated request windows. In the paper’s lower-bound construction, there is one worker type and just three request types, each supplied at \(1/2\) request per worker. For a workforce of size \(N\), each cohort therefore has \(N/2\) requests. This scaling keeps utilization meaningful as \(N\) grows; it is the per-capita-supply co-modification allowed by the programme. EF1 still compares the actual bundles workers receive, rather than averages of their bundles.

Theorem 3 (Offline EF1 Lower Bound: Unit-Length), proved in this paper, says that with \(m\) identical machines, unit-length intervals, and arbitrary nonnegative weights, no EF1 scheduling algorithm can guarantee a price of fairness strictly below \(3/2\). Its proof uses \(m/2\) pairs of machines and, for each pair, three unit-length intervals with weights \(1,1-\varepsilon,1\). The unconstrained schedule accepts all three per pair, while every EF1 schedule has total weight at most \(m\), giving a ratio approaching \(3/2\).

Here is the corresponding continuous problem, Unit-Length Weighted Continuum EF1 Scheduling: given finitely many unit-length request types, rational weights \(w_t\), and rational per-worker supplies \(q_t\), compute a maximum-weight EF1 distribution over feasible worker schedules, and determine its price relative to the unconstrained optimum. In the three-type instance, set
\[ a=[0,1),\quad b=[\tfrac12,\tfrac32),\quad c=[1,2), \qquad (w_a,w_b,w_c)=(1,1-\varepsilon,1), \qquad (q_a,q_b,q_c)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12). \]
Without EF1, half the workers can receive \(\{a,c\}\) and half can receive \(\{b\}\), for value \((3-\varepsilon)/2\) per worker. With EF1, bundles \(\{a,c\}\) and \(\{b\}\) cannot both occur: a worker with \(\{b\}\) still values the other bundle at \(1\) after one item is removed, exceeding \(1-\varepsilon\). If \(\{a,c\}\) occurs, only \(a\) and \(c\) can be used, with total value at most \(1\); if it does not, each worker gets at most one request, again giving value at most \(1\). That bound is attained by giving half the workers \(\{a\}\) and half \(\{c\}\). So the continuous price approaches \(3/2\) as \(\varepsilon\to0\). This is the theorem’s obstruction expressed with finite request support and a growing population.

For the general weighted optimization problem, my provisional complexity prediction is Class C: the mass variables still select whole interval bundles, and EF1 couples which bundles may coexist in the support. Theorem 3 establishes the price barrier; it does not establish this complexity prediction. A prover could test whether the pricing problem for such bundles is polynomial or hard as the request support grows, and whether fixed \(\tau\) or restricted weight sets change the answer.

A second, complementary anchor is Theorem 1 (Offline EF1 Upper Bound: Unweighted), also proved here. It states that for every unweighted offline instance on \(m\) identical machines, a polynomial-time algorithm returns an EF1 schedule with price of fairness at most \(3/2\). Its mirror is Unit-Weight Continuum EF1 Scheduling: use the same mass-allocation input and constraints, set \(w_t=1\), and compute the maximum accepted request mass subject to EF1. I expect this version to be Class A. In the unweighted case, EF1 means bundle sizes differ by at most one. For each possible load \(h\), one can optimize over distributions supported on feasible schedules of size \(h\) or \(h+1\), giving a configuration LP with one supply constraint per request type. Its pricing problem is weighted interval scheduling with a prescribed cardinality, solvable by dynamic programming. Enumerating \(h\) gives an exact algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. Scaling rational supplies to a finite number of machines also carries the paper’s \(3/2\) price guarantee into this mirror. Further questions include whether the LP has a compact formulation and how the result extends to machine types with different capabilities.

The authors should recognize both questions: workers are the machines, requests remain interval goods, each worker’s schedule remains feasible, and EF1 remains the same bundle guarantee. The mirror covers the paper’s offline maximum-EF1 objective and its price of fairness. It leaves the online competitive-ratio results outside its scope; their central question depends on sequential arrivals.

The weakest point is the scenario’s repeated-request assumption. The lower-bound mirror has three exact window-and-weight cohorts whose supplies scale with workforce size. A platform with millions of workers may have a much wider spread of request times and values. Still, standardized service windows and repeated request classes offer a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s own unit-length lower bound already uses precisely this compact cohort structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the unit-length lower-bound theorem is not a computational result: it proves a price-of-fairness bound, and its three-request-type gadget gives the same \(3/2\) obstruction after per-worker normalization. Treating that gadget as evidence of a new complexity question overstates what the theorem establishes. A more ambitious weighted optimization problem could be asked, but its complexity would be a new result, not a mirror of the lower-bound theorem.

That objection does not defeat the other anchor. The offline upper-bound theorem explicitly gives a polynomial-time algorithm, and the proposed high-multiplicity version is a natural computational question. The machines are identical agents, while request supplies scale with their population; the programme explicitly allows that co-modification. Existing fair interval-scheduling work concerns finite-agent models, so it does not establish that this population version is already solved continuously ([Equbal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04353), [Li, Li, and Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.11648)).

The strongest version of that mirror also seems to survive. For unit weights, EF1 forces bundles in the allocation’s support to have sizes \(h\) or \(h+1\), for some \(h\). Fixing \(h\) gives a configuration LP with one supply constraint per request type. Its pricing problem is interval scheduling with a prescribed cardinality, which is polynomial-time solvable. So the proposed mirror plausibly yields an exact algorithm in the support size and input bit length. The continuous \(3/2\) lower-bound gadget is also a legitimate instance of the same model.

The online lower bound is a weaker direct mirror: its adversary tracks one named machine, and lifting that argument to a mass population needs additional modeling. But that does not dispose of the offline anchor. I cannot make the universal negative case honestly. Theorem 3 is a weak computational anchor, but Theorem 1 leaves a credible, well-posed continuous mirror that I have not defeated.
