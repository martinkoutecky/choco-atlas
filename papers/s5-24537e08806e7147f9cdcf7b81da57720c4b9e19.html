What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-based characterization extends Theorem 1 to facility types; it does not cover a computational result, and the priority-rule characterizations and case study are left without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow population mirror of the paper’s permit-allocation rule. A plausible regime is a large number of similar facilities along a river, grouped into a modest number \(\tau\) of types by river reach, historical claim rate, and other characteristics relevant to allocation. A type represents genuinely interchangeable facilities; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of facilities of that type. This is a plausible high-multiplicity version of allocating permits among the paper’s ordered claimants.

One precise follow-up problem is High-Multiplicity River-Claims Rule Characterization. Given a finite ordered type support \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-facility claims \(c_t\), and a per-facility permit budget \(E\le C_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t c_t\), find a rule assigning per-facility allocations \(x_t\ge0\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=E\). The question is to characterize continuous rules satisfying population-level versions of the paper’s four axioms: independence of upstream null claims, budget additivity, redistribution additivity, and invariance to merging or splitting adjacent jurisdictions. For comparison, let \(D\) be the types at the furthest downstream location and \(\mu_D=\sum_{t\in D}\mu_t\). The direct analogue of the paper’s rule is \(x_t^\lambda=\lambda(E/C_\mu)c_t\) for \(t\notin D\), and \(x_t^\lambda=\lambda(E/C_\mu)c_t+(1-\lambda)E/\mu_D\) for \(t\in D\). The extra permits are spread across the downstreammost mass.

The natural named result to connect this to is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that a rule satisfies independence of upstream null claims, budget additivity, redistribution additivity, and merging/splitting proofness if and only if there is a \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) such that the rule is \(\phi^\lambda\), the externality-adjusted proportional rule. This is a real theorem about the underlying allocation problem, and the paper’s variable-population setting and river order make the proposed high-multiplicity version recognizable as a follow-up.

But Theorem 1 is not an eligible computational anchor under the requested standard: it characterizes rules axiomatically; it does not establish that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. The same is true of Propositions 1 and 2, which characterize priority rules. The paper has no named computational result to anchor a complexity mirror on, so there is no honest per-anchor complexity question or tractability prediction to give. Evaluating its explicit formula is easy, but that is not a complexity result in the paper.

The weakest point is therefore decisive for ChoCo: the proposed extension makes the population continuous, but its central question remains axiomatic rather than computational. The paper already allows divisible permit amounts, and its theorem is about which finite-agent rules satisfy certain axioms. A continuum of facility mass may be a sensible extension the authors would recognize, but it does not produce the computational question the programme is designed to chart.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes a family of allocation rules by axioms; Propositions 1 and 2 do the same for priority rules. The Tuojiang example applies a rule to six cities. None formulates a computational problem or establishes a complexity or algorithmic result.

The proposed mass model—many interchangeable facilities grouped by river reach, with per-facility claims and a downstream cohort receiving the extra permits—is a plausible population mirror. It is not, however, a computational mirror of Theorem 1. Its question is still an axiomatic characterization, which falls outside ChoCo’s stated scope. The paper’s continuous rules concern continuity as a property of maps on finite-agent problems; they do not make the population continuous. Divisible permit amounts do not change that.

One could try to create a computational question by asking an algorithm to choose \(\lambda\), enforce additional constraints, or optimize a quantified environmental objective. But the paper leaves \(\lambda\) to decision-makers, does not quantify the trade-off as an objective, and does not study those extensions computationally. That would be a new optimization problem built around the paper’s setting, not a mirror of one of its results.

There is a real limitation to this negative case: a mass-based river-allocation model may be a worthwhile axiomatic follow-up in its own right. I would not claim that no continuous extension could be worthwhile in any research programme. The case against is narrower and stronger: this paper supplies no computational anchor for the continuous complexity programme.
