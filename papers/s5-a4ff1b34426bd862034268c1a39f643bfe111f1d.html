The continuous mirror question

Given colleges \(C\), a finite set of student types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\nu_\theta\), type preference orders, rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\), continuous utilities \(U_c\) over assigned type masses, and a feasible rational initial assignment, does the fixed-order blocking-mass refinement reach a state with no blocking move or repeat an exact state after finitely many updates? For a finite computational encoding, take the \(U_c\) to be rational polynomials; at each step choose the first blocking type, source, and destination, then a feasible mass transfer maximizing the receiving college’s utility gain, with fixed lexicographic tie-breaking.

The model it lives in

A society consists of types \(\Theta\) carrying rational masses \(\nu_\theta\); assignment variables \(x_{\theta c}\) obey type-mass and per-capita capacity constraints. Colleges evaluate assigned type-mass vectors with continuous nonlinear utilities. A deterministic blocking-mass swap rule transfers mass from a less-preferred assignment to a preferred college, displacing mass to unmatched when needed, and resolves choices by fixed ordering and utility gain.

The objection that survived

The paper does not specify transfer amounts for mass moves, so the proposed utility-maximizing update rule adds dynamics whose termination behavior may differ from its individual-student algorithm.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s termination and cycle-detection claim; it does not cover Theorem 1, the Rawlsian max–min objective, or the experimental comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every trajectory under a specified finite encoding of utilities and the mass-update rule terminate or repeat?
  • Can a trajectory converge without reaching a stable assignment or repeating a state?
  • What bounds on the number of updates are possible in terms of the number of colleges, types, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. Its best computational anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It says that the deterministic greedy swap-based algorithm always terminates after finitely many steps; when it stops, it either outputs a stable matching or detects a repeated matching and certifies a cycle. The proof uses a deterministic state transition and the finiteness of the set of matchings; the paper bounds the number of iterations by \(O(|\mathcal M|)\), which is exponential in the number of students. This is an algorithmic result, but not a polynomial-time guarantee.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large centralized admissions market: many students apply to a modest number of colleges, and students with the same college ranking, background, and other relevant attributes are interchangeable for the allocation. For example, a system might assign hundreds of thousands of applicants across a few dozen colleges, with only tens or hundreds of distinct applicant types. A type \(\theta\) has mass \(\nu_\theta\), and its members still receive individual college assignments; the mass records their fraction of the population.

Here is the continuous question I would put to a prover, as the mirror of Theorem 2. Call it Continuous Type-Mass Swap-Refinement Termination. An instance contains colleges \(C\), a finite set of student types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\nu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), student preference orders \(\succ_\theta\) over \(C\cup\{\bot\}\), and rational per-capita college capacities \(\kappa_c\). A state is an assignment vector \(x_{\theta c}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_c x_{\theta c}\leq\nu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta c}\leq\kappa_c\); the remainder of each type is unmatched. Each college evaluates its assigned type-mass vector \(y_c=(x_{\theta c})_{\theta\in\Theta}\) using an explicitly encoded continuous nonlinear utility function \(U_c(y_c)\). One suitable family includes a type-quality term and a diversity term based on the masses \(Y_b\) of each background \(b\), for example \(a_c\cdot y_c+\lambda_c(Y-\sum_bY_b^2/Y)\), with the diversity term defined as zero when \(Y=0\).

A blocking mass move transfers some \(\delta>0\) of type \(\theta\) from a less-preferred assignment \(d\) (possibly \(\bot\)) to a college \(c\succ_\theta d\). If capacity requires it, the move displaces the same amount of incumbent mass from \(c\) to unmatched. It is blocking when it is feasible and strictly increases \(U_c\). Fix an order on types, colleges, and sources; at each step take the first pair admitting a blocking move, choose a feasible move giving the largest utility increase, and resolve ties by a fixed lexicographic rule. The question is: given the instance and a feasible rational initial assignment, does this deterministic process reach a state with no blocking move, or repeat an exact earlier state, after finitely many updates? A solution is a yes-or-no answer for that instance, with a finite terminal or cycle certificate for “yes.”

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: it retains student preferences, quotas, nonlinear college evaluation of groups, blocking moves, and the same deterministic refinement idea. The co-modification is that capacities and college evaluations act on type mass. That fits the programme’s scope: the population is the continuous object, while the college’s type-level utility is the corresponding way to describe group composition in that population.

I expect this question to be continuum-specifically hard, or to expose outright failure of finite termination in some instances. The paper’s finite-state proof no longer applies: a mass assignment can take infinitely many values, so a deterministic trajectory need not repeat just because it continues. It could approach a stable assignment without reaching it in finitely many moves. The paper establishes no potential function that rules this out. Further questions are whether every trajectory in a useful utility class terminates, whether exact termination is decidable from a finite encoding, and whether terminating runs admit bounds in \(m,\tau,L\).

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s termination and cycle-detection claim, not the paper’s Rawlsian max-min optimization or its experimental comparisons. Its weakest point is the anchor itself: Theorem 2 is a finite-state observation, not a complexity classification, and the continuous process requires a specified mass-level update rule. The proposed utility representation also narrows the paper’s arbitrary preferences over exact student sets to utilities over type composition. Those are real limitations. Still, large admissions systems with recurring, genuinely interchangeable applicant profiles make the high-multiplicity regime plausible, and the resulting termination question preserves the algorithmic issue the theorem actually addresses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers almost no complexity result to continue. Theorem 1 is an existence counterexample; the max–min section defines an optimization problem and reports heuristics, while explicitly disclaiming approximation guarantees. Theorem 2 is the one plausible algorithmic anchor, but its claim is generic: a deterministic process on a finite state space, with cycle detection, halts after at most \(|\mathcal M|+1\) states. The proof uses no feature of matching, college utility, or Rawlsian fairness.

The proposed mass version exposes the problem. If moves remain in units of \(1/n\), the state space is finite and the same cycle-detection argument applies; that preserves individual granularity rather than making the population continuous. If moves can transfer arbitrary mass, the paper supplies no rule for choosing how much to transfer. Choosing the move with the largest utility gain adds new dynamics, and with strict improvement that maximum may not exist. Other choices of step size can produce different termination behavior. A result about one such process would be a result about the newly specified update rule, not a continuation of Theorem 2’s finite-state observation. Without a precise finite encoding for utilities and moves, even the proposed termination question lacks a defined computational input.

A stronger candidate is a continuous max–min admissions problem over student types, with per-capita capacities and a utility function for type composition. Large admissions markets make that regime plausible, and the paper’s diversity term suggests a natural way to define such utilities. But the paper’s base utility ranks exact subsets of named students. Preserving those rankings can require types to retain identity; replacing them with a function of type masses changes the utility model. The resulting optimization problem could be worthwhile on its own, but the paper proves no computational claim about its max–min objective for a continuous mirror to extend.

The negative case is weakest at that last point: a well-chosen type-composition max–min model could be a good new ChoCo problem. But it does not rescue the proponent’s anchor. The paper’s only named algorithmic result yields a finite-state stopping fact, and a genuinely continuous termination question depends on dynamics the paper never specifies.
