The case FOR (proponent)

Its named results are preservation theorems about Bipolar ABA properties, not results classifying a computational problem as NP-hard, tractable, FPT, or similar. The paper cites complexity work by others, but does not establish those results itself.

The closest candidate is Theorem 11, proved in this paper: for \(|A|\ge 3\), no quota rule with \(1\le q<n\) preserves an assumption’s acceptability under preferred, complete, set-stable, well-founded, or ideal semantics. That could motivate a continuous threshold question: represent each complete framework \(\langle L,R_t,A,\bar{}\rangle\) as a type with rational mass \(\mu_t\), include a rule in the aggregate when its supporting mass reaches a threshold, and ask whether an assumption remains acceptable under a specified semantics. A population of experts or clinics with repeated opinion-framework types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

But that would be a new computational question attached to the paper’s model, not a mirror anchored in a named complexity result of the paper. The underlying population model is a natural fit; the required computational anchor is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the central gap: none of this paper’s theorems classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 11 is the closest anchor. It says that, with at least three assumptions, no quota rule with \(1\le q<n\) preserves an assumption’s acceptability across preferred, complete, set-stable, well-founded, or ideal semantics. That is a preservation result, not a complexity result.

The strongest mirror would give each complete framework opinion a type \(t\), assign types rational masses \(\mu_t\), and include a rule when its supporting mass meets a threshold \(\lambda\). One could ask whether an assumption acceptable in every positive-mass type remains acceptable in the aggregated framework. This is a coherent weighted version of Theorem 11. Repeated expert opinion types could make the population regime plausible, so the source’s seven-person medical example is not enough to rule it out.

But that mirror still asks for a preservation theorem over weighted profiles, not an algorithmic result of the kind ChoCo targets. Given the distribution, aggregation tallies each rule’s supporting mass; deciding acceptability in the resulting framework is a separate semantic query. To get a computational optimization problem, one could add a search task—such as finding the minimum mass transfer that changes acceptability—but that adds a manipulation problem the paper does not study. Such extensions might be worthwhile, but they are adjacent projects, not mirrors of its named result.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit and weak on the universal claim. The paper does not supply an anchor-compliant computational result to continuize. It does not follow that no useful continuous study could grow from its model.
