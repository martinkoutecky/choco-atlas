The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that a continuous population is a natural model for this voting rule, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. In a large recommendation or search setting, let each type be an approval set \(b\subseteq C\), and let \(\mu_b\) be the fraction of users with that type. The rule assigns candidate \(c\) score \(\sum_b \mu_b\,\mathds{1}_{c\in b}/|b|^{1/p}\), then selects the \(k\) candidates with highest scores. That is a sensible population model, and the paper’s Section 5 explicitly allows real-valued profiles. But the paper studies axiomatic, utility, likelihood, and distance justifications—not the complexity of a computational problem on those profiles.

The closest named results are Theorem 18, proved here, which says that for every \(p\in[1,\infty]\), \(1,p\)-approval is vote-distance rationalizable using the stated ballot metric; and Theorem 20, proved here, which says that for \(p\ne\infty\) and \(k>1\), \(k,p\)-approval is not vote-distance rationalizable. Neither asserts that a problem is in \(P\), hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The other named results likewise characterize voting rules or their justifications. So none can serve as the required anchor, and I can’t responsibly give a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor* without inventing a computational result the paper does not contain.

The natural follow-on would be continuous winner determination from rational masses over approval types. It is essentially direct score aggregation and top-\(k\) selection, rather than a complexity question established by this paper. My strongest positive point is that the authors already accept real-valued profiles as meaningful in their axiomatic analysis; my weakest—and decisive—point is that this provides no computational hardness or algorithmic result whose behavior continuization could test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its theorems characterize voting rules, utility and likelihood representations, or distance rationalizability; none studies an algorithmic problem or its complexity. More specifically, the paper already allows real-valued profiles in Section 5. Since approval ballots are anonymous types and positive scaling leaves the outcome unchanged, a normalized society over ballots is already a special case of the paper’s profile model. Large populations are a sensible regime here, but they do not supply a missing computational question.

For Theorem 18, the natural continuous question is whether the nearest-unanimous-election characterization extends from a finite election to masses \(\mu_b\) of each ballot type. It does: for \(1,p\)-approval, the mass-weighted distance to consensus on candidate \(c\) is
\[ \sum_b \mu_b\,|b|^{-1/p}\mathds{1}_{c\notin b} =\sum_b\mu_b|b|^{-1/p}-s_p(c,\mu). \]
That is a continuous version of the paper’s representation identity, but still a distance-rationalizability statement, not a computational result. Theorem 17 has the same character for committees under Hamming distance. Extending either theorem to mass profiles changes the domain of the representation claim; it does not turn the claim into an algorithmic problem.

For Theorem 20, the corresponding question is whether \(k,p\)-approval with \(k>1\) and finite \(p\) admits a vote metric that rationalizes it on every mass profile. That remains an axiomatic impossibility question. Allowing distributions over the same finite ballot types gives no computational task for an algorithm to solve. The other named results in the paper have the same limitation: their subjects are axioms, utilities, likelihoods, and representations.

The strongest more ambitious proposal would use the paper’s ballot metric as a cost for changing voters’ ballots, then ask for the minimum mass revision that makes a target committee win. That is a plausible continuous optimization problem, but it adds a revision objective and intervention model the paper never studies. Theorem 18 uses distance to a consensus election to justify a rule; it does not model strategic changes or robustness. Such a project may be worthwhile for ChoCo, but it would be a new problem inspired by the paper, rather than a computational mirror of one of its results.

So the negative case is strongest at the boundary of what counts as a mirror: a continuous population fits this paper unusually well, but the paper’s questions are already profile-level mathematical justifications, not computational questions. The case is weaker if “mirror” is broadened to include any new algorithmic problem inspired by the voting rules; continuous revision or robustness could be a sensible follow-on.
