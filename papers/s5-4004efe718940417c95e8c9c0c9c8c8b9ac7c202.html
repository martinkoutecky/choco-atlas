The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor I can offer is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that if \(N\ge 6\) and the priority structure satisfies Assumption 1—either it is strict or contains a four-way tie, and every pair of agents is ranked in both directions across objects or tied everywhere—then solvability implies that the structure is strict, HET, or TAU. Here solvability means the existence of a strategy-proof mechanism that always selects a constrained-efficient stable matching. This is only a necessary condition: the paper notes that strict and HET structures are solvable, while solvability of general TAU structures remains open.

Theorem 1 is a mechanism-existence result, not a complexity result: it gives no running time or complexity-class claim. So it is a borderline anchor for ChoCo, and the continuous question below should be read as the best extension of the paper’s result, not as a direct mirror of an algorithmic theorem.

A concrete extension would be Continuous Priority Solvability. Consider a city school-allocation system with many applicants, a finite set of school types, and seat capacities that grow with the applicant population. Applicants share a finite collection of priority signatures—such as combinations of distance and sibling categories—and, in any given profile, those with the same signature and ranking are indistinguishable. The number of applicants can be millions while the number of recurring signatures and rankings is much smaller.

An instance gives the objects \(O\), positive rational masses \(\mu_p\) for priority signatures \(p\), and rational per-capita capacities \(q_o\) for each object \(o\). The population is atomless within each signature class. A mechanism maps every measurable profile of strict preference reports to a measurable assignment: each individual receives one indivisible object or remains unmatched, while the mass assigned to an object is at most \(q_o\). Thus the type-level variables \(x_{p,R,o}\) are fractional averages over agents, even though almost every individual receives an integral assignment.

The question is whether there exists a direct mechanism for this population that is strategy-proof for every individual and, at every report profile, produces a stable matching that is Pareto-undominated among stable matchings. Stability extends the paper’s individual-rationality, non-wastefulness, and fairness conditions to the continuum; Pareto dominance compares assignments almost everywhere and requires a positive mass of agents to be strictly better off. The mechanism itself is the decision object. There is no scalar optimization objective in the paper; this is a feasibility question about the three mechanism requirements. A prover could be asked to characterize exactly which rational mass-supported, reversible priority structures admit such a mechanism.

The authors would recognize this as a close extension of their defined solvability question: it retains priorities, truthful preference reporting, stability, and constrained efficiency. Scaling seats with population is needed to keep matching meaningful as the applicant mass grows. The paper’s Theorem 1 motivates asking whether its three-class restriction survives in this setting, or whether atomless mass changes which priority structures are solvable.

I would tentatively expect a continuum-specific result (Class C), since individual deviations have zero mass while stability and welfare depend on aggregate allocations. The finite theorem does not settle that question. Further questions include whether the answer depends on the rational masses or only on the priority signatures, and whether any resulting characterization can be decided in time polynomial in \(m\), the number of signatures, and the input bit length.

The weakest point is substantial: this is a new mechanism-existence problem, not a computational problem the paper studies. Its standard complexity is also unclear until there is a finite representation for a mechanism over all measurable profiles. The mass model changes the role of individual tie-breaking and Pareto comparisons, so Theorem 1 does not establish that its discrete conclusion transfers. That leaves a genuine continuous analogue to investigate, but a weak ChoCo case under the programme’s computational-results criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes a necessary condition for the *existence* of a strategy-proof, constrained-efficient mechanism under stated restrictions on finite priority structures. Theorem 2 is another mechanism-existence characterization. Neither gives a complexity bound or classifies an algorithmic problem. The DA, TTC, and stable-improvement procedures discussed in the paper do not change that: their computational complexity is not what these theorems establish.

The proposed continuous solvability question stays with that same existence question. Adding an algorithmic follow-up—such as deciding from a finite priority matrix and rational masses whether a mechanism exists—would create a computational problem, but would not supply a computational result in this paper for it to mirror. Alternatively, asking whether the strict/HET/TAU restriction survives atomlessness would be a structural characterization of mechanisms, not a complexity mirror. Asking for an algorithm to find a matching for one reported profile would be computational, but it would change the target from universal mechanism existence to outcome computation.

The population setting itself is plausible: recurring student types and capacities that scale with population avoid the easy objection that multiplicity is unrealistic or that fixed supply makes the limit degenerate. But the proposed formulation also needs to settle what an individual deviation means in an atomless population. If outcomes are represented only by aggregate masses, changing one person’s report leaves those masses unchanged, so pointwise strategy-proofness may be ill-defined or vacuous. If the mechanism assigns indivisible objects pointwise, it needs a specified agent space and a rule for individual tie-breaking. Those choices may be manageable, but they define the new problem; they do not turn Theorem 1 into a computational anchor.

So the defensible negative verdict is narrow but firm: this is not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results under the programme’s computational-results criterion. It could inspire a worthwhile new project on computation or continuum mechanism design, but the paper does not provide the computational result that would make that project a mirror. I cannot honestly defend the stronger universal claim that no scenario could make such adjacent research worthwhile.
