The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many bidder classes \(a\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), finite-support value distributions \(F_a\), and per-capita service capacity \(\kappa\in(0,1)\), determine the sharp worst-case ratio of the optimal truthful, individually rational continuum mechanism to the best greedy posted-price policy, for revenue and welfare. Give a price-lottery algorithm attaining the guarantee.

The model it lives in

An atomless mass-one population with unit-demand agents, class masses \(\mu_a\), and private values drawn from \(F_a\), with capacity \(\kappa\). For objective \(J\), choose class sale rates \(q_a\) to maximize \(\sum_a\mu_a\bar J_a(q_a)\) subject to \(\sum_a\mu_aq_a\le\kappa\), where \(\bar J_a\) is the paper’s concave objective envelope; implement the rates with posted-price lotteries and compare with the best greedy policy.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(k\)-unit revenue and welfare guarantee in Theorem 4.1 when capacity scales with population; it leaves the matroid and \(p\)-independent guarantees, and the separate correlation-gap bounds, untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuum optimum for each objective equal the stated capacity program, and can its optimizer always be implemented by class-specific posted-price lotteries?
  • What convergence rate relates the finite guarantee to the continuum limit when \(k_n/n\to\kappa\)?
  • How does the limit change when supply stays fixed instead of scaling with population?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Fluid \(k\)-Unit Posted-Price Optimality. The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. Its \(k\)-unit entry says that greedy-SPM’s expected revenue is within a factor
\[ \beta_k=\frac{1}{1-\frac{k^k}{e^k k!}} \]
of Myerson’s optimal revenue, and that the welfare version of greedy-SPM is within the same factor of VCG welfare. Since \(\beta_k\) tends to \(1\) as \(k\) grows, the guarantee approaches optimality as supply increases. This is an approximation result, not an NP-hardness or running-time classification; the paper’s named results give no comparable hardness anchor. [Theorem 4.1 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.1843)

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study. An instance has finitely many bidder classes \(a\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), and a finite-support rational value distribution \(F_a\) for each class. Bidders are unit-demand and share their class’s prior and other public parameters; their private value \(v\) distinguishes types within a class. Equivalently, the population’s type mass is \(\mu_{a,v}=\mu_a F_a(v)\). The society is atomless, and the seller can serve mass \(\kappa\) per unit of population, for rational \(\kappa\in(0,1)\). Each person receives either service or no service, while the mass served from a type can be fractional.

For either \(J=\text{revenue}\) or \(J=\text{welfare}\), Fluid \(k\)-Unit Posted-Price Optimality asks: among all truthful, individually rational, feasible continuum mechanisms, what is the sharp worst-case ratio of the optimum \(J\) to the best greedy posted-price policy’s \(J\)? A solution should give the sharp ratio and an algorithm that returns a price lottery for each class and a fixed offer order attaining it. The benchmark is the continuum analogue of Myerson for revenue and VCG for welfare; posted prices and capacity remain the objects being compared.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, with ratio \(1\) in the atomless model. The reason is the same marginal-probability comparison used in the paper, now with mass in place of a finite random set. If a benchmark gives class \(a\)’s bidders average sale probability \(q_a\), feasibility requires \(\sum_a\mu_a q_a\leq\kappa\). Ironing gives a price lottery attaining the class’s best expected revenue at that sale probability; the paper gives the analogous construction for welfare. In an atomless class, demand at those prices has exactly the prescribed mass, so the total demand mass is at most \(\kappa\). There is no finite-population overflow that forces the SPM to turn away demand. Thus the price policy can attain the per-class upper bound on the benchmark, while remaining feasible.

The associated optimization is concrete: choose \(q_a\in[0,1]\) to maximize \(\sum_a\mu_a\bar R_a(q_a)\), subject to \(\sum_a\mu_aq_a\leq\kappa\), where \(\bar R_a\) is the concave revenue curve for class \(a\). For welfare, replace \(\bar R_a\) with the paper’s concave objective envelope \(\bar G_a\). With finite-support rational distributions these envelopes are piecewise linear, suggesting an LP whose size is polynomial in the input support and bit length. The main proof obligation is to show that this program exactly captures the continuum benchmark and that its price lotteries implement the optimum.

The regime is a large customer cohort for a platform or service with capacity proportional to its audience: many bidders, relatively few repeated classes of prior and public characteristics, and many individuals per supported type. This keeps the paper’s single-dimensional private values, unit demand, capacity constraint, truthfulness, and revenue-versus-welfare question. Scaling supply with population is a necessary co-modification: fixed \(k\) as population grows would describe vanishing per-capita capacity. The connection to the anchor is direct: for finite populations \(n\) with \(k_n\approx\kappa n\), Theorem 4.1’s factor tends to \(1\). The fluid problem asks for the corresponding continuous statement, rather than treating that finite sequence as already a continuous model.

I would expect this to be Class A. The result covers only Theorem 4.1’s \(k\)-unit case; I would not extend the claim to arbitrary matroids or \(p\)-independent systems without a separate mirror. Further questions include the finite-\(n\) convergence rate as a function of \(\kappa\) and type support, what changes when \(k\) stays fixed, and which other scalable feasibility systems admit an exact fluid posted-price benchmark.

The soft spot is the atomless step: it removes fluctuations in aggregate demand, the very source of the finite correlation gap analyzed by the paper. An author could object that this simplifies the comparison too far. I think the mirror survives because it retains private values, incentive constraints, integral service for each individual, and the same posted-price-versus-optimum benchmark; it changes the population and scales capacity with it. Theorem 4.1’s own \(k\)-dependent guarantee makes that limit especially plausible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1’s \(k\)-unit guarantee. I don’t see an honest way to argue that its fluid version is ill-posed. Repeated bidder classes with a per-capita capacity are plausible, and the paper’s Section 3.4 already points toward the relevant optimization.

The sharper objection is that the proposed fluid question changes what Theorem 4.1 is about. The theorem compares a correlated random winner set with an independent random demand set on a finite ground set. With an atomless population, aggregate demand can be represented as exact mass. The finite correlation gap that drives the theorem’s analysis disappears; sequential order can become irrelevant once the posted prices produce feasible total demand. Preserving that correlation-gap question would require retaining finite-population fluctuations or introducing a stochastic population model, rather than using a deterministic continuous society.

For the proposed fluid model, the computation is a small allocation problem. Write \(x_a(v)\) for the probability a bidder in class \(a\) with value \(v\) receives service. Truthfulness makes \(x_a\) nondecreasing, and feasibility is \(\sum_a \mu_a\,\mathbb{E}[x_a(V)]\leq\kappa\). Revenue maximization uses ironed virtual values as the objective coefficients; welfare uses \(v\). With finite supports, this is a linear program with monotonicity constraints and one capacity constraint. A lottery over posted prices implements any such monotone allocation. Thus the proposed mirror is credible, but it mainly asks to identify the continuum benchmark with a standard constrained mechanism-design program. It does not expose the kind of difficult pricing or separation problem that makes the ChoCo complexity map distinctive.

That is a case against spending ChoCo’s effort on this anchor, not a case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The fluid model could still be worth studying as a precise bridge from the finite theorem to its limit. The claim that *no scenario* makes such a mirror worthwhile is weaker than the evidence allows; a careful reader could reasonably green this one.
