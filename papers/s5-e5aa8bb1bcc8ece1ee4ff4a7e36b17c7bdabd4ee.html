The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), ranking types \(T\subseteq\operatorname{Perm}(C)\), rational masses \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), output multiplicities \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{C}\) with \(\sum_{j\in C}z_j=K(\varepsilon)\) such that the uniform lottery \(p_z(j)=z_j/K(\varepsilon)\) has worst-case metric distortion \(\operatorname{dist}_{\mu}(p_z)\le\frac52+\varepsilon\), with the supremum over hidden pseudometrics consistent with an atomless voter space carrying masses \(\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

A finite set of complete ranking types carries mass \(\mu\); hidden consistent metrics are adversarial, the decision variables are integer list multiplicities \(z_j\), and the objective is worst-case social-cost distortion of the induced uniform lottery.

The objection that survived

The opponent's best point is that the paper already quotients out voter identities into ranking statistics, so the continuous formulation may add only succinct encoding and no new population-side algorithmic bottleneck.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the bounded-randomness guarantees in Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 and the support-conditioned compression guarantee in Theorem 4.4. It does not by itself cover the unrestricted optimum, the lower bound on \(N^\star\), distinct-member committees, or improved dependence on \(\varepsilon\) and \(K\).

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the fixed-\(\varepsilon\) construction avoid \(m^{O(K(\varepsilon))}\) enumeration and become efficient in \(K\) or \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • Can the minimum list size for a given \(\mu\) be computed or bounded sharply, especially whether \(N^\star=2\)?
  • Do comparable guarantees survive when committee members must be distinct?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible positive mirror, although it is an algorithmic Class A case rather than a hardness-transfer case. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, coNP-hardness, parameterized-hardness, or other negative computational anchor; its hardness statements in the introduction are cited background. My lead anchor is therefore its own polynomial-time theorem.

The continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of a finite candidate set \(C\). To preserve the metric-distortion model faithfully, represent \(\mu\) by an atomless voter space \(V=[0,1]\), with each \(v\) assigned a ranking type \(\theta(v)\), and with \(\lambda(\theta^{-1}(t))=\mu_t\). A hidden pseudometric \(d\) may still vary between voters of the same ordinal type; it must merely respect their rankings. Define \(\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(j)=\int_V d(v,j)\,dv\), and for a lottery \(p\), define \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(p)\) as the supremum, over all consistent \(d\), of \(\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(p)/\min_i\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(i)\).

The action remains entirely finite: choose \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{C}\) with \(\sum_jz_j=K\), interpret it as the multiset containing \(z_j\) copies of \(j\), and use the uniform lottery \(p_z(j)=z_j/K\). Thus the population is continuous, while candidates, list entries, and randomization remain discrete.

This is a natural regime for a large municipal or national electorate choosing among a fixed shortlist of facilities, policies, or candidates. Millions of voters may share one of relatively few complete ordinal rankings induced by neighbourhood, commuting, or demographic categories. The relevant parameters are then the number \(\tau\) of ranking types and the bit-length of their masses, rather than the number \(n\) of named voters. Every rational \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) expands back to a finite profile, while expanding a succinct rational distribution into \(n\) voters may be exponentially larger than its binary encoding. That is a genuine high-multiplicity input regime, not merely a change of notation.

My strongest anchor is Theorem 1.2, proved in this paper. It states that for every constant \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a list size \(K(\varepsilon)\), independent of the numbers of voters and candidates, and a voting rule that deterministically identifies a \(K(\varepsilon)\)-element candidate multiset and then chooses a uniformly random entry, with metric distortion at most \(\frac52+\varepsilon\). More explicitly, with \(\bar\varepsilon=\min\{\varepsilon,\frac15\}\), it suffices to take \(K\ge\left\lceil\frac{27\pi}{8\bar\varepsilon^3}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\bar\varepsilon}{3}}\right)^2\right\rceil\). For each fixed \(\varepsilon\), the multiset can be found in polynomial time, although the polynomial degree may depend on \(K(\varepsilon)\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{Continuous\text{-}BRMV}_{\varepsilon}\): given \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types, output \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{C}\) with \(\sum_jz_j=K(\varepsilon)\) such that \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(p_z)\le\frac52+\varepsilon\). A solution is an algorithm that produces such a \(z\) for every \(\mu\).

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. The source lottery is still Mixed Integrated Veto combined with a Maximal Lottery; the output is still a deterministically chosen bounded-randomness list; the objective is still worst-case metric distortion. Only empirical voter frequencies have become population masses. The proof continues to use plurality masses, pairwise ranking frequencies, veto rates, and prefix probabilities, all of which have immediate \(\mu\)-weighted versions.

I would classify this mirror as Class A for every fixed accuracy parameter. The paper’s construction computes a Maximal Lottery by linear programming, follows the Integrated Veto trajectory through finitely many phases, and searches over \(K\)-element multisets. With \(\mu\) given explicitly by its \(\tau\) positive types, these operations are polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the encoding length of the masses, for fixed \(K\). This is not a claim of polynomial time in \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\): the paper only gives \(K=O(\varepsilon^{-3})\) and a search of roughly \(m^{O(K)}\). Improving that dependence is a natural further question.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 4.4, the Uniform-Support Compression Theorem, also proved in the paper. It says that if a lottery \(D\) has distortion at most \(\rho\), and every candidate in its support has deterministic distortion at most \(H\), then for every positive integer \(K\) there is a \(K\)-element multiset from \(\operatorname{supp}(D)\) whose uniform lottery has distortion at most \(\rho+(H+1)\sqrt{\pi/(8K)}\). The sharper bound \(\rho+(H+1)\kappa_K\) also holds, where \(\kappa_K\) is the expected one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic for \(K\) samples.

The continuous problem here is:

\(\mathsf{Continuous\text{-}Lottery\ Compression}\): given \((C,\mu,D,K,\rho,H)\) satisfying \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(D)\le\rho\) and \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(\delta_j)\le H\) for every \(j\in\operatorname{supp}(D)\), output \(z\) with \(\sum_jz_j=K\), \(z_j=0\) outside \(\operatorname{supp}(D)\), and \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(p_z)\le\rho+(H+1)\sqrt{\pi/(8K)}\).

A directly checkable sufficient certificate is obtained from the type-weighted prefix discrepancy. If \(a_1^t,\ldots,a_m^t\) is the preference order for type \(t\), let \(F_t(r)=D(\{a_1^t,\ldots,a_r^t\})\), let \(\widehat F_t(r)\) be the corresponding probability under \(p_z\), and require \(\sum_t\mu_t\max_r(\widehat F_t(r)-F_t(r))\le\kappa_K\). This is exactly the paper’s voter average with the finite population replaced by \(\mu\).

This problem is also Class A for fixed \(K\): enumerate the multisets, compute their type-weighted discrepancies, and retain one meeting the bound. For variable \(K\), the paper establishes no FPT result; its enumeration is an \(m^{O(K)}\)-type procedure. The interesting open questions are whether one can avoid enumeration, obtain an FPT or polynomial-in-\(K\) algorithm, or compress source lotteries without the support-wise deterministic-distortion hypothesis.

As a concrete finite-list anchor, Theorem 1.3 states, again as a result proved in this paper, that there is a voting rule which deterministically identifies a multiset of \(164\) candidates and uniformly randomizes over it with distortion strictly below \(3\), yielding \(2\le N^\star\le164\). Its continuous mirror is the fixed-list search problem: given \(\mu\), output \(z\) with \(\sum_jz_j=164\) and \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(p_z)<3\). This is a separate and natural question from approaching \(\frac52\): it asks for the smallest constant list size that breaks the deterministic barrier. The theorem gives a Class A solution for this fixed constant, while leaving open whether \(N^\star=2\).

The main weakness is that this paper is already highly anonymous. Its distortion bounds depend on normalized ranking frequencies, and the finite proofs often look as though they are written in population-mass notation already. An opponent can therefore argue that replacing a finite profile by \(\mu\) is a routine high-multiplicity reformulation, not a dramatic new computational phenomenon. There is also a modelling subtlety: voters with the same ranking need not have identical hidden metric distances. I would handle that by making the metric adversarial and latent, exactly as in the paper; the complete type for the computational input is the ordinal ranking, while the unobserved metric is universally quantified rather than an agent-specific price or parameter.

That concession limits the strength of the claim. This paper does not provide a hardness result whose discrete obstruction might dissolve, nor does it show a new Class C boundary. But it does provide a clean, referee-recognisable continuous-population search problem whose action, objective, and guarantees are unchanged, while the input becomes a distribution over voter types. The strongest honest conclusion is therefore: a solid Class A continuous mirror, especially for Theorem 1.2 and the compression theorem, with genuine high-multiplicity computational questions but modest novelty compared with the programme’s LP-collapse and hardness-transfer examples.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that all three anchors continuize a statistic the paper has already made sufficient. If \(t\) ranges over rankings and
\[ \mu_t=\frac{|\{v:\theta_v=t\}|}{|V|}, \]
then every quantity used by the paper is already a function of \(\mu\). For example,
\[ s_{I\succ j} =\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf 1[t\text{ ranks every member of }I\text{ above }j]. \]
Plurality scores, veto rates, \(\ell_I\), and every certificate in the proofs are obtained in exactly this way. A rational \(\mu\) is therefore just an alternative encoding of a finite profile obtained by duplicating each type. Real \(\mu\)'s add only the closure of the same finite-dimensional simplex.

This matters most for Theorem 1.2. The proposed problem of receiving \(\mu\) and outputting a \(K\)-entry multiset is not a new computational problem induced by a continuous population. It is the paper's existing rule, run on the sufficient statistics of a finite profile rather than on the voters one by one. The Maximal Lottery, Integrated Veto, and multiset enumeration already depend only on those statistics. The claimed polynomial-time algorithm in the continuous representation is therefore an input-compression observation, not a new continuous-optimization result: the population contributes coefficients, while all combinatorial work remains over candidates and list entries.

A more ambitious version—optimizing the best multiset for a given \(\mu\), or making the dependence on \(K\) or \(\varepsilon\) efficient—could certainly be interesting. But that would be a problem about candidate lotteries and metric certificates, not about continuizing the society. The paper's theorem supplies no population-multiplicity phenomenon for such a problem to expose.

Theorem 4.4 is even less suitable as a population mirror. Its compression argument samples candidates from \(D\); it does not compress or optimize over voters. The finite-population average in Lemma 4.1 is already
\[ \frac1{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\Delta_v^+ =\sum_t\mu_t\Delta_t^+. \]
Replacing the left-hand side by the right-hand side changes the notation but not the argument, the certificate, or the algorithm. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov error concerns the bounded lottery's candidate support, not population multiplicity. Even the strongest version—given a type-weighted prefix-discrepancy oracle and seeking a good multiset—remains a finite candidate-side compression problem whose difficulty is present for a single finite profile.

Theorem 1.3 has the same defect. The \(164\)-entry rule is a uniform guarantee over every finite profile. Consequently, it immediately applies to every rational distribution over rankings, and the same inequalities extend to arbitrary masses. Asking for an \(164\)-entry output on input \(\mu\) does not create a new high-multiplicity regime; it merely asks for the already-proved universal rule in compressed input format. The number \(164\) concerns bounded randomness in the outcome, not the number or multiplicity of voters.

There is also a modelling dilemma that the proposed atomless construction does not fully resolve. If a voter type is only a ranking, it is not a complete description of the agent's metric position: two voters with the same ranking may have entirely different distances to candidates, and those distances determine social cost. If full metric data are included in the type, then the society is no longer the paper's ordinal input and the rule has cardinal information. If the metric data remain hidden and adversarial, then the distribution over them cannot be used by the algorithm; the algorithm sees only the ranking marginal, returning us to the tautological \(\mu\)-encoding above. Thus the ranking-only model is faithful to the rule's information, but not a genuinely complete population model; the full-type model is complete, but changes the problem.

This is not the mistaken objection that “the answer is unchanged,” nor is it an objection based on existing high-multiplicity literature. The narrower point is that the paper itself has already quotiented out voter identity, and its substantive theorems operate on the resulting aggregate distribution. There is no population-side LP, transfer problem, multiplicity-sensitive hardness, or algorithmic bottleneck for continuization to reveal.

The negative case is nevertheless not airtight. Under the programme’s broadest standard, the proponent can reasonably call the compressed-input formulation a Class A high-multiplicity problem, and the atomless latent-metric model is coherent. If that alone counts as a worthwhile mirror, Theorem 1.2 survives. The best honest negative conclusion is therefore that the paper offers no *new* continuous-population computational phenomenon: its mirrors are faithful re-encodings of an already distributional finite-profile analysis. That is a strong novelty objection, but not a conclusive proof that no useful mirror can be formulated.
