What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue covers only Theorem 1’s fair-bound characterization of the constrained equal awards rule. The paper’s other rule characterizations and bound-fulfillment results are also axiomatic and supply no computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: it proves axiomatic characterizations of allocation rules, not complexity or algorithmic results. Its closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every finite claims problem, using the fair bound \(f_i(E,c)=\min\{c_i,E/n\}\), the constrained equal awards rule is the unique rule that respects that bound and satisfies constrained equal treatment of equals. That is a substantive anchor for an axiomatic mirror, but it does not meet your required computational-anchor test.

There is a plausible population mirror of Theorem 1. Consider a large group of claimants divided into a small number \(\tau\) of claim types, where type \(t\) has claim \(c_t\) and mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(e=E/n\) be the resource per claimant. The decision is an award \(x_t\) to each claimant of type \(t\), with \(0\le x_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=e\). The continuous fair bound is \(\min\{c_t,e\}\). A solution is the award schedule satisfying that bound and the continuum version of constrained equal treatment of equals; the characterization to investigate is whether it uniquely gives \(x_t=\min\{c_t,\lambda\}\), where \(\lambda\) makes the resource constraint hold. There is no optimization objective in the paper: the allocation is selected by its axioms.

A convincing regime would be a large bankruptcy or benefit-rationing population with many claimants but relatively few standardized claim amounts. Claim amount is a complete type because it is the only agent attribute used in the paper’s model. This is a credible high-multiplicity scenario, and the per-capita endowment is the natural scaling of the original aggregate endowment. For finite rational support, computing the resulting awards is tractable by sorting the claim amounts and finding \(\lambda\).

That is the strongest positive case I can make: the population mirror is concrete and plausibly recognizable to the authors, but it is a mirror of their axiomatic result. The computation is straightforward, and the paper supplies no named hardness or algorithmic result to anchor a continuization-complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1—the proponent’s anchor—characterizes a rule by axioms. The other main results likewise characterize CEA, Ibn Ezra, or their duals. The paper poses no algorithmic or complexity question.

That distinction survives the best population model. A large population with standardized claims is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: represent claim \(c_t\) by mass \(\mu_t\), scale the endowment to a per-person amount \(e=E/n\), and require \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=e\). The fair bound becomes \(\min\{c_t,e\}\), and the natural continuum version of the axioms could characterize \(x_t=\min\{c_t,\lambda\}\). I cannot honestly argue that this is an incoherent mirror; the claims and the relevant treatment are preserved.

But it is an axiomatic mirror. Asking for the complexity of evaluating the resulting allocation would add a computational problem that the paper does not study. It would be an analyst’s new question about implementing a characterized rule, not a computational result from this paper whose continuous-population version fills ChoCo’s stated gap. Other scenarios do not fix that mismatch: without adding an objective, search task, or decision problem, the paper’s contribution remains an axiomatic characterization.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit and weak on the universal claim. A credible continuous-population mirror exists; what fails is its status as the kind of computational mirror ChoCo is seeking.
