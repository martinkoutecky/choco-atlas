What the mirror covers

No computational theorem is mirrored; Theorems 1–4 concern implementability, and the paper’s examples and extensions remain finite-agent implementation results.

The case FOR (proponent)

My honest positive case is weak: this paper has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo needs to anchor a continuous mirror. Its theorems characterize when a social choice correspondence can be implemented; they do not classify the complexity of an algorithmic problem.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here. It says that if a social choice correspondence \(F\) satisfies Property Q and no veto power, and there are \(I\ge3\) agents, then some mechanism implements \(F\) in undominated Nash equilibrium: at every state \(s\), its undominated Nash equilibrium outcomes are exactly \(F(s)\). Property Q requires that an outcome selected at \(s\) remain selected at \(s'\) when preferences are unchanged or some agents become completely indifferent. No veto power requires that an outcome be selected when at least \(I-1\) agents rank it at least as high as every alternative. This is a substantive implementability theorem, but it says nothing about whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized-tractable. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 are further implementability extensions, not complexity results either.

A plausible *new* extension would model a large deliberative body whose members share a relatively small set \(T\) of preference rankings. A type would be a complete ranking \(t\), and its mass \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the body with that ranking. A mechanism could take reports and map their aggregate distribution to an alternative; the question would be whether a correspondence over such distributions can be implemented by the mechanism’s equilibrium outcomes. But that is not an anchored continuous problem from this paper, and its equilibrium notion needs care: in an anonymous atomless model, one person’s report may not affect the aggregate outcome, unlike the individually strategic deviations used in the paper’s constructions.

So there is a possible high-multiplicity setting to explore, but no named computational anchor to mirror and no continuous complexity question that can honestly be handed to a prover as the paper’s counterpart. The weakest point is decisive: the paper studies implementability, not computational complexity, and the proposed population model would require new choices about how individual incentives work in a continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, but it does not make a computational claim: it gives conditions under which some game implements a social choice correspondence. Theorem 1 and the extensions in Theorems 3 and 4 are implementation results of the same kind. The paper specifies no algorithmic input for “find an implementing mechanism,” no efficiency measure for the mechanism it constructs, and no complexity classification. Its construction even uses unbounded integer messages. Turning that into a runtime question would require choosing a finite representation of the correspondence \(F\), its domain of states, and the mechanisms being searched. Those choices could define a new complexity project, but they are not supplied by the paper’s result.

The proponent’s large deliberative body is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many people can share a small set of rankings. The deeper problem is what happens to the paper’s equilibrium concept when that population becomes atomless. If the institution’s outcome depends on the distribution of reports, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot change the outcome. Every report then gives that person the same payoff against every profile of others; no report is weakly dominated, and all reports are best responses. The undominated Nash refinement that drives Theorem 2 loses its force. Keeping positive-mass groups as strategic players avoids that collapse, but makes each group’s deviation a coordinated bloc deviation, not the individual deviation in the paper.

One could instead keep a large but finite electorate, preserving individual influence of \(1/N\), and study how implementation behaves as its preference frequencies approach a distribution. That is a coherent finite-size or limit question. But the key incentive constraints still depend on the discrete agent’s ability to change the outcome, while the limit removes that ability. A result about convergence, approximation, or a modified coalition equilibrium could be worthwhile in its own right; it would need new questions and new equilibrium definitions rather than mirror this paper’s theorem.

So the negative case is strongest against a faithful continuous mirror of the paper, not against every possible study of atomless implementation. Its honest weak point is that a deliberately redesigned continuum game might still be valuable. But that would be an independent research direction, and the paper offers no computational result to anchor it.
