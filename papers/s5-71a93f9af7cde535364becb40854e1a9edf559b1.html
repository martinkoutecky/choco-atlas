The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s Kemeny consensus computation. There is an important qualification: the supplied paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. So it has no anchor that meets your stated requirement, and I should not relabel a passage in the supplement as one. The closest claim is in Supplementary §1.2: computing the Kemeny/Condorcet optimum is NP-hard in general. The paper states this as known; it does not present it as a numbered result proved there. The same section says computing the full optimal set generally takes time proportional to the number of weak rankings, but that too is unnumbered.

If an unnumbered claim is acceptable as a fallback, its continuous mirror is precise. Let \(C\) be \(m\) candidates, and let \(\mathcal W(C)\) be their weak rankings, including ties. An instance is a support \(S\subseteq\mathcal W(C)\) and binary-rational masses \(\rho_r>0\), for \(r\in S\), summing to \(1\). A type is a complete weak ranking; its mass is the fraction of the population with that ranking. For each possible consensus ranking \(c\in\mathcal W(C)\), define

\[ D_\rho(c)=\sum_{r\in S}\rho_r\,d_{\mathrm{Kem}}(r,c). \]

The problem is to return a \(c\) minimizing \(D_\rho(c)\). This mirrors the paper’s Condorcet criterion, not its entire proposed analysis of the mean–standard-deviation frontier.

A plausible regime is a large survey or consumer poll in which many people rank the same shortlist of products, jokes, or movies. The paper’s examples include \(24{,}921\) joke raters and \(930\) movie raters, each ranking five items. For five candidates there are at most \(541\) weak-ranking types, though the paper does not report the actual support sizes. Such respondents share a type exactly when they produce the same ranking, including the same ties. The population can therefore be much larger than the support, with the input described by \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit lengths rather than by a list of voters. The authors should recognize this as their own consensus-ranking question with repeated ballots grouped by type.

I would expect this mirror to be hardness-transferring. Any finite profile with \(n_r\) voters of type \(r\) maps to masses \(\rho_r=n_r/n\), preserving every candidate’s mean Kemeny distance and every minimizer. Thus the discrete hardness carries over; its combinatorics lie in the number of candidates and possible output rankings. For fixed \(m=5\), direct enumeration is easy, as the paper itself observes. The computational question becomes consequential when \(m\) grows, even if \(\tau\) remains moderate.

This case covers the paper’s Kemeny-optimal consensus ranking, the \(\mu\)-minimizing endpoint of its analysis. It does not cover computing the full Pareto-efficient set in \((\mu,\sigma)\), selecting among its points, or the paper’s sampling and noise-stability experiments. Those suggest further continuous questions: compute the full mean–variance frontier over rankings, or find a ranking with minimum variance subject to \(D_\rho(c)\leq D_\rho^*+\varepsilon\).

The weakest point is twofold: there is no eligible numbered anchor, and the proposed mirror is very close to grouping the paper’s finite ballots into a histogram. That closeness helps with author recognition but limits the claim that the paper itself opens a new computational problem. As an honest positive case, this is a credible continuous-population formulation with a clean hardness-transfer story, but it cannot satisfy the requested anchor condition as written.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper offers no eligible computational result to mirror. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting a complexity result. Its Kemeny NP-hardness claim is background, and the supplement’s statement about the time needed to enumerate the optimal set is not a proved computational result. The paper’s contribution is the mean–standard-deviation proposal and its empirical illustrations. So the proponent’s Kemeny problem may be a sensible ChoCo question, but it does not meet the requested anchor condition.

There is a substantive limitation as well: the paper’s distinctive motivation uses finite-sample fluctuations. Its admissible band, \(\mu(\mathbf c)-\mu(\mathbf c^*)\sim 1/\sqrt n\), shrinks to zero as \(n\) grows, and the resampling noise disappears in a population limit. That weakens a mirror of the paper’s near-optimality and robustness story. It does not eliminate the cross-sectional variance criterion, though: for a distribution \(\rho\) over ballot types, one can define \(D_\rho(\mathbf c)=\sum_r\rho_r d_{\rm Kem}(r,\mathbf c)\) and \(V_\rho(\mathbf c)=\sum_r\rho_r d_{\rm Kem}(r,\mathbf c)^2-D_\rho(\mathbf c)^2\), then ask for the Pareto frontier of \((D_\rho,V_\rho)\).

That stronger question survives the substantive objections. Large surveys over a fixed shortlist are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and rational masses recover the finite-profile quantities exactly. Grouping identical ballots is precisely the intended population model. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The defensible negative is narrower: the proponent has not anchored their case in a computational result of this paper, and the paper’s most distinctive finite-noise motivation weakens under continuization. The broader weighted Kemeny and mean–variance-frontier questions remain viable research questions.
