The continuous mirror question

Fix rational \(\epsilon\in(0,1/6)\) and set \(p=1/6-\epsilon\). Given a rational initial society \(\mu_0=(a_0,b_0,q_0)\) with \(a_0+b_0+q_0=1\) and \(a_0-b_0\ge\eta>0\), and a horizon \(K\), iterate \(\mu_{t+1}=F_p(\mu_t)\). Decide whether some \(t\le K\) has \(a_t-b_t\in\Delta_\epsilon\), where \(\Delta_\epsilon=[2\sqrt{\epsilon}/(1+6\epsilon),\,1-2((1-6\epsilon)/12)^3]\); if so, return the least such \(t\) and \(\mu_t\).

The society is a distribution \(\mu=(a,b,q)\) over the three current states. For each state \(i\), let \(r_i=p+(1-3p)\mu_i\) be the mass of messages observed as \(i\). The continuous update is \(F_p(a,b,q)=(qr_\alpha+a(r_\alpha+r_q),\,qr_\beta+b(r_\beta+r_q),\,qr_q+ar_\beta+br_\alpha)\); the question is the first time its orbit reaches the low-noise bias band.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow: this paper has no named result in the usual \(\mathrm{P}\), NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity sense. Its strongest computational anchors are runtime guarantees for a randomized distributed consensus protocol. Those are still named algorithmic results, and they give a plausible mirror for the protocol’s behaviour on a large population.

The regime is a large, anonymous, fully mixed population. Each agent’s type is its complete current state: \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), or undecided. The society is \(\mu=(a,b,q)\), with \(a+b+q=1\); each coordinate is the fraction of agents in that state. Thus the support has at most three types while the number of agents can be very large. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s model: agents use the same update rule and noise parameter, and the complete graph makes their identities irrelevant.

There is a direct mean-field update for this society. Given noise \(p\), the distribution of the message an agent observes is
\[ r_\alpha=p+(1-3p)a,\qquad r_\beta=p+(1-3p)b,\qquad r_q=p+(1-3p)q. \]
The continuum society’s next state is the vector of expected next-round masses:
\[ F_p(a,b,q)= \bigl(qr_\alpha+a(r_\alpha+r_q),\ qr_\beta+b(r_\beta+r_q),\ qr_q+ar_\beta+br_\alpha\bigr). \]
This preserves the paper’s three states, communication rule, and noise model. The computational question is about the orbit \(\mu_{t+1}=F_p(\mu_t)\): its decision variable is the successive mass vector, and its objective is to reach a specified bias region as quickly as possible.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1 (Almost Majority Consensus), proved here. It says that for \(p=1/6-\epsilon\), with fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1/6)\), any initial configuration with signed bias \(s\ge\gamma\sqrt{n\log n}\) reaches, within \(\mathcal{O}(\log n)\) rounds and with high probability, a configuration with bias in
\[ \Delta_\epsilon= \left[ \frac{2\sqrt{\epsilon}}{1+6\epsilon}n,\ \left(1-2\left(\frac{1-6\epsilon}{12}\right)^3\right)n \right]. \]
It then stays in that bias range for \(\Omega(n^\lambda)\) rounds with high probability, for some constant \(\lambda>0\) depending on \(\epsilon\) and \(\gamma\). The result also holds symmetrically for negative initial bias.

The corresponding continuous problem is Low-Noise First-Hit. Given rational \(\epsilon\), an initial rational mass vector \(\mu_0=(a_0,b_0,q_0)\), a minimum signal \(\eta>0\), and a horizon \(K\), with \(p=1/6-\epsilon\) and \(a_0-b_0\ge\eta\), iterate \(F_p\). Decide whether the signed bias enters
\[ \left[ \frac{2\sqrt{\epsilon}}{1+6\epsilon},\ 1-2\left(\frac{1-6\epsilon}{12}\right)^3 \right] \]
within \(K\) steps; if so, return the first such step and its mass vector. The finite theorem’s signal scale corresponds to \(\eta=\gamma\sqrt{\log n/n}\), so its \(\mathcal{O}(\log n)\) time scale suggests \(\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eta))\) iterations in the mirror. I expect this low-dimensional orbit problem to be tractable by an invariant-region and hitting-time analysis. Further questions include proving forward invariance of the target band and pinning down what happens at \(p=1/6\).

A second anchor is Theorem 2 (Victory of Noise), proved here. For \(p=1/6+\epsilon\), with fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1/3]\), any initial configuration with \(\lvert s\rvert\ge\gamma\sqrt{n\log n}\) reaches, within \(\mathcal{O}(\log n)\) rounds and with high probability, a configuration with \(\lvert s\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\log n})\). It then remains at that scale for \(\Omega(n^{\lambda'})\) rounds with high probability, for some constant \(\lambda'>0\). Its mirror is High-Noise Signal-Erasure: given rational \(\epsilon,\mu_0,\eta,\kappa\), and horizon \(K\), with \(p=1/6+\epsilon\) and \(\lvert a_0-b_0\rvert\ge\eta\), decide whether the \(F_p\)-orbit reaches \(\lvert a-b\rvert\le\kappa\eta\) within \(K\) steps, returning the first hitting time and state. Here \(\kappa\) is a fixed target factor; the finite theorem corresponds to its bound \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\log n})\) after normalization. I also expect this fixed-dimensional problem to be tractable, through analysis of convergence toward the symmetric region. It raises the related questions of convergence rates and the critical case.

The two problems are separate mirrors of the paper’s two sides of the transition. They cover its biased-start results, not the entire paper, and neither is a hardness-transfer result: the paper supplies no named hardness anchor to continue. The paper’s Lemma 1, proved here, further supports the population interpretation by showing an equivalence between its oblivious-noise model and an appropriately populated stubborn-agent model.

My weakest point is that the mirror changes the phenomena that make the paper especially interesting. In the limit map, an exactly balanced state stays balanced by symmetry; it cannot reproduce the paper’s symmetry breaking from finite-population fluctuations. And a deterministic attracting region lasting forever is not the same claim as a stochastic metastable phase lasting polynomially many rounds. So the mirror is plausible for the biased-start phase transition, but it does not capture the paper’s fluctuation-driven results. A further computational challenge would be to relate exact or approximate orbit algorithms to those finite-population guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not argue that this population lacks a meaningful high-multiplicity regime. On the complete graph, agents are anonymous, their three current states determine the aggregate process, and the support is at most three. That is an unusually clean population model. The stronger objection is about fit: the paper analyzes the evolution and runtime of a stochastic protocol. It does not ask for an algorithm that chooses a change to a society, as in bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness. Passing its aggregate state to a continuum gives a dynamical system, but does not by itself create the kind of computational problem ChoCo is meant to chart.

Theorem 1 (Almost Majority Consensus) is a real algorithmic runtime result, so I would not dismiss it as having no computational anchor. But its natural mass map, including the proponent’s \(F_p\), replaces random next-round counts by their expectations. For normalized bias \(d=a-b\) and undecided mass \(q\), the expected update is \(d'=d(1-p+(1-3p)q)\). That map can support a worthwhile analysis of fixed points, basins, or a first-hitting time. Yet the proposed first-hit decision problem is a newly chosen query about iterating a three-coordinate recurrence; it is not an algorithmic task the paper studies. The theorem’s central guarantee is about a random execution reaching and remaining in a band with high probability. An exact mean trajectory does not express that guarantee.

A stronger low-noise mirror could ask how the deterministic map amplifies a small initial majority and where its polarized attractors lie. That is coherent, but it moves the work into analysis of a fixed-dimensional nonlinear system. The theorem allows initial bias as small as \(\gamma\sqrt{n\log n}\), which becomes mass bias \(\gamma\sqrt{\log n/n}\). At that scale, the proof’s concentration and fluctuations matter; at exact balance, the deterministic map preserves symmetry and cannot reproduce the paper’s symmetry breaking. One can instead start with a fixed positive mass bias, but that asks about the map’s ordinary macroscopic flow, not the delicate finite-population guarantee that makes this theorem distinctive.

Theorem 2 (Victory of Noise) has the same mismatch, more sharply at its endpoint. Its bounded-bias region has normalized width \(\mathcal O(\sqrt{\log n/n})\), which vanishes in the population limit. A deterministic map may show attraction toward zero bias from a fixed macroscopic signal, but it cannot represent the finite system’s fluctuation-scale residual bias, switching, or polynomially long metastable phase. A better formulation could retain a finite-size diffusion or study deviations around the mean trajectory; that would be a legitimate stochastic asymptotics project. Its state would be a fluctuation process scaled with \(n\), though, rather than just a continuous society of opinion masses. It would extend the paper’s probabilistic analysis, not give it a continuous computational mirror in ChoCo’s sense.

Lemma 1, the noise–stubborn-agent equivalence, supports the population interpretation but supplies no separate computational anchor. In mass terms its content is the direct substitution of stubborn mass \(p_{\text{noise}}/(1-p_{\text{noise}})\) with the stated opinion proportions. That identity is useful modeling machinery; it does not pose a new algorithmic problem. Likewise, one could add a minimum-seed or intervention-control problem on the mass map, but that would introduce an optimization objective absent from the paper. In this three-state, fully mixed setting, it is not apparent that such an addition would yield a meaningful complexity landscape rather than a new control problem.

So the negative case is a case against spending ChoCo effort here, not a claim that no continuous model can be written down. The paper’s two named theorems do have credible continuum analogues as dynamical questions, and the population regime is excellent. The weakness is that their most faithful content is stochastic runtime and finite-size behavior, while their cleanest continuous replacements are analyses of a small deterministic map. If ChoCo includes computational dynamics as a target, a mirror could survive; on the programme’s stated focus on computational social choice problems over societies, neither anchor gives a compelling complexity question.
