The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is continuous quota apportionment with house monotonicity. Let the types be districts, and let \(\mu_i\) be the fraction of the country’s population living in district \(i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A national census with hundreds of millions of residents and a few dozen districts gives the right regime: many residents per type, but only \(\tau=n\) distinct types. Residents of one district are indistinguishable for this problem; the relevant input is their district’s mass. Seats remain indivisible and are assigned to districts.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, the paper’s house-monotonicity and quota-compliance characterization, proved here. In its notation, for every population vector \(p\) and house size \(H\), the apportionments achievable at \(H\) by house-monotone, quota-compliant solutions are exactly \(\{A(z,H):z\in\mathcal E(p,\Phi(p,H))\}\). Here \(z_{i,t}\) records whether seat \(t\) goes to district \(i\), \(A_i(z,H)=\sum_{t=1}^H z_{i,t}\), and \(\mathcal E\) is the set of integral extreme points of the paper’s LP. That LP requires \(\sum_i z_{i,t}=1\) for each seat \(t\), \(z_{i,t}\ge 0\), and \(\lfloor t p_i/P\rfloor\le\sum_{\ell=1}^t z_{i,\ell}\le\lceil t p_i/P\rceil\) for every district and every \(t\le\Phi(p,H)\), where \(P=\sum_i p_i\). The recursively defined \(\Phi\) is a lookahead horizon chosen to make the prefix allocation extendible. The theorem is proved in the paper using the Balinski–Young characterization and the integral network-flow formulation.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Continuous House-Monotone Quota Realizability. Its input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(n\) district types, a house size \(H\), and a target allocation \(x\in\mathbb N_0^n\) with \(\sum_i x_i=H\). Ask whether there is a sequence of seat assignments \(a_1,a_2,\ldots\), one district per seat, such that for every \(h\ge 1\), the prefix counts \(X_i(h)=|\{s\le h:a_s=i\}|\) satisfy \(\lfloor h\mu_i\rfloor\le X_i(h)\le\lceil h\mu_i\rceil\), and \(X(H)=x\). A yes-solution is such a schedule; Theorem 3 gives a finite polyhedral characterization of which targets are realizable.

This is the paper’s own problem under the continuous-population reading: set \(\mu_i=p_i/P\). That is exactly why the mirror is plausible to the authors. The LP is already written in terms of fractional quotas, and its seat-by-seat variables are a natural sequential allocation of the population’s representation. The outcome remains integral, while the population input is a distribution of masses. The paper also proves \(\Phi(p,H)-H\le\max_i\lceil P/p_i\rceil=\max_i\lceil1/\mu_i\rceil\) for \(H\ge2\). Thus the direct formulation is polynomial in the lookahead \(K\); in a balanced regime with \(\mu_i=\Theta(1/n)\) and \(H=\Theta(n)\), that horizon is \(O(n)\). I expect a Class A result in that regime. For unrestricted binary-rational shares, the displayed bound can be exponential in the bit length of a tiny \(\mu_i\), so a prover should settle whether the horizon can be compressed or whether that regime has its own hardness. The theorem itself does not establish polynomial time in \(n\) and the input bit length.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 1, the main result of the stationary-divisor section, proved here. It says: if every \(k\)-level in every arrangement of \(m\) lines has complexity \(O(g(m))\), then every apportionment instance with \(n\) states has at most \(O(g(n))\) breaking points as the stationary rounding parameter \(\delta\) varies; conversely, if for every \(m\) some arrangement has a \(k\)-level of complexity \(\Omega(h(m))\), then for every \(n\) there is an \(n\)-state instance with \(\Omega(h(n))\) breaking points. Corollary 1 instantiates the known line-arrangement bounds: the number is at most \(O(n^{4/3})\), and some instance has \(n e^{\Omega(\sqrt{\ln n})}\) breaking points. The correspondence is proved in this paper; the line-arrangement bounds it uses are cited results.

Its mirror question is Continuous Divisor-Breakpoint Enumeration. Given rational district masses \(\mu\) and an integer house size \(H\), compute the ordered list of values \(\delta\in[0,1]\) where the set of \(\delta\)-divisor apportionments changes. The divisor rule uses a multiplier \(\alpha>0\) and admits \(x\) when \(\sum_i x_i=H\) and \(x_i-1+\delta\le\alpha\mu_i\le x_i+\delta\) for every \(i\). The output is the breakpoint list, with the apportionment sets on the intervals between consecutive breakpoints. I expect Class A: the paper describes an algorithm for computing the outcomes using the \(k\)-level connection, and the stated breakpoint bound is independent of \(H\). Its lower-bound construction may require large \(H\), so whether comparable bounds hold when \(H\) is restricted relative to \(n\) is a natural follow-up. Here \(\delta\) remains the paper’s method parameter; the continuization is the district population input.

These two problems cover the paper’s house-monotone quota section and its stationary-divisor output analysis. I would not stretch the mirror to encompass the variable-house-size randomization results: those chiefly change the randomized outcome and house-size model, rather than making the population continuous. Nor would I claim the paper proves an NP-hardness or a standard P result; its named anchors are structural and polyhedral results, with an algorithm described for the breakpoint problem.

The soft spot is also the reason this is a narrow case: for rational \(\mu\), the paper’s integer population vector and the distribution \(\mu=p/P\) encode the same quotas. Its mathematics is already largely about population shares, so the mirror may look like a reinterpretation rather than a distinct continuization result. The positive case is that the intended regime is still clear and sensible—large populations aggregated into a small number of district types—and Theorem 3 puts precisely that aggregate apportionment question into an integral flow formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors are computationally meaningful, but their proposed mirrors are already the same apportionment problems in normalized coordinates. For any positive rational distribution \(\mu\), choose a common denominator \(D\) and set \(p_i=D\mu_i\). Then \(P=D\), so for every house size \(h\),
\[ \left\lfloor \frac{h p_i}{P}\right\rfloor=\lfloor h\mu_i\rfloor, \qquad \left\lceil \frac{h p_i}{P}\right\rceil=\lceil h\mu_i\rceil. \]
Conversely, every integer population vector gives the rational distribution \(\mu_i=p_i/P\). Clearing denominators takes polynomially many input bits. Thus the continuous input range in the proposed mirrors is exactly the paper’s input range, up to encoding.

That directly defeats the house-monotone quota-realizability mirror. Its prefix constraints are the quota constraints in the paper’s network-flow LP, expressed with \(\mu_i\) instead of \(p_i/P\); its target schedules are the same seat sequences. The lookahead \(\Phi\) also depends on these normalized quotas. The characterization may leave an interesting algorithmic question when the LP horizon is large relative to the binary input, but that question is equally present for the paper’s integer inputs. It would be a worthwhile apportionment complexity problem, not a new consequence of making the population continuous.

The same equivalence defeats the breakpoint mirror. Replacing \(p_i\) by \(D\mu_i\) scales every line \(\ell_{i,t}(\delta)=(t+\delta)/p_i\) by the same factor \(1/D\). Their order, intersections’ \(\delta\)-coordinates, and breaking points are unchanged. So the paper’s breakpoint analysis and its algorithm for computing outcomes already apply to rational population shares. The varying parameter in that result is the rounding rule \(\delta\), not the society; writing the fixed society as shares instead of counts changes nothing.

The proposed census story is plausible: residents can be grouped by district, and the district masses are meaningful. But those masses are precisely the ratios the paper already uses. Adding resident-level identities or demographic types would not strengthen this mirror unless the apportionment rule used that information; once it does, the question is no longer about these results. Allowing irrational masses would go beyond the programme’s finite rational input model.

So the objection is not that the mirrors lack a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, or that their answers would be uninteresting. It is that for both anchors, every allowed continuous instance translates exactly to a discrete instance with the same quotas, feasible schedules, and breakpoints. The paper’s mathematical model already contains the proposed continuous-population formulation.
