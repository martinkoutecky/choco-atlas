What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-on concerns Theorem 2’s stability-versus-equilibrium claim; it does not cover the paper’s other equilibrium, incentive, or welfare results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive route is a large-market version of the paper’s stable-implementation question. The paper itself points toward large-market equilibrium analysis in Section 8, and school admissions can have millions of applicants. But there is a threshold problem for this case: the paper has no named result that qualifies as a computational-complexity anchor under your rule. Its numbered results concern equilibrium outcomes, stability, incentives, and school welfare; none states a hardness result, an algorithmic classification, or a computational formulation that the paper solves. The DA algorithm is part of the model, not a named complexity result.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that a generalized priority structure \((q,\succ,t)\) is within-tier acyclic if and only if, for every school-choice problem with that priority structure, the set of stable matchings equals the set of pure Nash equilibrium outcomes under the tiered deferred acceptance mechanism. This is the paper’s strongest possible bridge to a continuous question, but it is a mechanism-implementation theorem, not a complexity result. I would not present it as a qualifying anchor.

A plausible follow-on question would be Continuum TDA Stable Implementation. An instance would give a finite set of schools and tiers, rational school-capacity shares \(q_s\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) over a finite support of student types. Each type would include a true school ranking and a priority class at each school, so members of a type are identical for the model. Priorities would order types, with a school splitting a marginal type’s mass when its capacity cuts through that class. Students report rankings; tiered DA runs on mass, and its assignment is recorded as \(x_{t,s}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\). The question is to characterize the symmetric pure Nash equilibrium assignments and determine when they equal the stable mass assignments; a solution would give that characterization and, when equality fails, an equilibrium assignment that is unstable.

This has a credible regime: large admissions markets with relatively few preference and exam-score cohorts, so population size greatly exceeds the number of distinct types. The authors would likely recognize the large-market extension as relevant: they explicitly raise it as future work. The model also makes two consequential changes. Priority classes create ties within a type, and equilibrium is defined for infinitesimal students whose deviations do not change aggregate cutoffs. Thus it would test how the paper’s finite-player rejection-chain results behave in a continuum, rather than simply scale up the same game. A tractable characterization via mass DA and type-level cutoffs seems plausible, but the paper gives no basis for a complexity verdict.

So there is a defensible continuous research direction, anchored in spirit to Theorem 2 and the paper’s own large-market agenda. There is no anchor-compliant positive mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is the anchor. The paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2 characterizes when TDA’s Nash equilibrium outcomes equal the stable matchings; Theorems 1 and 3 characterize equilibrium outcomes and school welfare. None gives a complexity classification, algorithm, or approximation result. The deferred acceptance algorithm is part of the model, not a computational contribution of this paper.

The proponent correctly identifies Theorem 2 as the closest result, but their proposed “Continuum TDA Stable Implementation” remains an equilibrium characterization: describe symmetric pure Nash assignments and say when they coincide with stable mass assignments. Adding rational masses and a finite type support makes that question more concrete, but it does not make it a computational result that this paper has to mirror. One could impose a search or decision problem on the new model; that would be a new computational project, rather than a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

There is also a substantive gap between the finite game and its atomless version. The paper’s equilibrium analysis turns on an individual student’s deviation changing proposals and triggering a rejection chain. In an atomless population, one student has zero mass, so their deviation does not change aggregate cutoffs or other students’ assignments. If instead a whole type’s mass can deviate together, the type is acting as a coalition or representative player, and the game is no longer the paper’s individual preference-revelation game. Taking limits of equilibria in finite markets might preserve individual deviations, but that is an asymptotic equilibrium question with additional modeling choices. It still does not supply a computational anchor.

This is not a complaint about fractional assignments, cutoff ties, or another detail that could be repaired by a natural redefinition. It is a mismatch between the paper’s object—individual strategic incentives in a finite matching game—and the aggregate strategic object that a continuum model must specify.

The proponent’s strongest point is real: the authors explicitly name large-market equilibrium analysis as future work, so a continuum extension could be worthwhile to matching theory and recognizable to them. That makes the negative case less than airtight. But on the programme’s stated standard, that is an adjacent theory direction, not a qualifying continuous computational mirror. No anchor raised by the proponent survives that standard.
