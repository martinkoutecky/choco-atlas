What the mirror covers

The paper’s reported experimental findings have no computational result to anchor; no result is covered by a continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large cooperative, department, or irrigation group producing a shared good. A type could be a member’s endowment and productive capability; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of members of type \(t\). An allocator would assign the produced good across contributors, and the question of how allocations affect contribution incentives could survive at population scale.

But this paper has no eligible computational anchor under your criteria. Its numbered Results 1 and 2 report experimental findings: allocator treatments have higher contribution and efficiency than equal-share treatments, with smaller efficiency gains under heterogeneity. They are not theorems or complexity results about a computational problem. Predictions 1 and 2 are hypotheses, not such results either.

So I can’t honestly state an anchor-grounded continuous computational problem for this paper. One could formulate a new problem about computing or optimizing allocation rules for a population distribution of endowment and productivity types, but that would add a mechanism-design question the paper does not analyze. The positive case is that the population regime is economically plausible; its weakness is decisive for this task: the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Results 1 and 2 are experimental findings: the authors estimate how allocator treatments affected contributions and efficiency in their subject pool. The paper states no computational problem, algorithm, or complexity result. Taking a population limit of those comparisons would give a question about population behavior or treatment effects, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The proponent’s large cooperative or department is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Endowment and productivity are sensible starting types, so I would not reject the mirror on the ground that mass is meaningless. But the strongest computational version changes what the paper studies. For example, one could give a distribution \(\mu\) over endowment and productivity types and ask for a budget-balanced allocation rule that maximizes contributions subject to incentive constraints. That is a coherent mechanism-design project. The paper, however, studies how human allocators actually divide the good and how contributors respond. It supplies no formal fairness preferences, allocator objective over allocation rules, or behavioral model from which those responses could be computed. Adding them would determine the new problem; it would not continuize one the paper posed.

The closest attempt to preserve the experiment’s findings would need to represent contributors’ fairness views and their responses to past allocations. The authors’ groups are fixed across rounds, and allocators observe each person’s history. A type consisting only of endowment and productivity omits those differences. Expanding types to include preferences and histories is possible, but it adds the behavioral model the paper does not provide. Replicating many small experimental groups would instead take a continuum over groups, leaving each allocation problem finite.

So the negative case is strong for the specific claim that this paper has a continuous computational mirror: the paper offers no computational result to anchor one, and the plausible optimization question is a new formal problem. I would not claim the stronger universal impossibility. A population-scale allocation mechanism could be worth studying in its own right, especially given the paper’s organizational examples. That would support a new project, not establish a mirror of these experimental results.
