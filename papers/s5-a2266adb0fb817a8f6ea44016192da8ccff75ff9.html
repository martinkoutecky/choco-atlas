The continuous mirror question

Let \(T\) be the six complete ballot types or nine nonempty partial ballot types, and let \(p\) be their uniform mass vector. For a centered Gaussian direction \(Z\) on the simplex tangent space with covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^\top\), compute the probability that, for sufficiently small \(\epsilon>0\), the society with masses \(p+\epsilon Z\) has a losing candidate who can be made the unique IRV winner by adding positive mass \(\lambda\) of a ballot type ranking that candidate last. Also compute this probability conditioned on the plurality and IRV winners differing.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a mass vector \(\mu\) over ballot types. The decision is whether some initially losing candidate can be made the unique IRV winner by adding \(\lambda>0\) mass of an eligible ballot type; the resulting normalized society is \((\mu+\lambda e_r)/(1+\lambda)\). The proposition’s IC limit samples local mass directions around the uniform vector and measures the Gaussian probability of this feasibility event.

The objection that survived

The nontrivial Gaussian probability is centered on the uniform IC law; extending the question to arbitrary ballot laws adds little away from boundary profiles.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the IC limiting probabilities in Propositions 3 and 4, for complete and partial ballots. It leaves the IAC results in Propositions 1 and 2, the empirical analysis, and the comparisons to other paradoxes aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the mass-augmentation probability change with more candidates?
  • What is the complexity of computing the corresponding probabilities for general ballot-type laws?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s asymptotic probability of a negative participation paradox, not a complexity classification. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, tractability, or parameterized-complexity result. Its best anchors are probability results, so this is a narrower case for continuization than the ChoCo programme’s usual complexity questions.

For the mirror, a type is a ballot ranking among the three candidates: one of six complete rankings, or one of nine nonempty partial rankings. A society is a mass vector \(\mu\) over those types. For a losing candidate \(A\), choose a ballot type \(r\) that ranks \(A\) last, and add mass \(\lambda>0\) of type \(r\). The resulting society is \((\mu+\lambda e_r)/(1+\lambda)\). Define \(\lambda^\star(\mu)\) as the least added mass that makes \(A\) the unique IRV winner, minimizing over losing candidates and eligible ballot types; set it to infinity if no such addition works. This retains the paper’s underlying question: can adding identical ballots that rank a loser last make that candidate win?

The probabilistic continuous problem is: given a ballot type set \(T\) and an interior rational ballot law \(p\), let \(Z\) be a Gaussian mass fluctuation on the simplex tangent space, with covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^\top\). Compute the limiting probability that \(\lambda^\star(p+\epsilon Z)<\infty\) as \(\epsilon\) tends to zero, and the corresponding probability conditioned on the IRV and plurality winners differing. The output can be an exact Gaussian cone measure or a certified approximation. For the paper’s uniform IC instances, the susceptibility conditions become linear inequalities in the mass fluctuations, so this is a well-defined continuous population problem.

Lead anchor — Proposition 3, proved in the paper’s appendix. For three candidates with complete rankings under IC, the limiting probability of a negative participation paradox is approximately \(0.157737\); conditioned on the IRV winner differing from the plurality winner, it is approximately \(0.644717\). The proposed complete-ballot mirror asks for those two Gaussian measures of the continuous mass profiles on six ranking types.

Supporting anchor — Proposition 4, also proved in the appendix. With partial rankings under IC, the corresponding limiting probabilities are approximately \(0.143861\) unconditionally and \(0.672803\) conditioned on the two winners differing. Its mirror uses the nine nonempty partial-ranking types and IRV’s exhaustion rule.

These are proper population regimes for the model: at the three-candidate stage, many voters’ relevant information is their ballot type, while the number of possible types stays at six or nine as turnout grows. The mass added is a fraction of the electorate, which fits the paper’s “add \(z\) ballots” intervention at high multiplicity. The uniform IC law is best treated as the paper’s benchmark distribution, not as a claim that real voters choose rankings uniformly.

For both stated three-candidate problems, I expect the continuous calculation to be tractable: the susceptibility region is polyhedral and its Gaussian measure is a finite-dimensional cone calculation. The bridge is especially direct here. The propositions derive limiting probabilities from finite random ballot profiles and evaluate the resulting geometric regions. That finite stochastic model and the continuous mass-fluctuation model are distinct; the limit connecting them is a result, not merely a restatement. The mirror covers Propositions 3 and 4 only. It does not cover the paper’s empirical results or establish anything about variable-candidate complexity.

The natural follow-up questions are how to compute the same probabilities for arbitrary ballot laws, how the problem changes with more candidates, and how to compute the minimum added mass \(\lambda^\star\), rather than just whether it is finite. I would not predict the complexity of those extensions from this paper alone.

The weak point is also clear: for three candidates, the paper has already done the central continuous probability calculation. This is a credible continuous mirror and a useful bridge to ChoCo’s population model, but it is not a new complexity landscape. The best case for continuing it is the broader family of mass-augmentation and probability problems, where the paper supplies a sharply defined starting point rather than a hardness anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that this paper has no computational-complexity result to mirror. Propositions 3 and 4 give limiting probabilities under two specified random ballot models. Their inputs are the IC sampling laws, not arbitrary continuous societies supplied as instances. A high-multiplicity ballot model would be sensible here—many voters share one of six or nine ballot types—but that alone does not turn the paper’s probability estimates into computational questions about a continuous society.

The proposed Gaussian mirrors for Propositions 3 and 4 reproduce the calculations the paper already makes. Under uniform IC, empirical ballot proportions fluctuate around the uniform type distribution; their limiting directions have the spherical cone measures computed in the appendix. The Gaussian formulation is another way to express those same measures, including the conditional probabilities. That makes a legitimate connection between finite sampling and continuous geometry, but the connection and the answer are already in the paper. It is also a limit of randomness over ballot profiles, which the programme distinguishes from making the society itself the object of study.

A stronger deterministic mirror would ask, for a given mass profile, whether adding mass of a ballot type that ranks a losing candidate last can make that candidate win, and what the least added mass is. For three candidates, the paper’s own conditions already answer that after dividing the vote counts and the added ballots \(z\) by the electorate size \(V\). For complete ballots, the feasibility condition includes \(a_1+a_2>2b_2\), and the permitted additions lie in the interval given by equation (4), now expressed in proportions. Equations (7) and (8) do the same for partial ballots. The least mass is the lower endpoint, or the infimum when the endpoint is strict. Choosing among the finitely many eligible ballot types does not open a new algorithmic problem.

Nor does asking for Gaussian probabilities under arbitrary ballot laws supply a substantial extension of these anchors. Away from the boundaries of the paper’s linear susceptibility regions, the small-noise limit is simply zero or one; nontrivial probabilities occur at boundary profiles, where the question reduces to local cone measures. At three candidates, those regions and the relevant IC cone measures are already explicitly analyzed. Making candidate count variable could produce a different and potentially worthwhile project, but it would extend the paper’s scope rather than mirror either proposition.

The negative case is weakest at that boundary: IRV elections can have many voters distributed over relatively few ballot types, so a variable-candidate mass-augmentation problem is a plausible future ChoCo topic. But the paper does not state or investigate that computational problem. Its two strongest proposed anchors are already answered as limiting probability calculations, while the direct mass-transfer version for its three-candidate setting follows from the paper’s explicit inequalities. I would not count either as an open, worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
