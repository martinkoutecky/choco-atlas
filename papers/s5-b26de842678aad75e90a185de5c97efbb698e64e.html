The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) criteria and a society of \(\tau\) evaluator types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type reports positive rational ratios \(a^t_{ij}\) on a set \(E_t\) of criterion pairs, find \(y\in\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(y_n=0\) minimizing \(F_\mu(y)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E_t}\bigl(\log a^t_{ij}-(y_i-y_j)\bigr)^2\), to additive objective error at most \(\epsilon\). When is the minimizing priority vector \(w_i=\exp(y_i)/\sum_j\exp(y_j)\) unique, and what is the algorithm’s bit complexity in \(n,\tau\), input bit length, and \(\epsilon\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete evaluator report patterns: each specifies the observed criterion pairs and their rational comparison ratios. Their masses weight squared log-ratio errors for one shared priority vector. The resulting normal equations use the weighted Laplacian of the union comparison graph; connectedness gives a unique normalized vector.

The objection that survived

Shared-priority pooling is one of several plausible group objectives, so recognition rests on the authors accepting this explicitly defined consortium extension.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers incomplete LLSM estimation and its uniqueness condition; the Eigenvector Method completion result also admits a weaker population version, while the numerical examples and van Uden approximation are not covered.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow. This paper has no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or parameterized tractable. Its named results are about optimization structure and uniqueness. I can’t honestly present them as complexity classifications; the closest anchors are Theorem 4, which is my lead, and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper.

A plausible regime is a large procurement consortium whose members assess the same fixed set of criteria. Standardized training and reporting templates could lead to tens of thousands of evaluators but only hundreds of recurring *complete report types*. A type records exactly which criterion pairs its members compare and the ratio they report for each pair; its mass is the fraction of evaluators with that report. The setting matters: if nearly everyone’s report is unique, the support size \(\tau\) approaches the population size and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. Here, the population is the evaluators, not the criteria. The criteria remain a finite set.

Theorem 4, proved here, is the strongest anchor. It says that for the paper’s incomplete Logarithmic Least Squares Method (LLSM), the optimal normalized positive weight vector is unique if and only if the graph of observed comparisons is connected. Its objective sums squared log discrepancies between observed ratios and ratios implied by the weights; the paper rewrites the optimization in log-weights and shows that its Hessian is a graph Laplacian.

The population mirror is a mass-weighted version of that underlying weight-estimation problem. An instance has \(n\) criteria and \(\tau\) types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), comparison set \(E_t\), and rational positive ratios \(a^t_{ij}\) for \((i,j)\in E_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Find a common priority vector \(w\), normalized by \(\sum_i w_i=1\), minimizing \(F_\mu(w)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E_t}\bigl(\log a^t_{ij}-\log(w_i/w_j)\bigr)^2\). A solution is the minimizing vector, or an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate approximation to its log-ratios.

This is a plausible group version of the paper’s problem: each evaluator supplies the same kind of incomplete comparison data, and the consortium seeks one set of shared priorities. The population average preserves the paper’s log-squared loss rather than replacing it with a different social objective. The weighted Hessian is a graph Laplacian whose edge weights are \(q_{ij}=\sum_{t:(i,j)\in E_t}\mu_t\). Thus the paper’s connectedness condition carries over to the union of the types’ comparison graphs: the normalized solution is unique exactly when that union is connected. I expect this mirror to be tractable: it amounts to solving a weighted Laplacian system in log-weights, with a numerical approximation question for the logarithms and output precision. A formal bit-complexity result would still need to be stated.

Theorem 2, also proved here, supports a second, weaker mirror. It says that for the Eigenvector Method, minimizing the Perron eigenvalue over all reciprocal completions of an incomplete pairwise comparison matrix has a unique optimal completion if and only if the observed-comparison graph is connected. The paper also proves convexity after taking logarithms of the missing entries and uses that structure to develop a cyclic-coordinate algorithm.

For the population version, form a consensus incomplete matrix from the reports. For each pair \(e=(i,j)\) reported by positive mass \(q_e\), set its consensus ratio to \(\bar a_{ij}=\exp\!\left(q_e^{-1}\sum_{t:e\in E_t}\mu_t\log a^t_{ij}\right)\); leave pairs with no reports missing. Then ask for a reciprocal completion \(A_\mu(x)\) minimizing \(\lambda_{\max}(A_\mu(x))\), and return an optimal completion. The input is the criteria, type reports, and rational masses; the variables are the positive missing ratios. By Theorem 2, the optimum is unique precisely when the aggregate comparison graph is connected. I expect tractability for approximate optimization, based on the paper’s convexity results and the cost of evaluating a Perron eigenvalue, but its coordinate-descent algorithm does not itself establish a polynomial-time bound.

This second mirror relies on an extra modeling choice: geometric averaging turns several experts’ reports into one comparison matrix before completion. The authors might accept it as a natural consensus version of their incomplete-matrix problem, but that aggregation rule is not a result of the paper. The LLSM mirror is stronger because it keeps the population-weighted reports directly in the objective.

These mirrors cover the paper’s incomplete LLSM weight estimation and Eigenvector Method completion results. They do not claim that the paper’s existing continuous optimization over weights or matrix entries is itself population continuization; the added continuous object is the distribution over evaluator types. Further questions include the bit complexity of the Eigenvector completion, sensitivity of both outputs to changes in type masses, and when the population solution approximates the corresponding finite-panel problem.

The weakest point is that the paper studies one decision maker’s incomplete matrix, not a population of evaluators or the computational complexity of aggregating one. The positive case depends on treating a shared-priority group version as a problem the authors would recognize. The weighted LLSM objective makes that extension concrete, but it remains a genuine modeling step; neither theorem is a hardness result whose difficulty is shown to dissolve in a continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors begin with a single expert’s incomplete matrix. The paper asks how to infer that expert’s priorities or complete that matrix. It does not specify how a group of experts should reconcile different judgments. That missing group objective matters: the same panel could pool log ratios first, average experts’ losses at a shared priority vector, or estimate each expert’s priorities and then aggregate those. The paper does not choose among them.

For Theorem 4, the proponent’s shared-priority objective is a coherent group extension, but it is a modeling choice rather than a population version forced by the theorem. Once chosen, it compresses to weighted edge totals and weighted log-ratio sums. Its optimizer is a weighted Laplacian least-squares solution, and uniqueness depends on the connectedness of the union graph. That makes it a clean, tractable question; the negative case is that the computational content concerns this added consensus model, not a difficulty in Theorem 4 that the population formulation reveals. The alternative of solving each type’s LLSM separately and aggregating the resulting priorities avoids the shared objective, but then the population problem is simply a collection of individual problems plus a chosen aggregation rule.

Theorem 2 is more exposed to this objection. Completing a log-averaged consensus matrix gives a well-defined population model, and the paper’s theorem applies to it. But the averaging step has already reduced the society to one incomplete matrix. If instead each type gets its own completion, the problems separate. A coupled objective, such as minimizing average Perron eigenvalue under a common completion, needs additional rules for handling reports that disagree or omit different comparisons. Each formulation can be sensible, but the paper does not supply a canonical one.

There is also a tension in the high-multiplicity story. A type must record the complete pattern of comparisons and their ratios. With many criteria and fine-grained judgments, repeated complete reports may be rare, so the support size can approach the panel size. Standardized, recurring report types can make the regime plausible, but that requires a particularly structured setting. Conversely, if the reports are identical, population mass changes no optimizer; if they differ, choosing how they jointly determine one priority vector is the substantive new modeling step. This is a concern about the fit and reach of the mirror, not a claim that type-level costs or prices are objectionable.

I cannot make the universal negative case convincingly. Theorem 4’s group LLSM formulation is plausible, has an honest population axis, and is likely a problem these authors would recognize given the paper’s multiperson decision-making context. Its easy reduction to weighted least squares does not disqualify it under the programme’s standards. The objections are strongest against Theorem 2’s population interpretation and against treating either mirror as an inherited result of the paper. They do not defeat the LLSM mirror, so I would not recommend a red verdict on the claim that *some* worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
