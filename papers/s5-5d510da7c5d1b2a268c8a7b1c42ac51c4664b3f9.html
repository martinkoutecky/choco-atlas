The continuous mirror question

Given interchangeable network-role types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), initial target opinions \(b_t^0\), stubbornness values \(d_t\), and a rational column-stochastic influence matrix \(A\), a finite horizon \(h\), seed-mass budget \(\kappa\), and threshold \(\theta\), is there a vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\) whose target cumulative score is at least \(\theta\)? Seeding mass \(x_t\) sets that mass's target opinion and stubbornness to \(1\). For unseeded members, let \(z_t^0=b_t^0\), \(\bar z_t^j=(x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^j)/\mu_t\), and \(z_t^{j+1}=d_t b_t^0+(1-d_t)\sum_{s\in\mathcal T}A_{s,t}\bar z_s^j\); the score is \(J(x)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}[x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^h]\).

The model it lives in

A finite-horizon FJ model on a block-structured society: type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), initial target opinion \(b_t^0\), and stubbornness \(d_t\); each member receives influence shares \(A_{s,t}\) from the types. Choose seed mass \(x_t\) per type, with total \(\kappa\), to maximize \(J(x)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's cumulative-score decision problem at horizon \(t=1\). It leaves Theorem 2's plurality hardness, the submodularity results, approximation algorithms, random-walk and sketch guarantees, and experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity when the support size \(|\mathcal T|\) is fixed or small?
  • Do the paper's plurality, approval, and Copeland results have useful mass-seeding analogues?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s Theorem 1 (proved here in §III-A): the decision version of Problem 1 is NP-hard for the cumulative score. The paper reduces from Vertex Cover. It builds a bidirected influence network from an undirected graph, sets both candidates’ initial opinions and stubbornness values to zero, assigns each incoming neighbor equal influence weight, and uses horizon \(t=1\). A seed set of size at most \(k\) is a vertex cover exactly when the target’s cumulative score reaches \(n\).

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Type-Mass FJ Cumulative Seed Selection. An instance has a finite set \(\mathcal T\) of network-role types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational initial target opinions \(b_t^0\), stubbornness values \(d_t\), and a rational column-stochastic matrix \(A\). The entry \(A_{s,t}\) is the share of influence that each member of type \(t\) receives from type \(s\). It also has a finite horizon \(h\), a seed-mass budget \(\kappa\), and a score threshold \(\theta\). Decide whether there is a mass allocation \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), with \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\), that gives cumulative target score at least \(\theta\).

The \(x_t\) units of seeded mass in type \(t\) have target opinion \(1\) at every time step, as in the paper’s intervention. Let \(z_t^j\) be the opinion at time \(j\) of the unseeded members of type \(t\), and let
\[ \bar z_t^j=\frac{x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^j}{\mu_t} \]
be the type’s average opinion. Set \(z_t^0=b_t^0\), and update unseeded members by
\[ z_t^{j+1}=d_t b_t^0+(1-d_t)\sum_{s\in\mathcal T}A_{s,t}\bar z_s^j. \]
The score to maximize is
\[ J(x)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\left[x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^h\right]. \]
A solution to the decision question is a feasible mass vector \(x\) with \(J(x)\ge\theta\). Opinions being real-valued is already part of the paper’s model; the continuization here is the population and seed action, measured in mass.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the authors’ problem: many users share a network role, including the same initial opinion, stubbornness, and influence proportions to other roles. For example, a large campaign population might contain many people in each of several recurring community roles. The finite role graph can still have varied connectivity. A realization with \(M\) interchangeable users per role has \(N=M|\mathcal T|\) users, while the continuous instance records only the role masses and interactions. The model preserves seed selection, the FJ update, the fixed horizon, and the cumulative objective; it changes the unit of intervention from a named node to a fraction of a role.

The anchor remains hard even when seeds may be split fractionally. Given a Vertex Cover instance with \(n\) vertices and no isolated vertices, use one role per vertex, set \(\mu_v=1/n\), \(b_v^0=d_v=0\), and set \(A_{u,v}=1/\deg(v)\) when \(u\) is adjacent to \(v\), and zero otherwise. Set \(h=1\), \(\kappa=k/n\), and \(\theta=1\). The unseeded opinion of role \(v\) is then the average of the seeded fractions of its neighbors. Each role contributes at most \(\mu_v\) to \(J\). If \(J=1\), every role must contribute its full mass. Thus any role that is not fully seeded must have all its neighbors fully seeded. The fully seeded roles therefore form a vertex cover, of size at most \(k\). Conversely, given a cover of size at most \(k\), seed its full mass; every role outside the cover has only fully seeded neighbors and hence opinion \(1\) at time \(1\). Any remaining budget can be placed fractionally on those outside roles without lowering the score. So the continuous decision instance has score \(1\) exactly when the graph has a vertex cover of size at most \(k\).

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B) for this variable-type problem. Fractional seeding does not remove the vertex-cover choice at the maximum-score threshold. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s cumulative-score decision result only; it makes no claim about the paper’s other scores.

The weakest point is that the reduction uses one type per graph vertex, so the number of types grows with the hard instance. The population can be much larger still because each role contains many interchangeable people, but this does not show hardness when the number of types is fixed or very small. That is a useful boundary for follow-up questions: does fixed-\(\tau\) tractability hold, how does complexity depend on \(\tau\), and what happens to the other voting scores under mass seeding?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this mirror is that “type” becomes demanding in a social network. To preserve the paper’s diffusion dynamics, a type must capture the details that determine how its members receive and exert influence, as well as their initial opinions and stubbornness. In the paper’s real networks, those details are largely individual. Grouping users by broad labels such as research area or community would generally change the influence process; exact network roles are a much narrower regime. The reduction also uses one role per Vertex Cover vertex, so its number of types grows with the hard instance.

That is a serious limit on how much the mirror tells us about compression in realistic networks. But it does not defeat the strongest version. A block-structured network with many interchangeable users per role is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, and mass seeding is a natural version of choosing seeds there. The model preserves the FJ update and cumulative objective while expressing intervention in population shares. The paper’s prior opinion-maximization work, as described here, still chooses discrete seed nodes and studies a finite network; continuous opinion values alone do not answer this population-mass question.

Nor does fractional seeding dissolve the anchor’s hardness. At threshold \(J(x)=1\), every role must contribute its full mass. Any role that is not fully seeded must therefore receive opinion \(1\) from its fully seeded neighbors. The fully seeded roles cover every edge, yielding a Vertex Cover. Conversely, a cover can be fully seeded and any leftover budget placed fractionally without lowering the score. So the mirror has a valid NP-hardness result even when seed mass is divisible.

The main boundary is that this reduction proves hardness when the support size \(\tau\) grows with the graph; it does not establish hardness for fixed or small \(\tau\). Also, the mass formulation need not be an LP: even at one step, the score can be quadratic in the seed masses. Those points limit what the anchor establishes, but they do not make its question unworthy. Under the programme’s criteria, “continuization does not remove this hardness” is an admissible finding.

So the honest negative case is weak. The network-role assumption makes the mirror narrower than the paper’s general setting, but an authors-recognizable block-network regime survives, and the hardness reduction survives fractional mass. I cannot defeat this anchor without relying on objections the brief rules out.
