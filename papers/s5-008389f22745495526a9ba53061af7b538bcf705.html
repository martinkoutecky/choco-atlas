The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_i\), a rational strongly connected contact matrix \(P\), initial pro and opposing zealot masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\), and a feasible budget \(B\), choose rational targeting masses \(x_i\) with \(0\le x_i\le \mu_i-\alpha_i-\beta_i\) and \(\sum_i x_i=B\). For each choice, let \(v_i(x)\) be the probability that an unconverted type-\(i\) agent eventually reaches a pro zealot before an opposing zealot under the stated type-mixing process, and maximize \(J(x)=\sum_i[\alpha_i+x_i+(\mu_i-\alpha_i-\beta_i-x_i)v_i(x)]\). For fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), determine whether a feasible \(x\) with \(J(x)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\max_x J(x)\) can be found in time polynomial in the number of types \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A society has \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_i\), and agents of type \(i\) contact type \(j\) according to rational probabilities \(P_{ij}\), sampling uniformly within that type. Given initial pro and opposing zealot masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\), choose a budget-\(B\) vector of converted masses \(x_i\); the resulting type-level harmonic hitting probabilities \(v_i(x)\) determine aggregate pro influence \(J(x)\). The computational problem is to approximate the maximum of \(J\) in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

The objection that survived

The vertex-cover gap in Theorem 6 may disappear when targeting can be divided fractionally within each type, so the theorem does not establish hardness for the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror carries over Theorem 6’s fixed-budget influence optimization and approximation-hardness question. It leaves the finite-graph submodularity guarantee, convex relaxation, symmetry result, and experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the constant-gap hardness from the vertex-cover reduction persist when targeting mass is divisible within each type?
  • Is the typed problem tractable for fixed \(\tau\), even if hardness persists when \(\tau\) grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is continuous targeting influence on a finite block network. A type is a class of agents with the same interaction profile and targeting cost. Mass is the fraction of the population in that class. In a plausible regime, a large platform or political network has many members in each of a modest number of audience or community classes, and interactions follow stable class-to-class rates. The population can be much larger than the number of types. Targeting a fraction of one class means converting that mass of people to zealots; each person still receives an integral opinion, while the class average is fractional.

Here is the model I would hand to a prover. Let \(S=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be the types, \(\mu_i>0\) their rational masses with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and \(P\) a rational, strongly connected, row-stochastic matrix. An agent of type \(i\) samples a contact from type \(j\) with probability \(P_{ij}\), uniformly within that class. Let \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_i\) be the initial masses of pro and opposing zealots in type \(i\), with \(\alpha_i+\beta_i\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i\beta_i>0\). Given a targeting budget \(B\), choose masses \(x_i\) satisfying
\[ 0\leq x_i\leq \mu_i-\alpha_i-\beta_i,\qquad \sum_i x_i=B. \]
The selected mass becomes pro zealots. For an unconverted agent of type \(i\), let \(v_i(x)\) be the probability of eventually reaching a pro zealot before an opposing one. It satisfies
\[ v_i(x)=\sum_j P_{ij}\left[ \frac{\alpha_j+x_j}{\mu_j} +\frac{\mu_j-\alpha_j-\beta_j-x_j}{\mu_j}v_j(x) \right]. \]
The influence to maximize is
\[ J(x)=\sum_i\left[\alpha_i+x_i+ (\mu_i-\alpha_i-\beta_i-x_i)v_i(x)\right]. \]
The computational question is: for fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), can one find a feasible rational \(x\) with \(J(x)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\max_x J(x)\), in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the bit length \(L\) of the rational input? This is my lead mirror: it preserves the paper’s harmonic opinion dynamics, zealots, fixed-size intervention, and aggregate influence objective. It changes the network to an exchangeable, type-mixing regime, which is a natural large-population case for that model.

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that obtaining a \((1-\varepsilon)\)-approximation to the targeting influence problem is NP-hard. The proof reduces a gap version of vertex cover on cubic graphs: it adds an opposing zealot node and shows that a size-\(k\) targeting set yields a separated influence value depending on whether the graph has a size-\(k\) vertex cover or every such set leaves many edges uncovered. The vertex-cover gap hardness is cited from elsewhere; the influence-hardness theorem is proved here. The hard instances use a target budget growing with the graph, so the result concerns large-scale campaigning rather than a fixed handful of conversions.

For the unrestricted typed problem, my expectation is that hardness will survive when \(\tau\) grows, because the reduction’s combinatorics lie in the network’s interaction structure, which \(P\) can represent. I would therefore initially expect a Class B result, with fixed-\(\tau\) tractability a separate possibility. But Theorem 6 does not establish that conclusion: the mirror allows fractional targeting within each type, and the reduction’s vertex-cover gap may disappear under that relaxation. A prover would need to show that it survives, or find an algorithm that exploits the fractional masses. A useful next question is whether the discrete optimum and the typed continuum optimum converge for large finite block networks, and with what error.

The weakest point is exactly that fractionalization issue, compounded by the graph reduction’s potentially large number of distinct roles: encoding a cubic graph may require \(\tau\) to grow with its size. Still, the proposed problem is not merely a softened version of the paper’s question. A sponsor converting a share of a large audience class, while opinions propagate through class-level interaction rates, is a recognizable targeting-influence problem. The paper’s Theorem 12, by contrast, relaxes the targeting variable on a finite graph; it is not itself a population mirror. Its discussion of geometric-graph limits is a nearby continuum direction, but the question above is specifically about the population masses and the computational complexity of choosing them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is that Theorem 6’s hardness is carried by the wiring of a particular graph. To preserve the harmonic process exactly, a type would need to retain the agent’s full interaction role; in a generic network, that can leave almost every vertex in its own type. Replacing those roles with a class-to-class matrix \(P\) makes a different process: hitting probabilities now come from exchangeable class mixing, not the particular paths through the original graph. That is a real narrowing of the paper’s central influencer-targeting story, where location and connectivity make a node valuable.

But this does not defeat the best mirror. On an exact block-mixing network, many agents can share a type and its contact distribution, and targeting a fraction of that class is a meaningful population-level intervention. The proposed \(x_i\) model is coherent there, and asking how to optimize it is a computational question. Theorem 6 does not answer it. Whether the vertex-cover gap survives fractional targeting is precisely a possible result of the mirror; it cannot count against asking the question.

There is some nearby computational work: for example, *Shadowing and shielding* studies continuous targeting strengths on a finite network, and this paper itself develops a continuous optimization relaxation. Those treatments keep the individual vertices as the population, so they do not already answer the class-mass problem. Likewise, the paper’s suggested geometric continuum is a spatial limit, not a continuous population. These are reasons to make the new question’s contribution precise, not novelty collisions.

So the negative case is weak against the universal claim. I can argue that the mirror applies to a narrower, exchangeable network regime than the paper’s general graph model, but I cannot honestly rule out that regime as sensible or worthwhile. The proposed exact block-mixing version survives the strongest objection to the anchor. [Related continuous-strength work](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8213046/) supports the point about neighboring methods, but does not make the population-continuous problem already done.
