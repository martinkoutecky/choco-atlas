The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow, and it does not meet your anchor rule literally: this paper has no numbered result establishing a computational complexity classification or algorithmic runtime. It does contain named approximation and limit results, but those are mathematical statements. The introduction’s general observation that computing most power indices is NP-hard is not a numbered result about a specified problem.

The closest positive anchor is Theorem 3, cited from Lindner and Machover [6, Theorem 3.6], not proved here. It says that if player weights come from a finite set, all but finitely many belong to a finite set \(W\) of positive integers with greatest common divisor \(1\), and each weight \(a\in W\) accounts for at least a fixed fraction \(\rho>0\) of the total weight for all sufficiently large electorates, then in games with relative quota \(1/2\), the ratio of the Penrose–Banzhaf indices of any two players with weights \(w_i,w_j\in W\) converges to \(w_i/w_j\).

A plausible population regime is a large cooperative federation with a small number of recurring voting-rights tiers. A type is a member’s voting weight \(a\); mass \(\mu_a\) is the fraction of members in that tier. Each tier has positive mass, and the number of members grows while the type set stays fixed. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: members within a tier are interchangeable for the weighted-voting problem.

The candidate mirror is an asymptotic type-level Banzhaf computation. Its input is a finite set \(A\) of positive integer weights with greatest common divisor \(1\), and positive binary-rational masses \((\mu_a)_{a\in A}\) summing to \(1\). Form each finite game by replicating the types in those proportions, with quota half the total weight. For type \(a\), let \(\beta_a\) be the limit of the fraction of all individual swings attributable to players of that type. The question is to compute the vector \((\beta_a)_{a\in A}\); a solution is that vector. Theorem 3 implies
\[ \beta_a=\frac{a\mu_a}{\sum_{b\in A}b\mu_b}. \]
Thus this particular limit-computation problem is tractable: its output follows from a linear number of rational arithmetic operations in the support size and input bit length. The result is a limit of finite-game indices; I would not assign a separate swing-counting measure to an atomless society.

The mirror covers only the fixed finite-type, half-quota limit regime represented by Theorem 3. It does not cover the paper’s counterexamples or its conjectures about broader bounds. Its weakest point is also central: the proposed computation is a direct type-level restatement of an existing analytic limit theorem, not a computational result established by this paper. Moreover, the theorem gives no effective rate, so it does not tell us how large a finite electorate must be before the approximation is accurate. The paper’s two-type counterexample in Proposition 3 also marks a boundary: its high-weight class has vanishing population fraction while retaining a substantial share of total voting weight, outside the fixed-positive-type-mass regime above.

So I would offer this as the best positive *research direction*—compute limiting power by recurring voter type, then seek finite-size error bounds—but I would not present it as a compliant, paper-anchored ChoCo computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a paper-anchored mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its propositions and theorems establish mathematical facts about power indices and their limits; the introduction’s claim that computing many power indices is NP-hard is background motivation, not a complexity result for a specified problem. That leaves no algorithmic or complexity finding here for ChoCo to mirror.

The proponent’s anchor, Theorem 3, is the closest fit, but it is a limit theorem cited from Lindner and Machover. Their proposed type-level output \(\beta_a=a\mu_a/\sum_b b\mu_b\) is a reasonable aggregate of individual powers along a fixed-support sequence of finite games. It is not, however, a computational result established by this paper. Treating it as one would broaden the assignment from mirroring a paper’s computational finding to turning any mathematical result into a new computation question.

A stronger adjacent question would ask for exact or approximate Penrose–Banzhaf power from a high-multiplicity type distribution. That is a sensible voting problem, but the continuous population alone gives each individual zero mass; a type-level power measure needs an additional definition, such as a limit along finite replications. In the fixed finite-type regime, Theorem 3 already supplies the proposed aggregate formula. Other replication regimes may require separate definitions and can have different limiting behavior. Finite-size error bounds would be a further question, but they are not a computational result in this paper.

So I would reject this paper as a strong paper-anchored ChoCo case: the proponent has found a plausible continuous limit, not a computational result of the paper. I would not claim the universal negative honestly, though. Large voting bodies with recurring weight tiers are a credible high-multiplicity setting, and their limiting aggregate power is a well-posed question. The negative case is strong at the anchor gate and weak against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
