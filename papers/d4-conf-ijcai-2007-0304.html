What the mirror covers

The paper presents algorithms for completing a preorder and answering ordering queries, but establishes no qualifying computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational anchor in this paper. It numbers Definitions 1–3 and Algorithms 1–2, but gives no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithm 1, attributed to Benferhat et al. (2001), computes a least-specific possibility distribution; Algorithm 2 answers ordering queries. Neither is a complexity classification, and the paper does not establish a running-time bound or hardness result for them.

The most plausible population scenario would be a large cohort of students, with a type \(u\in S^n\) representing a qualitative grade vector and \(\mu_u\) its fraction of the cohort. One could ask for the least-specific preorder of the grade-vector types, subject to generic principles and example comparisons. But the masses do not affect that preorder: changing \(\mu\) leaves the paper’s constraints and completion procedure unchanged. Making mass affect the ranking would change the underlying problem. The paper’s possibility degrees \(\pi(u)\in[0,1]\) are also not a continuous population; they describe alternatives.

So the student-cohort story is a possible setting for the paper’s ranking task, but it does not support an anchored continuous-mirror case with a defensible complexity direction. There are no named results here from which to state one continuous problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no named computational anchor to defeat. That is the paper’s strongest obstacle to a continuous mirror: it gives Algorithms 1 and 2 for completing a preorder and answering queries, but states no complexity theorem or hardness result about either. A complexity study of those algorithms could be worthwhile on its own; it would not mirror a computational result established here.

The student-cohort proposal does not repair that gap. Students in the paper’s examples are alternatives, represented by grade vectors in \(U=S^n\); they are not agents whose preferences are aggregated. The algorithm ranks possible grade vectors from generic constraints and examples. Giving grade vectors population masses leaves that ranking unchanged, because the masses never enter the constraints or completion procedure. And the possibility degrees \(\pi(u)\) are a qualitative encoding of the preorder, not a distribution of people.

A stronger attempt might treat many students or evaluators as sources of preference examples, then ask how a mass of example-givers determines a ranking. But the paper does not aggregate individual judgments: it takes one constraint set as given, and its conclusion distinguishes this extrapolation task from learning. Treating repeated examples as more influential, or adding a rule to reconcile different people’s constraints, would define a new problem. The central difficulty is therefore not that a continuous version might have a different answer. It is that making population mass matter requires changing what the paper’s ranking procedure is for. The universal claim is hard to prove against every conceivable new model, but none of these population readings yields a convincing continuous mirror of the paper’s stated task.
