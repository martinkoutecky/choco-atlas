The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying anchor, so this paper falls under the brief’s “no named computational result” case. Its Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that a social choice correspondence is virtually Nash implementable with admissible support \(h\) if and only if it satisfies extended monotonicity with respect to \(h\). In substance, when agents’ lower contour sets expand at the selected alternative and at alternatives receiving positive probability in its implementing lottery, the selected alternative must remain selected and those lottery alternatives must remain admissible. That is a structural characterization of implementation, not a result about an underlying problem’s computational complexity. The paper’s other results likewise concern implementation and support restrictions. [Theorem 1 in the paper](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=fb4b36b09c3f0efb2249a629e9a668027c50191d&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

A plausible population setting would be a large civic consultation in which citizens share a small set of complete preference rankings over policy alternatives; each ranking’s mass is its population share. One could ask whether a mechanism virtually implements a target social choice correspondence while respecting specified lottery supports. But that would be a speculative extension, not a continuous problem anchored by a named computational result here. In a literal atomless population, a single agent’s deviation does not change an aggregate outcome, so the paper’s Nash incentive question would need a new strategic model. The paper’s lotteries make outcomes probabilistic; they do not make the society continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject a ChoCo mirror of this paper. Its Theorem 1 is a characterization of virtual Nash implementation with admissible support: it relates implementation to extended monotonicity. It does not establish the complexity of a computational problem. The other results likewise concern implementation and support restrictions. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous version would extend the paper’s contribution. The proponent’s “no qualifying anchor” observation is therefore a reason against the mirror, not a case for one.

The strongest candidate for a mirror is to ask whether a mechanism can virtually implement a social choice correspondence in a large society whose members share a finite set of preference types. That society is plausible, but the paper’s central condition is incentive compatibility under an individual agent’s deviation. If the mechanism sees only the distribution of reports, one atomless agent’s report changes no mass and leaves the outcome unchanged. Incentive constraints then become vacuous: any report gives the agent the same outcome. That does not computationally continuize the paper’s result; it removes the strategic problem the result characterizes.

A stronger proposal might let a positive mass of agents deviate together, or model the society as finitely many strategic blocs. The first changes unilateral Nash implementation into a coalition or mass-deviation concept. The second makes blocs the players, so the continuous masses describe the sizes of a finite strategic game rather than replacing its population. Either could motivate new research, but neither is a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The lotteries mentioned in the paper do not solve this: they make outcomes probabilistic, not the population continuous.

The civic-consultation scenario is thus a sensible setting for a new model, but it supplies no computational question anchored in this paper. I cannot rule out every future model someone might devise; the case is strongest and clearest for the paper as given: it offers an axiomatic characterization, and its individual-deviation foundation loses its force under an atomless aggregate population.
