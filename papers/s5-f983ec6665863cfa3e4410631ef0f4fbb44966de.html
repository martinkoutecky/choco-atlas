The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a valid computational anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered result asserting that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its lemmas and propositions concern economic properties of finite networks: stability, efficiency, component size, and core existence. For example, Lemma 8, proved here, says the \(r\)-core of \(w_v\) is nonempty if and only if \(w_v(N)/n \geq w_v(S)/|S|\) for every coalition \(S\) with \(|S|\leq r\). That is a mathematical characterization, not a complexity result. So there is no anchor from which I can honestly state the requested continuous computational problem or predict its complexity.

A plausible population regime would be many otherwise interchangeable firms or organizations forming links, with one or a few types and mass representing their share of the population. A possible continuous model would use a measurable network on a unit-mass population and ask whether it is efficient or stable against deviations by coalitions of mass at most \(\rho\). But that would require choices the paper does not make: how its arbitrary network value functions extend to measurable networks, how connected components and component-wise payoffs are defined there, and whether the deviation bound \(r\) stays finite or scales with population size. It would be a new continuum network-formation question, not a continuous version of a named computational result in this paper.

The positive case is therefore limited to saying that the paper’s anonymous-player setting suggests a high-multiplicity scenario. Its weakest point is decisive for this task: the paper studies structural economic questions, not computational ones, so there is no qualifying anchor and no honest lead mirror to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Its lemmas and propositions characterize when finite networks are stable, efficient, or core-supported; none classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm. Lemma 8 is the closest candidate, but it is an economic characterization: the \(r\)-core is nonempty exactly when \(w_v(N)/n \geq w_v(S)/|S|\) for every coalition of size at most \(r\). That is not a complexity result.

A continuous version of Lemma 8 might ask whether \(w(1) \geq w(s)/s\) for every coalition mass \(s\leq\rho\). But the paper provides no input model for computing \(w\): its value function is arbitrary on finite network topologies, and \(w_v(S)\) itself maximizes over networks on \(S\). To ask about complexity, one must first choose how that function is represented and how it extends across population sizes. With a chosen parametric representation, the resulting question could be interesting, but its computational content would come from that new representation, not from Lemma 8.

The best population scenario is many otherwise identical organizations forming links. That makes high multiplicity plausible; I would treat it as support for the regime, not as an objection. But a distribution over organization types does not capture the network structure the paper studies. Connectivity, components, and critical players depend on which agents are linked to which. A graphon or finite-block model could preserve some of that structure, but the paper gives no consistent value functional, scaling rule, or continuum notion of deviation for those objects. Supplying them would define a new network-formation model. A finite-block version might yield a worthwhile computational project, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So I would reject the proponent’s Lemma 8 anchor and the suggested firm-population scenario as grounds for a ChoCo mirror. The honest limit of the negative case is that one could invent a useful computational problem on continuous or block networks inspired by this paper. I cannot rule that out universally. The stronger, supportable claim is that this paper gives no named computational result from which such a mirror can be anchored; any worthwhile project would need its own model and computational question.
