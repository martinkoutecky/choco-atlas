The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. It contains numbered theorems, but none classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The closest is Theorem 2, proved here: under Assumptions 1 and 2, the authors define an efficient carbon-tax path by equation (64), using the efficient allocation’s discounted future marginal climate damages; that tax makes the resulting aggregate equilibrium allocation efficient. This is an economic implementation result, not a complexity result, and it does not give a runtime for finding the efficient allocation. I would not present it as an anchor under your rule.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is therefore a candidate extension, not an anchored case: *Type-Distribution Optimal Climate Implementation*. Its instance is a finite support \(\Theta\) of regional types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\geq 0\), \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\). A type specifies every regional characteristic used by the model—such as productivities, labor supply, initial endowments, and climate damages—with common preferences and climate dynamics. Mass represents the fraction of standardized jurisdictions of each type. A plausible regime would be many local jurisdictions under a shared climate policy, with many jurisdictions but relatively few complete economic types.

Given welfare weights \(\omega_\theta\geq0\), normalized by \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\omega_\theta=1\), the question is to find a type-indexed allocation maximizing
\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\omega_\theta \sum_{t\geq0}\beta^t u(c_t(\theta)), \]
subject to the paper’s type-local production, labor, and energy constraints; global capital and resource constraints; and climate dynamics, with sums across regions replaced by mass-weighted sums. A solution consists of the optimal trajectories for each type and a uniform tax path implementing their efficient aggregate allocation. Its natural candidate tax is the type-aggregate version of (64), replacing the sum of regional marginal damages by
\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta \frac{D_\theta'(S_{t+n})Y_{t+n}(\theta)} {1-D_\theta(S_{t+n})}. \]
For a chosen weighted consumption distribution, one could also return type-level transfer shares whose mass-weighted sum is one and that implement that distribution.

I would provisionally expect the finite-support version to have a Class A structural story: with finitely many types, the damage term becomes a weighted sum, and the paper’s separation between the efficient aggregate allocation and its distribution across regions has a direct type-mass analogue. Further questions include whether a finite, rationally encoded version admits an algorithm polynomial in support size and input length, how finite regional instances embed as atomic measures, and whether the transfer construction survives under a continuum of types. The paper itself establishes none of those algorithmic claims. Its infinite-horizon paths and broadly specified production functions would also need a precise finite encoding before ordinary complexity bounds could be stated.

The scenario is not implausible on the paper’s own terms: it allows arbitrary numbers of regions, and describes them as potentially representing groups of countries. But the weakest point is substantial: the paper studies a planner’s climate-policy problem, not a computational social-choice problem such as selecting or manipulating a winner. Its “agents” here would be jurisdictions, not voters, and the paper supplies no hardness result whose dissolution in a continuous population could be studied. So the mirror is a plausible extension of its model, but it does not make a qualifying positive case under the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own caveat is decisive for a ChoCo mirror: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes a tax path that implements an efficient allocation; it does not classify the complexity of finding that allocation or give an algorithm with a runtime. The other numbered results are also economic characterizations and welfare claims. And the underlying model is not a finite computational instance as stated: it allows arbitrary production and damage functions and infinite sequences of exogenous inputs. One could encode a restricted version, but the choices needed to do that would define a new computational problem.

The strongest repair is the proposed finite-support model of regional types, perhaps with a finite horizon and rationally encoded technologies, asking for an optimal tax and transfers. That could be a worthwhile climate-economics or numerical-optimization project. But its units are jurisdictions: each has its own labor and energy markets, resource endowments, representative household, and political autonomy. A mass of jurisdictions is not a mass of people sharing a social-choice type. Replicating identical jurisdictions can be made coherent by scaling their inputs and constraints, but the resulting question is about a continuum of governments and a planner’s policy problem—not the computational complexity of a social-choice problem in this paper.

So the best proposed extension does not rescue the mirror: it supplies the missing computational question and changes the relevant population unit at the same time. I cannot rule out a useful continuum-of-jurisdictions model in economics; the negative case is weaker if “worthwhile” means any adjacent research project. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, though, there is no convincing computational social-choice mirror here.
