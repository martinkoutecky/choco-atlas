The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is that this paper supplies no eligible named computational anchor. The numbered results are about existence and characterization, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar:

  • Theorem 1 proves that a stable matching exists in any economy satisfying diversity of preferences.
  • Theorem 2 proves that, given a regular market-clearing rational-expectations pair and locally peer-smooth utilities, sufficiently large finite economies sampled from the continuum economy have an \(\varepsilon\)-stable matching with high probability, close in cutoffs and statistics to that pair.
  • Theorem 3 applies those existence results to linear-preferences economies with bounded-density independent taste shocks, giving continuum existence and, under additional regularity, approximate stability in large sampled economies.

Lemma 1 characterizes stable matchings by market-clearing, rational-expectations cutoff and statistics pairs. The paper uses that characterization in an existence proof; it does not give or analyze an algorithm for computing a matching. So neither the lemma nor the theorems meet the required anchor rule.

There is a plausible research question nearby: given an explicit description of a continuum peer-dependent matching economy, compute a stable matching—or, for a sampled finite economy, compute an \(\varepsilon\)-stable one. The natural variables would be the assignment of student mass to schools, with cutoffs \(P\) and matching statistics \(s\); a solution would satisfy capacity constraints, market clearing, and rational expectations. A credible regime is a large school-choice market with a fixed number of schools and a population described by a distribution over student characteristics, preferences, and priorities. Theorem 2 gives a bridge from that regime to large finite samples.

But that is a computational follow-up, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational result. The paper already makes the population continuous: it defines an economy by a measure \(\eta\) over student types and a measurable assignment of that population. Its central existence result is already about that object. The continuum regime is sensible, and the sampled-economy result is useful support for its relevance; neither supplies the required computational anchor. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive problem: there is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 proves existence in a continuum economy; Theorems 2 and 3 prove approximate stability and an application of those existence results. Lemma 1 gives a cutoff characterization, but it does not analyze an algorithm or complexity. So none of the proposed anchors survives the paper’s own terms.

The strongest computational follow-up would take a rational, finite-support distribution over complete student types and ask for an exact or approximate stable matching using the cutoff and statistic variables \((P,s)\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But it is a new algorithmic problem layered onto a paper whose population is already continuous. In the paper’s full generality, arbitrary measures and preference functions also lack the finite encoding and effective regularity bounds needed to state complexity in \(m,\tau,L\). Restricting to a computable parametric family could fix that, but would define a narrower new problem, not a mirror of one of these theorems. Theorem 2 may motivate algorithms for sampled finite markets; those would be computational follow-ups too.

That makes the case against a *continuous mirror of this paper* strong. The broader claim that no scenario could make related computational work worthwhile is weaker: a structured finite-type algorithmic study could be worthwhile on its own. The honest negative conclusion is that this paper supplies no eligible computational anchor, not that its model has no worthwhile computational questions.
