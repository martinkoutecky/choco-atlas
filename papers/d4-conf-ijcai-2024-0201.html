The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a population-level version of the paper’s necessary stable epistemic acceptance problem.

My anchor is Theorem 8, stable-semantics entry: for an EAAF \(\Delta\) and an epistemic goal argument \(g\), deciding whether \(g\) is accepted in every stable world view is \(\Pi_2^P\)-complete. The paper presents this as a result proved here, not merely a pointer to earlier work. The underlying question is exactly the paper’s NEA\(_{st}\) problem: each world view contains stable extensions, and the epistemic argument must be accepted throughout every such world view.

A plausible setting is a large civic consultation using a finite menu of argumentation templates. Each participant’s issue is represented by a complete EAAF and a designated epistemic claim. A type \(t\) is the full pair \((\Delta_t,g)\): the same arguments, attack relations, and claim. Participants with that same pair are indistinguishable for this query. Millions of participants might fall into a few dozen or hundred template types, so the population size \(n\) is large relative to the support size \(\tau\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants of type \(t\), given as a binary rational.

Call the mirror Continuous Necessary-Acceptance Rate. Its instance consists of a finite support \(T\) of valid EAAFs \(\Delta_t=\langle A,\Omega_t,\Psi_t,\Phi_t\rangle\) over a common argument set, with \(g\in\epsilon(\Delta_t)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and the fixed threshold \(1/2\). Set
\[ a_t = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if }g\text{ is accepted in every stable world view of }\Delta_t,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad R_\mu=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t. \]
The question is whether \(R_\mu\ge 1/2\). The solution is the yes/no answer; equivalently, one could ask for the exact rational rate \(R_\mu\). There is no intervention variable in the anchor: the decision is whether a majority of the population’s type-level claims are necessarily accepted.

This keeps the paper’s stable-world-view semantics intact. The population mass is over participants’ complete reasoning contexts, not over arguments, extensions, or possible worlds. A discrete consultation with \(n_t\) participants of each type gives \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), so the continuous input is the high-multiplicity version of that repeated-query population.

The expected result is hardness transfers. With just one supported type, its mass is \(1\), and the majority threshold answer is exactly the NEA\(_{st}\) answer from Theorem 8. Thus the mirror is already \(\Pi_2^P\)-hard at \(\tau=1\); the attack-graph and epistemic-cycle structure carries the hardness, not population multiplicity. For variable \(\tau\), evaluating each type with a polynomial number of NEA\(_{st}\) oracle calls gives a straightforward \(\Delta_3^P\) upper bound. A sharper classification of this aggregate threshold problem is a natural follow-up.

I would expect the authors to recognize this as a plausible high-multiplicity deployment of their acceptance problem: each local query is literally theirs, and the aggregate asks for the share of a large population for whom the claim is forced. The weakest point is that mass does not change the semantics within any one EAAF. A referee could reasonably call this a continuous population of repeated reasoning instances rather than a new continuous argumentation model. I think it remains a defensible mirror because it makes the population itself the object being measured, with exact shared types and fractional mass, while clearly limiting the claim to Theorem 8’s acceptance result. It does not mirror the paper’s other complexity results or its general semantics contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that it turns a distribution over whole problem instances into a population. Theorem 8 is a genuine computational result, and the inherited \(\Pi_2^P\)-hardness is allowed under the programme’s rules. The objection is about the model, not the answer.

In the proposed consultation, each person carries a complete EAAF and a goal argument. The mass of a type never enters that EAAF’s attacks, reducts, extensions, or world views; it is used only after solving the separate necessary-acceptance query for each type, to compute \(R_\mu\). This is a population of query cases. In the paper, by contrast, one EAAF is the shared semantic universe, and its arguments are the objects whose acceptance is determined. The participant-to-framework mapping therefore needs more justification than the proposal gives.

A stronger variant would put the population inside one shared argument map: people endorse argument types, and the question asks what share endorse arguments accepted in every stable world view. That is easier to imagine as a civic statistic. Yet the masses still leave EAAF semantics unchanged; they weight a tally of acceptance results. Making mass affect acceptance would require choosing a rule for how mass changes attack, defence, or epistemic defeat. The paper’s definitions do not select such a rule, and the resulting problem would need its own model and complexity analysis.

This is a real objection to the claim that the proposed mirror continues the paper’s computational problem. It does not establish that the resulting population statistic is unworthy: researchers interested in measuring the prevalence of necessarily accepted claims could reasonably want it. I cannot honestly rule out that scenario, so the negative case is limited. Its force depends on whether the authors would recognize a weighted population summary of EAAF queries as their problem.
