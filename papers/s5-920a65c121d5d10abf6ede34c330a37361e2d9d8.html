The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a compliant anchor for this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as tractability or hardness. Its numbered Results 1–6 are empirical findings. The closest is Result 4, proved here experimentally: strategic and combined information increase stability under student-proposing Gale–Shapley, but do not change it under Boston. The reported stable-matching rates are 33.33%, 100%, and 87.50% across the three Gale–Shapley treatments, and 0%, 12.50%, and 0% across the Boston treatments. This is evidence about behavior in the experiment, not a complexity result.

A plausible follow-on question, though not a mirror anchored to a qualifying result, is Minimum-Mass Stability Repair under Boston. Consider a large school district with schools \(U\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_u\), and a finite set \(T\) of complete applicant types. Each type specifies a true ranking and school priority class; \(\mu_t\) is its population share. Assume many applicants share each type, with ties within a priority class handled by an explicitly specified lottery. A solution assigns mass \(x_{t,r}\) of each true type \(t\) to each submitted ranking \(r\), preserving \(\sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t\). Run the Boston mechanism on those reports and capacities. Given a rational bound \(B\), ask whether reports can be chosen so that at most \(B\) of the population misreports and the resulting assignment is stable with respect to true rankings and priorities. Stability means individual rationality, no preferred school with an unfilled seat, and no justified-envy blocking pair. Equivalently, the optimization version minimizes the misreporting mass.

The regime is recognizable from the paper’s subject: a large admissions market in which many applicants share rankings and priority classes, while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) is far smaller than the number of applicants \(N\). Capacities must scale with population size, and the output is a fractional assignment by type. The computational question concerns the population and the mass of strategic reports; it does not continuize outcomes in place of the population.

I would leave its complexity open. The finite type and report sets make the question precise, but the Boston round process and stability constraints do not immediately give an LP. Discrete hardness might transfer in some regimes, while fractional mass could also make instances tractable. The main weakness is that stability repair is a planner’s intervention: the paper studies applicants’ choices and information treatments, not the minimum mass of reports needed to repair an outcome. So this is a credible adjacent research problem, but without a qualifying computational anchor, I would not present it as a positive mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper gives us no computational result to mirror. Its numbered Results 1–6 report experimental findings about strategy choices, stability rates, cognitive reflection, and fairness ratings. Result 4—the proponent’s closest anchor—says that information increased stability in the Gale–Shapley lab treatment. That is an estimated treatment effect in five-student markets, not a complexity claim or an algorithmic problem. Aggregating stable outcomes into population fractions would make a continuous statistic, but would not turn the result into a computational question. The same holds for Result 6’s fairness ratings and the strategy-frequency results.

A large admissions market with repeated applicant types is plausible, and scaling capacities with population is a natural co-modification. But the experiment’s markets were separate groups of five; pooling participants across groups would erase the interactions and capacity constraints that generated the measured outcomes. Scaling one market up instead changes the strategic environment. Applicants’ responses to information at that scale are not specified by the paper’s results.

The proposed stability-repair problem makes that change explicit. It asks a planner to choose or alter reports to minimize misreporting mass while producing a stable Boston outcome. The paper studies how applicants respond to information, not how much an authority should rewrite their reports. A closer-sounding version—choosing advice to induce stable outcomes—still needs a model of how applicants of each type respond to advice at large scale. The experiment supplies estimates for its particular small markets, not that response model. Assuming applicants comply turns the question into planner-controlled manipulation; supplying a behavioral model creates a new problem. Either could be worthwhile research, but neither is a computational mirror of Result 4.

This does not show that continuous computational work on school choice is unpromising. Large admissions markets may support high-multiplicity models, and existing high-multiplicity work supports that regime. The negative case is narrower: none of this paper’s named findings establishes an algorithmic problem whose population version is the result being studied. I would reject this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror, while admitting that the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise from its subject matter is harder to defend.
