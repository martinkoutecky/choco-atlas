The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible positive case, but it has a clear limit: the paper contains no named complexity or algorithmic result of the kind the brief asks for. Its theorems classify when voting rules satisfy WCSP. I can use them as structural anchors for new computational questions, but not honestly present them as results that the paper proves are in P, NP-hard, or similar.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: an anti-plurality rule with fixed-order tie-breaking is WCSP if and only if \(n\geq 2m-1\). It gives an exact boundary where a non-strategy-proof rule acquires the paper’s weaker robustness property as the electorate grows.

I would mirror it with Fractional Anti-Plurality WCSP. An instance has \(m\) alternatives, a fixed tie order, a rational mass \(\epsilon\) for a distinguished voter, and a listed set \(T\) of possible rankings for the other population. Their continuous distribution is \(\nu\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t=1-\epsilon\). For a true ranking \(P\), the score of \(x\) is
\[ \epsilon\,s(r(P,x))+\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t s(r(t,x)). \]
The question is whether the rule is WCSP over this continuous population: for every true ranking \(P\), every report \(P'\), and every \(\nu\) that produces a worst-case truthful outcome for \(P\), is the truthful outcome at least as good under \(P\) as the outcome after reporting \(P'\)? A no-instance should provide \(P,P'\), and a distribution \(\nu\) witnessing the violation. The main version takes \(T\) to contain all rankings.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large electorate has many voters but relatively few distinct rankings, and the other voters’ ranking masses are represented continuously. At \(\epsilon=1/n\), restricting \(\nu\) to multiples of \(1/n\) recovers the paper’s finite electorate. The continuous question is whether its threshold survives when those masses can be fractional. I expect this problem to be Class A: anti-plurality outcomes depend only on the mass assigned to each alternative’s last-place ranking, reducing the relevant background to \(m\) veto masses and linear winner comparisons. The exact fractional boundary is the result to find; Theorem 1 does not establish it.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 3, also proved in the paper: for every \(n\geq2\) and \(m\geq3\), no Borda rule is WCSP. Its mirror is Fractional Borda WCSP: use the same distinguished-voter and continuous-background model, with Borda scores, and determine the values of \(\epsilon\) for which WCSP holds over every allowed background distribution. The solution is an exact parameter characterization, with a violating distribution whenever WCSP fails. The finite theorem supplies negative cases at \(\epsilon=1/n\); it leaves the behavior at arbitrary fractional masses open. I expect Class A, since the witness conditions are linear in the background masses and optimizing a linear score over rankings has an efficient pricing formulation. That expectation is not a result of this paper.

The weakest point is that these mirrors preserve a voter’s influence by retaining a distinguished mass \(\epsilon\). In a fully atomless population, one person’s report changes no aggregate score, so WCSP becomes vacuous. Keeping a pivotal voter while making the rest continuous is a meaningful computational model, but it is a co-modification that the authors might not regard as their original axiom. The other gap is sharper: the paper’s anchors are axiomatic classifications, not computational-complexity results. So there is a concrete, potentially tractable continuous research programme here, but it is a new algorithmic study of the paper’s axiom, not a continuization of a complexity result the paper already proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes when anti-plurality satisfies WCSP; Theorem 3 says Borda never does. Neither states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. Testing WCSP on rational mass distributions could become a worthwhile computational study, but it would be a new study of the axiom, rather than a continuous version of a computational result this paper establishes.

Theorem 1 is the stronger anchor, and its proposed focal-voter repair exposes the central modeling problem. WCSP asks whether one agent can improve the outcome by changing their report while the other agents’ reports stay fixed. In an atomless society, one agent has mass zero, so their report does not change the score or the winner; WCSP then holds vacuously. Giving that voter positive mass \(\epsilon\) restores influence, but makes the “voter” a bloc. If \(\epsilon=1/n\) tracks one voter in a growing electorate, it vanishes in the limit; if \(\epsilon\) stays positive, the manipulator remains macroscopic. Cohort or bloc WCSP could be interesting, but it changes the axiom’s strategic actor.

There is also a definition to settle before either proposed mirror is a problem. In the paper, “worst case” ranges over the other agents’ possible preference profiles. If a distribution \(\nu\) is fixed, it does not by itself specify which backgrounds the worst case ranges over. If \(\nu\) varies over a simplex, that simplex is the uncertainty model, and the listed distribution is not the instance in the way the proposal suggests. A careful version could choose the full simplex and a positive-mass focal agent, but that is a particular hybrid model with a newly specified uncertainty set.

Theorem 3 has the same limitation. A fractional Borda characterization in that hybrid model might be mathematically clean, but it would still characterize an axiom for a focal bloc in a continuous background. It would not supply the missing computational result from the paper. The fact that either characterization might be easy, hard, or unchanged does not settle whether it is worth asking; the issue is that the paper itself gives no complexity landscape for it to extend.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: these are plausible follow-up questions, especially the anti-plurality threshold, but they do not make a strong continuization of this paper under ChoCo’s computational brief. I cannot honestly establish that no version would be worthwhile. A focal-agent study could be worth pursuing on its own; the case against is that preserving WCSP’s individual meaning prevents the population limit from carrying the axiom’s content.
