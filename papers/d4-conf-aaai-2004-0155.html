The strongest rescue is to treat that algorithm as an implicit anchor and imagine many people following temporal plans. But the algorithm solves one jointly constrained plan: its variables are named event times, its disjunctions link particular events, and its output is a schedule satisfying the whole network. For independent copies of a plan, adding population mass does not change consistency or each copy’s WLO optimum. Aggregating WLO across copies gives the worst value among positive-mass types, so the weights still do no work. A continuum would also make WLO an essential infimum, which can ignore exceptional measure-zero types. A share-sensitive population objective would require changing WLO.

A more compelling cohort-scheduling version could make mass matter through shared appointment capacity, congestion, or fairness. That is a plausible neighboring research direction. Those interactions, however, add the central coupling constraints and population objective that this paper does not study; they do not give a continuous-population version of its algorithmic result. The case against is strongest on this mismatch and the missing named anchor. It is not a proof that the authors could never recognize a cohort-scheduling model as their problem, but this paper supplies little basis for claiming that such a model would be a worthwhile mirror.
