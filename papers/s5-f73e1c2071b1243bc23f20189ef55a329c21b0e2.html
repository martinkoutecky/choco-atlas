The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its central contributions are extensions of inconsistency indices and a Monte Carlo comparison of how they respond to deleted comparisons. The only numbered theorem, Theorem 13, proves an equivalence between two definitions of inconsistency for a *single complete matrix*. It says nothing about the complexity of computing an index or solving a computational problem.

A crowd of assessors could still be a sensible high-multiplicity setting: a type could be an assessor’s complete comparison matrix, and its mass the share of assessors with that matrix. But the paper gives no population-level question to carry into that setting. Averaging an index across the crowd, or measuring the mass of matrices above an inconsistency threshold, would add an aggregation objective that the paper neither studies nor motivates. Lifting Theorem 13 to a distribution would be immediate from its pointwise statement, not a computational result. And treating the paper’s random perturbations and deletions as the population would continuize the experiment’s randomness, not the society.

One could devise a richer problem—such as choosing which mass of assessors’ judgments to review to bring a population statistic below a threshold—but that would introduce a new intervention problem and objective. It would not be a continuous mirror of a result in this paper. The negative case is therefore strong for this paper under the programme’s stated gate: plausible populations exist, but none rescues the absence of a paper-anchored computational question.
