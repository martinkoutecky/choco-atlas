The continuous mirror question

Given a finite metric type set T, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to 1, a cluster count k, and a clustering \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{j=1}^k x_{tj}=\mu_t\), audit its FJR approximation. A coalition chooses portions \(0\le\eta_{tj}\le x_{tj}\) with \(\sum_{t,j}\eta_{tj}\ge 1/k\), and has type masses \(\nu_t=\sum_j\eta_{tj}\). Let \(b_{tj}=\max_{u:x_{uj}>0}d(t,u)\) be the baseline maximum loss for type t in cluster j, and let \(D(\nu)=\max_{t,u:\nu_t>0,\nu_u>0}d(t,u)\) be the coalition's maximum loss. Determine the exact audit value \(\rho(x)=\max\{1,\sup_{\eta}\,\min_{(t,j):\eta_{tj}>0} b_{tj}/D(\nu)\}\), with positive-over-zero interpreted as infinity and zero-over-zero as 1; or return a \(\lambda\)-approximation \(\theta\) satisfying \(\theta\le\rho(x)\le\lambda\theta\).

The model it lives in

A mass-weighted extension of non-centroid clustering over a finite metric support: types carry rational population mass, cluster assignments may split that mass, and FJR auditing compares participating mass's worst baseline loss with its maximum loss after deviation.

The objection that survived

A practical clustered-learning instance needs an exact finite catalogue of client profiles, and maximum loss can make rare profiles decisive; the proponent supplies this as a plausible regime but does not establish how often it occurs.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6's maximum-loss FJR auditing lower bound and Corollary 1's average- and maximum-loss FJR clustering guarantees. It leaves the core results, exact FJR existence, other auditing results, and experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the factor-2 hardness for maximum-loss FJR auditing persist when coalition mass can be split fractionally among types of equal mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s fairness audit. Its entitlement rule already has the right population interpretation: a coalition is entitled to deviate when it represents at least \(1/k\) of society. Replacing a headcount threshold of \(n/k\) with a mass threshold of \(1/k\) keeps the question intact. It also leaves the outcome discrete in the relevant sense: there are still at most \(k\) clusters. What becomes continuous is the population assigned to them.

A plausible regime is clustered federated learning with many clients drawn from a modest set of recurring data-generating profiles. A type is a profile that makes clients indistinguishable under the model’s distance and loss; its mass is that profile’s share of the clients. For example, a service might have millions of clients but only hundreds or thousands of recurring site or device profiles. Clusters represent groups collaborating on models, and a client’s maximum loss is the distance to the most dissimilar profile in their group. This is close to the paper’s own motivation: it explicitly studies non-centroid clustering where agents care about their peers, including clustered federated learning.

The first anchor is Theorem 6, proved here. It says that, unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time \(\lambda\)-approximate FJR auditing algorithm exists for maximum loss for any \(\lambda<2\). The paper’s auditing problem takes a clustering and estimates its exact FJR approximation \(\rho\), returning \(\theta\) with \(\rho\in[\theta,\lambda\theta]\). The theorem proves the lower bound by reducing CLIQUE: it constructs a clustering whose exact FJR approximation is \(2\) if the graph has a clique of the target size, and \(1\) otherwise.

My lead mirror is Mass-Weighted Maximum-Loss FJR Auditing. An instance consists of a finite metric type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a cluster count \(k\), and a clustering \(x\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to cluster \(j\), with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\). A coalition is a choice of masses \(\eta_{tj}\le x_{tj}\) with total mass at least \(1/k\). Write \(\nu_t=\sum_j\eta_{tj}\) for its mass of type \(t\). Its maximum loss after deviating is \(\max_{t:\nu_t>0}\max_{u:\nu_u>0}d(t,u)\); its worst baseline loss is the minimum, over participating type-and-cluster cells, of that cell’s maximum distance to the types in its current cluster. The task is to return \(\theta\) approximating the largest of \(1\) and the ratio of worst baseline loss to deviation loss, within a specified factor \(\lambda\). A solution is that estimate.

I expect the paper’s factor-\(2\) lower bound to transfer. In its CLIQUE construction, each of the \(n\) graph-derived types has mass \(1/n\), and \(1/k=t/n\), where \(t\) is the clique target. A qualifying coalition must therefore draw from at least \(t\) types. Allowing fractional portions of a type does not change the construction’s \(1\)-versus-\(2\) gap: the losses depend on which types have positive coalition mass, and the threshold still requires at least \(t\) types. Replicating every type into \(M\) identical agents gives \(N=M\tau\) without changing the distribution or the reduction. Thus this is hardness that survives a high-multiplicity realization, though the graph’s combinatorics remain in the number of distinct types.

A second, algorithmic anchor is Corollary 1, proved here: the efficient GreedyCapture algorithm returns a clustering that is \(4\)-FJR for average loss and \(2\)-FJR for maximum loss. The paper notes that the same clustering achieves both guarantees simultaneously. Its mirror is Mass-Weighted Dual-Loss FJR Clustering: given \((T,\mu,d,k)\), return a mass assignment \(x\) such that no coalition of mass at least \(1/k\) improves its average loss by a factor greater than \(4\), or its maximum loss by a factor greater than \(2\), relative to the worst baseline loss among its members.

I expect this clustering problem to be tractable. The direct weighted version of GreedyCapture repeatedly finds the smallest-radius ball containing mass \(1/k\), assigns that much mass to the next cluster, and splits a type’s mass at the boundary if needed. The paper’s triangle-inequality argument should carry over: the type support is finite, and the relevant bounds compare distances and mass-weighted averages just as the discrete proof compares distances and headcounts. Its natural running-time parameters are \(k\), support size \(\tau\), and the bit length of the masses and distances. Further questions are whether the \(4\) and \(2\) guarantees can be improved, and whether exact FJR can be computed in polynomial time.

The scope here is those two results: maximum-loss FJR auditing and FJR clustering under average and maximum loss. I am not extending every result in the paper, and I am not treating fractional cluster assignments as the point of the exercise; they express how a continuum of indistinguishable clients can be divided among clusters.

The weakest point is that Theorem 6’s reduction uses an arbitrary shortest-path metric and a type set that grows with the graph. Replication makes the population much larger than the support, but it does not make the reduction’s hard combinatorics come from multiplicity. So the hardness case is strongest for the general metric audit problem; its force in a particular federated-learning domain depends on whether that domain’s type metric can express similarly rich instances. The authors should still recognize the mirrors as their questions: the coalition threshold, non-centroid loss, FJR criterion, and auditing task are all retained, with population shares replacing headcounts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is about whether the paper’s applications really contain a useful population of repeated types. For its metric losses, a type must preserve the distances that determine every agent’s loss. In an ordinary data set, most points are distinct; grouping nearby points into a type changes those distances. That can be especially consequential for maximum loss, where even a tiny positive mass of a distant type can change the loss. So a large number of records or federated clients does not by itself give a small-support society. The proposed clustered-learning scenario needs an additional assumption: clients share a finite catalogue of profiles that fully determines their relevant losses. That assumption is plausible, but the paper does not establish it for its datasets or applications.

This limitation weakens the practical motivation, but it does not defeat the two mirrors. Theorem 6’s reduction transfers to a mass model: with \(n_0=|V'|=|V|t\), give each graph-derived type mass \(1/n_0\), so the entitlement threshold is \(1/k=t/n_0\). A coalition at the threshold must draw mass from at least \(t\) types, preserving the clique-based \(1\)-versus-\(2\) gap. Replicating each type preserves those masses while making the headcount arbitrarily large. The reduction may not describe a particular learning service, but it does show that maximum-loss FJR auditing remains a coherent continuous-population question.

Corollary 1 is also hard to dislodge. A weighted GreedyCapture can repeatedly take mass \(1/k\) from the smallest-radius ball, splitting a type at the boundary. The triangle-inequality bounds underlying the \(4\)-FJR and \(2\)-FJR guarantees appear to carry over to mass-weighted average and maximum loss. The finite-support setting does not remove the arguments; it changes headcounts into masses.

So the strongest negative case is that the mirrors’ usefulness in real applications depends on validating a restrictive exact-type model, and maximum-loss guarantees may be sensitive to rare profiles. That is a real modelling concern, but it does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. I cannot honestly defeat either anchor: the audit lower bound transfers, and the clustering guarantee looks adaptable.
