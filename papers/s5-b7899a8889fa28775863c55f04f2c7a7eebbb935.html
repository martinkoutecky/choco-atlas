The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\), localities \(L\), and services \(S\), an instance lists rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), integer needs \(\nu_{ts}\), a strict locality ranking for each type, finite priority classes at each locality, and rational per-capita capacities \(b_{\ell s}\). Find whether there is a rational assignment \(x_{t\ell}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_\ell x_{t\ell}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\nu_{ts}x_{t\ell}\le b_{\ell s}\) that is weakly envy-free and typewise first-order stochastically dominates every other feasible weakly envy-free assignment; if so, output it. For \(u\) with \(x_{u\ell}>0\), define \(G_{u\ell s}(x)=\sum_{v:\,v\triangleright_\ell u}\nu_{vs}\sum_{\ell':\,\ell\succeq_v\ell'}x_{v\ell'}\). A violation occurs if some higher-priority type \(t\) has positive mass assigned to a locality it ranks below \(\ell\), while \(G_{u\ell s}(x)+\epsilon\nu_{us}\le b_{\ell s}\) for every service \(s\) needed by \(u\), for some \(\epsilon>0\). Typewise first-order stochastic dominance means that for every type \(t\) and every prefix \(P\) of its locality ranking, the assignment gives at least as much \(t\)-mass to \(P\) as any other feasible weakly envy-free assignment.

The model it lives in

A society of finitely many family types with rational masses, integral service needs, locality rankings, and priority classes; the decision variable \(x_{t\ell}\) is the mass of each type assigned to each locality, and per-capita capacities constrain aggregate service use. Weak envy tests whether a positive mass could move given the remaining capacity after higher-priority types that prefer the locality are accounted for; family-optimality compares assignments by typewise first-order stochastic dominance.

The objection that survived

Under the paper’s strict individual rankings, including exact rank in a type generally makes every family a distinct type, so the proponent’s moderate-support instance is not a mirror as stated; finite priority classes replace strict priorities with ties.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

There is one qualification under your anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result that explicitly says a problem is NP-hard, in P, or in another complexity class. Its claim that stable-matching existence is NP-complete appears in unnumbered prose in Section 5.1 and is attributed to earlier work. I would not use it as a numbered anchor.

The strongest nearby anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that the output of the Cascading Multidimensional Deferred Acceptance (CMDA) algorithm is the unique family-optimal weakly envy-free matching: it is weakly envy-free and weakly Pareto dominates every other weakly envy-free matching. The theorem states the matching property rather than a complexity class, but Algorithm 3 and its accompanying runtime discussion give a polynomial-time algorithm in the explicit number of families and localities. That is the result I would mirror.

The continuous version I find convincing is Family-Optimal Weakly Envy-Free Resettlement\(_\infty\). Take a large annual intake of refugee families and a fixed set of localities. A type \(t\) completely describes a family for this problem: its integer service-needs vector \((\nu_{ts})_{s\in S}\), its strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of localities, and its priority rank at each locality. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of families of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The instance lists only the \(\tau\) types with positive mass. Let \(x_{t\ell}\) be the fraction of the whole intake consisting of type \(t\) assigned to locality \(\ell\), including the null locality. Require
\[ \sum_{\ell}x_{t\ell}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t \nu_{ts}x_{t\ell}\le b_{\ell s}, \]
where \(b_{\ell s}\) is locality \(\ell\)’s service capacity normalized to the cohort size. A fractional \(x_{t\ell}\) means that this fraction of otherwise indistinguishable families goes to \(\ell\); each family still receives one locality, not a fractional placement. The per-capita capacity convention is the needed scaling as the cohort grows.

For precision, adapt the paper’s weak-envy condition to positive mass. For a type \(u\) assigned positive mass to \(\ell\), the protected higher-priority demand for service \(s\) is
\[ G_{u\ell s}(x)= \sum_{v:\,v\triangleright_\ell u}\nu_{vs} \sum_{\ell':\,\ell\succeq_v\ell'}x_{v\ell'}. \]
Thus it counts higher-priority families whose current assignment is \(\ell\) or a less-preferred locality. A positive mass of type \(u\) can be added alongside those families precisely when \(G_{u\ell s}(x)<b_{\ell s}\) for every service \(s\) that \(u\) needs. The assignment is weakly envy-free if there are no types \(t,u\) and locality \(\ell\) such that \(x_{u\ell}>0\), \(t\triangleright_\ell u\), and a positive mass of \(t\) is assigned to a locality it likes less than \(\ell\), while \(G_{u\ell s}(x)<b_{\ell s}\) for every service \(u\) needs. That is the continuum counterpart of the paper’s test: a higher-priority family may envy only when the locality could accommodate the family it envies alongside the relevant higher-priority families.

Call a weakly envy-free assignment family-optimal if it first-order stochastically dominates every other feasible weakly envy-free assignment type by type: for every type \(t\) and every prefix \(P\) of its locality ranking, \(x\) assigns at least as much type-\(t\) mass to \(P\) as the other assignment does. The problem is: given binary-rational masses and normalized capacities, the service-needs vectors, and the type preference and priority lists, decide whether such a greatest assignment exists and, if it does, output its rational mass vector. The target running time is polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of localities, the number of services, and the input bit length. The expected direction is tractable, through a compressed type-level version of CMDA; the finite theorem does not itself establish that compression or the bit-length bound.

This regime is plausible for a large resettlement intake in which many families share standardized need profiles, inferred locality-preference rankings, and priority categories. For example, an intake of tens of thousands of families might have hundreds of supported types. The authors themselves describe systematic assignment at substantial scale, suggest eliciting preferences over locality attributes, and allow service capacities and family sizes to capture several dimensions. The necessary co-modification is that locality capacity scales with cohort size. The delicate modeling choice is the type definition: because preferences and priorities are part of a complete type, the type count could grow sharply if those profiles are highly individualized. The mirror is strongest in an intake system with standardized assessments and a moderate number of distinct profiles.

The authors should recognize this as their matching problem: families are the agents, localities are the destinations, preferences and priorities remain central, and multidimensional service capacity still constrains feasible assignments. The aggregation changes the population representation, not the mechanism’s substantive question. The mirror covers Theorem 5’s family-optimal weak-envy-free outcome; it does not claim a continuous version of the strategy-proofness results or of the paper’s stable-matching discussion. Its weakest point is the extension of weak envy-freeness: in a continuum, testing whether *some positive mass* fits differs from testing whether one whole family fits. That is a real modeling choice to put before the authors. It is also explicit and preserves the original distinction between harmless priority violations and violations that block a genuinely feasible assignment.

The mirror is a stronger computational result only if the algorithm runs in the support size and rational bit length, rather than expanding the masses into an explicit population. That is the open work the question hands to a prover. If it succeeds, natural next questions are whether the continuous optimum recovers CMDA on equal \(1/N\) masses, how to round it with bounded loss for finite intakes, and whether a type-level strategy-proofness guarantee survives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to the proponent’s anchor is that Theorem 5’s central input is not just a distribution of family needs and preferences. It is a strict ranking of named families at every locality, and that ranking is part of the weak-envy test. Under the paper’s model, two families cannot have the same complete type if type includes their priority ranks: their ranks differ. The proposed intake of tens of thousands of families with only hundreds of supported types therefore cannot preserve the theorem’s input as stated.

That matters for the result, not just for bookkeeping. Which family gets a locality can determine whether a higher-priority family strongly envies them. Aggregating families with the same needs and preferences erases which copies have higher priority. Keeping priority rank as a type attribute restores that information, but typically makes the support grow with the population. A continuum of priority scores would preserve it, but would replace the paper’s finite, explicitly listed types with a function over a continuum.

The stronger version of the mirror would group families into priority classes, treating within-class ordering as irrelevant or as a tie-break. That is a plausible resettlement model, but it changes the priority relation used to define weak envy. Its type-level family-optimality result would be about a tied-priority variant of CMDA, not a direct continuization of Theorem 5. The proponent’s first-order stochastic dominance is a sensible way to compare split assignments of identical families; it does not restore the priority information lost by that grouping. The paper’s finite algorithm may also suggest an efficient type-level procedure, but the theorem does not establish one for fractional assignments.

This is a real weakness in the proposed mirror, but it does not defeat the best version of the case for one: authors could reasonably accept priority classes as the natural population-scale model, and the resulting computational question is well-posed. Theorem 5 is the only anchor raised, and I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt asks for. My case against is strongest when fidelity to the paper’s strict family-level priorities is required; it weakens substantially once standardized priority classes are admitted.
