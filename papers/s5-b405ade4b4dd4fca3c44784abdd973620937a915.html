What the mirror covers

The proposed mixture extension would concern CFP and CDP, and thus the axiomatic relationships in Theorems 2 and 3; it does not cover a computational result, and Arrow's impossibility is also axiomatic.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive bridge is the paper’s move to variable-size populations: it represents combining two electorates by concatenating their preference profiles. A plausible regime is a large policy consultation with many respondents but relatively few distinct complete preorders. Types would be those preorders, and mass their population fractions. Concatenating subgroups then corresponds to mixing their distributions, \(\lambda\mu_1+(1-\lambda)\mu_2\), with \(\lambda\) equal to one subgroup’s population share. That makes a continuous analogue of the paper’s profile-consistency ideas easy to imagine.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor the required mirror. Theorem 2, proved here, equates CFP with KP under Explicaciones Transitivas; Theorem 3, also proved here, equates CDP with KP*. Theorem 1 states Arrow’s impossibility for the listed axioms, with its proof referred to Lehmann and Magidor (1992). These are axiomatic results, not claims about an algorithm, complexity class, or computational problem.

One could define continuous CFP or CDP by replacing profile concatenation with mixture of population distributions. That would be an axiomatic extension of the paper’s subject, not a continuous computational problem mirroring one of its named results. The soft spot is decisive: the population setting is a natural fit, but the paper supplies no eligible computational anchor, so presenting that extension as the requested positive mirror would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result to mirror, even under the best population model. A large consultation with finitely many ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. Give each type a mass \(\mu_t\), and replace concatenating two profiles with mixing their distributions, \(\lambda\mu_1+(1-\lambda)\mu_2\). Then CFP and CDP become conditions on how a choice function behaves under mixtures; Theorems 2 and 3 become corresponding axiomatic characterizations. Arrow’s theorem remains an impossibility result from a set of axioms. These are natural continuous extensions, but none asks an algorithmic question or establishes a complexity result.

The paper’s numbered theorems and propositions characterize relationships among properties; its Borda and Condorcet discussions define rules and illustrate them. A question such as whether a given rule satisfies a mixture version of CFP would add a computational task the paper does not pose or solve. The consultation scenario makes the population model plausible, but it cannot turn these axiomatic results into computational ones. This does not make a continuous axiomatic theory uninteresting; it means that developing one would be a different project, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.
