What the mirror covers

The proposed audit covers whether the majority vector from one realized distribution is consistent. It does not compute Proposition 1’s universal claim over procedures and admissible profiles, or cover Propositions 2–3, the truth tracking analysis, or the incentive analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

This article has no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. The closest is Proposition 1, proved in the paper: for a nonsimple agenda and at least three individuals, no decision procedure can satisfy all three demands of pluralism (every combination of individual judgments is admissible), majoritarian responsiveness (the group’s judgment on each proposition is its majority judgment), and integrity (the group’s accepted propositions are jointly consistent). That is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a complexity classification or an algorithmic result. Propositions 2 and 3 are also impossibility results; the paper credits them to earlier work.

The strongest continuous-population candidate is a Continuous Majority-Integrity Audit, using Proposition 1 as a conceptual hook rather than a qualifying computational anchor. An instance gives a finite agenda \(\mathcal A\), its logical consistency conditions, and a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete, individually consistent judgment types. The support has size \(\tau\), with each type specifying one member’s judgments on the whole agenda. Assume no proposition has exactly half the mass on each side. Compute the proposition-by-proposition majority vector; decide whether it is collectively consistent; if not, return an inconsistent subset of its judgments as a certificate.

A plausible regime is a large citizen consultation or assembly considering a fixed set of policy claims and reasons. Citizens with the same complete pattern of judgments form one type. The population \(N\) can be large while the number \(\tau\) of observed patterns remains moderate. This makes the society itself a distribution over agents’ judgment types; it does not rely on fractional outcomes.

The article’s three-member discursive-dilemma example transfers directly. Put mass \(1/3\) on each of the three consistent types in Table 1: one accepts both premises, one accepts only the first, and one accepts only the second. The continuous majority accepts both premises and rejects the conclusion, so it violates integrity. In this sense the continuum is a natural regime for the phenomenon, and Proposition 1’s obstruction survives it. The expected result for the audit is tractability when the agenda is fixed: summing the masses takes \(O(\tau|\mathcal A|)\) arithmetic operations, followed by a fixed-size consistency check. If the agenda grows, logical consistency may add work, but the paper provides no complexity result for that problem; any such difficulty would come from the agenda representation.

This candidate covers only the realized majority-versus-integrity conflict in Proposition 1. It does not capture the paper’s universal classification of decision procedures, its strategy-proofness result, or its truth-tracking analysis. It suggests further computational questions, such as finding the least mass that must change types to restore a consistent majority.

The weak point is substantial: the proposed audit turns a theorem about procedures into a per-society computation. For a fixed agenda that computation is simple, and the \(1/3\)-mass example already reproduces the discrete paradox. So this is a plausible continuous-population formulation of the article’s subject, but it does not supply the computational anchor or complexity landscape the ChoCo programme is meant to study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest on fit: this article has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 is an axiomatic impossibility theorem about decision procedures; Propositions 2 and 3 concern other axiomatic demands. The remaining results analyze disagreement, truth tracking, and incentives. None asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity.

The proposed audit is a coherent high-multiplicity model: citizens with the same complete judgment pattern can form a type, and the one-third distribution from Table 1 reproduces the discursive dilemma exactly. But the audit asks whether one given society’s majority judgment is consistent. For a fixed agenda, that means summing the masses in \(O(\tau|\mathcal A|)\) arithmetic operations and checking the result. It does not compute the impossibility in Proposition 1, which quantifies over procedures and admissible profiles. If agenda complexity is allowed to grow, checking consistency may add work, but that would be a new agenda-logic problem; continuity has not supplied the missing computational result.

A stronger proposal would be to find the least mass that must change judgment types to restore a consistent majority. That could be a sensible continuous bribery or campaigning problem. It also adds costs and an intervention objective absent from the paper. It may be worth studying in ChoCo, but it is a new problem suggested by the article, not a computational mirror of one of its results.

The other tempting extensions have similar gaps. A single person has zero mass in an atomless society, so individual manipulation becomes vacuous; positive-mass coalition manipulation is a more meaningful replacement, but it changes the question. The paper’s truth-tracking and frequency analyses could be recast using distributions over types, but that would continuize probabilistic analysis, not give the article a computational anchor.

So the proposed audit does not survive as the required mirror, and the article is a poor fit if “mirror” must be anchored in a named computational result. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no continuous computational problem inspired by this subject could be worthwhile: mass-based integrity repair or coalition manipulation could be good research questions. The negative case is strong on what this paper establishes, and weak on ruling out those new, adjacent questions.
