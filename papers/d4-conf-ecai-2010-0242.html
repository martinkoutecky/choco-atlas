What the mirror covers

The proposed model would cover Theorem 2 and the fixed-price case in Theorem 1 if their equilibrium question carried over; it does not. It leaves Proposition 1 and the finite-market experiments aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a plausible follow-on model for logistics or energy markets. Let a type specify whether an agent wants one market’s capacity or a complete bundle, along with its valuation; let \(\mu_t\) be the population share of that type. As the population grows, scale each auction’s option supply with it. A precise computational question would be: given finite rational type masses, per-capita supplies, and the paper’s lower and upper exercise-price bounds, compute a symmetric equilibrium policy for the sequential mass auctions and its aggregate welfare and revenue. Price each mass auction at its marginal losing bid, then apply the paper’s rule \(K_j=\max\{K_j^L,\min\{K_j^H,z_j\}\}\), where \(z_j\) is that bid. This keeps the exposure problem and the option mechanism central, and the logistics setting is one the authors themselves discuss.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It derives a Bayes–Nash equilibrium strategy for the synergy bidder in \(m\) sequential second-price auctions with \(N_j\) local bidders per auction, local values drawn from \(F_j\), and lower and upper exercise-price bounds. In the last auction the equilibrium bid is \(v-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}K_i\). In an earlier auction \(r\), if \(v\le \sum_{i=1}^{r-1}K_i+K_r^H+\sum_{i=r+1}^m K_i^L\), the bid is \(v-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}K_i-\sum_{i=r+1}^m K_i^L\); otherwise it is \(K_r^H+EP^*_{r+1}(v,\vec K_r^*(K_r^H))\). The theorem gives recursive formulas (Equations 5–7) for those future expected profits, using \(H_j(x)=F_j(x)^{N_j}\). This is a substantive equilibrium computation, but it is not a theorem about polynomial time, hardness, or any other complexity class.

That distinction is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the specified kind. Theorem 1 gives the fixed-exercise-price equilibrium strategy, with its proof omitted as a special case of the later analysis. Proposition 1, proved here, says that finitely many synergy bidders are strategically equivalent to one whose value is the highest among them. Neither supplies a complexity classification. The Monte Carlo comparisons are experimental results, not named complexity results. So there is no eligible anchor, and no anchor-backed mirror question to classify.

The mass-auction question above is therefore only a plausible research direction, not a result this paper supports. Its weakest point is substantial: scaling supply and replacing a finite second-price auction with marginal-price mass auctions changes the strategic game, so Theorem 2 does not carry over. The paper offers a credible setting and a nearby equilibrium problem, but not evidence that this continuous version is tractable—or even that its authors would accept it as the computational mirror of their stated results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a mismatch between the paper’s source of value and the natural population limit. The options protect a bidder against uncertainty about completing a fixed bundle: in each later auction, a finite set of local bidders might outbid it. In the paper, that uncertainty enters through the order statistic \(H_j(x)=F_j(x)^{N_j}\). A deterministic mass of bidder types does not have the same order-statistic uncertainty.

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and I would not dismiss it merely because it is not a complexity theorem. It derives equilibrium strategies and recursive expected profits. But it does not classify the complexity of computing an equilibrium from a finite input. The proposed mirror—computing equilibrium in sequential mass auctions—needs a new allocation and pricing model before it becomes a computational problem. With one option for sale in each market, the largest local bids concentrate at the upper end of the value distribution as \(N_j\) grows. With supply scaled to population, marginal losing bids concentrate at deterministic quantiles. In either case, conditional on the society, prices become predictable and the bidder’s chance of failing to complete the bundle largely disappears. Its options lose the exposure risk they were designed to address.

The logistics or energy version is plausible, but its natural repairs change the question. Scaling capacity with population creates a market for quantities rather than the paper’s second-price auctions for single options; allocating a positive mass of completed bundles then requires rules for coordinating allocations across markets. Keeping one strategic synergy bidder preserves the individual decision, but leaves that bidder as an atom outside the continuous population. Giving synergy bidders positive mass instead creates a cohort whose competition and rationing are different from the paper’s setting. Common market shocks or random rationing could restore uncertainty, but that uncertainty would come from an added stochastic mechanism, not from continuizing the population. These are coherent research directions; the paper’s theorem supplies no computational result for them.

Theorem 1 is a special case of the same finite-auction analysis, so it does not provide a separate anchor. Proposition 1 is especially tied to finite multiplicity: with finitely many synergy bidders, only the highest-valued one matters. In an atomless limit, a single highest-valued bidder has zero mass; if the highest value belongs to a positive-mass cohort, the result no longer reduces the game to one bidder. Preserving the proposition’s original setting therefore preserves the finite players whose population the mirror was meant to continuize.

The Monte Carlo comparisons establish performance for a specified finite market and parameter choices. They do not state an algorithmic or complexity result. A continuous welfare or revenue comparison could be formulated, but under a deterministic type distribution it would compare outcomes at predictable clearing prices. Preserving the paper’s stochastic performance question would require additional uncertainty assumptions and would become a new mechanism-design study.

So the negative case is strongest against treating this paper as a close ChoCo mirror: its named result is equilibrium analysis for a finite, stochastic auction, and its mechanism’s central benefit fades in the direct continuum limit. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous auction problem can be built from the paper. A mass market with carefully specified shocks and allocation rules might be worthwhile. The weaker point is that such a problem would need its own justification and computational formulation; this paper does not supply an anchor for it.
