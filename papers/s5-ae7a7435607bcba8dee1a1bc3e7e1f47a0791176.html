What the mirror covers

No result yields a continuous computational mirror; the convergence theorems, dissensus examples, and simulations concern opinion dynamics rather than computational tasks.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive intuition is that this paper studies a population-scale phenomenon: its SOM− clique result concerns whether an entire society converges, and its simulations include networks of over two million agents. A possible high-multiplicity setting would group platform users who share an initial opinion, tolerance, majority threshold, and exposure pattern. Their masses would be population fractions.

But there is no named computational-complexity result here to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that SOM− models on cliques with at least three agents converge to consensus. Theorem 1, also proved here, says the extreme opinions converge in any SOM− model. Both are dynamical claims; neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The large-scale simulations are not named complexity results either.

One could formulate a continuous-population consensus question for those cohorts, but it would be a new dynamics question, not a mirror of a qualifying computational anchor. The paper’s continuous-valued opinions and large finite simulations do not make its population continuous. So the positive intuition is real, but under the requested standard there is no anchor on which to build the required continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest reason to reject this as a ChoCo mirror is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 1 establishes convergence of the extreme opinions; Theorem 2 establishes consensus for SOM− cliques with at least three agents. The dissensus examples show that consensus is not guaranteed in other settings, and the simulations measure consensus rates. These are results about dynamics and convergence, not complexity or algorithms for a computational problem. That is a mismatch with the programme’s stated target, not a claim that a continuous version would have the same answer.

The best rescue is a dense, exchangeable platform population: users share the same clique-like exposure pattern and silence parameters, while their opinions vary. A mass distribution over opinions and speaking states is a sensible continuous model, and Theorem 2 makes this the strongest anchor. But its result is still a universal convergence claim. Asking for the limit, deciding consensus on a more general input, or computing an approximate convergence time could create computational questions; none is a problem the paper formulates or classifies. They would be new work inspired by its dynamics, not mirrors of its named result.

Theorem 1 and the network examples are harder to carry over faithfully. In arbitrary graphs, a type that records only opinion and silence parameters omits the influence relationships that determine the dynamics. A complete type would also have to capture an agent’s network role and, for SOM+, their latest public opinion. In sparse networks, bridge positions can be decisive and may be unique. One could move to a dense graphon or another continuum network model, but that co-modifies the network along with the population, and still leaves the paper’s contribution as an analytic dynamics question. The two-million-agent simulations do not establish a small type support: scale alone does not make network positions interchangeable.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo candidate. It is weaker as a universal claim that no continuous-population version could be worthwhile. The clique setting supports a coherent population limit, and someone could formulate a computational problem around it. But that would extend the paper’s agenda rather than mirror one of its computational results, because the paper has no such result to mirror.
