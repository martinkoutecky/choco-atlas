The continuous mirror question

Given finite public types \(q\), topics \(j\), rational public masses \(\mu_q\), application sets \(C_q\), rankings, values \(v_{qj}\), topic capacities \(b_j\), and total capacity \(\kappa\), each complete type is \((q,r)\), where \(r\) determines an acceptable set \(A_{q,r}\). The actual masses \(\nu_{q,r}\) are hidden, subject to \(\sum_r \nu_{q,r}=\mu_q\). A policy may sequentially offer a fixed topic to a chosen mass of a public type, observe exact aggregate acceptances and rejections, and make later offers to remaining mass. For every admissible hidden distribution and response history, can a polynomial-time policy guarantee a final accepted assignment of value at least \(\frac12\operatorname{OPT}(\nu)\), where \(\operatorname{OPT}(\nu)\) is the maximum-value feasible matching using the realized acceptable sets and capacities?

The model it lives in

A robust continuous matching problem over mass \(\nu_{q,r}\) of complete types \((q,r)\), with public projected masses \(\mu_q\) and an ambiguity set of hidden distributions. Policy decisions are sequential fixed offers to portions of residual type mass; responses reveal accepted and rejected masses. The benchmark is the maximum-value feasible mass assignment using the realized acceptable sets, with per-type, topic, and total capacities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2’s \(1/2\)-approximation guarantee for sequential fixed offers. It leaves the paper’s other results, including flexible offers, mixed protocols, and their bounds, unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time sequential policy guarantee \(\frac12\operatorname{OPT}(\nu)\) for every admissible hidden distribution?
  • Is the \(1/2\) guarantee tight when offers and responses operate on mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would build the case around Proposition 2, and stop at one anchor. The paper does not give an NP-hardness or polynomial-time complexity classification; its named algorithmic result is an approximation guarantee. Proposition 2 states that the sequential protocol Max achieves approximation ratio \(1/2\): it considers application pairs \((s_i,t_j)\) in non-increasing order of \(v(s_i,t_j)\), makes a fixed offer of \(t_j\) to \(s_i\) if that student has not already accepted, and stops after \(k\) acceptances or when it has considered every pair. The resulting acceptable matching \(M\) has \(v(M)\ge \tfrac12 v(M^\ast)\), where \(M^\ast\) is the best matching using the students’ actual acceptable topics. This is a result of the paper, though its proof is omitted as similar to the greedy matching argument cited there.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large internship or research-grant programme with many applicants and repeated project tracks. Applicants share standardized public profiles: the same tracks applied to, the same ranking of those tracks, and the same score vector used by the jury. A public type \(q\) records that information and has mass \(\mu_q\). The number of applicants \(n\) can be very large while the number of such types \(\tau\) remains much smaller. Competing offers give applicants different private outside options, so the jury still does not know which offers they will accept. Track capacities and total grants scale with the population; each person receives at most one integral assignment, while assignments aggregated over a type can be fractional.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous Fixed-Offer Grant Allocation. An instance has finite public types \(q\), topics \(j\), rational masses \(\mu_q\), application sets \(C_q\), rankings \(\succsim_q\), values \(v_{qj}\), topic capacities \(b_j\), and total grant capacity \(\kappa\). A complete type also includes a private outside-option tier \(r\), which determines the acceptable set \(A_{q,r}\), an upper contour set of the ranking or the empty set. The actual mass \(\nu_{q,r}\) of each complete type is hidden; the jury knows \(\sum_r\nu_{q,r}=\mu_q\), but not the decomposition. A protocol may sequentially offer a fixed topic to a chosen mass of a public type. Responses give the exact accepted and rejected masses; acceptors commit to that topic, and rejected mass may receive later offers. The guarantee is worst-case over hidden acceptance profiles and response histories consistent with them, with no probability model.

For the realized hidden society, the benchmark is the omniscient optimum

\[ \operatorname{OPT}(\nu)= \max \sum_{q,r,j\in A_{q,r}}v_{qj}x_{q,r,j}, \]

subject to \(\sum_j x_{q,r,j}\le \nu_{q,r}\), \(\sum_{q,r}x_{q,r,j}\le b_j\), and \(\sum_{q,r,j}x_{q,r,j}\le\kappa\). The continuous problem is: given only the public instance, compute a polynomial-time sequential fixed-offer policy whose final accepted assignment has value at least \(\tfrac12\operatorname{OPT}(\nu)\) for every admissible hidden society. Its solution is the adaptive offer policy and the resulting feasible mass assignment. This asks whether Proposition 2’s guarantee survives when the population and supplies are scaled together, not whether a fractional outcome space is useful.

I expect a \(1/2\)-guarantee to be tractable: the finite result gives a concrete greedy lead, the type support is finite, and the clairvoyant benchmark is a capacitated matching optimization problem. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of the paper. Fractional mass may allow batching or rationing that improves the guarantee, so I would leave open whether \(1/2\) is tight. Further questions include how many aggregate offer rounds are needed, whether the guarantee changes when topic capacities do not scale with population, and which response information suffices for a type-compressed policy.

The scope is just Proposition 2’s sequential fixed-offer approximation problem; I would not claim this mirrors the paper’s flexible-offer or mixed-protocol results. The paper’s original LAMSADE scenario has few, individually distinctive candidates, so the high-multiplicity case needs a different but recognizable application regime. My weakest point is the information model: preserving the paper’s unknown acceptance requires keeping outside-option tiers private, whereas the standard continuous society is often supplied as a known distribution over complete types. The mirror therefore supplies public type masses and treats the latent decomposition as adversarial uncertainty. That extension is deliberate and is the main point a referee could reject. The underlying question still preserves the authors’ matching objective, fixed offers, acceptance uncertainty, and comparison with the best matching under actual acceptances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the only anchor, and it is a real algorithmic result: Max guarantees \(v(M)\ge \tfrac12 v(M^\ast)\). I would not argue that the underlying people must be unique. A large internship cohort with standardized applications, rankings, and jury values is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; topic capacities can scale with the cohort.

My strongest objection is that the proposed mirror has to hide part of the society that ChoCo’s central model treats as part of its type distribution. A complete type must include the outside-option tier, since that determines which offers the student accepts. If the masses of those complete types are known, the hidden acceptance profile is known in aggregate, and the paper’s worst-case information problem has changed. If those masses are hidden, the input is instead public-type masses plus an ambiguity set over latent populations. That can be a coherent robust matching problem, but it is not the stated model of a supplied distribution over complete types.

The stronger repair—finite public cohorts, latent acceptance tiers, offers to whole residual cohorts, and exact aggregate response masses—makes the issue precise. It also shows why this is a scope objection rather than a claim that the continuous question has an easy or unchanged answer: the repair’s central object is a partially observed population and an adaptive policy over aggregate feedback. Whether that counts as a ChoCo mirror depends on broadening the programme’s input model to include hidden population distributions. The paper’s key uncertainty then survives, but as a separate modeling layer.

So I would vote against the mirror under ChoCo’s stated central model, while calling the negative case weak against the universal claim. The internship setting and the aggregate-feedback repair are plausible; if ChoCo admits ambiguity sets over complete type distributions, Proposition 2 has a worthwhile continuous mirror.
