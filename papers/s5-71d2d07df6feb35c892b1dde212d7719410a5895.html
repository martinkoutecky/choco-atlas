The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based computational case from this paper: it has no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorem 2.3, proved here (with its representation result also obtained in earlier work), characterizes when a preorder over lotteries on countable \(Z\) has an expected multi-utility representation and gives uniqueness. Theorem 2.4, also proved here, gives an uncountable-\(Z\) counterexample. Those are representation results, not complexity results. Treating either as a computational anchor would misstate the paper.

There is a plausible population connection in the motivation: the paper explicitly describes a decision maker composed of several agents and uses Pareto dominance in policy settings as an example of incomplete preference. One could model many such agents by utility types \(t\), with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), and define \(p\succsim_\mu q\) when \(\mathbf{E}_p[u_t]\ge\mathbf{E}_q[u_t]\) for every type of positive mass. In a large, repeatable population with few shared utility types, this is recognizable as the paper’s multi-agent motivation. But the masses’ magnitudes do not affect unanimity; only the support matters. And the paper studies continuity over lotteries, not a continuous population.

So this is a possible population restatement of one motivation, not a continuous computational mirror of a named result. A comparison task for that model would be easy to invent, but it would not be anchored in a computational problem the paper studies. I therefore can’t honestly state one prover-ready continuous problem per anchor or predict a complexity class. The case’s weakest point is decisive: the paper supplies no computational result for the continuization programme to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive issue is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2.3 characterizes when an abstract preorder over lotteries has an expected multi-utility representation and establishes uniqueness; Theorem 2.4 gives a counterexample for uncountable outcome sets. The other results likewise concern representations, continuity, and cones. The paper specifies no finite input encoding, computational task, algorithm, or complexity bound. Its central input can be an arbitrary preference relation on lotteries over a countable or uncountable outcome set. Complexity in parameters such as \(m,\tau,L\) cannot be asked without adding a new model of the input.

The strongest population scenario is the paper’s own example of a decision maker composed of several agents, such as stakeholders in public policy. Let each agent’s utility function be their type \(t\), with population distribution \(\mu\), and define \(p\succsim_\mu q\) when \(\mathbf{E}_p[u_t]\ge\mathbf{E}_q[u_t]\) for every type present. That is a sensible high multiplicity interpretation of Pareto dominance. But for a finite type set, the masses \(\mu_t\) cancel: the relation depends only on which types are present. A rare type vetoes a comparison just as a common type does. For diffuse populations, requiring the inequality for every positive-mass type fails because individual types may have mass zero; requiring it almost everywhere is a reasonable repair, though the result still depends on which types have positive measure, not on their population shares in the programme’s usual sense.

Weighting utilities by population share would make mass matter, but it changes the object. It replaces unanimity with an aggregate utility criterion and generally removes the incompleteness that motivates this paper. Majority, threshold, or quantile comparisons would likewise introduce new social choice rules. None gives a population version of the paper’s representation theorems.

Even the strongest repaired scenario therefore offers a population interpretation of the motivation, not a computational mirror of a result. One could add a campaigning or intervention problem over utility types, but that would supply the computational task the paper itself never studies. The proponent’s concession that there is no computational anchor is the strongest part of their case; the weakness in my negative case is that a natural stakeholder population can be imagined. Its type masses simply do not give the paper’s theorems a computational question to answer.
