Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 6 — survived: The repeated-type mass model preserves the theorem’s additive utilities, complete interaction, and socially beneficial gift moves.
  • Theorem 7 — survived: The opponent accepts the continuous three-type deadlock: every positive-mass bilateral move lowers the essential minimum, while a three-way reallocation reaches an optimum.

The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many agent types \(T\) with additive utilities \(u_t(r)\), rational type masses, an initial rational mass distribution over integral bundles, and conserved per-capita supplies of resource classes \(R\), can one compute a utilitarian-optimal final bundle distribution and a finite sequence of equal-mass one-copy gifts reaching it? Each gift moves resource class \(r\) from type \(t\) to type \(s\) only when \(u_s(r)>u_t(r)\), and interaction is complete.

The model it lives in

Types carry fixed additive utility vectors; \(x_{t,B}\) records the mass of type \(t\) holding integral bundle \(B\), and gift variables move equal mass between donor and recipient bundle states while conserving each resource supply. Maximize \(\sum_{t,B} x_{t,B}u_t(B)\), with each move requiring \(u_s(r)>u_t(r)\).

The objection that survived

The proposed problem omits the paper’s decentralized initiator and offer behavior, so it mirrors Theorem 6’s gift-reachability guarantee rather than the full agent mechanism.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 6’s complete-graph utilitarian gift guarantee and Theorem 7’s egalitarian bilateral deadlock; they leave the simulations, restricted-graph results, and Nash and elitist settings aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit-complexity and output-size bounds suffice to construct an optimal integral-bundle mass distribution and a finite acceptable gift sequence from rational input?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its strongest named computational results are about what negotiation processes can achieve. Those are still good anchors for mirrors about continuous-mass reallocation and reachability.

I would make Theorem 6 the lead. Proved in this paper, it states: “Within an utilitarian society where agents express their preferences by means of additive utility functions, negotiation processes based on complete social graphs always converge towards a global optimum using only social \(\langle 1,0\rangle\) transactions.” In full: with additive utilities, if every pair of agents can interact, then successive socially acceptable gifts of one resource, with no return gift, suffice for negotiation to reach a globally utilitarian-optimal allocation.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large network of public-service branches reallocating standardized, indivisible equipment. Branches share a finite set of preference profiles, and a central dispatch network lets any branch arrange a transfer to any other. Here a type is a branch’s complete fixed profile for the problem: its utility for each equipment class and its negotiation rules. Mass is the fraction of branches of each type. The number of branches \(n\) is much larger than the support size \(\tau\); the theorem 7 example below uses just three cohorts. Equipment copies remain indivisible for each branch, while a fraction of a cohort can receive or give a copy.

Here is the lead mirror, Continuous Utilitarian Gift Optimization. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), resource classes \(R\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and additive utilities \(u_t(r)\). It also gives an initial distribution \(\lambda_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{R}\): \(\lambda_{t,B}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents who initially hold \(B\). The per-capita supply of class \(r\) is \(q_r=\sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}B_r\). A legal move pairs equal mass of donors and recipients and transfers one indivisible copy of \(r\) as a gift. It is socially acceptable when the recipient values that copy more than the donor, \(u_s(r)>u_t(r)\). The question is to compute a feasible final bundle distribution maximizing utilitarian welfare, together with a finite sequence of such mass gifts that reaches it. Every agent still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of agents in each bundle state is fractional.

I expect this problem to be Class A. Its welfare-maximizing assignment has the LP \(\max\sum_{t,r}u_t(r)y_{t,r}\), subject to \(\sum_t y_{t,r}=q_r\) and \(y_{t,r}\ge 0\). With no bundle-size restrictions, each resource class can be assigned to a type that values it most, so the optimum is \(\sum_r q_r\max_t u_t(r)\). Such assignments can be realized by dividing cohort mass among integral bundles. The complete interaction assumption and the one-good gift rule preserve the core of Theorem 6; the result’s sorting argument also suggests a finite sequence of bulk mass gifts. The finite-agent theorem already supplies evidence for this direction, though it does not analyze the continuous problem’s input size or running time.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here: “Within an egalitarian society where agents express their preferences by means of additive utility functions, bilateral transactions cannot guarantee the achievement of an egalitarian optimum, independently of the social graph considered.” In full, the paper exhibits an instance where even a complete contact graph and socially acceptable bilateral trades cannot reach an egalitarian-optimal allocation. It proves this using three agents, three resources, and a deadlock among gifts and swaps.

That counterexample gives a precise second mirror, Continuous Bilateral Egalitarian Reachability. Use the same instance format, with egalitarian welfare defined as the essential infimum of individual utility across the population. A bilateral mass transaction matches equal mass of agents and applies the same trade of integral bundles to each pair; acceptability requires that the transaction not decrease the population’s egalitarian welfare. Given an initial state and complete contact, determine whether an egalitarian-optimal allocation is reachable by a finite sequence of acceptable bilateral mass transactions. A “yes” solution is a feasible transaction path to an optimum; a “no” answer needs an instance where no such path exists.

For that instance, take three types of mass \(1/3\), three resource classes with supply \(1/3\) each, and utilities
\[ u_1=(2,1,5),\qquad u_2=(5,2,1),\qquad u_3=(1,5,2). \]
Initially, type \(t\) holds one copy of resource \(r_t\), so everyone has utility \(2\). Any positive-mass gift leaves some donors with utility \(0\); any pairwise swap leaves some participants with utility \(1\). Either move lowers the essential minimum. Yet a three-way cycle—give \(r_3\) to type 1, \(r_1\) to type 2, and \(r_2\) to type 3—gives everyone utility \(5\), which is optimal. Thus bilateral trades cannot reach the optimum even in the continuum. I expect this obstruction to survive as a B-like discrete obstruction, not as a complexity-theoretic hardness result: the finite counterexample embeds by replicating its three cohorts. A further question is how allowing transactions among three or more cohorts changes reachability, and whether broader classes of instances admit bilateral paths to a max-min optimum.

Both mirrors use the same plausible regime: many public-service units with repeated preference and starting cohorts, finite standardized resource classes, and a shared dispatch network. The starting allocation is part of the instance; after transfers, the model tracks each cohort’s distribution over current integral bundles. This keeps type-level averages fractional without giving any individual a fractional item.

My weakest point is that the paper emphasizes unique, tagged resources and restricted social graphs. Repeated resource classes are a co-modification needed to keep supply meaningful as the population grows, and the lead theorem specifically assumes complete interaction. The egalitarian mirror also depends on the essential minimum: even a small positive mass of agents whose utility falls can lower it. Those are real limits on scope, but they do not change the continuous object: the population is the continuum, while resource bundles remain integral. These mirrors cover Theorems 6 and 7 only; they do not claim to continuize the paper’s simulation results or its other welfare and graph settings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that neither anchor is a complexity result. Theorem 6 proves a convergence guarantee under complete contact, and Theorem 7 gives a counterexample to guaranteed bilateral reachability. That leaves a gap between the paper’s claims and a complexity landscape for continuous societies. But this is a limitation of the paper’s existing results, not a sound reason to reject every mirror.

For Theorem 6, the proposed mass model turns the endpoint problem into independent choices for each resource class \(r\): assign its supply \(q_r\) to a type maximizing \(u_t(r)\). The optimum is \(\sum_r q_r\max_t u_t(r)\), and a finite sequence of mass gifts can implement it. Computing the endpoint and producing such a sequence are straightforward for rational input. The strongest objection is that this strips away much of the paper’s focus: who initiates negotiations, how offers change, and how local interaction shapes the process. A richer model could preserve those dynamics, but then the complexity question would need its own precise formulation. Still, the endpoint version is a coherent high multiplicity problem, and its simplicity does not disqualify it under the programme’s rules.

Theorem 7’s example also survives the continuous translation. With three types of mass \(1/3\), the same utility vectors, and the same initial bundles, any positive mass gift lowers the essential minimum to \(0\), while any positive mass swap lowers it to \(1\). The three way reallocation gives everyone utility \(5\). So the example remains a valid obstruction in a nonatomic society; the minimum does not become vacuous because each affected group has positive mass. The negative case is that this establishes one no instance, not the complexity of deciding reachability across instances. But the proponent’s general reachability question is a plausible extension, and the fact that this particular answer follows from a small discrete example is not, by itself, a collision.

Nor do the proposed population and resource choices give me a sound universal objection. Repeated branch types and resource classes whose supplies scale with population are reasonable co modifications for this problem, and the complete contact assumption matches Theorem 6’s own setting. The paper’s emphasis on unique resources and restricted graphs makes those mirrors narrower than the paper’s broader motivation, but the programme expressly allows that kind of model adjustment.

So the honest negative case is weak. I can argue that Theorem 6’s simplest mirror may be too immediate to reveal much about the programme’s complexity landscape, and that Theorem 7 motivates a new decision problem rather than supplying a complexity result. I cannot make those objections defeat the better versions of both anchors. At least the utilitarian mass reallocation mirror remains well posed and computational; the egalitarian obstruction also has a faithful continuous instance. On the standard given here, the case against does not hold.
