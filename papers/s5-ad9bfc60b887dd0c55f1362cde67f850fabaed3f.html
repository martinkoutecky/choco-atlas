The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting in this paper’s own domain. In a large hospital network, many residents may rotate through duties and locations, with recurring endowment schedules and a small number \(\tau\) of distinct preference-and-endowment types among \(n\) residents, where \(n\gg\tau\). A type must include both a complete ranking over bundles and the resident’s endowment class. Its mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of residents of that type. Each resident still receives one whole bundle; only the aggregate allocation is fractional. That is a sensible continuization of multiple-type housing markets.

One precise candidate is Mass Pairwise-Efficient Reallocation. The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(\Theta\) of types, each with ranking \(R_\theta\) and endowed bundle \(e_\theta\). For each object type \(t\) and object class \(a\), let \(q_{t,a}\) be the mass endowed with \(a\). Find nonnegative rational masses \(x_{\theta,b}\), where \(x_{\theta,b}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) assigned bundle \(b\), satisfying
\[ \sum_b x_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta,\qquad \sum_{\theta,b:\,b^t=a}x_{\theta,b}=q_{t,a}. \]
Require individual rationality: \(x_{\theta,b}>0\) only if \(b\,R_\theta\,e_\theta\). Require pairwise efficiency: there are no types \(\theta,\phi\) and bundles \(b,d\) with \(x_{\theta,b}>0\), \(x_{\phi,d}>0\), \(d\succ_\theta b\), and \(b\succ_\phi d\). The task is to find such an allocation or report that none exists; it is a feasibility problem, not welfare maximization. A natural expectation is tractability via a mass version of bundle TTC, but whether that version is well-defined and consistent with finite replication is itself a question.

The nearest result in the paper is Theorem 3, proved here: for multiple-type housing markets with strict preferences, only bTTC satisfies individual rationality, group strategy-proofness (or strategy-proofness together with non-bossiness), and pairwise efficiency. That is a mechanism characterization, not a computational result. The other named results likewise concern characterizations or impossibilities, not complexity classes or algorithmic bounds. The introduction’s statement that Pareto improvement is computationally hard—even with additive preferences—is attributed to outside work and is not a numbered computational result of this paper.

The proposed allocation problem captures an efficiency criterion the authors study, but it drops strategy-proofness and does not capture Theorem 3’s uniqueness claim. Extending strategy-proofness to a continuum also needs care: for an atomless society, an individual has zero mass, so the deviation notion must say whether coalitions of positive mass may misreport. That is the weakest point of the positive case. The scenario and population model are convincing; the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. So this is a credible new question for ChoCo, but it is not an anchored continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mass problem is not a mirror of the result it invokes. Theorem 3 characterizes an entire mechanism: across preference profiles, individual rationality, group strategy-proofness, and pairwise efficiency uniquely identify bTTC. The proposed feasibility problem takes one population and asks for one allocation satisfying individual rationality and pairwise efficiency. It drops the incentive conditions and the uniqueness claim that make the theorem distinctive.

A stronger version would define a mass mechanism and ask whether those axioms characterize a mass analogue of bTTC. But an aggregate assignment \(x_{\theta,b}\) does not specify what happens when one agent changes their report: that agent has zero mass, so the aggregate input is unchanged. To retain individual strategy-proofness, the model needs a tagged-agent response in addition to the population distribution. Requiring instead that positive-mass coalitions cannot benefit changes the deviation concept. Either version may be worth studying, but the paper gives no computational result about it; it is a new axiomatic characterization.

The same gap applies to Theorem 1’s cTTC characterization and the paper’s impossibility results. They concern properties of mechanisms over domains, not the complexity of a computational problem. The computational hardness mentioned in the paper is attributed to outside work on Pareto improvement. A continuous search for such improvements could be a useful project, but it would mirror that cited result, not a result established here.

The obvious computational alternative is to aggregate the paper’s explicit TTC algorithms over repeated preference and endowment types and study their running time in the support size. That is more faithful to the paper’s model, but it is a new runtime question about a stated algorithm, not a mirror of its main theorems. The paper identifies no unresolved computational bottleneck for those procedures.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible: a large hospital network with recurring schedules and repeated preference-and-endowment types is a sensible regime. That makes the model admissible, but does not supply the missing computational anchor. The negative case is strongest as an argument about fit with ChoCo’s stated programme. It does not establish that no useful continuous problem could be built from this setting; that universal claim is too strong to support honestly.
