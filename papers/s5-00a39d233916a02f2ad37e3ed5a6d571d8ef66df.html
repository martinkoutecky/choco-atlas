The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a population size \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral, \(k\) goods, bundle size bound \(d\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), compute a randomized allocation for the \(N\) named agents. A type \(t\) specifies a valuation \(v_t(B)\in[0,1]\) for each bundle \(B\) of size at most \(d\); the type-level allocation \(x_{tB}\) records the fraction of its mass assigned bundle \(B\). Require \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-joint differential privacy when one named agent changes type, and seek high welfare with small total supply violation under supplies \(Nq_j\).

The model it lives in

Use types \(t\in T\), masses \(\mu_t\), and type-level bundle fractions \(x_{tB}\), maximizing per-capita welfare \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_B v_t(B)x_{tB}\) subject to \(\sum_B x_{tB}\leq 1\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{B\ni j}x_{tB}\leq q_j\). For privacy, realize the mass allocation as outputs to \(N\) named agents and impose the paper’s single-agent adjacency definition of joint differential privacy.

The objection that survived

A purely atomless version has no nontrivial named-agent joint-privacy guarantee; the mirror therefore needs finite-\(N\) realizations, with privacy stated for individual type changes.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the \(d\)-demand allocation guarantee, expressed using type masses and per-capita supplies; it does not cover the general convex-program framework, the truthfulness extension, or the exact-feasibility variants.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the welfare and joint-privacy guarantees be implemented from the type-mass encoding in time polynomial in \(k,d,|T|\), and the encoded input length, with only logarithmic dependence on \(N\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s \(d\)-demand allocation result. The supplied text prints it as an unnumbered Corollary under “The \(d\)-Demand Allocation Problem”; no theorem number is visible to quote. It is proved here, by applying the paper’s general solver result. There is no NP-hardness result to anchor on: this is an algorithmic, approximation-and-privacy anchor.

Lead question — Jointly Private \(d\)-Demand Allocation over a Continuous Population. Let \(G\) be \(k\) goods and \(\mathcal B_d=\{B\subseteq G:|B|\le d\}\). An instance gives a finite support \(T\) of complete agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). A type specifies an agent’s valuation \(v_t:\mathcal B_d\to[0,1]\); agents of that type share the same valuation and feasible choices. Let \(q_j\) be the per-capita supply of good \(j\).

Choose \(x_{tB}\in[0,1]\), the fraction of type \(t\)’s mass assigned bundle \(B\), subject to \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_d}x_{tB}\le 1\). Each unit of mass gets one integral bundle; fractional \(x_{tB}\) records how the type’s mass is divided among bundles. Maximize per-capita welfare
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_d}v_t(B)x_{tB} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{B\ni j}x_{tB}\le q_j \quad\text{for every }j\in G. \]
For a population of size \(N\), take \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and total supply \(Nq_j\). Require the mechanism assigning bundles to those agents to be \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-jointly differentially private when one named agent changes type. The problem is to return a randomized allocation with high welfare and small total supply violation. A compact type-contingent allocation rule can describe the mass allocation; the finite-\(N\) realization supplies the individual outputs needed to define joint privacy.

The paper’s anchor says that for the discrete \(d\)-demand market with \(n\) agents, \(k\) goods, and general valuations in \([0,1]\), \(\PDD\) returns, with probability at least \(1-\beta\), a fractional allocation whose total supply violation is at most
\[ \alpha=O\!\left(\frac{kd\sqrt{\log(nd/\delta)}}{\epsilon}\log\frac{ndk}{\beta}\right), \]
and whose welfare is at least \(\OPT-\alpha\). The paper instantiates its parameters as gradient sensitivity \(\sqrt{2}d\), dual bound \(1\), and width \(nd\); its privacy theorems give the joint differential privacy guarantee. It also notes that this allocation problem can be implemented efficiently given a demand oracle returning a bundle maximizing valuation minus item prices. The mirror preserves the underlying welfare-allocation question and the individual bundle choices, while aggregating agents with identical complete types.

I expect the aggregate welfare problem to be tractable: with explicit bundle values it is a linear program over type–bundle variables; with a demand oracle, pricing is the relevant algorithmic subproblem. The paper’s guarantee gives the continuous regime a concrete motivation: when \(N\) grows, the displayed additive loss and violation, divided by \(N\), vanish up to logarithmic factors. For supplies proportional to \(N\), this is a vanishing per-capita error. The relevant parameters are \(k,d,\tau\), valuation input size or oracle cost, bit length \(L\), \(N\), and the privacy parameters.

There is a real qualification. The corollary is a finite-population result, and the algorithm uses a number of rounds polynomial in its width, which here grows with \(n\). It therefore supports a polynomial-in-\(N\) mechanism, but does not establish an algorithm polynomial in the compressed description length and \(\log N\). A useful follow-up is whether type aggregation can retain the privacy and welfare guarantees with runtime governed by \(\tau\) and the encoded masses, apart from the cost of issuing individual outputs.

The weakest point is that the paper already formulates fractional allocations over agents, so grouping identical agents is a natural high-multiplicity compression rather than a new optimization principle. Also, standard joint privacy is defined for named individuals, so the atomless model needs the finite-\(N\) interpretation above. Still, the mirror looks plausible to the authors: it keeps their general-valuation welfare objective, bundle decisions, and shared supply constraints, while making the population distribution itself the input object. It covers the \(d\)-demand allocation result, not the paper’s full convex-program framework, its truthfulness extension, or its exact-feasibility variants.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The \(d\)-demand corollary is the strongest anchor, and its natural market scenario is hard to dismiss. The best objection is that the paper’s central guarantee is about a *named-agent mechanism*: neighboring inputs differ in one person’s report, outputs are assigned to named people, and joint privacy protects each person from the coalition of everyone else. A mass allocation \(x_{tB}\) specifies none of that. The same type distribution \(\mu\) can describe populations of size \(N\) and \(rN\); changing one person shifts a type mass by \(1/N\) in one case and \(1/(rN)\) in the other, and the output spaces differ. A type-level allocation plan alone also does not ensure privacy: a quota-based realization, for example, could let the other recipients infer who changed type.

In an atomless limit, changing one person has zero mass, so the privacy condition becomes vacuous at the aggregate level. Per-capita welfare loss and supply violation also vanish under the corollary’s bounds as population and supply grow. To retain the paper’s meaningful guarantee, the better mirror must include \(N\), named recipients, single-agent adjacency, and a randomized finite allocation. That repairs the formulation, but makes it a high-multiplicity encoding of a finite mechanism problem, rather than a self-contained continuous-population privacy problem. The proposed type-mass LP still captures aggregate welfare and supply; it does not capture the result’s privacy semantics.

This is a real weakness in the mirror, but it may not defeat it under ChoCo’s stated scope. The programme treats high-multiplicity formulations as evidence in favour, and a type-compressed JDP mechanism with explicit dependence on support size and \(N\) is a sensible computational question. The paper’s polynomial-in-\(n\) algorithm does not settle whether a compressed implementation helps. So I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the privacy objection rules out a *purely atomless* version, while the finite-\(N\) version remains a plausible mirror.
