The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a natural high-multiplicity setting, but it contains no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror.

A plausible regime is a large school-funding referendum. A voter type would be \((x,k)\): their preference parameter and information service. The mass \(\mu(dx,k)\) is the fraction of the electorate with that type; each voter then receives a private signal and votes \(Q\) or \(A\). A large electorate with relatively few preference and service groups is plausible, though the paper’s preference parameter is continuous, so representing it with finite support would require an extra modeling choice. The paper’s question is strategic equilibrium, not optimization against a central objective.

The closest named results are proved here, but they are analytical. Proposition 1 establishes existence of a symmetric equilibrium and characterizes equilibria by ordered cutpoints satisfying pivotal indifference. Theorems 1–3 establish asymptotic claims about informative action, vote shares, and full-information equivalence; Theorem 4 studies how uncertainty about the preference distribution changes those claims. None gives a running time, complexity classification, or algorithm for a computational problem.

One could propose computing equilibrium cutpoints from a distribution of voter types, but that would be a new algorithmic follow-up, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. There is also a modeling wrinkle: in an ordinary atomless electorate, one voter cannot affect the result, so the pivotal-voting incentives need to be defined through a finite-electorate limit. The paper already studies that limit analytically. I would therefore not name a lead mirror problem or claim a positive ChoCo case from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no continuous mirror to defeat: they expressly identify none. That is the strongest case against this paper as a ChoCo anchor. Proposition 1 is an equilibrium existence and characterization result; Theorems 1–4 describe equilibrium behavior and information aggregation as the electorate grows. None states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm whose continuous counterpart could be classified.

The best repaired proposal is to encode a society by masses over preference and information-service types \((x,k)\), then ask for an algorithm to compute equilibrium cutpoints—or to determine how large \(n\) must be for full-information equivalence to hold within a chosen error. Those are coherent questions, and the type groups can make high multiplicity quite plausible. But they add a computational task the paper does not study. With finite-support rational inputs, one also changes its smooth, full-support preference model; with a continuous distribution, one must specify how that distribution is represented computationally. Either way, this is a follow-up agenda, not a mirror of one of the paper’s results.

The pivotal-voting feature makes the translation less direct, but I would not rest the case on that: an atomless formulation loses an individual voter’s chance of being pivotal, while a finite-electorate sequence can preserve it. Even granting that repair, the missing computational anchor remains.

The honest limit is that this cannot show equilibrium computation would never be worthwhile. It can show that this article supplies no computational result to continuize, so a case for such work would need to stand as a new research proposal rather than a mirror of this paper.
