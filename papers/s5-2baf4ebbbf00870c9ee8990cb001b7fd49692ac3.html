What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns payment choice and cutoff implementation; it does not make Theorems 4–7 computational results or analyze a population-size input.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make the required *anchored* case: the paper has no named computational result about complexity or algorithms. Theorems 4 and 6 characterize equilibria; Theorems 5 and 7 give expected-cost comparisons for specified mechanisms. They are proved in this paper, but none establishes a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized-tractable.

The closest possible mirror is a payment-design problem for a crowdsourcing platform with many workers doing repeated, standardized tasks. Let a worker’s type be their normalized quality \(x\in[0,1]\), with common effort cost \(C\), and let \(\mu\) give the fraction of workers of each type. For a target cutoff \(x^\star\), choose agreement and disagreement payments \((\tau_a,\tau_d)\) to minimize expected payment per participating report, subject to a Bayes–Nash equilibrium in which workers with \(x\ge x^\star\) participate, exert effort, and report truthfully, while lower-quality workers pass. The likely direction is tractability for finite rational-support \(\mu\) and fixed \(x^\star\): the active population’s mean quality is computable from \(\mu\), and the incentive constraints are linear in the two payment variables.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, but it is not an honest anchor for the requested case. Theorem 7, the nearest result, proves a cost comparison for the paper’s uniform quality distribution; it does not give a complexity result for the mass-distribution problem above. And because the paper already integrates over a continuous quality distribution, the proposed mirror risks restating part of its existing model. That is the positive case’s weakest point.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed payment-design question is the only plausible anchor, and it does not give this paper a computational result to mirror. Theorems 4 and 6 characterize equilibria; Theorems 5 and 7 compare expected costs. For the paper’s uniform quality distribution, the authors already compute the expected payment and optimize the two payment parameters for a fixed cutoff. They also use the quality distribution directly to calculate agents’ expected payoffs. Recasting that distribution as population mass largely restates the model the paper already analyzes.

Replacing the uniform prior with a finite rational-support distribution would be a legitimate extension, but it is not a discrete-to-continuous complexity question from this paper. The number of agents never appears as an input to the results. Under the paper’s common effort cost and quality ordering, a proposed active set is an upper quality cutoff; for each candidate cutoff, its mean quality and incentive constraints can be computed from the support, then payment design is a two-variable optimization. That could make a useful generalization, but its computational content comes from broadening the prior, not from continuizing a population problem the paper studies.

A richer model with types that also vary in effort cost or task-specific skill might create a more substantial screening problem. But that changes the paper’s primitives and asks a new mechanism-design question. It is not a stronger mirror of one of its named results.

I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario: a platform with many workers doing repeated, standardized judgments could have meaningful masses of workers in quality or cost categories. That makes the universal negative case weak. But it does not rescue the proposed anchor: this paper establishes no computational complexity result, and its nearest design problem is already solved for its stated continuous prior. The paper is a poor anchor for ChoCo’s computational continuization programme.
