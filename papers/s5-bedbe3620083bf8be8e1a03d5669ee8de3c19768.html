The strongest positive direction is a mirror of the paper’s underlying fair influence maximization objective, rather than of a numbered result. A plausible regime is a large regional health-information campaign: many residents fall into a modest number of repeated protected-group and contact-community types, with similar transmission patterns within each type. Represent type \(t\) by mass \(\mu_t\), and choose seed masses \(x_t\), with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the campaign’s per-capita seed budget. A type-level contact kernel specifies how influence spreads between types. The output is a seed-mass vector maximizing the paper’s \(F=\lambda MF-(1-\lambda)DCV\), with group influence measured as the final activated mass and the diversity benchmark scaled to each group’s mass.

That is a plausible continuous-population question because it preserves the paper’s seed-allocation decision, IC-style diffusion, and fairness objective while allowing fractional mass at the type level. Its weakest point is substantial: it replaces the paper’s individual graph with a type-level contact model, and the paper’s community detection and node-ranking algorithm do not carry over directly. This candidate therefore covers the FIM formulation, not CEA-FIM’s algorithmic contribution, and it cannot serve as the requested theorem-anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offers no named theorem, lemma, or corollary to anchor a mirror. CEA-FIM’s experimental comparisons and Appendix A.1’s running-time analysis are the paper’s algorithmic contributions, but neither is a numbered complexity result of the kind requested. So the case for this paper has to rest on the broader FIM optimization problem.

That broader mirror is plausible, but its proposed types—protected group and community, with a contact kernel between types—do not retain the paper’s input in general. Under the IC model, a seed’s value depends on which paths it reaches and how those paths overlap. Two networks can have the same group masses and type-to-type contact rates while giving different influence spreads to the same seed allocation. To preserve that information, types would need to encode much of each node’s neighborhood and position in the graph. In realistic networks that can leave nearly one type per node, removing the high-multiplicity gain. A coarse kernel instead defines a block-model variant of FIM; it may be worth studying, but it no longer mirrors the paper’s graph-based problem or its community-aware search.

The better version is to make the contact model part of the continuous instance and choose seed mass by type. Even then, the limiting regime is delicate. With dense contacts and a positive seed fraction, cascades can saturate groups and make the fairness objective insensitive to the precise allocation. With sparse contacts and a fixed number of seeds, the normalized influence fractions used by \(MF\) and \(DCV\) can vanish. A nontrivial limit may require choosing a particular scaling of contacts, seed budget, and objective. That could yield a useful model, but the paper gives no reason to privilege one such scaling as the continuous form of its problem.

This is a serious reason to doubt that the proposed type-and-kernel formulation is a worthwhile mirror of *this paper*. It is not a universal defeat: a repeated-cohort network or a carefully specified block model could support a sensible continuous FIM question, and the paper’s group-fair objective would carry over naturally. The negative case is therefore strongest against the paper as an anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme, and weak against the claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile mirror.
