The case FOR (proponent)

I’d make one narrow case: Continuous Thiele-Addition Exclusion Threshold, for the single-representative case \(\ell=1\). It is a credible population mirror, though the paper already develops it in essentially continuous form.

My anchor is Theorem 7.8, proved in this paper. For Thiele’s addition method, it states that, for every \(S\), the thresholds for tactic, same, PJR, and EJR with \(\ell=1\) are all
\[ \pi^{\mathrm{Th-add}}_{\mathrm{tactic}}(1,S) =\pi^{\mathrm{Th-add}}_{\mathrm{same}}(1,S) =\pi^{\mathrm{Th-add}}_{\mathrm{PJR}}(1,S) =\pi^{\mathrm{Th-add}}_{\mathrm{EJR}}(1,S) =\frac{1}{1+\alpha_S}. \]
Here \(\alpha_S\) is the minimum total vote weight needed to elect \(S\) candidates, each with score at least \(1\), under Thiele’s addition method. The paper defines \(\alpha_S\) through the linear program in (7.32), and computes small cases from it. I find no named result in the paper classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or parameterized-hard; Theorem 7.8 is the closest computational anchor, and qualifies through the prompt’s LP-formulation exception.

The continuous problem is: given \(S\), find the least adversarial mass \(\alpha_S\) that can keep a cohesive target candidate out of the elected set, when the target coalition has mass \(1\). The candidate set can be \(a\) plus \(S\) rivals \(B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_S\}\). A type is an approval ballot, a subset of those candidates. Put coalition mass \(1\) on \(\{a\}\); choose nonnegative rival masses \(x_\sigma\) for each nonempty \(\sigma\subseteq B\). At round \(r\), a ballot \(\sigma\) contributes \(x_\sigma/(1+|\sigma\cap\{b_1,\ldots,b_{r-1}\}|)\) to each candidate it approves. Minimize \(\sum_{\sigma\ne\varnothing}x_\sigma\), subject to the rivals being elected in sequence, with ties resolved to allow that outcome. The answer to this mass-allocation problem is \(\alpha_S\); the corresponding largest target share that can still lose is \(1/(1+\alpha_S)\). The theorem shows this reduced construction captures the threshold for all four named scenarios, rather than only a convenient special case.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization electing an \(S\)-person board by approval ballot. Members who submit the same approval set are one type; mass is their share of membership. The total membership \(n\) may be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots, especially when candidates and ballot templates are fixed. Every unit of mass still receives an integral ballot, and the \(S\) winners remain discrete: only the population is continuous. The authors would recognize the question: it is exactly their single-representative exclusion threshold expressed in ballot-type masses.

My forecast is Class A for an explicitly represented type set, since the threshold has an LP formulation. The displayed LP has up to \(2^S-1\) ballot variables, however, and the paper does not provide a pricing algorithm that makes it polynomial in \(S\) or in the support size of an optimal society. The promising next question is whether the LP admits efficient separation or a sparse optimum. The order-based score coefficients depend on how many approved candidates occur in each prefix, which suggests a dynamic-programming pricing approach, but that still needs proof. I would not claim a tractability theorem from this paper.

The scope is just Thiele’s addition method at \(\ell=1\), across the four scenarios in Theorem 7.8. It does not mirror the paper’s other methods or its higher-\(\ell\) results. Further questions include whether \(\alpha_S\) has a general formula or asymptotic form, and how small an optimal ballot support can be.

The weakest point is also decisive for novelty: this is already a continuous-population result in the paper’s own treatment. Section 7.4 explicitly allows positive real vote weights, and Remarks 2.2–2.3 explain the weighted and homogeneous formulations. So this is a strong example of a mirror the authors would accept, but a poor claim that continuous computational social choice for this problem remains to be done. Theorem 7.8 has already answered the central threshold question through an LP.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 7.8 is not a computational result for a problem that still needs continuizing. It identifies the \(\ell=1\) thresholds with \(1/(1+\alpha_S)\), where \(\alpha_S\) is already defined by an LP over ballot weights. Nothing in the theorem classifies that LP’s complexity or gives an algorithm for solving it. Calling it a computational anchor because it has an LP formulation stretches the programme’s scope: the programme asks for complexity and algorithms, not just an optimization characterization.

More seriously, the proposed mirror is already in the paper’s model. Remarks 2.2–2.3 allow weighted ballots with real vote totals, and §7.4 explicitly uses arbitrary positive real vote weights to define \(\alpha_S\). Its variables \(x_\sigma\) are masses of approval-ballot types; normalizing them gives the proposed continuous society directly. Theorem 7.8 then relates that mass optimization to the exclusion threshold. This is a genuine continuous-population treatment in the paper itself, so restating it as a population distribution would be a collision, not a new mirror.

The best strengthened version would ask for an efficient algorithm, a sparse optimum, or a pricing method for \(\alpha_S\). That is a sensible research question, and the authors might recognize it: the paper explicitly poses open problems about a formula and asymptotics for \(\alpha_S\). But it is a new algorithmic attack on an already continuous LP, not a continuization of a discrete result. Its natural input is \(S\), with the possible ballot types chosen by the optimization; there is no supplied society with support size \(\tau\) whose intervention or outcome the algorithm computes. Restricting the adversary to a supplied support, or adding costs and a baseline society, could create such an input, but those changes would define a different problem rather than mirror Theorem 7.8.

The membership-organization scenario is plausible, and mass is a sensible way to represent repeated approval ballots. That makes the model admissible; it does not make it new. Nor does the proposed Class A forecast follow from the theorem: its LP has up to \(2^S-1\) ballot variables, and the paper gives no pricing algorithm.

So the case against this anchor is strong on scope and novelty, but weak as a claim that the underlying algorithmic question has no value. A complexity or pricing result for \(\alpha_S\) could be worthwhile in its own right. I cannot honestly rule that out; the narrower point is that it would advance a continuous problem the paper already formulated, rather than provide the continuous mirror the programme seeks.
