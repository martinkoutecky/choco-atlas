What the mirror covers

Corollary 3 and Theorems 1–9 give performance bounds and convergence rates for fixed relay schemes; none yields an anchored computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The sensor-network setting has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large deployment may contain many leaf sensors from only a few hardware or measurement-channel classes, so \(N\gg\tau\). A type could be a sensor’s pair of likelihood distributions under \(H_0\) and \(H_1\), and \(\mu_t\) its population fraction. The paper’s identical-leaf assumption is the special case \(\tau=1\).

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its closest result is Corollary 3, established here: for an \(M\)-ary relay tree using majority dominance, with \(\lambda_M=\lfloor(M+1)/2\rfloor\), the total error satisfies \(\log_2 P_N^{-1}=\Theta(N^{\log_M\lambda_M})\). That is a convergence-rate theorem, not a result that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

An unanchored extension could ask: given rational sensor-channel types and masses, a prior, and an \(M\)-ary tree, find level-wise fusion rules minimizing the root’s Bayes error, returning the rules and the optimum value. This is recognizable as hierarchical fusion design. Yet it adds an optimization question the paper does not answer; moreover, with heterogeneous sensors, the type masses alone may not determine root error unless their placement among subtrees is specified.

So the honest positive case is limited to plausibility of the population regime. I would not present that extension as a valid mirror anchored in this paper: there is no qualifying anchor, and the paper’s principal contribution is analytic convergence-rate analysis. The nonbinary-message results also concern message-alphabet size, an outcome-side change rather than a computational result about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own closest anchor, Corollary 3, is a convergence-rate theorem, not a computational result. It bounds the statistical error under a fixed relay architecture and fusion rule: \(\log_2 P_N^{-1}=\Theta(N^{\log_M\lambda_M})\). Theorem 1–9 likewise analyze error probabilities, compare fusion rules, or bound message sizes. None poses an algorithmic problem with encoded input and output whose complexity could be charted. Under the requested standard, there is no named computational result here to mirror.

A large sensor deployment can certainly have repeated channel types. But a distribution \(\mu\) over those types does not, by itself, describe this paper’s input: relay agents fuse exactly \(M\) messages at a time, and the arrangement of heterogeneous sensors among sibling subtrees can affect the root’s error. Include the complete tree and assignment, and the input retains one entry per leaf, so type masses have not replaced the population. Assume instead that channel types are drawn independently from \(\mu\), and each sensor knows its type; then \(\mu\) specifies an identical mixed observation channel at each leaf. The number of leaves and the tree height remain the quantities driving the paper’s theorem. A continuum over channels may parameterize that detection model, but it does not turn the paper’s asymptotic analysis into a computational question about a continuous society.

The proposed optimization extension—choose fusion rules to minimize root Bayes error—could be a legitimate neighboring research problem. Its best formulation would need to specify the tree, how channel types are placed or sampled, what rules agents may use, and what information they know. The paper fixes fusion rules and analyzes their performance; it does not study that optimization problem or establish its complexity. The nonbinary-message results do not supply a further anchor either: they vary message alphabets, not the population representation.

So the negative case is strong as a verdict on this paper as a computational mirror: every result offered as an anchor is analytical, and the more ambitious optimization proposal changes the question. It is weaker as the universal claim that no related continuous-sensor model could be worthwhile. Repeated sensor classes are plausible, and a carefully specified fusion-design problem might merit study on its own. The honest objection is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize—not that every continuous model inspired by it would be pointless.
