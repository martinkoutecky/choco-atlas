The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a rational positional scoring vector \(s\), a polynomial-time backup tie-break \(f\), and a society \(\mu\) supported on at most \(\tau\) complete rankings with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), compute the winner selected by the MFP refinement of the score rule. Determine whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings; its score winners are \(W_s(\mu)=\arg\max_c\sum_R\mu_R s(\mathrm{rank}_R(c))\). Let \(H_\mu=\{\sigma\in S_m:\sigma(\mu)=\mu\}\) and \(F_\mu=\{c:\sigma(c)=c\text{ for every }\sigma\in H_\mu\}\). If \(W_s(\mu)\cap F_\mu=\varnothing\), return \(f(\mu,W_s(\mu))\); otherwise choose a relabeling \(\rho^*\) that makes the permuted mass histogram lexicographically greatest and return the highest-priority candidate in \((\rho^*)^{-1}(W_s(\mu)\cap F_\mu)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the full-ranking, single-winner scoring-rule slice of Theorem 4. It does not claim to extend Theorem 1's finite-population classification or cover the paper's other preference and decision spaces.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the MFP winner be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) for binary-rational masses, without expanding the society into an electorate of size equal to a common denominator?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s full-ranking, single-winner tie-breaking algorithm. The paper has no named NP-hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor here.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in the paper. It says that for any polynomial-time computable backup tie-break \(f\), and any Common Setting with preferences \(E=L_m\) and decision space \(D=L_k\) or \(M_k\), Algorithm 1 computes the most-favorable-permutation tie-break \(MFP_f\) in time polynomial in \(m\), the number \(n\) of voters, and \(|D|\). The algorithm computes a most-equitable refinement: it chooses a co-winner that preserves anonymity, neutrality, and resolvability wherever that is possible, and uses \(f\) where it is not.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, Continuous MFP winner computation. There are \(m\) candidates, a rational positional scoring vector \(s\), a polynomial-time backup tie-break \(f\), and a society \(\mu\) given by at most \(\tau\) complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). For each candidate \(c\), let
\[ \mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(c)=\sum_R \mu_R\,s(\mathrm{rank}_R(c)), \]
and let \(W_s(\mu)\) be the set of score-maximizing candidates. The task is to return the winner selected by the MFP refinement.

Precisely, let \(H_\mu=\{\sigma\in S_m:\sigma(\mu)=\mu\}\) be the candidate relabelings that leave the society unchanged, and let \(F_\mu=\{c:\sigma(c)=c\text{ for every }\sigma\in H_\mu\}\). If \(W_s(\mu)\cap F_\mu\) is empty, return \(f(\mu,W_s(\mu))\): no neutral resolute refinement can select a score winner at this society. Otherwise, choose a relabeling \(\rho^*\) that makes the mass vector \((\mu_{\rho^{*-1}(R)})_R\) lexicographically greatest in a fixed ranking order. Return
\[ (\rho^*)^{-1}\!\left(\max_{\triangleright}\rho^*(W_s(\mu)\cap F_\mu)\right), \]
where \(\triangleright\) is a fixed priority over candidates. The MFP construction makes this choice independent of which maximizing \(\rho^*\) is used. Because the input is \(\mu\), anonymity is built into the representation; neutrality is the substantive constraint, and resolvability is the requirement to return one winner.

This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: for example, millions of voters ranking six candidates, with a few hundred distinct rankings among at most \(720\) possible types. Each type is a complete ranking, so its members are indistinguishable for the scoring rule and tie-break. Its mass is its fraction of the electorate. The outcome remains one discrete candidate; the population is the continuous object. Anonymity on labeled finite profiles is exactly dependence on the histogram, and \(\mu\) is the normalized histogram. The paper already makes histograms and their candidate-permutation symmetries central, so this is a close match to the authors’ problem.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length of the masses and scoring vector. Algorithm 1’s steps appear to admit a sparse weighted version: find stabilizing permutations by matching support rankings of equal mass, consider the at most \(\tau\) maximum-mass rankings when constructing an MFP, compare the resulting sparse mass vectors, and compute the score winners and fixed candidates. This avoids expanding a rational society into a list of voters. It is a plausible algorithmic extension of Theorem 4, not a result already proved there.

The weakest point is exactly that gap: Theorem 4 measures time against an explicit \(n\)-voter profile, whereas the continuous input uses binary-encoded masses. Its polynomial-time guarantee does not by itself imply a polynomial bit-time guarantee for this weighted representation. The sparse adaptation looks direct because the relevant operations compare rankings and mass coordinates, but it needs a proof.

This case covers the full-ranking, single-winner slice of Theorem 4. I would not claim that the paper’s Theorem 1 carries over unchanged: its finite-\(n\) partition and divisibility conditions use a common population size, while arbitrary rational masses change that arithmetic. The paper’s symmetry analysis supports the mirror, but the continuous feasibility statement needs its own treatment.

The mirror also raises follow-up questions: can the same sparse algorithm handle approval ballots or partial rankings without an \(m!\) search; how does its complexity depend on \(\tau\); and what replaces the finite-\(n\) impossibility classification when societies are restricted by support size?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a strong case that defeats the Theorem 4 anchor. It is a named polynomial-time result about computing a refinement, and full rankings with a small support among many voters are a natural high-multiplicity regime. The fact that the winner remains one discrete candidate is no objection: the population is what becomes continuous.

The strongest case against is that this mirror may offer little new mathematics. Every rational society \(\mu\) is a normalized finite histogram for some electorate size \(n\). Theorem 4 already handles those histograms, though its running time is polynomial in \(n\), not in the binary input length of the masses. For this particular task, a sparse weighted version appears direct: enumerate the at most \(\tau\) supported rankings tied for maximum mass to find the MFP; enumerate the at most \(\tau\) possible images of one supported ranking to find the society’s stabilizers; then compare rational masses and select among fixed-point winners. Those steps look polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

That makes the proposed mirror likely tractable and perhaps a modest bit-complexity extension of the paper’s algorithm. But under the programme’s criteria, that is not enough to reject it. The paper does not prove the binary-mass bound, and a succinct input for a huge electorate is exactly the high-multiplicity question the programme is meant to ask. Anonymity becoming automatic in the distribution representation also leaves a substantive neutrality and winner-selection problem.

So the negative case is weak: the best objection is about research priority, not whether a worthwhile mirror exists. The anchor survives.
