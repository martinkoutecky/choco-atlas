The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item kinds, \(\tau\) buyer types with normalized monotone super-additive valuations \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\ge0\), compute a welfare-maximizing market-clearing equilibrium. The allocation variables \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) must satisfy \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\); each positive-mass bundle \(S\) must maximize \(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\) at item prices \(p_j\ge0\), and unused supply must have price zero. What is the complexity in \(m,\tau\), input bit length, and valuation-oracle access?

The model it lives in

A replicated market with a unit-mass population of complete buyer types and rational per-capita supplies. Each infinitesimal buyer receives an integral bundle; \(x_{t,S}\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(S\). The configuration LP maximizes \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\) subject to type-mass and item-supply constraints, with item prices supporting demanded bundles and clearing supply.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the super-additive MC-CWE welfare objective behind Theorem 3.2, though the replica LP yields exact welfare optimality rather than transferring its \(O(\sqrt m)\) guarantee or local-search algorithm. It leaves the value-query result, the single-minded result, budget-additive results, and revenue claims untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • How can an exact mass equilibrium be rounded to finite replica markets with integral type counts and supplies while preserving market clearing and welfare guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Replicated Market-Clearing CWE Welfare. The anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper: Algorithm SuperAdditiveMC-CWE returns a market-clearing CWE whose social welfare is an \(O(\sqrt m)\)-approximation to the optimal welfare over all assignments, and the algorithm uses a polynomial number of demand queries. The supplied text has no numbered hardness theorem to anchor on; its hardness claims appear in prose or are attributed to earlier work.

Here is the mirror. There are \(m\) item kinds and a population of mass \(1\), with \(\tau\) buyer types \(t\). A type is a complete valuation function \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), normalized, monotone and super-additive; its population share is a binary rational \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Item copies remain indivisible, but stock scales with the population: item kind \(j\) has per-capita supply \(b_j\in(0,1]\), so a finite market of \(N\) buyers has \(Nb_j\) copies. The type masses and supplies are rational, and \(N\) can be chosen to make their counts integral.

A solution gives prices \(p_j\ge0\) and masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j. \]
Every positively assigned bundle must maximize its type’s utility at the prices:
\[ x_{t,S}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ S\in\arg\max_{R\subseteq M}\left(v_t(R)-\sum_{j\in R}p_j\right). \]
Any unused supply must have price zero. The objective is to maximize
\[ W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S). \]
This asks for an \(O(\sqrt m)\)-approximate market-clearing equilibrium; an exact welfare-maximizing equilibrium is an even stronger solution. A Walrasian equilibrium with singleton item bundles is a valid MC-CWE.

The types’ mass may be split among different bundles, but no individual receives a fractional item: \(x_{t,S}\) describes subgroups of identical buyers receiving different integral bundles. That is the high-multiplicity step. It keeps the paper’s valuations, prices, welfare objective, and equilibrium requirement intact while scaling inventory with population, as a market with many buyers requires.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable, and in fact exactly welfare-optimal. Its configuration LP has one variable \(x_{t,S}\) per type and integral bundle, type-mass equalities, and item-supply constraints. Its dual has a price \(p_j\) for each item and a utility bound \(\alpha_t\) for each type; the pricing question for type \(t\) is precisely to find a utility-maximizing bundle, the type’s demand query. Strong duality and complementary slackness give a welfare-maximizing mass assignment in which every assigned bundle is demanded and unused goods have zero price. Thus the continuous market has an exact equilibrium supported by the LP, even though the LP has exponentially many bundle variables. With value and demand oracles, the expected target is polynomially many oracle calls in \(m,\tau,L\); the same formulation is an ordinary LP for explicit valuation tables.

The scope is the welfare and equilibrium result in Theorem 3.2, not the paper’s revenue claims or its budget-additive results. I would not sell continuum-agent equilibrium existence as a novel discovery: the paper itself cites continuum-market work, which is relevant machinery. The computational question is the finite-support representation and the complexity of finding the welfare-maximizing equilibrium. Natural follow-ups are how the exact continuum solution rounds to a finite high-multiplicity market, how the guarantee changes when supplies do not scale cleanly, and whether analogous results hold with budget constraints or revenue objectives.

The weakest point is the supply scaling. The paper has \(m\) goods total; this mirror has \(Nb_j\) copies of each kind for \(N\) buyers. If the authors reject that co-modification, or insist on fixed inventory as the population grows, the mirror loses its positive-mass market interpretation. The individual goods and bundles are still indivisible, however, and the session’s scope explicitly permits per-capita supplies that scale with the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is that it changes the resource regime that Theorem 3.2 studies. The theorem gives a named computational result, so absence of an anchor is no objection. But its market has \(m\) indivisible objects whose supply stays fixed as the number \(n\) of buyers grows. If that inventory stays fixed in a continuum limit, at most \(m\) buyers receive anything; for bounded per-buyer values, welfare per capita is \(O(m/n)\), and the positive-mass allocation disappears. In that regime, the goods remain atoms that cannot be assigned to a positive-measure group.

The proponent’s stronger rescue scales inventory with population. That gives a coherent replica market, but its mass allocation is the configuration LP with type-mass constraints and per-capita supply constraints. Its dual has item prices and one utility bound per type; demand queries separate the dual constraints, and complementary slackness supports the resulting allocation as an exact equilibrium. The structure of this LP is generic: it does not depend on super-additivity or on the paper’s bundling algorithm. Continuum-market equilibrium is longstanding, and computational work also treats Walrasian equilibrium through aggregate demand and LP-based methods. [Aumann’s continuum-trader model](https://math.huji.ac.il/raumann/pdf/Markets%20with%20a%20Continuum%20of%20Traders.pdf), [Paes Leme and Wong on computing Walrasian equilibria](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-018-1334-9).

That makes the negative case a research-priority argument: the proposed mirror may be a routine application of the standard mass-allocation LP, rather than a new complexity landscape for the paper’s MC-CWE mechanism. But I cannot honestly turn that into a universal objection. The programme explicitly permits per-capita supply when population continuization requires it, and repeated buyers for replicated goods can be a sensible high-multiplicity market. Theorem 3.2 therefore has a plausible mirror; my case against it is weak.
