The continuous mirror question

Given \(L>0\) and a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over location types \(x\in[0,L]\), define \(\operatorname{ER}(y,\mu)=\max_{x\ne x'\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\frac{L-|y-x|}{L-|y-x'|}\), with value \(1\) for singleton support, and \(\operatorname{ER}^*(\mu)=\min_{y\in[0,L]}\operatorname{ER}(y,\mu)\). Does the rule \(F(\mu)=L/2\) give a deterministic mass-group-strategyproof mechanism with \(\sup_\mu \operatorname{ER}(F(\mu),\mu)/\operatorname{ER}^*(\mu)\le 2\)?

The model it lives in

The input is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over location types \(x\in[0,L]\), with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\); the outcome is a facility point \(y\in[0,L]\). A rule is mass-group-strategyproof if no positive-mass coalition can jointly misreport so that almost every member strictly reduces their distance to the facility. The objective is the worst-case ratio \(\operatorname{ER}(F(\mu),\mu)/\operatorname{ER}^*(\mu)\), and the candidate rule is \(F(\mu)=L/2\).

The objection that survived

The guarantee depends only on the fixed interval and utility range, so the masses do no work and the mirror yields no support-sensitive complexity classification.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's fixed-interval deterministic \(2\)-approximation guarantee. It does not carry over Theorem 1 as a computational lower bound, the randomized bounds in Theorem 3, or the relative-interval results in Theorems 4–8.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the lower-bound argument of Theorem 1 transfer to finite-support societies under positive-mass group strategyproofness, making \(2\) the exact deterministic minimax value?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a defensible mirror in the paper’s fixed-interval, deterministic results. Its fit to ChoCo is limited: the paper has named approximation-mechanism results, but no theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or similar. The mirror is about the quality of truthful mechanisms, not a new complexity boundary.

Consider residents of a long, bounded community who want a public facility placed along one corridor. Suppose many residents share one of \(\tau\) neighborhood locations, with \(n\gg\tau\). A type is a location \(x\in[0,L]\), and its mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of the population at that location. The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses. The facility remains a point \(y\in[0,L]\), as in the paper; the continuization is of the population, represented by \(\mu\).

For this society, define \(u(y,x)=L-|y-x|\) and let \(ER(y,\mu)\) be the maximum ratio \(u(y,x)/u(y,x')\) over occupied types \(x,x'\). A mechanism \(F\) maps each reported distribution to a facility location. To keep strategyproofness meaningful for a continuum population, use mass group strategyproofness: no coalition of positive mass can change its reports so that almost every member is strictly closer to the facility. The designer’s objective is to minimize the worst-case ratio of \(ER(F(\mu),\mu)\) to the best envy ratio achievable by choosing a facility location for \(\mu\).

The paper’s Theorem 1, proved here, says that every deterministic strategyproof mechanism in the fixed-interval model has approximation ratio at least \(2\). Its continuous counterpart is the question: *does every deterministic mass-group-strategyproof rule on finite-support societies have worst-case approximation ratio at least \(2\)?* The expected answer is yes. The paper’s proof uses a society with mass at the two endpoints, then moves a same-location coalition to force the mechanism’s outcome on a second profile. Those are positive-mass types in the mirror, so the lower-bound argument does not depend on preserving the identity of a single vanishing-mass agent.

The lead construction is Theorem 2, also proved here: the rule that always places the facility at \(L/2\), independently of reports, is group strategyproof and has approximation ratio \(2\). Its continuous counterpart asks: *given \(L\), provide a mass-group-strategyproof rule with worst-case ratio at most \(2\).* The answer is \(F(\mu)=L/2\). At that point every type’s utility is between \(L/2\) and \(L\), so the envy ratio is at most \(2\), while the optimum envy ratio is at least \(1\). A society supported on \(0\) and \(L/2\) attains the bound.

Together, these are two prover-ready questions about the same mirror: establish the universal lower bound, then exhibit a matching rule. They predict an exact minimax value of \(2\), an A-style tractable result in the programme’s broad trichotomy, and no transfer of discrete hardness. The result concerns mechanism quality; evaluating the proposed rule itself is constant time. This mirror covers the deterministic fixed-interval results only. It suggests follow-up questions about the exact randomized guarantee for mass-group-strategyproof rules and the deterministic minimax guarantee when the facility is restricted to the paper’s relative interval.

The weakest point is the strategic notion. In a nonatomic population, a unilateral agent has zero mass, so a mechanism that depends only on \(\mu\) can make individual strategyproofness vacuous. Mass group strategyproofness avoids that problem and matches the paper’s group-strategyproof construction; the lower-bound proof also works through a same-location coalition. Still, this is a modeling choice the authors and a referee would need to accept. The other limitation is substantive: although this is a credible population mirror, it is a weaker fit to ChoCo’s central interest in computational complexity than a hardness result would be.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper’s results are approximation and incentive theorems, not computational results. They give guarantees for strategyproof mechanisms; they define no instance-level computational problem, complexity measure, reduction, or algorithm whose running time depends on support size or input bits. Mirroring them over a distribution \(\mu\) would pose another mechanism-design theorem about rules \(F\), rather than chart the complexity landscape ChoCo is built to study.

Theorem 1 has a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation: residents at the same location form a type, and a positive-mass cohort can deviate together. But individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous when one person has zero mass and cannot change \(\mu\). Requiring immunity to positive-mass coalitions gives a substantive question, though it is a theorem about bloc incentives. Even the strongest version—finding the exact minimax guarantee over all such rules—remains a quantification over mechanisms, with no computational task or running time for an algorithm to classify.

Theorem 2 makes the gap especially clear. The fixed-center rule is constant-time, and its guarantee follows from the utility range and \(ER\ge 1\). The witness uses the support \(\{0,L/2\}\); the masses do no work. Computing the envy ratio from a finite-support input takes a scan of the support, while the optimum is characterized by the support’s extremes. A better randomized or distribution-dependent mirror could be an interesting mechanism-design project, but it still would not supply the computational result ChoCo seeks. Extensions to multiple facilities or other spaces would introduce new problems rather than strengthen these anchors.

This is a scope-based negative case, not a claim that continuous populations are implausible: the resident-cohort model is credible, and mass-group strategyproofness is worth studying on its own. The case is weaker if ChoCo intends to include incentive guarantees as a parallel research track.
