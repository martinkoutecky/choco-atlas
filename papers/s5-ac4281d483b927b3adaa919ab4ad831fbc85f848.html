The continuous mirror question

Fix an alternative set \(X\), a crash fraction \(\rho\), and a tolerance \(\varepsilon\). For each sufficiently large \(N\), \(N\) processes receive private rankings from \(L(X)\), with empirical type distributions converging to a fixed-support society \(\mu\); at most \(t_N=\lfloor \rho N\rfloor\) processes may crash. Does there exist a protocol family, uniform over these input profiles, such that in every admissible synchronous execution the correct processes' output distribution \(\nu_N\) has Kendall-tau diameter at most \(\varepsilon\), and their decisions respect every pair unanimously ranked by the correct processes? The paper's cyclic witness has support on \(m\) rankings with mass \(1/m\) each, and its impossibility applies when \(\rho m>1\) and \(\varepsilon<\lfloor m^2/4\rfloor\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity version of synchronous distributed preference aggregation: rankings in \(L(X)\) are agent types, empirical frequencies represent society, and a protocol family must satisfy the original pointwise agreement and unanimity guarantees in every finite realization.

The objection that survived

The distribution \(\mu\) alone does not encode crash schedules or each correct process's decision, so the mirror depends on accepting asymptotic families of finite protocols as a continuous-population problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the full-domain approximate Kendall-tau impossibility in Corollary 5.14.4 and the full-domain \(k\)-set impossibility in Corollary 5.14.1. It leaves the other cyclic-profile bounds and the paper's preliminary distributed aggregation results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the sharp crash-fraction thresholds for fixed-support societies with unequal type masses?
  • For restricted ranking domains, when do the cyclic-profile impossibility bounds become tight?

The case FOR (proponent)

The credible mirror is a high-multiplicity version of distributed preference aggregation. Let the agents be identical replicas of a preference-aggregation service. An agent’s type is its strict ranking \(R\in L(X)\); its mass is the fraction of replicas holding that ranking. For a population of \(N\) replicas, write \(\mu_N(R)\) for each ranking’s fraction, and let \(\mu_N\) converge to a distribution \(\mu\) with fixed finite support as \(N\) grows. A concrete regime is an anonymous pool with \(N\gg m\), where each of \(m\) ranking types is held by about \(N/m\) replicas. The paper allows process identities; this scenario assumes identical, exchangeable replicas. Its impossibility results still apply, since they cover the more permissive identity-aware setting.

The distributed nature of the task matters to the mirror. Each replica sees its own ranking and communicates; the distribution \(\mu\) is the population’s aggregate state, not a centrally supplied input. A protocol produces a ranking for each correct replica. Those decisions induce an output mass distribution \(\nu_N\) over rankings. Thus the population is continuized while individual inputs and decisions remain complete rankings. A scalable protocol family is a protocol for each sufficiently large \(N\), correct on every allowed input profile and execution. That gives a precise high-multiplicity question without assuming a new continuum message-passing model.

My lead anchor is Corollary 5.14.4 (ǫ-Approximate Kendall Tau: Full Domain Impossibility), proved in this paper. It says: when \(W_I=W_O=L(X)\), if \(m\ge n(t_{\mathrm{sync}})/t\), then no algorithm solves \(\varepsilon\)-approximate preference aggregation under Kendall tau when \(\varepsilon<\lfloor m^2/4\rfloor\); in particular, impossibility holds when \(\varepsilon<\tfrac12\operatorname{diam}_{KT}(L(X))\). This is a distributed solvability impossibility, rather than an NP-hardness result.

Continuous Approximate-KT Preference Aggregation is the corresponding population problem. Its instance specifies \(X\), the full type and output domains \(L(X)\), a crash fraction \(\rho\), and a tolerance \(\varepsilon\). In each synchronous \(N\)-replica realization, at most \(t_N=\lfloor\rho N\rfloor\) replicas may crash. A protocol family solves the task if, in every admissible execution, the output mass has Kendall-tau diameter at most \(\varepsilon\), and every unanimous pairwise preference among the correct replicas is respected by all their decisions. The question is whether such a scalable family exists. For fixed \(m\), \(\rho m>1\) puts the finite realizations in the corollary’s regime for all sufficiently large \(N\); at \(\rho m=1\), take \(N\) through multiples of \(m\). The cyclic witness uses only \(m\) ranking types, each with mass \(1/m\), even though the full type space has \(m!\) rankings. I expect the impossibility to transfer to this high-multiplicity regime: the relevant structure is already present in a fixed number of types as the population grows.

A second useful anchor is Corollary 5.14.1 (k-Set Impossibility: Full Domain), also proved here. It says: if \(L(X)\subseteq W_I\subseteq P(X)\), \(m\ge n(t_{\mathrm{sync}})/t\), and \(k<\min\{m,n\}\), then no algorithm solves \(k\)-set preference aggregation on \(W_I,W_O\) in synchrony model \(t_{\mathrm{sync}}\). The Continuous k-Set Preference Aggregation mirror uses the same population setup, with a fixed \(k<m\). It asks whether a scalable protocol family can ensure that the output mass is supported on at most \(k\) rankings in every execution, while preserving unanimous pairwise preferences. The same \(m\)-type, equal-mass cyclic witness gives the expected answer: impossibility transfers along arbitrarily large realizations. The approximate-KT problem is my stronger mirror because it carries the paper’s explicit metric threshold into the mass-based output description.

Both questions preserve the paper’s central concern: can distributed agents with private rankings reach compatible decisions despite crashes? They vary the population regime, not the outcome space or the preference metric. Further questions include the sharp crash-fraction thresholds for restricted domains and unequal type masses, and whether asynchronous protocols have a corresponding sharp threshold. The paper’s cyclic-profile theorem gives lower bounds; the continuous analysis could ask where those bounds become tight as \(N\) grows with the type support fixed.

The soft spot is that this is an asymptotic family of finite distributed systems, not a new protocol model whose agents literally have infinitesimal mass. Correctness depends on local information, communication, and indistinguishability across executions; \(\mu\) alone does not capture those features. The case survives if the programme accepts high-multiplicity protocol families as continuous population mirrors: here the number of types stays fixed, their masses remain positive, and the paper’s impossibility persists as the number of replicas grows. The conclusion should remain modest: these anchors offer a credible Class-B-like boundary for continuization, not a standard complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The equal-mass cyclic witness is a serious point for the proponent: it can be realized by arbitrarily large finite populations, and the paper’s impossibility theorem then rules out a protocol at each such size. My negative case is that this gives an asymptotic restatement of a finite distributed-computability result, rather than a computational problem whose input is a continuous society.

Corollary 5.14.4, approximate Kendall tau, has the strongest proposed mirror. Fix \(m\) ranking types in the cyclic witness, give each mass \(1/m\), and let the number of replicas grow while the crash fraction stays fixed. The resulting question—whether a scalable family of protocols meets the agreement and unanimity requirements—is coherent. But its instances are still finite systems: a protocol receives individual rankings, and correctness quantifies over crash sets, schedules, and every correct process. The measure \(\mu\) does not specify those execution details. Supplying them requires a sequence of finite refinements, which is precisely where the impossibility already applies.

An output measure also cannot faithfully retain the paper’s exact requirement. If its Kendall diameter is computed on its support, one process with a distant decision can determine the finite system’s diameter; its mass tends to zero and that decision disappears from the limit. If correctness remains pointwise for every correct process, the proposed mirror remains a family of finite protocol tasks. A genuinely continuous version would need new semantics for communication, failures, and correctness among a continuum of processes. The equal-mass witness establishes a useful asymptotic lower bound, but it does not supply those semantics or a new algorithmic problem over \(\mu\).

Corollary 5.14.1, \(k\)-set preference aggregation, has the same issue more sharply. “At most \(k\) decisions” is a cardinality condition: a decision made by one process counts as much as one made by half the population. Defining it as at most \(k\) rankings with positive output mass preserves that feature, but makes the condition insensitive to the sizes of the masses. Defining it by a mass threshold instead drops rare decisions and changes the paper’s task. The equal-mass cyclic profile again transfers the finite impossibility, but the resulting mirror is still about scalable protocol families, not computation on a society distribution.

The natural replica-pool scenario is plausible, so I would not dismiss these results on the ground that multiplicity is impossible. Nor is “the answer is already known” the objection: a continuous treatment could still be valuable even if its answer matched the finite one. The stronger concern is that this paper’s operative object is a distributed execution, not an aggregate preference profile. A full type would have to account for any identities, roles, or communication circumstances the protocol uses; retaining those can make the support grow with the system. Erasing them calls for an exchangeable or anonymous model and a separate account of how the adversary chooses faults and delays.

There is a worthwhile research question nearby: determine sharp asymptotic crash thresholds for fixed-support, exchangeable populations of replicas. But that is a new distributed-computability programme. Under ChoCo’s stated remit—algorithms and complexity for problems given by a distribution over agent types—the proposed mirrors lack a continuous input-to-computation relation. My negative case is therefore strongest as a scope argument, and weakest if ChoCo intends to include scalable families of distributed protocols as continuous computational problems.
