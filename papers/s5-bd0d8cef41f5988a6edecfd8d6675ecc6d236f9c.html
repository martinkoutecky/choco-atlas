The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of item categories \(B\) and supported types \(T\), where each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), a strict ranking of \(B\), and a currently assigned category \(b(t)\), decide whether there are values \(x_{tu}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_u x_{tu}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), \(\sum_t x_{tu}=\mu_u\) for every \(u\), \(x_{tu}>0\) only if type \(t\) strictly prefers \(b(u)\) to \(b(t)\), and the directed support graph with arc \(t\to u\) when \(x_{tu}>0\) is strongly connected on \(T\).

The model it lives in

Types encode complete rankings over interchangeable item categories and each type’s current category; \(\mu_t\) is the population mass of type \(t\). The variables \(x_{tu}\) describe how much mass of type \(t\) receives the category currently held by type \(u\); feasibility and strongly connected support express one strict exchange involving the whole population.

The objection that survived

The mirror requires genuinely interchangeable item categories; the paper’s motivating specific houses or jobs may have distinct identities and preferences that category-level grouping would erase.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the full-coalition, single-layer Verify-OA case of Theorem 6 through its violation question; leaves the Verify-SOA and Verify-UOA branches, multi-layer coalition notions, and other parameterized results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the strongly connected feasible-support condition be recognized in polynomial time in \(|B|\), \(|T|\), and the binary input length of the masses?
  • Does every rational feasible mass flow with strongly connected support correspond exactly to a single full-population trading cycle in a finite replication?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I find defensible is Full-Population Cycle Verification\(_\infty\), for the paper’s single-layer Verify-OA case.

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in the paper. It establishes coNP-hardness for Verify-OA and Verify-SOA when \(k=n\), \(\ell=1\), \(\alpha=1\), and the maximum preference-list length is \(d=3\); it establishes the same for Verify-UOA with \(\ell=2\) and the other restrictions unchanged. The proof reduces directed Hamiltonian Cycle on graphs of maximum degree 3: each graph vertex becomes an agent and an item, each agent is assigned its corresponding item, and its list places the items of its outgoing neighbors above that item. For Verify-OA, the full group of \(n\) agents admits a trading cycle exactly when the graph has a Hamiltonian cycle. I use the Verify-OA branch as my lead. [Theorem 6 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.10434)

A plausible regime is large-scale housing allocation: many households share the same ranking over a small set of housing categories and the same currently assigned category. Let \(T\) be the supported household types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type specifies its ranking and its current category \(b(t)\). The number of households \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau=|T|\); the supply of each category scales with the population. Mass is a fraction of households. Individual households still receive one whole unit; fractional quantities describe how much of a type is assigned to each category in aggregate.

An instance consists of the finite category set, the supported types and their binary-rational masses, their strict rankings, and their current assignments. Ask whether the whole population can participate in a strict cyclic exchange. A witness is a nonnegative matrix \(x_{tu}\), where \(x_{tu}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving an item currently held by type \(u\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_u x_{tu}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tu}=\mu_u, \]
and \(x_{tu}>0\) only when type \(t\) strictly prefers \(b(u)\) to \(b(t)\). Also require the directed graph with edge \(u\to t\) whenever \(x_{tu}>0\) to be strongly connected on the supported types. That last condition makes this a full-population cycle question: all mass participates in one connected exchange, rather than a collection of disconnected exchanges.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, likely in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). The mass transfer is a transportation flow with fixed rational margins; whether an allowed edge can carry positive flow can be checked by a flow or LP test. Combining feasible edges gives a connected-support witness exactly when those edges span a strongly connected graph. This is a plausible Class A outcome for the continuous question; it is not a claim that Theorem 6’s coNP-hardness transfers.

The scope is deliberately narrow: one layer and a full-population exchange. I would leave the multi-layer coalition notions and the paper’s other parameterized results aside. Further questions include how this mass-cycle verification relates to finite replications with a common denominator, and what the right mass analogue is for coalitions smaller than the whole population.

My weakest point is the meaning of “one cycle” in an atomless population. Strong connectivity of type-level flow is a reasonable analogue, but it permits mass to split across trade partners, unlike a finite Hamiltonian cycle through distinct agents. Also, the reduction in Theorem 6 uses \(n\) distinct preference patterns, so its hardness does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is small. The mirror stands as a natural population version of the authors’ verification question, with a changed cycle structure that should be made explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is the coNP-hardness theorem in Section 5 (Theorem 6): Verify-OA with \(k=n\), \(\ell=\alpha=1\), and \(d=3\). Its witness is a Hamiltonian cycle through all agents, with the reduction encoding an arbitrary directed graph in agents’ preferences over their specifically assigned items. If those items remain distinct, the assigned item is part of each agent’s type, so the reduction has \(\tau=n\). It supplies no evidence that the hard part has a finite-type, high-multiplicity structure.

The best improved mirror would group interchangeable item copies into categories and ask whether all mass can make a strict cyclic exchange. That does change the goods model: the paper’s actual inputs are individually named items, while the mirror needs category-level substitutability. This makes the proposed housing story depend on a real modeling assumption, not merely on normalizing population size. For specific houses or jobs, treating distinct positions as copies of one category may erase preferences the allocation uses.

But this objection does not defeat the mirror. With genuinely interchangeable copies, the proposed flow has a strong finite-replication interpretation. Scale its rational masses to integers and read each positive transfer as a directed multigraph arc between types. The balance constraints make that multigraph Eulerian; strong connectivity gives one Euler tour, hence a single cycle through the replicated agents. Conversely, a finite full-population cycle projects to such a balanced, strongly connected type-level flow. That is a credible dictionary for the authors to recognize, and a coherent computational question. Its likely tractability is not an objection under the programme’s criteria.

So the negative case rests on whether interchangeable item categories are convincing for this paper’s assignment settings. That is a legitimate caveat, but not a universal rebuttal: repeated seats, slots, or genuinely equivalent housing units provide plausible regimes. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The case against is weak, and the other analyst’s mirror appears to survive the stated gate.
