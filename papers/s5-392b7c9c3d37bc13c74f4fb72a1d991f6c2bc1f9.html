The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result of the kind the brief requires. Its numbered results are mathematical existence or characterization claims. For example, Theorem 2, proved here, establishes that every non-oligarchic collegial simple game has a SREFS; Theorem 3, attributed to Jordan, characterizes the unique vNM stable set in pillage games; and Theorems 4–6 give stable-set or REFS/SREFS characterizations. None states that a computational problem is in \(P\), hard, or belongs to another complexity class.

Theorem 5 is a tempting starting point: it proves that \(B\) is a SREFS for the paper’s finite-player pillage game. But a population-continuum version would need to define what measurable coalitions can do and how their aggregate power works. Those choices could change the result, and the paper poses no computational task about finding or recognizing \(B\). That would be a new theory problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational anchor.

The paper’s simplex of wealth allocations is continuous, but it is continuity in the outcome space; the population remains finite. So there is a conceivable population-continuum research extension here, but no honest, prover-ready continuous *computational* problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 2 proves existence of a SREFS in a class of simple games; Theorem 3 characterizes Jordan’s stable set; Theorems 4–6 characterize farsighted stable sets, REFS, and SREFS in pillage games. These are results about stability concepts and their predictions. None states a complexity bound, gives an algorithm, or proves a computational hardness result. A new question about computing or recognizing one of these solution concepts would be a computational extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result it contains.

Theorem 5 is the most tempting anchor because it proves that \(B\) is a SREFS in the finite-player pillage game. One could plausibly model a large population with repeated wealth or political-power types, and describe coalitions as measurable masses. That might make a worthwhile continuum theory of pillage and stability. But the theorem’s claim is still about which outcomes a solution concept supports, not the cost of finding those outcomes. Adding a computational task—say, deciding whether an allocation belongs to a SREFS—would require choosing a finite input representation and a computational problem the paper never studies. The same applies to the simple-game results: a continuum of voters with repeated voting-power types is plausible, but Theorem 2 is an existence theorem, not a complexity result.

The population axis is not inherently meaningless here. Large electorates with repeated voting types, or populations holding small claims to a common pool, could support sensible mass-based models. Nor is the paper’s continuous wealth simplex itself evidence of population continuization: the agents and coalitions remain finite. Even granting the best population-continuum versions of the models, however, the candidate results remain stability characterizations. They do not supply the computational anchor this programme is meant to continuize.

So I would not claim that no useful continuum research could grow from this paper. That universal claim is too strong. The narrower negative verdict is firm: under ChoCo’s stated remit, none of the proponent’s cited results qualifies as a computational anchor. The paper could inspire a new continuum coalition-stability project, but that would be a different programme of work.
