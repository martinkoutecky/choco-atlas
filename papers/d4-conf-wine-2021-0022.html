The continuous mirror question

Given a finite catalogue of good categories \(M\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of initial agent types \(t=(S_t,D_t)\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), what is the maximum total number of exchanges per unit mass achievable by a finite schedule of legal dynamic cycle actions? Each agent holds and demands integral sets of categories; copies are interchangeable, and a cycle action of mass \(\lambda\) uses disjoint cohorts of each participating type, updates their states by the paper’s cycle rule, and cannot use more mass of a type than is available.

The model it lives in

A repeated-edition exchange market with a finite set of interchangeable book categories and rational masses of recurring integral portfolios and wishlists. Decision variables are the masses assigned to successive legal cycle actions; the objective is the total cycle exchanges weighted by action mass, with cohort mass and category-copy availability tracked as states change.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the exact and approximation hardness results for optimal dynamic executions in Theorem 4.4 and Theorem B.3. It leaves the static-execution algorithm, strategyproofness results, and approximation bounds for strategyproof mechanisms without a mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the schedule be represented by a polynomial-size flow or linear program over reachable type states and legal cycles?
  • Can the pricing or separation problem for the resulting cycle schedule be solved efficiently, or does dynamic sequencing preserve hardness?
  • What conditions on catalogue size, type support, and rational input masses give a formal correspondence with large discrete copy markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror for the paper’s computational results on optimal dynamic execution. The natural regime is a large book or textbook exchange platform with many users, a fixed catalogue of editions, and recurring combinations of owned and wanted books. Users with the same complete bundle and wishlist form one type; the instance gives each supported type its population mass. In a standardized exchange programme, the number of users \(n\) could be far larger than the number of recurring types \(\tau\). Copies of an edition remain indivisible for each user, while the supply of copies scales with the population. That co-modification fits the paper’s durable-goods setting and retains its central question: how many exchanges can a sequence of legal cycles achieve?

I would call the lead problem Fluid Optimal Dynamic Exchange. Its instance is a finite catalogue \(M\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of complete initial states \(t=(S_t,D_t)\), with \(S_t\cap D_t=\varnothing\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). A current state records the goods an agent holds and the goods they still demand, updated by the paper’s cycle rule: when an agent gives \(g\) and receives \(h\), the state changes from \((S,D)\) to \(((S\setminus\{g\})\cup\{h\},D\setminus\{h\})\). A legal cycle is a cycle in the resulting trading graph. It may be executed for mass \(\lambda\): that mass of agents in each participant state performs the same cycle, with disjoint cohorts used if a state appears more than once. Each represented agent still receives and gives whole goods; only cohort mass is fractional. The question is to return a finite schedule of such mass-cycle actions maximizing \(\sum_j |C_j|\lambda_j\), the exchanges per unit of society mass.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper. It states that computing an optimal execution is NP-hard. The proof reduces from 3D-matching; Proposition 4.10 establishes that the constructed trading graph has an execution of size \(8n\) exactly when the 3D-matching instance has a perfect matching. The corresponding continuous question is whether Fluid Optimal Dynamic Exchange can be solved in polynomial time in the catalogue size, support size, and input bit length. I would expect this version to be tractable: the reduction’s choice among disjoint tuple gadgets may become a fractional cycle-mass problem, much as fractional 3D-matching is an LP. That is a reason to investigate an LP over cycle schedules and its pricing problem, not a proof that such an LP has efficient pricing.

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem B.3 in Appendix B.2 of the supplied full text. It proves that for some constant \(c>1\), no polynomial-time \(c\)-approximation for computing an optimal execution exists unless \(P=NP\). The paper proves the transfer from a gap version of 3D-matching, citing that gap hardness from Petrank (1994). The corresponding problem, Fluid \(c\)-Approximate Dynamic Exchange, has the same instance and legal schedules; its solution is a schedule whose value is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/c\), for the constant \(c\) in the theorem. I would also expect this fluid version to be tractable, for the same fractionalization reason. Establishing that result would show directly that the paper’s approximation hardness depends on integral choices among exchanges.

These two questions cover the paper’s computational study of optimal dynamic executions in Section 4. They do not claim a mirror for the strategyproofness results or the static-execution mechanism. The paper itself presents its model as an abstraction of durable-goods markets rather than a full account of one particular platform, which makes a large repeated-type market a plausible setting for the authors to recognize.

The soft spot is that the reduction uses specifically linked agents and items, while the proposed regime groups users with the same state and treats copies of an edition as interchangeable. If goods are genuinely one-off, or users’ complete states are mostly unique, then \(\tau\) may grow with \(n\), and this is a poor high-multiplicity regime. Even in the proposed regime, dynamic chronology could make pricing or scheduling hard after the fractional relaxation. I would still ask the authors to study these fluid questions: they preserve integral bundles per person, the cycle rule, durable goods, and the objective of maximizing exchanges, while making the population itself the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the two anchors are really one. Theorem 4.4’s NP-hardness and Theorem B.3’s inapproximability both use the same 3D-matching construction and the same dynamic-execution structure. B.3 strengthens the computational stakes, but it does not supply a separate continuous question.

That construction is a poor example of population multiplicity. Its tuple gadgets use distinct agents and goods, with incidence arranged so a particular sequence of cycles represents a particular triple. Under the paper’s literal item identities, agents with nonempty endowments cannot simply be repeated as the same type: endowments partition the goods. In the reduction, most gadget agents therefore have distinct complete types, and the support grows with the construction. To make many copies of each type, one must treat physical copies of a good as interchangeable and represent holdings and demands as quantities. That is a plausible co-modification, but the reduction’s tuple-specific goods and cycle incidence do not carry over automatically. The hardness result itself is not evidence that population continuization dissolves its combinatorics.

The best version of the mirror repairs that gap: a large exchange platform for copies of book editions, with recurring integral portfolios and wishlists, represented by rational masses of types. Cohorts can execute the same legal cycle, and each person still gives and receives whole items. This is a coherent dynamic-exchange question. But it studies a homogeneous copy market; it does not automatically preserve the paper’s arbitrary identity-level trading graphs. The authors may well recognize it as their problem, especially given their book examples. So this distinction weakens the claimed correspondence; it does not rule out the mirror.

The fluid schedule also needs a careful formal account. State changes must track each cohort’s integral holdings and remaining demands, while cycle actions must ensure that no mass is used twice and that the exchanged goods are available at the right stage. Those obligations look formalizable, but the proposed mass-cycle description does not by itself establish a tractable LP or an exact high-multiplicity dictionary. That is a reason to ask for the model and its limits, not a reason to dismiss the question.

So the negative case can fairly say that neither anchor, as proved, demonstrates a population-driven hardness result, and that B.3 adds no independent mirror. It cannot honestly establish the requested universal claim. A repeated-edition market is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting continuous dynamic-exchange problem preserves the paper’s central objective. My case against is therefore weak precisely where the burden is hardest: the paper has at least one worthwhile candidate mirror, even though the proposed reduction does not validate it.
