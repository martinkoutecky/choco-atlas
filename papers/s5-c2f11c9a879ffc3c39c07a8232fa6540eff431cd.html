The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant anchored case for this paper: it contains no named computational result about an underlying problem being tractable, hard, or otherwise classified. Its theorems compare decision accuracy; they do not analyze algorithms or computational complexity.

The closest positive hook is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: when \(p\in(1/2,1)\) is each juror’s probability of voting correctly, and \(n-k>1\), a committee whose size is \(n+k\) or \(n-k\) with equal probability has expected accuracy at least that of a fixed committee of size \(n\), under unanimity. That is a substantive result about flexibility, but it is not a computational anchor under the requested rule.

An unanchored research lead would be to model a large pool of potential jurors as a distribution \(\mu\) over a small set of competence types, then choose a lottery \(q\) over explicitly listed committee sizes to maximize the probability of a correct decision under unanimity, subject to a bound on expected committee size. The regime would be a large pool with few types; the action would be \(q\), and the objective expected accuracy. For a finite list of sizes, this becomes a linear optimization problem. But the paper neither states nor analyzes that optimization problem. Moving from its accuracy comparisons to a budgeted committee-design problem is an extension, and the main weakness is that unanimity panels are usually small—so the high-multiplicity pool may be plausible while the committee itself remains a small finite object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that there is no compliant computational anchor. Theorem 1 is the closest hook, but it compares closed-form accuracy probabilities for fixed committee sizes. Theorems 2–4 do the same for other size lotteries or competence assumptions. None defines an algorithmic problem or makes a complexity claim.

The high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own model is especially thin: under its assumptions, every juror has the same competence, so the society is just one type with mass \(1\). Enlarging a pool of potential jurors leaves the results unchanged if committee sizes stay fixed. The committee itself remains a finite selection problem. If instead committee size grows with the population, then for \(p\in(1/2,1)\),
\[ \pi_{f_h}^n(p)=\tfrac12\bigl(p^n+1-(1-p)^n\bigr)\longrightarrow\tfrac12. \]
The unanimity accuracy signal disappears in the continuum limit.

The best repaired version is the proposed lottery over committee sizes under an expected-size budget. With the paper’s homogeneous competence assumption, the pool’s type distribution has no role; the lottery optimization is a new committee-design problem. A richer version could give a large expert pool several competence types and choose whom to seat. That might be worth studying, but the computational question would concern allocating experts across committees, a problem this paper neither formulates nor analyzes. Its continuous-population aspect would come from the added pool and allocation model.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as a mirror candidate: every named result is an accuracy comparison, and the natural population continuizations either leave those results untouched or make the unanimity limit degenerate. The universal claim is harder to establish: one can invent adjacent high-multiplicity design problems from the paper’s setting. But those would be new research questions, not computational mirrors of its results.
