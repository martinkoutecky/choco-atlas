The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t make the requested positive case without stretching the rules. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes the solutions satisfying six properties as \(y^a=2a(y^k-y^f)+y^f\). Theorems 2 and 3, also proved here, characterize the folk and Kar solutions by adding Core Selection or strengthening Problem Separation. These are axiomatic characterizations; none asserts a complexity classification or algorithm for a computational problem. The formulas and Prim’s algorithm in the setup do not supply a numbered computational result either.

A continuum of households grouped by connection-cost or location type might motivate a new cost-sharing model. But this paper does not define that model, and its theorems do not analyze computing allocations for it. Turning their axioms into type-level mass conditions would continuize an axiomatic question, outside the programme’s stated scope. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover here. The paper’s strongest near-miss is Theorem 1, but treating it as a computational anchor would misrepresent what it proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no anchor to defeat. But the absence of a complexity theorem does not show that the paper has no worthwhile computational mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the entire family of cost sharing rules, and the paper explicitly presents that characterization as useful for implementation. That gives a concrete output to compute.

Consider a fixed cost electricity or communications network serving many households. A type is a neighborhood with a complete connection cost profile; its mass is the fraction of households there. Assume households in the same neighborhood can connect to one another at zero cost, while the network’s edge costs remain fixed, as in the paper. This is a natural high multiplicity regime for the paper’s model.

For each positive rational mass vector \(\mu\), replicate each type \(t\) into \(n_t\) identical agents, with \(n_t/\sum_u n_u\to\mu_t\). Ask for the limiting aggregate cost share of each type under the rule \(y^a\). This is a computational question over a continuous population, not a continuized axiom. In the replicated instance, only the first household of a type to enter a random Shapley ordering can change the cost. As the population grows, the order in which types first appear has a well-defined limit: each next type \(t\) appears with probability proportional to its remaining mass. The type’s limiting Kar share is its expected marginal cost at that arrival; the folk share is the same construction using the irreducible cost matrix. The limiting \(y^a\) share is their affine combination.

One can therefore ask whether those shares can be computed exactly or approximately in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and the input bit length, or whether useful parameterized algorithms are possible. Theorem 1 supplies the rule family; Theorems 2 and 3 identify its folk and Kar endpoints. Axiomatic results can anchor a computational question when the paper itself makes computing the resulting allocation part of implementation.

This is not a mirror of a complexity result the paper already proves, and the construction assumes genuinely interchangeable households within each neighborhood. Those are real limits to the case. But the authors’ focus on allocating fixed network costs across agents makes this a recognizable extension of their problem, so “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” goes too far.
