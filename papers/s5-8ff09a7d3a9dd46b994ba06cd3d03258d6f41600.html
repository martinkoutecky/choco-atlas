The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the paper’s epidemic social-bubble model, but it does not have a named computational anchor of the kind this exercise requires. Its numbered results are about core structure, existence, and equilibrium inclusion; none asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm. So I can offer a plausible continuous research question, but not honestly present it as a mirror anchored in a computational result of the paper.

The nearest results are Theorem 9 and Corollary 10, both proved in this paper. For homogeneous players with ideal number of neighbours \(k\), Theorem 9 says the core consists of a single outcome: fully connected components of size \(k+1\), with every player receiving the same payoff. Corollary 10 says the core is nonempty exactly when the number of players is divisible by \(k+1\). These are structural results, not computational claims.

A reasonable continuous research question would be Continuum Social-Bubble Core. Take a large community under epidemic risk, with a finite set of sociability types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational, piecewise-linear, concave benefit functions \(g_t(q)\). A bubble has mass \(q\); each member of type \(t\) receives per-capita benefit \(g_t(q)\) and bears exposure cost \(q\). Thus a bubble with type masses \(x_t\) has transferable value
\[ \sum_{t\in T} x_t\bigl(g_t(q)-q\bigr),\qquad q=\sum_{t\in T}x_t. \]
An outcome is a measurable partition of the population into bubbles, together with payoff transfers that balance each bubble’s value. The question is: does a pessimistic recursive core exist, and if so, produce one outcome; otherwise certify that none exists? Deviations would be by measurable subpopulations, with the remaining population responding according to the same recursive-core logic as in Definition 2.

The regime is many residents with only a few sociability types. Making contact and exposure depend on population fractions is a co-modification of the epidemic model; the population remains the continuous object. For the one-type case, I would first investigate a Class A characterization when the ideal bubble mass is simple. The paper itself does not establish that tractability, and the heterogeneous recursive problem could be harder. Further questions include whether the continuum recursive core is well-defined, how its existence depends on the sociability curves, and whether finite instances converge to its outcomes.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it retains endogenous contact networks, epidemic externalities, coalition deviations, transfers, and the social-bubble application. The weak point is substantial, though. In the paper’s homogeneous result, \(k\) is an ideal number of neighbours, so as the population grows the stable bubbles can remain finite. A continuum mirror with positive-mass bubbles instead scales ideal contact size with the population. That may be a natural per-capita epidemic model, but it is a modification rather than a direct limit of Theorem 9. And because Theorem 9 and Corollary 10 are not computational anchors, this candidate cannot meet the exercise’s required anchor condition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchors, Theorem 9 and Corollary 10, do not state computational results. They characterize the recursive core in the homogeneous case and say when it is nonempty. The paper gives no input encoding, algorithm, or complexity claim for finding a core. Turning those structural results into a search problem for heterogeneous populations may be a worthwhile new project, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed social-bubble mirror also runs into a substantive scaling problem. In Theorem 9, \(k\) is an ideal *number* of neighbours, so each stable bubble has \(k+1\) people. In a population of size \(n\), its mass is \((k+1)/n\), which tends to zero. A mass-based model cannot retain the finite bubble’s contact benefits and exposure cost: treating it as a zero-mass bubble loses those quantities. The proposal avoids that limit by making bubbles have positive mass \(q\) and assigning them payoff \(g_t(q)-q\). That can define a sensible dense-population model, but it changes the crucial scale of the preferences. With the paper’s fixed concave sociability function \(s(d)\), a finite ideal degree does not become an ideal positive fraction of the population as \(n\) grows. To preserve macroscopic bubbles, one needs a population-dependent function such as \(s_n(d)=n g(d/n)\). That is a new assumption, not a limit of the result being mirrored.

There is a further mismatch in carrying over the recursive core. In the paper it is defined by induction on the finite number of players: every proper deviating coalition leaves a smaller finite residual game. In an atomless population, a positive-mass deviation generally leaves another continuum, while zero-mass deviations may still represent the finite groups that matter in the original model. Defining which deviations count, how residual responses are selected, and how outcomes are represented would require a new solution concept and computational input model. The paper’s contagion payoff adds another obstacle to a generic network limit: joining two large components with a single edge changes the exposure of every member, even though that edge has vanishing density in a large network.

A finite-type, proportion-scaled epidemic model might still be interesting. But it would be a new dense-contact game whose computational questions and stability concept need to be supplied; Theorem 9 and Corollary 10 do not establish them. That is the strongest negative case the paper supports. I cannot honestly rule out every plausible re-modelling: the case is strong against a *mirror of this paper’s results*, and weaker against the broader claim that its application could inspire a worthwhile continuous computational project.
