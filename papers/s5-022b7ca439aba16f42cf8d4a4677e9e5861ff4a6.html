The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), chore intervals \(I_k\) of lengths \(\ell_k\), and rational type-pair disutility densities \(w_{tqk}\) normalized so that \(\sum_q\mu_q\sum_k w_{tqk}\ell_k=1\), choose per-capita assignments \(a_{qk}\ge 0\). Require \(\sum_q\mu_q a_{qk}=\ell_k\) for each interval, define \(D_t(a)=\sum_q\mu_q\sum_k w_{tqk}a_{qk}\), and impose \(D_t(a)\le 1\) for every type and \(\sum_k(w_{ttk}a_{tk}+w_{tqk}a_{qk})\le\sum_k(w_{ttk}a_{qk}+w_{tqk}a_{tk})\) for every type pair. Compute a rational feasible assignment minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_tD_t(a)\).

The model it lives in

A divisible chore is assigned per capita to each recipient type, with supply constraints weighted by type mass. Decision variables are the type-level assignments \(a_{qk}\); the objective is mass-weighted total disutility, with proportionality and swap envy imposed for every type.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s optimal fair allocation problem. Consider a large recurring operation—such as conference or hospital support—where undesirable shifts are divided among many workers. Workers fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of cohorts, defined by role, contract, shift costs, and their full externality profiles. A worker’s disutility can depend both on their own shifts and on which cohort receives other shifts, through coordination or supervision burdens. The number of workers \(n\) may be in the thousands or millions while \(\tau\) stays in the tens or low hundreds. The volume of shifts scales with the workforce, keeping the per-person burden meaningful.

The lead anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It states that for \(n\) agents with piecewise-constant disutilities over \(m\) chore intervals, Algorithm 1 computes in polynomial time an allocation that is both proportional and swap envy-free and minimizes total disutility among allocations satisfying those fairness conditions. “Optimal” here means minimizing \(e(A)=\sum_i V_i(A)\). The theorem is an algorithmic anchor; the paper gives no comparable named hardness result.

I would name the mirror Continuous-Type Optimal Proportional and Swap-Envy-Free Chore Allocation. Its instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and chore intervals \(I_1,\ldots,I_m\) of lengths \(\ell_k\). A coefficient \(w_{tqk}\) gives the disutility per unit of interval \(k\) to an agent of type \(t\) when that work is assigned to a member of type \(q\). Types encode the full type-pair cost profile: agents of a type agree on the disutility information the problem uses, including how other types value them as recipients. Normalize so that \(\sum_q \mu_q\sum_k w_{tqk}\ell_k=1\) for each \(t\).

The decision variable \(a_{qk}\geq 0\) is the per-capita amount of interval \(k\) assigned to each member of type \(q\). The supply of each interval scales with the population, so feasibility is \(\sum_q\mu_q a_{qk}=\ell_k\). Define the normalized disutility of a type-\(t\) agent by
\[ D_t(a)=\sum_q\mu_q\sum_k w_{tqk}a_{qk}. \]
The problem asks for an allocation minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_tD_t(a)\), subject to \(D_t(a)\leq 1\) for every type and, for every pair \(t,q\), the swap-envy condition
\[ \sum_k\bigl(w_{ttk}a_{tk}+w_{tqk}a_{qk}\bigr) \leq \sum_k\bigl(w_{ttk}a_{qk}+w_{tqk}a_{tk}\bigr). \]
A solution is an exact minimizing allocation \(a\), represented by its rational per-capita assignments. The \(D_t\leq 1\) bound is proportionality after per-capita scaling; swap envy compares a type-\(t\) person’s bundle with a type-\(q\) person’s bundle.

This is an extension of the paper’s model, but the link is concrete. In a finite clone with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), let \(c_{tqk}\) be the paper’s type-pair disutility density, and take \(w_{tqk}=n c_{tqk}\). If \(x_{i,k}\) is the fraction of interval \(k\) given to individual \(i\), then the corresponding per-capita amount is \(a_{qk}=n\ell_k x_{i,k}\) for a type-\(q\) member. The paper’s resource constraints become \(\sum_q\mu_q a_{qk}=\ell_k\); an individual’s original disutility is \(D_t/n\); and the swap inequalities agree after rescaling. Conversely, rational masses can be cleared to make a finite clone. The remaining key proof obligation is to show that allocations giving every member of a type the same bundle lose no feasible optimum. Linearity supports this: averaging bundles within types preserves resource use and each type’s aggregate disutility, while averaging the pairwise swap inequalities gives the type-level constraints.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Once the symmetry and normalization argument is established, the displayed model is an LP with \(\tau m\) variables and polynomially many rational constraints, so its running time should be polynomial in \(\tau\), \(m\), and the input bit length \(L\). It asks the paper’s underlying question—minimum-disutility chore allocation under its fairness conditions—rather than asking to solve an encoding. The follow-up questions are whether the symmetry argument holds for the full allocation semantics, whether Theorem 9’s approximation guarantee extends to type masses and general Lipschitz disutilities, and what changes when the chore supply does not scale per capita.

The mirror covers Theorem 7 only. Its weakest point is that Theorem 7 already supplies an LP-based polynomial algorithm, so the continuous version may be a succinct high-multiplicity restatement rather than a new tractability discovery. Also, the paper already has a divisible chore; the continuization here is specifically of the population, with per-capita supply scaling. The externality model is convincing when effects are cohort-level, and weaker when costs depend on named relationships between workers. Those limitations temper the contribution without erasing the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case starts with the fact that Theorem 7 is a real computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for minimizing total disutility subject to proportionality and swap envy-freeness. So the decisive objection that there is no named algorithmic result does not apply.

The narrower objection is that this result is already an ordinary linear program. In a population with \(\tau\) complete types and \(m\) chore intervals, identical agents can be given identical average bundles: average their allocations within each type, preserving feasibility, the linear disutility bounds, and the pairwise swap inequalities. The resulting LP has \(O(\tau m)\) variables and \(O(m+\tau+\tau^2)\) constraints. It has no exponential set of choices or pricing problem to solve. The continuous mirror is therefore a direct symmetry quotient of Theorem 7, rather than a new tractability route.

That leaves the mirror’s value resting mainly on compression: replacing a long list of repeated agents and their profiles with rational type masses. For the paper’s unrestricted externalities, a type must capture the full disutility profile toward recipients. If those relationships are idiosyncratic, the number of types can grow with the number of agents and the compression disappears. A cohort setting—such as workers with common shift costs and common effects from assignments to other roles—can keep \(\tau\) small, but it is a special structure the scenario must justify. Even there, the optimization is a routine LP once the cohorts are specified.

There is also a scaling choice to settle. In the proposed finite-to-continuous mapping, the per-type bundle is scaled relative to each finite agent’s physical bundle; keeping workload per worker meaningful instead calls for scaling the total chore supply with population. That is repairable, and the programme explicitly allows such co-modifications, but the two versions describe different population regimes.

This makes the negative case incremental-value criticism, not a fundamental modeling objection. Theorem 7’s symmetry quotient is sound in a plausible cohort regime, and high-multiplicity tractability is precisely a relevant ChoCo question. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: a reader could reasonably consider the compressed LP worthwhile, even though it adds little new optimization machinery.
