The continuous mirror question

Given two nonatomic voter types, geographic and functional, each of mass \(1/2\), let \(x,y\in[0,1/2]\) be the masses supporting a bill in the respective chambers, and let \(z\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether the government proposed it. The bill passes when \((z=1\text{ and }x+y>1/2)\) or \((z=0\text{ and }x>1/4\text{ and }y>1/4)\). Find the minimum \(k\) such that this rule, for every allowed \(x,y,z\), is exactly the conjunction of \(k\) quota tests \(a_i x+b_i y+c_i z>q_i\), with nonnegative weights and strict quotas.

The model it lives in

A two-type, atomless society with \(\mu_G=\mu_F=1/2\), plus a discrete agenda indicator \(z\). The decision variables are quota-test weights and thresholds; the objective is to minimize the number of tests whose conjunction represents the passage rule for all support masses. The proposed representation uses \(x+y>1/2\), \(x+z/2>1/4\), and \(y+z/2>1/4\).

The objection that survived

Dimension is a representation invariant of the voting rule rather than an intervention on a society; the proponent establishes recognition of the dimension question but does not show that it sits centrally within ChoCo’s operational agenda.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a continuous version of the paper’s dimension calculation. The best anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that Legco with \(2n\) ordinary members and the virtual government member has dimension \(1\) for \(n=1,2\), dimension \(2\) for \(n=3,4\), and dimension \(3\) for every \(n\ge5\). Dimension here is the minimum number of weighted-majority games whose intersection gives exactly the same winning coalitions. This is an exact computation of the quantity the paper studies; it is not a theorem that Legco dimension is NP-hard or in P. The paper cites general hardness of computing dimension, but that is not a Legco hardness result to use as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large bicameral assembly whose members are interchangeable within each chamber: for example, a large federation with equal geographic and sectoral delegate blocs. The paper’s own mathematical model treats the \(n\) members of each bloc as equivalent, and its theorem considers arbitrary \(n\). As \(n\) grows, there are many delegates but only two ordinary-member types. The government-proposal marker remains discrete: it represents the agenda distinction that is central to the paper, not a population to be continuized.

Call the mirror Continuous Legco Dimension. Let the geographic and functional chambers each have mass \(1/2\), with type-level weights uniform within each chamber. For a bill, let \(A_G,A_F\) be the measurable sets of members supporting it, and put \(x=\mu(A_G)\), \(y=\mu(A_F)\). Let \(z\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether the bill was proposed by the government. The continuous rule passes the bill exactly when

\[ (z=1\ \text{and}\ x+y>\tfrac12) \quad\text{or}\quad (z=0\ \text{and}\ x>\tfrac14\ \text{and}\ y>\tfrac14). \]

These thresholds preserve the paper’s simple-majority requirements: a government bill needs more than half of all ordinary members, while a member’s bill needs more than half of each chamber.

The problem is: find the least \(k\) such that this rule is exactly the conjunction of \(k\) weighted quota tests, for every measurable pair \(A_G,A_F\) and both values of \(z\). A test has the form \(a_i x+b_i y+c_i z>q_i\), with nonnegative type weights and the tie convention stated explicitly. A solution consists of the minimum \(k\), the tests witnessing it, and a proof that no smaller \(k\) works. Strict quotas capture the continuum version of the paper’s “more than half” rule.

For this rule, three tests suffice:

\[ x+y>\tfrac12,\qquad x+\tfrac12 z>\tfrac14,\qquad y+\tfrac12 z>\tfrac14. \]

When \(z=1\), the last two tests always pass, leaving the overall-majority test. When \(z=0\), the last two require each chamber to exceed one quarter of total mass, which already implies the first. Two tests cannot suffice: at \(z=0\), the winning region is a rectangle with separate geographic and functional boundaries; at \(z=1\), it is a diagonal majority region. Two halfspaces that create the rectangle’s two separate boundaries cannot also create that diagonal boundary. Thus the continuous rule has dimension \(3\).

I expect this mirror to be tractable: for this two-type rule, the exact answer follows from a constant-size geometric argument, and it matches Theorem 1’s \(n\ge5\) result. The authors should recognize it as their problem’s continuous form because it preserves the agenda switch, the two chamber thresholds, and the objective of minimizing the number of weighted-majority tests. It asks about the voting rule itself, not a continuous outcome space or an axiomatic property.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s large-\(n\) branch, not its small finite cases, the power-index calculations, or the \(C\)- and \(W\)-dimension results. Further questions include whether a similar exact correspondence holds for broader continuous voting rules, how dimension behaves as the number of types \(\tau\) grows, and when discrete tie conventions survive a continuum limit.

My weakest point is the regime: real legislatures are finite, and the paper’s Hong Kong example has only seventy ordinary members. The case rests on the authors’ own arbitrary-\(n\) family and its explicit symmetry within chambers; it is strongest as a mirror for that mathematical family, not as a claim that every legislature is a limit population. Also, this is a tractability mirror, not evidence that a discrete hardness result dissolves: the paper supplies no named Legco hardness result to carry over.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is about what Theorem 1 studies. It computes the dimension of a particular voting game: the minimum number of weighted-majority games whose intersection represents it. That is a structural classification of a rule, not a complexity result about an operation on a society. The paper’s citation to hardness of computing dimension in general does not turn its Legco theorem into an algorithmic result. Theorem 2 and the power-index calculations likewise study structural properties of the game.

The proponent’s continuous version is coherent: with two bloc masses \(x,y\) and an agenda bit \(z\), three quota tests represent the rule. But this remains a continuous version of the same representation invariant. The input is the legislature’s winning-coalition rule, and the output is its dimension. There is no distribution of voter preferences to alter, no bribery or control action, and no winner-robustness question over a continuous society. Making the quota tests geometric does not bring the question into ChoCo’s computational programme; it extends voting-game dimension to a continuum domain. A broader project on continuous voting games could reasonably pursue it, but that is a different scope.

This objection does not rest on the answer being \(3\), or on its matching the large-\(n\) result. The authors might well recognize it as a natural extension of their dimension calculation. The problem is that even the best version stays an axiomatic or representational question, while the programme is about computational questions on populations. To make it fit that programme, one would need to add a problem such as finding the least mass transfer that changes passage. That could be worthwhile, but it would be a new operational question rather than a mirror of Theorem 1.

The negative case is weak on the broader claim that no useful continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The paper explicitly studies arbitrary \(n\) and treats members within each chamber symmetrically, so a large assembly with two member types is a credible high-multiplicity regime. What I can defend is narrower: the strongest proposed anchor is a good candidate for a continuous voting-games project, but not a computational-population result for ChoCo.
