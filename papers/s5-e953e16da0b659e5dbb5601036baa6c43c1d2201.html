The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is in environmental regulation of a very large number of small firms. Suppose millions of firms repeatedly choose whether to pollute or treat waste, and a regulator sets state-dependent taxes or subsidies to keep water quality above a threshold. Firms with the same technology, action costs, objective, and exposure to regulation are one type. If there are millions of firms but only dozens of such types, describing the share of firms of each type is more natural than naming every firm. The paper’s example of two firms and one community is too small for this regime; the case comes from a different scenario for the same incentive-design problem.

My lead mirror is Universal Static Mean-Field Incentive Synthesis. An instance has a finite set of environmental states and propositions, an initial state, a finite set \(T\) of firm types, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type specifies a complete agent description: available actions, an LTL[F] utility formula, private action costs, and intervention exposure. At each history, \(z_{t,a}\) is the fraction of society consisting of type-\(t\) firms choosing action \(a\), with \(\sum_a z_{t,a}=\mu_t\). A finite, rationally encoded transition rule maps the current state and these action masses to the next environmental state. A type’s utility can depend both on that shared environmental history and on its own action history.

A static incentive policy maps each environmental state to a grid-valued adjustment of the controllable proposition weights, applied equally to firms of the same type. The policy is memoryless in the paper’s sense: it gives the same incentive at a state regardless of how the system arrived there. A mean-field equilibrium is a type-wise distribution of strategies in which almost every firm’s strategy is a best response to the aggregate action masses and resulting environmental path. The decision question is whether there exists such a policy for which every mean-field equilibrium achieves a given aggregate LTL[F] objective \(\phi\) at least to threshold \(c\). A yes-solution is a policy meeting that guarantee. The input size should account for the type support \(\tau=|T|\), the binary encoding of the masses, the transition rule, the formulas, and the incentive grid.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for each \(\zeta\in\{\mathrm{DSE},\mathrm{NE},\mathrm{RE}_m\}\), with \(m\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), both static synthesis variants—\(\zeta\)-S-E-INCENTIVE-SYNTHESIS, asking whether some \(\zeta\)-equilibrium meets the objective threshold, and \(\zeta\)-S-A-INCENTIVE-SYNTHESIS, asking whether all \(\zeta\)-equilibria meet it—are 2EXPTIME-complete. I rely specifically on its universal Nash row: given a game, objective, threshold, incentivized variables, and incentive granularity, decide whether a static incentive scheme exists that makes the objective reach the threshold on every Nash equilibrium. The theorem is proved here; its hardness argument reduces from previously established rational-synthesis hardness, cited to Kupferman, Perelli, and Vardi (2016), while its upper-bound argument uses SL[F] model checking.

I expect the mirror to remain hard, with hardness transfer as my provisional prediction. The anchor’s complexity is tied to temporal objectives and strategic equilibrium reasoning, not to a large number of named agents. The paper’s lower-bound construction even makes the intervention itself inconsequential, which suggests that merely replacing named firms by a type distribution will not remove the hard part. The prediction is not a hardness proof for the mean-field question: one must still show that the rational-synthesis structure survives the change to negligible individual influence and aggregate action masses. The corresponding existential-equilibrium version, the dynamic-policy version, and the dependence on \(\tau\) and mass bit length are natural follow-up questions.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous version of their incentive-synthesis problem: the regulator still chooses incentives, agents still optimize temporal utilities, and the guarantee still ranges over equilibria. The population is what has been continuized. The aggregate transition rule and mean-field equilibrium are necessary co-modifications to describe how a large society changes the shared environment; the fractional action masses are not a shift to fractional outcomes.

The weakest point is the equilibrium change. In the paper’s finite game, one agent’s deviation can change the play; in an atomless population, a single firm’s deviation has zero effect on aggregate state evolution. The mirror must therefore keep the agents’ individual incentives meaningful through their own action-dependent utility traces, while using the mass of actions to drive environmental dynamics. If the authors regard that mean-field best-response model as a different strategic question, this mirror loses force. The paper’s named two-firm example does not resolve that concern, though the large-population regulation scenario makes the continuous version plausible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 2’s universal Nash synthesis problem does not pass unchanged to the proposed firm population. In the paper, an equilibrium test asks whether one named agent can change their strategy and improve their utility. In an atomless population, one firm’s deviation has zero effect on the aggregate state, so the test becomes a best response to a fixed population flow, coupled to a fixed point over type-level action distributions. That changes the strategic object at the centre of the theorem.

Replicating the firms finitely many times does not remove the gap. At every finite population size, an individual deviation can still affect the shared state; in the limit, it cannot. A guarantee over every finite-game Nash equilibrium need not carry over to a guarantee over every mean-field equilibrium. In particular, indifference may admit additional equilibria in the limit. The paper’s hardness reduction from rational synthesis therefore provides no evident bridge to the proposed mirror.

The best version of the mirror survives this objection as a coherent new problem: many firms share a finite environmental state, rational masses of each type choose actions, a compact aggregate transition rule updates the environment, and the regulator seeks a static policy that guarantees an objective across all mean-field equilibria. The firms scenario makes high multiplicity plausible, and the aggregate transition rule is a reasonable co-modification. But the computational question would depend on that new transition representation and a newly specified equilibrium notion. It would be mean-field incentive synthesis, rather than a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s finite-game synthesis problem.

That distinction gives the negative case some force: Theorem 2 does not itself identify the continuous question or transfer its complexity landscape, and the paper’s model has no built-in anonymity or aggregate-action structure. Yet this is a weak objection under ChoCo’s scope. The authors could reasonably recognize the firm model as their incentive-design problem in a large-population regime, and establishing the computational theory for it could be worthwhile precisely because the strategic limit changes. I can make a case against treating the proposed mirror as an immediate continuation of Theorem 2; I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
