The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a substantive but formally weak mirror. Under the programme’s strict anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor: it proves no \( \mathrm{P} \), NP-hardness, parameterized, approximation, or encoding result. Its numbered results are structural.

The best structural anchor is Theorem 15, proved in this paper. It states that if \( \triangleright \) is a comparison relation and \(P\) is \(D_c\)-stable, then:

  • every sequence of preferred merge and split operations reaches \(P\);
  • \(P\) is the unique \(D_p\)-stable partition; and
  • \(P\) is the unique \(D_c\)-stable partition.

Theorem 10, also proved here, gives the supporting global characterization: a partition is \(D_p\)-stable exactly when it is \( \triangleright \)-maximal, and a \(D_p\)-stable partition exists whenever \( \triangleright \) is semi-linear.

My lead research question would be:

*Continuous \(D_c\)-Stable Merge–Split Normal Form.*

An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \( \mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{T} \) with \( \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1 \), and a finitely represented type-symmetric coalition-value function \(g\). Type \(t\) represents agents identical in every preference, valuation, endowment, and coalition-relevant parameter. The population is an atomless space \(X\), partitioned into type classes of masses \( \mu_t \).

A coalition is a measurable subset \(S\subseteq X\), represented only by its type-composition vector \(z(S)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{T}\). A population partition \( \mathcal P \) assigns every individual to exactly one coalition; individuals are not fractionally assigned. It can be represented compactly by a finite measure \( \Pi \) over coalition-composition vectors satisfying

\[ \int z\,d\Pi(z)=\mu . \]

For a finite collection \( \mathcal C \) of deviating coalitions, define

\[ V(\mathcal C)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal C} g(z(S)). \]

The restriction \( \mathcal C[\mathcal P] \) is defined exactly as in the paper: intersect the deviating population with the incumbent coalitions. The partition \( \mathcal P \) is continuously \(D_c\)-stable if, whenever \( \mathcal C[\mathcal P]\neq\mathcal C \),

\[ V(\mathcal C[\mathcal P])>V(\mathcal C). \]

A merge replaces several positive-mass coalitions by their union when this strictly increases \(V\); a split replaces one coalition by a measurable finite partition when this strictly increases \(V\).

The computational task is to output a finite rational description of a \(D_c\)-stable partition, or report that none exists, and to decide whether every finite-support starting partition and every sequence of improving merge/split moves terminates at the same partition. A solution therefore consists of the coalition-pattern measure \( \Pi^\star \), together with a stability certificate and, for the confluence version, a certificate that no infinite improving sequence or alternative terminal partition exists.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: \(n\gg |T|\), with many interchangeable participants of each type. Plausible settings include large organizations forming teams from a small number of skill/preference types, or exchange communities with repeated endowment and preference profiles. Clearing denominators in \( \mu \) recovers finite societies with many cloned players, so this is genuinely a population continuization rather than merely fractionalizing an outcome.

The authors would recognize the mirror: it preserves their partitions, comparison relation, \(D_c\)-stability notion, and merge/split operations. The necessary co-modification is that coalition values become functions of type composition and population mass. For restricted piecewise-linear value functions, the \(D_p\) version looks like a candidate Class A configuration or column-generation problem. The full \(D_c\) confluence problem is more plausibly Class C: the paper’s finite termination argument depends on there being only finitely many partitions, whereas an atomless population admits infinitely many increasingly fine deviations and potentially infinite improvement sequences.

The weakest point is decisive. Theorem 15 assumes stability and proves structural uniqueness; it does not provide an algorithm for finding \(P\), and the paper never studies computational complexity. Moreover, arbitrary comparison relations are not given by a finite input representation. Thus this is a natural continuous extension of the paper’s central mathematics, but not a compliant continuization of a named computational result. If the anchor requirement is enforced literally, the correct verdict is: promising structural mirror, but no eligible computational anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s formal gate: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 10 and Theorem 15 are structural statements about an already supplied comparison relation \( \triangleright \). They contain no encoded input, search or decision problem, running-time claim, approximation guarantee, or complexity classification. Theorem 15’s termination argument merely uses the fact that a finite player set has finitely many partitions. That is not a computational result in the ChoCo sense.

The proponent’s two anchors therefore fail together. A continuous version of Theorem 10 would say that \(D_p\)-stability is equivalent to maximality under some continuous comparison relation. But that is still a characterization, not an algorithmic problem. A continuous version of Theorem 15 would say that improving merge–split sequences converge to a stable partition. Again, the paper supplies neither a finite representation of the comparison relation nor an algorithm for finding the partition.

The proposed model also does not actually continuize those theorems without adding almost all of the substantive content. It replaces the paper’s arbitrary \( \triangleright \) by a particular additive welfare function \(V(\mathcal C)=\sum g(z(S))\), assumes that coalition value depends only on type composition, imposes a finite type space, chooses a representation of measurable partitions, and asks for a finite rational certificate. Each of these may be reasonable, but together they define a new computational coalition-formation problem. The paper proves no result about it.

There is a more specific problem with the proposed representation by a measure \( \Pi \) over coalition-composition vectors. The operation \( \mathcal C[\mathcal P] \) depends on how a deviating coalition intersects the incumbent coalitions. The marginal distribution of incumbent coalition compositions does not record that incidence structure. Two partitions can have the same \( \Pi \) while inducing different intersection patterns for the same deviation. Thus \( \Pi \) is sufficient for an aggregate welfare objective, but not for the paper’s \(D_c\)-stability condition. Retaining the full measurable partition repairs this, but then the output is generally an infinite-dimensional object with no finite certificate supplied by the paper.

One can impose finite-support partitions, a restricted catalogue of coalition patterns, and piecewise-linear \(g\), perhaps obtaining a configuration LP. That would be a legitimate new ChoCo project. It would not, however, be a computational mirror of Theorem 10 or Theorem 15: the tractability would come from the newly imposed representation and value structure, while the paper contributes only the abstract merge–split vocabulary. Conversely, preserving the paper’s generic Nash, leximin, hedonic, or exchange-economy comparisons requires defining genuinely new continuum orders and new representations of stability.

The high-multiplicity story itself is not the objection. Large organizations with repeated skill types could sensibly motivate a continuous coalition model. The objection is that this plausibility does not turn a structural finite-player theorem into a computational result. If the anchor rule is relaxed, the proponent has identified a potentially worthwhile new research direction. Under the stated rule, however, both proposed anchors fail, and the paper provides no compliant continuous mirror to green.
