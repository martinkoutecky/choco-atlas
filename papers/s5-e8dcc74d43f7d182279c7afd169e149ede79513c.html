The continuous mirror question

Given an atomless probability space \((X,\mu)\) and a tagged individual \(h\) of zero population mass, choose a measurable undirected link relation \(E\) on \(X\cup\{h\}\). Let \(\deg_X(x)\) count \(x\)'s neighbors in \(X\), and require \(\int_X \deg_X(x)\,d\mu(x)<\infty\). Require every pair of distinct vertices to have a common neighbor, and minimize \(\ell(E)=\frac{1}{2}\int_X\deg_X(x)\,d\mu(x)+\int_X\mathbf{1}_{\{xh\in E\}}\,d\mu(x)\); return an optimal \(E\) and a proof of optimality.

The model it lives in

A sparse measurable graphing on a unit-mass population \(X\), with a tagged null individual \(h\). Links are counted per participant, not by product mass; the objective is \(\ell(E)\). The windmill limit links every \(x\in X\) to \(h\) and pairs the members of \(X\) by a measurable matching, giving candidate cost \(\frac{3}{2}\).

The objection that survived

The mirror uses a tagged zero-mass hub and a pointwise matching, which an ordinary finite-type distribution does not encode; this is nonfatal if the network is modeled separately as a sparse graphing, but that modeling choice is essential.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a narrow network-design mirror, anchored only in Theorem 3, proved in the paper. It is an exact optimization result, though the paper does not frame it as a P-versus-hardness classification. The theorem says that a network in which friend-based comparisons can produce a complete ranking needs at least \(3(n-1)/2\) links when \(n\) is odd and \(3n/2-1\) when \(n\) is even. For odd \(n\), the unique sparsest architecture is the friendship network; for even \(n\), it is the modified windmill with one three-node sail. Theorem 1’s mechanism-existence characterization helps explain why this network property matters, but it is not itself a computational anchor.

Call the mirror Minimum-Link Complete Friend Observation\(_\infty\). Consider a mass-one cohort \(X\) of interchangeable peer graders, with \(\mu(A)\) denoting the fraction in any measurable group \(A\), and one distinguished editor \(h\) whose population share tends to zero. Choose a measurable undirected link relation \(E\) on \(X\cup\{h\}\). Require every pair of distinct agents—including \(h\) and any \(x\in X\)—to have a third agent linked to both. Among feasible networks, minimize links per ordinary participant:

\[ \ell(E)=\frac{1}{2}\int_X \deg_{X}(x)\,d\mu(x) +\int_X \mathbf{1}_{\{xh\in E\}}\,d\mu(x). \]

A solution is a feasible network attaining the minimum, together with a proof of optimality. The finite windmill suggests a candidate of cost \(3/2\): connect every grader to \(h\), and pair graders through a measurable matching. Any two graders share \(h\); \(h\) and a grader share that grader’s matched partner.

This fits a large peer-grading or review program with many structurally interchangeable participants and an editor who can coordinate or verify comparisons. The paper itself discusses designing reviewer networks and the tradeoff between link count and the hub’s workload. The type is the participant’s role, rank preferences, and link cost; mass is the share of graders in that role. The output is a network, so the continuization is of the population, not of the ranking outcome.

I would expect the basic version to be tractable, perhaps with \(3/2\) as the optimal per-capita cost, but Theorem 3 does not prove that for continuum networks. Further questions include whether the minimizer is unique up to measure-preserving relabeling, whether near-minimum networks must resemble a windmill, and what happens under a cap on the editor’s workload. This mirror covers only Theorem 3’s sparse complete-observation design problem; it does not extend the paper’s incentive-compatibility results or its empirical analysis.

The weak point is substantial: the limit retains a distinguished zero-mass hub and identity-specific matching links, so it is a mixed continuum-and-exceptional-agent model rather than a plain distribution over positive-mass types. If those links and roles must be encoded as complete agent types, the type compression may fail. The finite theorem gives this proposal a concrete foothold, but does not settle whether the continuum problem remains the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor, but its continuous mirror faces a choice of scaling that changes the problem. The theorem is an exact extremal result for finite graphs: minimize an integer number of links subject to every pair having a common friend. Its input is just \(n\), and the paper gives the minimum and the unique sparsest architecture. It does not open a complexity question with a family of instances, costs, or capacities.

If links are priced by mass in an ordinary continuum model, the requirement can become nearly free. Let a broker group \(B\) have mass \(\epsilon>0\), and let everyone in \(B\) link to everyone. Leave the remaining agents unlinked to one another. Any two ordinary agents share a broker; a broker and an ordinary agent share another broker; two brokers share an ordinary agent. The mass of links per capita is \(\epsilon(1-\epsilon)+\epsilon^2/2\), which tends to zero. The infimum is zero and is not attained while a positive mass of brokers is required. Relax the requirement to hold only for almost every pair, and null groups can disappear from the constraint altogether.

The proposed cost of \(3/2\) preserves the finite theorem’s per-person count, but only in a sparse graph limit. That limit needs a zero-mass hub with unbounded degree and a specific matching among the atomless graders. A distribution over a fixed set of types does not encode those features. Including each person’s partner or neighborhood in their type makes the type space identity-specific; omitting it loses the link structure the theorem characterizes. The hub is also a ranked, strategic participant in the paper. Treating it as an editor outside the ranked population changes the mechanism.

A sparse limit with a tagged hub and a measurable matching is coherent, and the authors might recognize it as a useful network-design question. That is the negative case’s weak point. But in the homogeneous setting it appears to carry the finite windmill answer into a new graph-limit formalism, without adding the algorithmic question ChoCo is organized to study. Adding workload caps or heterogeneous link costs could make a richer optimization problem, and the paper’s discussion of reviewer burden gives those additions some motivation. They would be new constraints, though, rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 3.

So I would argue against spending programme time on this anchor: mass-weighted links degenerate, while the faithful per-capita version depends on a singular hub and identity-level pairing. I would not call that airtight. A carefully defined sparse continuum network model could still be worthwhile, but the case for it rests on a new graph-limit framework rather than on a robust high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s result.
