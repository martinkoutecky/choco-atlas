The continuous mirror question

Given colleges \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_u\), each type specifies a preference list and integer score \(s_{tu}\) at each college. For a nonnegative integer cutoff vector \(\ell\), all mass of type \(t\) goes to its first listed college \(u\) with \(s_{tu}\ge\ell_u\), or remains unmatched. Compute the applicant-best and applicant-worst H-stable cutoff vectors \(\ell_A^H,\ell_C^H\), where H-feasibility requires assigned mass \(x_u(\ell)\le\kappa_u\) and H-stability requires lowering any positive \(\ell_u\) by one to violate that college’s capacity; certify that every H-stable vector \(\ell\) satisfies \(\ell_A^H\le\ell\le\ell_C^H\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete preference-and-score profiles carrying rational mass. Integer score cutoffs assign each type’s mass to its first eligible college; rational per-capita capacities define H-feasibility, and lowering a positive cutoff by one must exceed capacity for H-stability. The task is to compute the componentwise applicant-best and applicant-worst stable cutoff vectors.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s applicant-best and applicant-worst H-stable limits; it leaves the L-stable result, comparisons between H and L stability, and the manipulation examples aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the extremal vectors be computed exactly in time polynomial in the number of colleges \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does aggregating types preserve the paper’s procedures with a support-sensitive bound, or do some instances require work proportional to a scaled cohort size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. There is no named NP-hardness result or theorem placing one of the paper’s problems in a complexity class, and Theorem 4.1 gives no running-time bound. It is an algorithmic optimality result: for any H-stable score limits \(l\), the applicant-oriented limits \(l_A^H\) and college-oriented limits \(l_C^H\) satisfy
\[ l_A^H \le l \le l_C^H. \]
Thus the applicant-oriented algorithm finds the best H-stable limits for applicants, while the college-oriented algorithm finds the worst. I would use this as the anchor, with the complexity caveat stated plainly.

Mass H-stable score-limit extremes is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. An instance consists of colleges \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of applicant types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational per-capita capacity \(\kappa_u\) for each college. A type \(t\) specifies the applicant’s complete preference list and score \(s_{tu}\) at each college; applicants of that type are identical for everything the admissions rule uses. A decision is an integer score-limit vector \(\ell\). Each unit of type \(t\)’s mass goes to the first college on its list where \(s_{tu}\ge\ell_u\), or remains unmatched if there is none.

Let \(x_u(\ell)\) be the mass assigned to college \(u\). The limits are H-feasible when \(x_u(\ell)\le\kappa_u\) for every college. They are H-stable when they are feasible and, for each \(\ell_u>0\), lowering \(\ell_u\) by one while holding the other limits fixed makes the assignment infeasible. The task is to return the applicant-best and applicant-worst H-stable limit vectors, with their induced assignments, such that every H-stable vector lies componentwise between them. The objective is this componentwise extremality, not a new scalar welfare measure.

The type and mass interpretation fits centralized admissions for a large cohort: for example, applicants to a set of degree programmes with recurring preference lists and standardized score profiles. A type includes both the full list and every programme-specific score, so the support \(\tau=|T|\) can be much smaller than the number \(N\) of applicants only in regimes where these complete profiles repeat. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort with that profile, and \(\kappa_u\) is the programme’s quota divided by cohort size. The paper itself points to Azevedo and Leshno’s continuum-student admissions model; that makes a continuum of applicants a familiar setting, while the H-stable tied-score rule remains the specific object here.

I expect this problem to be tractable, in the support-sensitive sense. The paper’s applicant- and college-oriented procedures can be run on type masses: identical applicants move together, colleges update limits at represented score tiers, and each type has only finitely many colleges to propose to. This suggests an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the masses, capacities, and scores, rather than in \(N\). That bound is a question for the mirror, not a claim made by Theorem 4.1. For discrete instances with \(n_t\) applicants of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(\kappa_u=Q_u/N\) reproduces the paper’s feasibility and stability tests after scaling by \(N\). Conversely, rational masses and capacities can be scaled to an integer cohort. The mirror therefore has a direct high-multiplicity dictionary.

The scope is Theorem 4.1’s extremal H-stable limits. It does not cover the paper’s manipulation examples or make a claim about the other results. Further questions include whether the type-aggregated algorithm has the expected polynomial bit complexity, how the analogous L-stable computation compares, and how the extrema change when masses or per-capita quotas vary.

The weakest point is that the paper studies finite applicants and gives no complexity analysis in \(m,\tau,L\). Also, complete types include every score and preference, so their support may approach \(N\) in some admissions markets. The mirror is strongest where standardized profiles recur often; its value would then be an exact computation over a compressed high-multiplicity input.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.1 is an order-theoretic result, not a complexity result. It says the applicant- and college-oriented algorithms produce the extreme H-stable score limits; it gives no bound in \(m,\tau,L\), and its main content is the structure of stable outcomes. A continuous complexity question would therefore extend the paper’s result rather than mirror a complexity claim it makes.

That objection does not defeat the proposed mirror. Large admissions cohorts with recurring preference lists and programme-specific score profiles give a credible high-multiplicity regime. The right type includes the full list and all relevant scores, so applicants within a type are interchangeable for the theorem. Their mass can be assigned together under the score-limit rule, while per-capita quotas preserve the capacity condition. If tie groups have positive mass, lowering a cutoff can still violate capacity; the stability notion does not vanish. Rational masses and quotas also scale to a finite replicated instance, giving a clear high-multiplicity dictionary.

A stronger version of the mirror would ask for the exact support-sensitive complexity of computing both extreme H-stable limit vectors from that mass input. The algorithms appear amenable to type aggregation: each type can progress through its finite preference list, and colleges update cutoffs at represented score levels. That makes a simple algorithm plausible, but an easy answer is not by itself a reason the question is ill-posed or out of scope. The paper’s mention of a continuum-student admissions model is likewise supporting context, not evidence that this computational question is already answered.

So the negative case is weak. The best remaining concern is about research value: the continuous problem may reduce to a routine weighted version of deferred acceptance, rather than expose a substantial new complexity landscape. That is a reason to temper expectations, not a convincing reason that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
