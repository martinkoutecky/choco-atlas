The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\) and a society \(\mu\) with finite support over weak-order types, decide whether every triple \(S\subseteq C\) has an alternative \(a\in S\) and a rank position \(r\in\{1,2,3\}\) such that \(\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\cap V_S}\mu_t\,mPM_t^S(a,r)=0\), where \(V_S\) is the set of types concerned over \(S\), and \(mPM_t^S(a,r)\) indicates whether type \(t\) permits \(a\) at position \(r\) within \(S\).

The model it lives in

Represent the society by rational masses \(\mu_t\) on a finite support of complete weak-order types. For each triple, aggregate the mass of concerned types that permit each alternative at each rank; value restriction holds exactly when at least one such cell has zero mass.

The objection that survived

An arbitrarily small positive mass on a new type can destroy value restriction, so the test is sensitive to changes in support.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1''’s value-restriction check; it leaves aside the odd-concerned-count assumption and the resulting guarantee of majority transitivity.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is there a natural mass-based condition that yields a sufficient transitivity guarantee for weighted majority?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result: it does not establish that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classify its complexity. The strongest foothold for a positive case is Theorem 1’’, which I would count under your formulation exception. It gives a \(0\)-\(1\) criterion for checking Sen’s condition, and the authors calculate with that criterion in Example 2. The underlying transitivity theorem, Theorem 1, is credited to Sen (1966); Theorem 1’’ is the paper’s own reformulation of it using membership preference maps.

Theorem 1’’ says that for a finite set of alternatives and individuals with weak-order preferences, majority yields a transitive social preference satisfying the stated Arrow conditions if, for every triple, the number of concerned individuals is odd and some alternative-position cell is zero across those individuals’ membership preference maps. In the paper’s notation, that cell condition is \(\min_i\min_j\{\sum_{k\in V_t}mPM^{(k)}_{ti,j}\}=0\).

Lead mirror: Continuous Sen-condition verification. Take a large public consultation in which millions of respondents rank the same \(m\) alternatives, but the observed rankings have support size \(\tau\) much smaller than the population. A type is a complete weak ordering; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of respondents with type \(t\). Given the listed types and their positive rational masses, decide whether every triple satisfies Sen’s value-restriction condition in the population’s support. For each triple \(S\), the test asks whether there are an alternative \(a\in S\) and a rank position \(r\in\{1,2,3\}\) such that
\[ \sum_{\substack{t\text{ in the support}\\t\text{ concerned over }S}} \mu_t\,mPM_t(a,r)=0. \]
A “yes” instance returns yes; a “no” instance can return a triple for which every alternative-position cell has positive mass. This is a direct population-measure version of the equation in Theorem 1’’. The authors could plausibly recognize it: their stated aim is to make Sen’s condition easier to examine with a computer.

I expect this verification problem to be tractable: scan the \(\binom{m}{3}\) triples and the \(\tau\) listed types, using exact arithmetic. This mirror covers the paper’s testable value-restriction condition, not the whole majority-transitivity guarantee. The odd number of concerned individuals has no invariant continuous counterpart, and I would not quietly substitute a parity condition on the support.

That is also the weakest point. The masses matter only through whether they are positive; the test is essentially support-based. And while the paper supplies a computationally usable characterization, it does not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. The mirror is therefore narrow, but it is anchored in the paper’s explicit computer-testing purpose rather than in an unrelated continuization. It raises the further question of what conditions on weighted masses could replace the oddness hypothesis when the goal is transitivity of weighted majority itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1’’ reformulates Sen’s value-restriction condition as a matrix equation; it does not classify a computational problem or give an algorithm. The authors’ stated interest in computer testing gives the proponent a foothold, but the proposed task is a new recognition problem built from that reformulation.

For the proposed continuous test, the masses do no work: because each listed type has positive mass, a cell’s weighted sum is zero exactly when no supported type contributes to it. The test therefore depends only on which rankings occur. It scans the support across triples and positions, and its output is unchanged by any redistribution of mass that preserves the support. That is a sensible finite-support check, but it offers little of the continuous-population structure ChoCo is meant to investigate.

The better mirror would take the majority-transitivity aim seriously and ask whether weighted majority over a distribution is transitive. But that is a different question from verifying Sen’s sufficient condition. The paper supplies no computational result for it. One can calculate pairwise weighted margins from the listed types and check for cycles; that makes the question well-defined, but does not turn this paper’s contribution into a complexity problem. Nor does the theorem’s oddness hypothesis transfer canonically: the same population proportions need not encode a unique finite headcount or parity. Dropping oddness and studying weighted-majority transitivity may be worthwhile, but it would be a new result, not a continuous version of the paper’s theorem.

There is also a stability problem for any attempt to give the zero-cell test a robust population interpretation: an arbitrarily small mass on a type that fills the missing cell can destroy value restriction. A threshold-based variant could avoid that fragility, but the threshold would add a new condition absent from the paper.

This is the strongest negative case I can make, but it does not establish the universal claim. Large consultations with repeated rankings are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors explicitly want their condition to be computer-testable. The proponent’s mirror is narrow and largely support-based; still, I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every scenario.
