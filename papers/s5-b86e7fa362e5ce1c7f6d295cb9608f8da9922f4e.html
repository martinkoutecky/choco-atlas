The continuous mirror question

Given a common set of \(n\) entities, a distribution with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) over supported binary clusterings \(t\), where \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\), and a rational threshold \(K\), decide whether there is a binary clustering \(s^\star\) such that \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{hd}(s^\star,t)(n-\mathrm{hd}(s^\star,t))\le K\).

The model it lives in

The population consists of respondents who each submit a binary clustering of the same entities. A type \(t\) is a complete clustering submission, \(\mu_t\) is its population share, and the consensus \(s^\star\) remains a discrete binary clustering; its objective is the expected Mirkin distance to a respondent’s submission.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers binary clustering aggregation under the Mirkin objective, including the NP-hardness, ETH lower bound, and support-parameterized algorithmic result; it leaves multiway clustering and other distance measures or aggregation functions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(2^{2^\tau}\) dependence of the weighted algorithm be improved, or is there a matching lower bound in the support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Mass Mirkin Distance Minimization. The population consists of sources or respondents who each submit a binary clustering of the same \(n\) entities. A type \(t\in\{0,1\}^n\) is one complete clustering; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that clustering. Given the \(\tau\) supported types, their positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(K\), ask whether there is a binary clustering \(s^\star\in\{0,1\}^n\) such that

\[ \sum_{t}\mu_t\,\mathrm{hd}(s^\star,t)\bigl(n-\mathrm{hd}(s^\star,t)\bigr)\le K. \]

A solution is the clustering \(s^\star\). The expression is the expected Mirkin distance from a population member’s clustering to the consensus. The output remains a discrete binary clustering; the population of input clusterings is what becomes continuous.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime when many contributors use shared tools or templates, so the number \(M\) of contributors is large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct clusterings is comparatively small. The paper’s ensemble-of-views setting is one possible source of these inputs. Its connection to voting theory and binary-relation aggregation offers another: contributors submit equivalence relations over a common set of alternatives. A type records everything the objective uses about a contributor’s input.

The discrete problem embeds directly. For a multiset \(S\) of \(M\) clusterings, set \(\mu_t\) to the multiplicity of \(t\) divided by \(M\). Then the continuous objective is the paper’s total Mirkin distance divided by \(M\), so threshold \(k\) becomes \(K=k/M\). This is a polynomial-size transformation on the paper’s explicit instances. I claim hardness transfers in this direction; expanding arbitrary binary-rational masses back into repeated copies could require many copies.

Lead anchor — Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that Mirkin Distance Minimization is NP-hard: given \(m\) binary strings of length \(n\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether there is a length-\(n\) string whose sum of Mirkin distances to the input strings is at most \(k\). The paper proves this by a polynomial many-one reduction from NAE-3SAT. The mass problem above is the normalized version of that same underlying decision problem. I expect hardness to transfer: the paper’s instances map to rational distributions with exactly the same yes/no answer.

Theorem 1’s ETH strengthening — Corollary 1, derived here from Proposition 2 and prior ETH results. It states that, unless ETH fails, no algorithm solves every instance \(I'\) in time \(2^{o(\hat n)}|I'|^{O(1)}\), where \(\hat n\) is the input-string length. The continuous question is whether Mass Mirkin Distance Minimization admits such a subexponential-in-\(n\) algorithm. The paper’s constructed instances have polynomial-size representations as distributions: count each repeated string and encode its multiplicity as a rational mass. Thus this lower bound also transfers. This is a stronger running-time boundary for the same mirror, not a separate change to the problem.

Support-parameter anchor — Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that Mirkin Distance Minimization is fixed-parameter tractable in the number \(m\) of input strings, with a column-type enumeration running in \(O(2^{2^m}mn)\) time. The corresponding continuous question is whether the weighted-mass problem is FPT in support size \(\tau\), with rational bit length \(L\) included in the input-size factors. I expect it is: group entity-columns by their \(\tau\)-bit signature across supported types. The paper’s exchange argument implies that an optimum assigns equal output bits to columns with identical signatures; its strict-improvement calculation extends to positive rational weights. There are at most \(2^\tau\) signatures, so enumerating their output bits gives a direct \(2^{2^\tau}\operatorname{poly}(n,\tau,L)\)-time algorithm. This is a weighted extension of the paper’s proof, not a result the paper states. It gives a parameterized tractability result alongside the unrestricted hardness.

So the unrestricted problem falls on the hardness-transfers side of the programme’s trichotomy, while the support-parameterized version has a credible tractable algorithm. The mirror covers binary clustering aggregation under the Mirkin objective; it does not claim results for arbitrary multiway clusterings or the other distance and aggregation functions mentioned in the paper.

The weak point is the population interpretation: the paper’s examples are often a finite collection of data views, rather than a literal large population of interchangeable people or sources. The mirror asks us to view each submitted clustering as one population member’s input, which is natural for a large contributor or voting setting but less compelling when each view is unique by design. The mathematical fit is unusually tight, though: the paper already permits multisets, and its voting-theory connection supports treating the clusterings as population opinions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about the paper’s setting, not the mathematics. Its motivating inputs are often different analyses of the same entities: one clustering from friendship links, another from location traces, and so on. Those views are not naturally members of a population, and they may all be distinct by design. In that regime, treating them as interchangeable respondents would change the story, and when nearly every respondent has a unique full clustering, the continuous representation offers little compression.

That objection does not rule out the stronger mirror. Ask a large group to partition the same set of alternatives, and let each respondent’s complete partition be their type. The mass objective is then the expected Mirkin distance to a consensus partition. This is a coherent population model, and the paper’s own connections to voting theory and binary-relation aggregation make it plausible that the authors would recognize it as their problem. The mismatch with the paper’s main examples is a limitation, but not a fundamental objection.

Theorem 1 survives the move cleanly. For a discrete multiset, assign each clustering its multiplicity divided by the number of respondents; the mass objective is the paper’s objective divided by that number, with the threshold scaled accordingly. The reduction instances therefore transfer their NP-hardness. Theorem 1’s ETH consequence transfers as well: the construction uses polynomially many explicitly listed copies, so their multiplicities can be encoded as rational masses with polynomial-size descriptions. These anchors establish hardness for the mirror rather than defeating it.

The support-parameter anchor also survives. For supported types, group entity-columns by their signature across those types. If two columns have the same signature but the proposed consensus gives them different bits, making them agree strictly lowers the weighted objective: the exchange improvement is positive for each type and remains positive after weighting by masses that sum to one. There are at most \(2^\tau\) signatures, so enumerating the consensus bit for each gives a \(2^{2^\tau}\operatorname{poly}(n,\tau,L)\)-time algorithm. That is a credible weighted extension of Theorem 2.

So the negative case is weak. The mirror is a fairly direct weighted version of a problem the paper already formulates for multisets, and the paper’s ensemble-clustering examples do not themselves supply a population. Those points make the contribution feel less novel and less central to the paper’s motivating applications. But they do not eliminate the sensible respondent-based scenario, and no stronger obstruction defeats the three anchors.
