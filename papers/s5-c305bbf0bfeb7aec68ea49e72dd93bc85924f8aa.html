The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a population-continuum analogue of the paper’s mechanism-existence questions. It is not a ChoCo computational mirror under the stated standard: the paper contains no named result about an algorithm, computational complexity, or parameterized complexity.

The closest anchors are Theorem 1, proved here, which says that for \(n\ge4\) no assignment mechanism satisfies upper invariance, lower invariance, ordinal efficiency, and symmetry; and Theorem 2, also proved here, which says that for \(n\ge4\) no mechanism satisfies swap monotonicity, lower invariance, ordinal efficiency, anonymity, neutrality, and non-bossiness. Both are axiomatic nonexistence results, not computational results. They can motivate continuous questions, but I would not present them as qualifying computational anchors.

A plausible setting is a large school-allocation system: many students rank four school categories, and the supply of seats in each category scales with the student population. Let \(T\) be the strict rankings of \(J=\{a,b,c,d\}\), and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of students with ranking \(t\). Each ranking type receives a lottery vector \(x_t\), with
\[ \sum_{j\in J}x_{t,j}=1,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{t,j}=\tfrac14\quad\text{for each }j. \]
Thus the society is a distribution over preference types, with per-capita supply \(\sigma_j=1/4\). A large population can have support size \(\tau\le24\), so its number of students can greatly exceed its number of distinct ranking types.

My lead candidate is Continuous Upper-and-Lower-Invariant Assignment Feasibility. Its instance is the four school categories, equal per-capita supplies, and the domain of all finite-support rational populations \(\mu\) over \(T\). The question is whether there is one rule \(F\) defined on that domain that returns feasible assignments, is ordinally efficient, and treats everyone with the same ranking identically. For every adjacent swap from \(P\) to \(P'\), also require upper and lower invariance when an \(\varepsilon\)-mass cohort reports \(P'\): set \(\mu'=\mu-\varepsilon e_P+\varepsilon e_{P'}\), and compare the cohort’s lottery \(x_P(\mu)\) before the change with \(x_{P'}(\mu')\) after it. The rule \(F\) is a solution if it satisfies all those conditions for every allowed population and cohort change.

This is a recognizable population version of Theorem 1’s assignment-rule question. In this formulation, I expect the no-go result to transfer directly: the four-agent witness can be represented by four cohorts of mass \(1/4\), and the allocation and feasibility constraints have the same form. That is a transfer of an impossibility proof, not transferred computational hardness; the programme’s A/B/C complexity trichotomy does not apply. Theorem 2 has a separate possible analogue with swap monotonicity, lower invariance, anonymity, neutrality, and cohort-level non-bossiness. Its expected outcome is likewise a direct no-go from the four-agent witness, not a complexity classification.

The authors would recognize the allocation-rule problem and the ranking distribution as a population model. The soft spot is the incentive interpretation: the paper’s axioms concern one agent changing their report. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass, so the population distribution does not change with their report. The formulation above instead tests changes by positive-mass cohorts. If the authors require unilateral, infinitesimal deviations, the finite four-agent proof does not establish the proposed continuous impossibility. That gap is why I would call this a plausible axiomatic mirror, not a strong ChoCo computational mirror.

The mirror covers Theorems 1 and 2 only. It suggests further questions about which restricted population domains admit such rules, whether approximate versions of the axioms can coexist, and what the genuine atomless unilateral version implies. The continuous object here is the population distribution; the paper’s random assignment probabilities are already part of its finite-agent model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither new theorem is a computational result. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 prove that certain combinations of axioms are incompatible; the other results are also axiomatic. The paper gives no decision problem, complexity bound, or algorithm to continuize. So these are poor anchors for ChoCo’s computational programme, even though they could motivate a separate axiomatic study.

The proposed school-allocation model is sensible as a population model: one can describe a society by ranking masses \(\mu_t\), give each type an assignment vector \(x_t(\mu)\), and scale school capacities per capita. But the proposed \(\varepsilon\)-mass swap changes the reports of a bloc. The paper’s axioms concern one agent changing their report. A bloc change is a new group-deviation question, and the four-agent proof does not transfer automatically: its steps compare profiles after individual reports change, whereas the proposed continuum rule is evaluated at different population distributions.

A better atomless version would compare a single agent’s assignment \(x_t(\mu)\) with \(x_{t'}(\mu)\) after they report an adjacent ranking, holding \(\mu\) fixed. That avoids treating a positive-mass bloc as one agent, but it is a new axiom-compatibility problem. The paper’s proof does not settle it, and even a new impossibility theorem would remain axiomatic rather than computational.

Theorem 2’s non-bossiness condition makes the mismatch sharper. For a measure-zero agent, a unilateral report cannot change the aggregate distribution, so aggregate non-bossiness is vacuous. Requiring that a cohort’s report not affect anyone else instead creates a group version of non-bossiness. Also, if the continuum mechanism assigns the same vector to everyone of a given type, symmetry is built into the representation rather than an independent constraint.

I would not claim that no atomless assignment question could be worthwhile: the fixed-\(\mu\) version might be interesting in assignment theory. That is the weak point in a universal negative. But it does not rescue a ChoCo mirror of this paper. Turning it into a computational question would require choosing a new task—such as computing approximately axiom-compatible rules on a restricted domain—and that task would go beyond the paper’s results.
