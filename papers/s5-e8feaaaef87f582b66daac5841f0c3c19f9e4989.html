The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(A\), a family \(S_1,\ldots,S_r\) of \(k\)-subsets, exact rational distributions \(\mu_j\) over vote-prediction pairs in \(\mathcal L(S_j)^2\), and the paper’s uniform-prior concentric-mixture Mallows promise with parameters satisfying Assumption 1 and a common hidden ranking \(\pi^\star\), return the top-\(t\) set of \(\pi^\star\) using a specified \(t\)-consistent aggregator.

The model it lives in

A continuum crowd induces, for each queried subset \(S_j\), rational mass \(\mu_j(\sigma,\rho)\) on vote-prediction report types. Compute the population SP score for each partial ranking in \(\mathcal L(S_j)\), select a maximizing ranking on each subset, and apply the specified \(t\)-consistent aggregator.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the partial-ranking recovery guarantee in Theorem 1 and the multi-subset top-\(t\) guarantee in Corollary 1. It leaves aside the experiments, the Mallows model-fit analysis, and the other aggregation comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the dependence on \(k!\) in exact-mass evaluation be reduced, especially for the paper’s pairwise variant?
  • Which weaker noise assumptions preserve identifiability of \(\pi^\star\) from the report-mass distributions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s partial-ranking recovery problem. One caveat up front: the paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its named theoretical results concern sample complexity. Those still give a concrete anchor for a continuous-population problem, but they do not establish a conventional running-time result.

The regime I find plausible is a very large crowd asked to rank objectively measurable alternatives—say, products by sales or places by population. Each person sees only a small, fixed-size subset. Within a subset, a person’s complete type for the aggregation problem is their vote and prediction report; if expertise class matters to the reporting model, it can be included too. The society’s mass on a report type is the fraction of respondents who produce it. With subset size \(k\) fixed, there are at most \((k!)^2\) Rank-Rank report pairs per subset. So the number of people can grow far beyond the number of distinct types, even as the full alternative set grows. The paper’s expert/non-expert mixture gives this regime a concrete behavioral model.

For a precise lead problem, call it Continuous Partial-SP Top-\(t\) Recovery. The input is an alternative set \(A\), a family \(S\) of subsets of size \(k\), and, for each \(S_j\), an exact rational distribution \(\mu_j\) over report pairs \((\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal L(S_j)^2\). Here \(\sigma\) is a voter’s partial-ranking vote and \(\rho\) is their partial-ranking prediction. The instance is promised to arise from the paper’s uniform-prior, concentric-mixture Mallows model around one hidden ground-truth ranking \(\pi^\star\), with expert fraction \(p\), dispersions \(\phi_E<\phi_{NE}<1\), and the separation assumption used by the paper. A fixed, efficiently computable \(t\)-consistent aggregation rule is also specified. The task is to return the set of the top \(t\) alternatives in \(\pi^\star\). A solution is that set.

The population quantities can be computed directly from the input masses:
\[ f_j(\sigma)=\sum_{\rho}\mu_j(\sigma,\rho), \qquad g_j(\rho\mid\sigma)=\frac{\mu_j(\sigma,\rho)}{f_j(\sigma)}. \]
The exact SP score is
\[ V_j(\sigma)=f_j(\sigma)\sum_{\sigma'\in\mathcal L(S_j)} \frac{g_j(\sigma'\mid\sigma)}{g_j(\sigma\mid\sigma')}. \]
The continuous algorithm selects the maximizing partial ranking on each subset, then applies the specified aggregation rule. Under the paper’s separation condition, the local maximizing rankings are the true restrictions of \(\pi^\star\); the consistency condition then gives the correct top \(t\).

My lead anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper. In the same Mallows setting and under the same assumption as Theorem 1, it says that if each subset in \(S\) receives at least
\[ n\ \ge\ \frac{k!\sqrt{10k\log(2|S|k/\delta)}}{\mu} \]
samples, where
\[ \mu= p\frac{Z(\phi_E,m-k)}{Z(\phi_E)}\phi_E^{k(k-1)/2} +(1-p)\frac{Z(\phi_{NE},m-k)}{Z(\phi_{NE})}\phi_{NE}^{k(k-1)/2}, \]
then Partial-SP, followed by any \(t\)-consistent voting rule, recovers the top \(t\) alternatives with probability at least \(1-\delta\). This anchors the multi-subset problem directly: it handles local partial information and a global top-\(t\) answer, rather than merely recovering one small ranking.

The supporting anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here. For one subset \(T\) of size \(k\), under Assumption 1 and the same mixture model, it states that at least
\[ n\ \ge\ \frac{k!\sqrt{10k\log(2k/\delta)}}{\mu} \]
samples suffice for the SP algorithm to recover the true ranking restricted to \(T\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\). Assumption 1 is
\[ \left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^2 \ge 2\left(\frac{Z(\phi_{NE})}{Z(\phi_E)}\right)^2 Z(\phi_{NE},k)\phi_E^{k(k-1)/2}. \]
Here \(Z(\phi,q)\) is the Mallows normalization sum on \(q\) alternatives, and the paper writes \(Z(\phi)\) for the sum on \(m\) alternatives. The theorem’s single-subset counterpart, Continuous SP Partial-Rank Recovery, takes an exact report distribution \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal L(T)^2\) under this promise and asks for the maximizing SP ranking on \(T\); its solution is \(\pi^\star\) restricted to \(T\). The theorem’s finite-sample guarantee supports this exact-population question: with exact masses there is no sampling error, and the paper’s separation argument makes the correct local ranking the unique maximizer.

I expect the lead problem to be tractable when parameterized by \(k\), for a fixed aggregation rule: explicitly evaluating the score over the \(k!\) rankings gives a bound of the form \(f(k)\operatorname{poly}(|S|,L)\), with \(f(k)\) at least factorial. That is an analysis of the continuous problem as stated, not a runtime theorem from the paper. It is not a claim of polynomial time in \(k\). Natural next questions are whether the pairwise version can reduce this dependence, as the paper suggests, and whether the guarantee survives outside the Mallows and expert-separation assumptions.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the theoretical partial-SP recovery results, not the paper’s experiments, all nine elicitation formats, or every claim about ranking recovery. The weakest point is also substantial: the anchors are sample-complexity guarantees, not computational-complexity classifications, and the exact-mass problem is a direct population-limit formulation of the paper’s frequency-based algorithm. That makes the computational contribution modest. Still, the population is genuinely the continuous object here: report-type fractions replace respondent counts, while the task remains recovery of the same ground-truth ranking. The paper’s finite-sample theorem and its exact-population counterpart are distinct questions, so the mirror is a reasonable extension rather than a claim that the paper already studies a continuous society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that they are sample-complexity results, not computational-complexity results. Theorem 1 bounds how many sampled reports suffice for a confidence level; Corollary 1 adds a union bound across subsets. Neither gives a runtime classification or a hard computational problem. That matters here because replacing finite report counts with exact masses removes the very quantity those results analyze: the sample size \(n\) and its associated failure probability \(\delta\).

For the proposed single-subset mirror of Theorem 1, the exact distribution over partial vote–prediction pairs supplies the frequencies and conditionals used by SP directly. For the multi-subset mirror of Corollary 1, the same calculation is repeated for each subset, then passed to the specified aggregation rule. With an explicit mass table, this is finite arithmetic over the report alphabet—at most \(k!\) rankings and \(k!^2\) report pairs per subset—followed by an efficiently computable aggregation step. The resulting running time is polynomial in the explicit table size; as a parameterized bound, factorial dependence on \(k\) is immediate. The papers’ sample bounds do not establish any more interesting computational boundary. If one instead gives a compact Mallows description and asks the algorithm to recover the hidden ranking, that is a different inference problem; the sample-complexity theorems do not classify its runtime.

There is a stronger proposed mirror than the two stated: let \(k\) vary, model the population by a finite-support distribution over complete report types, and ask for exact or robust recovery of the top \(t\) alternatives. But the obstacle remains. Under the paper’s promise, this is still an explicit aggregation calculation; outside that promise, the new difficulty is model inversion or robustness under a changed statistical model. Those could be worthwhile research questions, but neither is a computational result established by Theorem 1 or Corollary 1. More generally, optimizing which subsets to query or how to elicit predictions would add decisions absent from these theorems.

I would not object that high multiplicity is implausible: a large crowd can naturally produce repeated report types, and the proposed population model is coherent. The negative case is narrower: neither named result anchors a substantial ChoCo complexity problem. A continuous mass formulation may be a useful statistical extension, but treating it as a computational continuation risks turning the theorems’ finite-sample guarantees into a routine exact-frequency calculation. That is a strong reason to deprioritize these anchors; it is not an honest proof that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror.
