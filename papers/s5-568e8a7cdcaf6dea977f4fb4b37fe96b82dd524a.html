The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is Proposition 2, but it does not meet your anchor rule: this paper contains no numbered computational result about a problem being tractable, hard, or otherwise classified by complexity.

Proposition 1 says trade occurs with positive probability in every almost strict Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Proposition 2 says that, for every admissible \(F\), there is a unique full-trade equilibrium: voters with intensity below \(w'''\) sell and those above it buy, where \(w'''\) is the unique solution of
\[ 2n\bigl([1-F(w''')]^n-1\bigr) = w'''F(w''')^n-(2n+w''')F(w'''). \]
Proposition 3 says that, in every equilibrium, all voter types expect weakly higher utility when trade is allowed than when it is forbidden, with a positive measure expecting strictly higher utility. These are equilibrium and welfare claims, not computational-complexity results.

There is a plausible *adjacent* continuous model: let a large electorate be a distribution \(\mu\) over complete types \((\text{preferred party},w)\); let each type’s mass choose to sell, buy, or abstain; and define trading and vote shares from those masses. One could ask for a full-trade equilibrium cutoff and its induced price and vote shares. A large electorate under proportional representation, with many voters but relatively few distinct preference-and-intensity types, is a plausible regime.

But that candidate is not a faithful computational mirror of Proposition 2. Its finite-\(n\) equilibrium depends on uncertainty about the other voters’ actions and on each voter’s effect on the market price. In an atomless population those features change or disappear, so the proposition’s threshold equation does not simply carry over. And the resulting task is to characterize a continuum-agent equilibrium—the kind of analytic question the programme explicitly distinguishes from its computational scope. My weakest point is therefore decisive: the paper supplies no named computational anchor, and the most promising continuous extension changes the strategic problem whose result it would supposedly mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–3 concern equilibrium existence, a cutoff characterization, and welfare. They define no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity claim. Proposition 2’s cutoff is the unique root of a scalar equation. Asking how to compute it would require new choices about how \(F\) is represented and what counts as an output; the paper supplies no natural computational task whose complexity the population limit could illuminate.

The best candidate is Proposition 2: represent a large electorate by masses of voters with complete types \((\text{preferred party},w)\), then ask for the full-trade equilibrium and its cutoff. The electorate is a plausible population, but the paper assumes \(w\) has an absolutely continuous distribution. Exact complete types therefore have zero mass, so its stated model has no finite high-multiplicity support. Replacing \(F\) with finitely many intensity classes could create one, but then the task is an equilibrium problem for a new finite-type mass market, not a computational version of Proposition 2. Retaining arbitrary \(F\) leaves the proposed input without a finite encoding.

There is a further obstacle to treating Proposition 2’s finite-\(n\) market as the canonical limit. Its cutoff comes from uncertainty over other voters’ actions and the resulting price and vote-share effects. The paper itself reports that the cutoff grows without bound as \(n\) grows. Since \(F\) has support on \([0,\infty)\), the buyer share \(1-F(w''')\) then tends to zero. The literal full-trade limit has vanishing buyer mass and a price tending to zero; it does not yield a regular positive-mass trading equilibrium. A separately defined atomless market could still be studied, but that would be a new equilibrium model, not a computational mirror of the paper’s result.

Proposition 1’s positive-trade claim is especially fragile as a population question: with mass-zero individuals, a unilateral deviation cannot create a positive mass of buyers and sellers, so the finite game’s strict-equilibrium argument has no direct population counterpart. Proposition 3’s weak welfare comparison also offers little computational content: abstaining supplies the no-trade benchmark, while any strict population gain depends on selecting an equilibrium with positive mass trading.

So the proponent’s strongest candidate is coherent as a possible analytic economics extension, but it does not establish a worthwhile computational mirror under ChoCo’s remit. The negative case is strongest at that boundary: it cannot rule out a useful new mass-market model, but the paper itself provides no encoded computational problem for continuization to classify.
