The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying anchored case for a continuous mirror. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Conclusions 1–3 are named empirical findings: they report treatment differences in bargaining outcomes, voting, and retaliation. The equilibrium discussion is a theoretical benchmark, not a computational result about solving a problem. The regressions and coding analyses do not change that.

The most tempting anchor is Conclusion 3: behavior after rejection depends on the previous round, and retaliation is stronger when communication is allowed. One could imagine a different model with a continuum of legislators, types recording preferences and bargaining histories, and a proposer choosing a distribution that wins support from enough mass. But the paper studies groups of three, with randomly assigned roles, individually identifiable players, and free-form communication. Its conclusion about history dependence does not specify a computational problem, objective, or algorithmic task. Turning it into a continuum model would require new choices about how coalitions form among mass, how histories update, and what the proposer optimizes. I don’t think the authors would be compelled to recognize that as the continuous form of a problem they solve here.

So there are no qualifying anchors, and therefore no per-anchor continuous questions or defensible predictions about tractability or hardness. The weak point is the foundation of the positive case itself: the paper’s central results are experimental findings about small-group behavior, not computational social-choice results about a population regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no qualifying anchor, and that is the strongest point in the negative case. The paper’s numbered Conclusions 1–3 report experimental findings about proposer shares, voting, and retaliation. They do not define a computational problem with an input, an algorithmic output, or a complexity claim. There is therefore no result here whose high-multiplicity version could be classified as tractable or hard.

Conclusion 3 is the most tempting basis for a mirror: one could define voter types by preferences and bargaining history, then ask how a proposer should allocate shares across a continuum of types after rejection. But that would turn an empirical finding about retaliation among three identified members of the same committee into a new allocation problem. In the experiment, rejection returns the same trio to bargaining, and the prior proposer’s identity matters. Aggregating members into masses does not itself specify how that history affects offers, how a coalition forms, or what an algorithm is supposed to optimize.

A more ambitious version could draw committees from a large population of types, or replace the three voters with a continuum and define approval by a mass threshold. Both might be sensible research directions. But either changes the game the paper studies; neither mirrors a computational question the authors asked or answered. The same issue applies to Conclusions 1 and 2: treatment differences in equilibrium-like outcomes and vote acceptance are empirical comparisons, not computational results. A continuum model might explain or predict those patterns, but that would be a new behavioral or analytic model, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper.

This is a paper-specific negative case, not a claim that bargaining cannot be studied over a continuum. Its weakness is that a valuable computational project could be built from the paper’s subject matter. The stronger reason against a mirror is that the paper itself supplies no computational result to continue.
