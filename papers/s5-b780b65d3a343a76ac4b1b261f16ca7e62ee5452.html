The continuous mirror question

Fix a finite set of publicly known bundles \(\mathcal R\), rational cohort masses \(\lambda_R\), positive binary values \(0<L<H\), and per-capita item supplies \(q_j\). At replica scale \(N\), use cohorts whose sizes divided by \(N\) converge to \(\lambda_R\), and supplies divided by \(N\) converge to \(q_j\). Does there exist a family of deterministic, individually rational, obviously strategyproof mechanisms, each querying individuals and giving each served individual the whole desired bundle, whose worst-case welfare approximation ratio has limit superior strictly below \(2\)?

The model it lives in

The continuous society has types \((R,v)\), with \(R\in\mathcal R\), \(v\in\{L,H\}\), and masses \(\mu_{R,v}\) satisfying \(\sum_v\mu_{R,v}=\lambda_R\). Each served unit of mass receives the whole bundle \(R\); feasibility requires \(\sum_{R\ni j,v}x_{R,v}\le q_j\) for every item \(j\). Define OSP through finite replica mechanisms that satisfy individual OSP at every scale, and assess their welfare guarantee by the worst-case limiting ratio.

The objection that survived

The proposed convergence condition does not specify uniformity over report profiles, so the limiting guarantee could depend on the replica sequence or query order; define it through the worst-case ratio over all replica profiles at each scale.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s deterministic IR OSP lower bound for known bundles and binary values. It leaves the matching mechanism of Theorem 3, the instance-independent bound of Theorem 5, the large-domain and unknown-bundle results, and the randomized mechanisms unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the worst-case limiting approximation ratio of deterministic IR OSP mechanisms remain at least \(2\) when cohort service is fractional in the limit?
  • Can a precisely defined replica-limit mechanism family achieve a uniform limiting ratio below \(2\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the paper’s Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that with known desired bundles and a two-value declaration domain \(\{L,H\}\), no deterministic individually rational obviously strategyproof mechanism can guarantee a social-welfare approximation ratio below \(2\). The proof gives a family of instances with two groups of bundles that conflict across groups, and shows that every such mechanism has instances on which its ratio approaches \(2\). This is an approximation lower bound for OSP mechanisms, not an NP-hardness theorem.

A continuous mirror I would take seriously is the continuous binary-domain OSP gap for single-minded markets. An instance has \(m\) item categories, a finite set \(\mathcal R\) of publicly known desired bundles, rational cohort masses \(\lambda_R\) for \(R\in\mathcal R\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\) of each item category, public values \(0<L<H\), and a target ratio \(\alpha\). The population consists of mass \(\lambda_R\) of agents whose desired bundle is \(R\); each agent privately has value \(L\) or \(H\). Thus the complete types are \((R,L)\) and \((R,H)\), and \(\mu_{R,v}\) is the fraction of society with type \((R,v)\), with \(\sum_v\mu_{R,v}=\lambda_R\).

The question is whether there is a deterministic, individually rational, obviously strategyproof mechanism for this market that guarantees ratio at most \(\alpha\) for every private-value profile. It may query agents and charge payments, as in the paper. Its outcome serves measurable subsets of the cohorts: if \(x_{R,v}\) is the mass served from a reported type, each served agent receives the whole bundle \(R\), and feasibility requires
\[ 0\le x_{R,v}\le\mu_{R,v}, \qquad \sum_{R\ni j,\,v}x_{R,v}\le q_j \quad\text{for each item }j. \]
The objective is \(\sum_{R,v}v x_{R,v}\); payments are needed for the incentive and individual-rationality conditions. Fractional \(x_{R,v}\) means a fraction of a cohort is served, not that each agent gets a fractional bundle.

For a formal continuous version of OSP, I would define a solution as a convergent family of finite replica mechanisms. At scale \(N\), replace each mass unit by \(N\) agents and each per-capita supply \(q_j\) by \(Nq_j\) copies; require the finite mechanism to be deterministic, IR and OSP, and require normalized allocations and payments to converge as report masses converge. The approximation guarantee is measured in that limit. This keeps OSP’s individual worst-case-versus-best-case incentive test intact while making fractional cohort service a genuine mass limit.

The paper’s authors should recognize this as their problem: the agents still have known bundles, private binary values, quasi-linear utilities and individual OSP incentives; the objective is still welfare approximation under bundle feasibility. The regime is a large market with many buyers but relatively few public request templates—for example, recurring allocation of standardized capacity bundles to large cohorts of firms, with stock scaling with the market. The supply scaling is a necessary part of this mirror: with a fixed stock of unique indivisible items, growing the population would not yield a meaningful per-capita allocation problem.

For the continuous offline welfare benchmark, the displayed constraints give a linear program with one variable per supported type. So the question is not whether the fractional welfare optimum is computationally hard. It is whether OSP still forces a factor-\(2\) loss when the society is represented by masses. My prediction is that the \(2\) barrier may survive as a continuum-specific incentive inapproximability result: the local OSP constraint remains, even though the aggregate allocation is fractional. In the programme’s terms, that would be a Class C style boundary, not a transfer of the paper’s cited discrete welfare-optimization hardness. The concrete question for a prover is: for this continuous market class, is there any deterministic IR OSP mechanism with a uniform guarantee \(\alpha<2\), or is \(2\) the limiting barrier?

The weak point is substantial. Theorem 4’s adversarial construction uses distinct bundles and assigns values in response to the mechanism’s query sequence. Replicating those agents creates large cohorts, but then a mechanism can serve only a fraction of a cohort; the paper’s proof does not show that the lower bound survives that extra flexibility. Nor is the per-capita supply model identical to the paper’s single-copy item setting. That is why this should be posed as a mirror problem, not presented as a transferred theorem. The mirror remains worthwhile because it preserves the paper’s central incentive question and asks exactly whether mass allocation changes its answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the right anchor to challenge: it is a named lower bound on the welfare approximation of deterministic IR OSP mechanisms. There is no honest objection that the paper lacks computational results, and scaling item supplies with population is a reasonable co-modification for bulk allocation.

The difficulty is that OSP is defined over individual agents’ choices in an implementation tree. The proposed continuous instance records masses \(\mu_{R,v}\), and its variables \(x_{R,v}\) record how much of each type is served. Those masses do not specify which individuals are served, what each pays, or which outcomes remain possible after an individual chooses a report. Those details are part of the OSP test. If the mechanism sees only aggregate reports, one individual cannot change the aggregate; if it queries individuals in sequence, the model needs a protocol over the continuum. Treating a whole cohort as one reporting agent would supply a protocol, but would change the strategic unit and its utility.

The replica proposal retains individual agents at each finite scale, but aggregate convergence alone does not settle the limit. OSP compares the worst outcome after a truthful choice with the best outcome after a deviation across future branches. Convergence of normalized allocations and payments by report masses does not specify how those individual comparisons behave. Two replica sequences with the same limiting masses could use different query orders, tie-breaking, and assignments within cohorts. Requiring convergence of the individual paths as well may repair this, but then the limit carries structure beyond the population distribution. Theorem 4’s query-dependent construction makes this especially visible: its argument is built around the sequence of named agents the mechanism queries, and a cohort version needs a rule for how the continuum is queried.

That is a serious objection to the proposed mirror as stated: the binary-domain mass market does not yet determine a continuous OSP problem. But it is not a decisive objection to every repair. A labelled atomless population with measurable individual allocations and payments, together with a precise limit notion that preserves OSP comparisons, could define a legitimate asymptotic mechanism-design question. The large-cohort auction scenario is plausible, and the supplied material does not show that such a formulation is uninteresting or that the authors would reject it.

So the negative case is weak at the universal claim. It can say that the mirror needs a richer model than masses and fractional service, and that the replica limit must preserve individual incentives explicitly. It cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
