The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), integral per-agent endowments, TBBL trees with valuation bounds, and parameters \(\Delta^\star,\delta,\epsilon>0\), compute a feasible mass allocation \(y\), common item prices \(\pi\), and a synchronized type-level ICE transcript using \(\delta\)-MRPAR and \(\epsilon\)-DIAR; determine whether the process terminates with \(W_v(y)\ge \Delta^\star\operatorname{OPT}_v\), and characterize its complexity in the type/profile encoding as \(\epsilon\to0\).

The model it lives in

A configuration LP over complete bidder types: \(y_{t,\lambda}\ge0\) is mass of type \(t\) assigned the integral trade \(\lambda\in\Lambda_t\), maximizing \(W(y)=\sum_{t,\lambda}y_{t,\lambda}v_t(\lambda)\), subject to \(\sum_{\lambda}y_{t,\lambda}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,\lambda}y_{t,\lambda}\lambda_j=0\). Mass is divisible across agents of a type, but every individual receives an integral bundle; type representatives use common prices and synchronized bound refinements.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified positive one. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness or polynomial-time complexity theorem: its statement that winner determination is NP-hard is an unnumbered claim citing Rothkopf et al. My lead anchor is therefore the paper’s own algorithmic result, Theorem 6, proved in this paper.

The natural mirror is a high-multiplicity exchange among many standardized trading agents. A type \(t\) consists of a complete bidder description: an integral initial endowment \(x_t^0\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{g}\), a TBBL tree, its true valuation function \(v_t\), and the relevant valuation bounds and parameters. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{\tau}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Thus \(\mu_t\) is the mass of agents having exactly that endowment, valuation structure, prices, and strategic parameters. Goods are still indivisible for each individual agent; only population mass is divisible.

For each type \(t\), let \(\Lambda_t\) be its set of feasible integral trades. The continuous efficient-clearing problem, which I will call Continuous ICE-Welfare Clearing, has variables \(y_{t,\lambda}\ge0\), where \(y_{t,\lambda}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving trade \(\lambda\). It asks for a feasible mass allocation maximizing \(W_v(y)=\sum_{t}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_t}y_{t,\lambda}v_t(\lambda)\), subject to \(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_t}y_{t,\lambda}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_t}y_{t,\lambda}\lambda_j=0\) for every good \(j\). The solution is the mass allocation \(y\), not a fractional bundle assigned to an individual.

This is exactly the population-level version of ICE’s efficient trade. Its master problem is a linear program over trade configurations, while the pricing problem for a type is the ICE best-response problem \(\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda_t}\{v_t(\lambda)-\pi\cdot\lambda\}\). With valuation bounds, the corresponding robust pricing tests are the type-level versions of MRPAR and DIAR. The continuous exchange may therefore output a final \(y\), a sequence of type-level bound refinements, and linear prices \(\pi\), with the requirement that \(W_v(y)\ge\Delta^\star\operatorname{OPT}_v\), or an equivalent additive welfare guarantee.

The regime is plausible in a cloud-capacity or airport-slot exchange. Imagine thousands or millions of standardized jobs, flight operations, or service units exchanging CPU, GPU, storage, or landing-slot bundles. There may be only a few dozen or few hundred complete types: for example, “long-haul morning flight with an evening-slot substitution option,” or “AI-training job initially holding CPU capacity and willing to exchange it for GPU-plus-storage.” Agents of one type are genuinely interchangeable for the market: they have the same endowment, TBBL valuation tree, willingness-to-pay, and relevant constraints. The number of agents can be enormous while \(\tau\) remains moderate. Resource supplies scale per capita, so the continuum is not pretending that a fixed airport with seven slots serves an infinite population.

The high-multiplicity bridge is exact for rational instances. If \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), then \(N\) agents with \(N_t\) copies of type \(t\) induce the same normalized welfare problem. Conversely, a rational mass solution can be expanded into a finite exchange after clearing denominators. Thus this is not merely “replace bidders by a distribution”; it is the high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s own efficient-trade problem.

The anchor is:

Theorem 6, proved here. “When ICE incorporates \(\delta\)-MRPAR and \(\epsilon\)-DIAR and when all bidders are straightforward, then the exchange terminates with a trade that is within target approximation error \(\Delta^\star\), for any \(\Delta^\star\ge0\) as \(\epsilon\to0\).” In the paper’s notation, this means the final trade satisfies the required relative-efficiency guarantee \( \mathrm{EFF}(\lambda)\ge\Delta^\star \). The theorem is proved here from the DIAR convergence argument and the direct and price-based efficiency bounds; it is not imported from another paper.

The continuous question corresponding to Theorem 6 is therefore:

Given a finite set of bidder types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), integral per-agent endowments, TBBL trees with public lower and upper valuation bounds, and access to one truthful valuation representative for each positive-mass type, run a type-level ICE process using common linear prices, \(\delta\)-MRPAR, and \(\epsilon\)-DIAR. Output a feasible mass allocation \(y\) and a transcript certifying that the true welfare of \(y\) is at least \(\Delta^\star\) times the optimum continuous welfare, with termination as \(\epsilon\to0\).

“Straightforward” becomes a natural type-level condition: every representative keeps the common true valuation \(v_t\) inside its reported bounds. The repeated agents of type \(t\) do not independently reveal different information, since that would contradict their being one complete type. A type’s mass simply weights its welfare and resource constraints. The paper’s proof strategy is well suited to this lift: the individual utility inequalities can be multiplied by \(\mu_t\) and summed over types, while DIAR needs to inspect each distinct tree only once rather than once per named bidder.

I would expect the full unrestricted continuous problem to fall in Class B rather than Class A. The combinatorics in ICE are not primarily caused by the number of named bidders; they live in bundles, interval-choice trees, and the number of goods. A finite discrete exchange embeds by giving each original bidder its own type and mass, so the known winner-determination hardness is not expected to disappear merely because mass is divisible. But this boundary makes the mirror interesting: for unit-demand types, structured TBBL trees, or other classes with polynomial pricing oracles, Continuous ICE-Welfare Clearing may become a genuine Class A configuration-LP problem. The paper’s pricing and constraint-generation machinery gives a concrete route to studying that boundary.

The main follow-up questions are whether the type-level process has a complexity bound depending on \(\tau\), tree size, goods, and input bit length rather than the population size; which TBBL subclasses admit efficient separation; how the paper’s \(n\)-dependent approximation bounds normalize when total population mass is \(1\); and whether threshold payments and budget balance have a clean mass-scaled analogue.

The weakest point is that Theorem 6 is a convergence and approximation theorem, not a polynomial-time complexity classification. It assumes straightforward bidders, lets \(\epsilon\) tend to zero, and relies computationally on MIPs. Moreover, the continuous theorem itself is not proved in the paper: the type-weighted lift and the scale-normalized efficiency bound would be new work. That is a real gap, but it is precisely the kind of gap a continuous-computational mirror should expose. The proposed question preserves ICE’s underlying combinatorial exchange, linear prices, valuation elicitation, and welfare objective while replacing redundant named bidders by a genuine distribution over complete bidder types. It is therefore a credible continuous mirror, even if its eventual classification is likely to be “hard in full generality, tractable for identifiable pricing subclasses.”

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case has one real vulnerability: Theorem 6 is not a computational-complexity result. It is a convergence guarantee for a finite, interactive mechanism, stated only “as \(\epsilon\to0\),” with no finite round bound, encoding, or polynomial-time guarantee. The paper’s actual computational core is winner determination and the MIPs used for pricing and activity-rule checks; those are not captured by Theorem 6’s conclusion.

More importantly, the proposed lift preserves only the paper’s static welfare problem, not ICE itself. The configuration LP over \(y_{t,\lambda}\) is mathematically legitimate, but it replaces each bidder’s individual provisional trade, elicitation history, activity-rule response, and payment by a centralized allocation of mass. Multiplying the paper’s inequalities by \(\mu_t\) does not establish that a type-level MRPAR/DIAR process exists: the original rules constrain each named bidder’s private bounds and successive reports.

This becomes fundamental once one remembers that valuation bounds are not fixed type attributes. Two agents with the same true valuation can reveal different bounds or follow different elicitation histories. If those histories are included in the type, the type space evolves with the interaction and may contain exponentially many, or even continuously many, report states. If all copies are forced to share one history, they are no longer independent ICE bidders; they are one coalition or one representative bidder with mass. Either choice changes the mechanism whose theorem is being mirrored.

The payment side is likewise not inherited. Threshold payments are defined using the economy with bidder \(i\) removed. In a normalized continuum, removing one individual has zero effect, while removing the whole mass of type \(t\) has a finite and entirely different effect. There is no canonical continuum version of the paper’s per-bidder threshold discount. Replacing it with marginal or dual prices would be sensible, but that would be a new market mechanism rather than a continuization of ICE.

The strongest rescue is therefore to discard elicitation and payments and study only the high-multiplicity efficient-clearing LP. That produces a sensible configuration-optimization problem for standardized jobs or slots. But it is a generic high-multiplicity combinatorial-exchange formulation, not a continuous version of the paper’s named result: the paper neither proves its type-weighted algorithm nor supplies a finite computational guarantee for it. The interesting work would concern a newly designed configuration LP and its pricing oracle, not ICE’s convergence theorem.

This negative case is not airtight. The standardized-cloud or airport-cohort scenario makes the static high-multiplicity welfare problem genuinely plausible, and the resulting separation questions could still be worthwhile for ChoCo. What cannot honestly be claimed is that Theorem 6 itself lifts to a finite-type continuous ICE mechanism. Thus the evidence supports rejecting the proposed anchor as stated, but not the universal claim that no valuable continuous mirror of the paper can exist.
