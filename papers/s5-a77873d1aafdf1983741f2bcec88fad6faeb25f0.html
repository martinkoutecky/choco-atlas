The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) sites, a piece limit \(k\), rational population mass \(N\ge 1\), and \(\tau\) types with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(N\) and rational per-unit values \(v_{tj}\), assign each type’s mass to bundles \(z\in[0,N]^m\) with at most \(k\) positive entries. Each assigned bundle must give type \(t\) value at least \(\rho N\sum_j v_{tj}\), and total assigned land at each site must not exceed \(N\). What is the largest \(\rho\) guaranteed for every such instance—is it \(\min\{1/N,k/(m+N-1)\}\)—and can an attaining assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k\) and the input bit length, independent of the numeric value of \(N\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of mass \(N\) is distributed over valuation types. Each type may split across bundles with at most \(k\) sites, while each site has supply \(N\). A bundle’s value is \(\sum_j v_{tj}z_j\); every assigned bundle must meet the same proportional floor, and the total assigned mass-weighted land at each site is capacity constrained.

The objection that survived

The uniform-site model may reduce pricing to fractional knapsack, making its algorithmic contribution modest; the proponent anticipates approachability but does not establish a nontrivial computational barrier.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s exact absolute-value guarantee and constructive allocation for uniform per-site values; it leaves Theorems 2–3, the rectilinear-polygon application, and the envy-free results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact universal guarantee equal \(\min\{1/N,k/(m+N-1)\}\) for rational \(N\) and arbitrary rational type masses?
  • Can an attaining allocation be computed in polynomial bit complexity in \(m,\tau,k\), and the input length, without dependence polynomial in \(N\)?
  • Does the guarantee or algorithm extend to nonuniform value densities within sites?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think the strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity multicake division problem. Picture a large land trust allocating plots across \(m\) estate sites to many beneficiary households. There may be millions of households but only \(\tau\) distinct valuation profiles: households with the same type value each site in the same way and share the same allocation constraints. The estate portfolio scales with the population, so each site has \(N\) units of land for population mass \(N\). That per-capita supply is part of the model.

Here is a concrete version. Let \(N\ge 1\) be rational, and let the type masses be \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=N\). A type \(t\) has a rational per-unit-land value \(v_{tj}\) for each site \(j\). Take value to be uniform within each site, a natural regime for homogeneous estate blocks. A household’s bundle is a vector \(z\in[0,N]^m\) with at most \(k\) positive entries: it gets land from at most \(k\) sites, and its value is \(\sum_j v_{tj}z_j\). Uniform values mean each site allotment can be represented by one interval.

Call the problem Mass-Multicake Guarantee. Given \(m,k,N\), the type masses, and the valuation vectors, find the largest \(\rho\) and a mass assignment of bundles such that:

  • Each type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is assigned among bundles that each give type \(t\) at least \(\rho\) times its value for the full portfolio, \(N\sum_j v_{tj}\).
  • The mass-weighted land assigned at each site is at most its supply \(N\).

The output can be a finite list of bundles and the mass assigned to each. Each unit of mass receives a bundle with at most \(k\) pieces; fractional values describe how much of a type is assigned to each bundle. Thus the model keeps the paper’s individual bundle constraint and fairness guarantee while making the population the continuous object. It is not a lottery whose expected value substitutes for an individual guarantee.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, for every positive integer \(m,k,n\), the largest fraction of total multicake value that can always be guaranteed to every agent with at most \(k\) pieces is \(r(m,k,n)=\min(1/n,k/(m+n-1))\). The paper proves the matching lower bound constructively with a polynomial-time algorithm; its upper-bound example uses identical valuations, with \(m-1\) small islands worth \(1\) each and one large island worth \(n\). This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result: the paper gives no named NP-hardness result for me to mirror.

The corresponding continuous question is whether, for population mass \(N\), the same worst-case guarantee survives with \(n\) replaced by \(N\): \(\rho=\min(1/N,k/(m+N-1))\). More generally, what is the exact largest universal \(\rho\), and can an allocation attaining it be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k\) and the binary input length \(L\), without a dependence polynomial in the numeric value of \(N\)? The paper’s algorithm is polynomial in the number of agents; the mass version asks whether that work can be compressed when the input is a short list of types and their rational masses.

I expect the mass problem to be tractable, though that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence already proved by the paper. For a fixed \(\rho\), it has the shape of a configuration LP: each type can split across bundles meeting its value floor, subject to \(m\) site-capacity constraints. The key algorithmic question is whether pricing over bundles with at most \(k\) nonzero site allocations has an efficient separation procedure. With uniform per-site values, that looks approachable through continuous-knapsack structure and sorting; establishing the required running time is a real part of the problem. Natural follow-ups are whether the exact bound holds for rational non-integer masses, whether arbitrary within-site value densities preserve tractability, and whether an envy-free mass allocation can be computed.

The paper’s continuous cake is already central to its model; the proposed mirror continues the population. The land-beneficiary regime makes high multiplicity plausible: many households share a small catalogue of complete valuation types, and the number of households can greatly exceed \(\tau\). Existing high-multiplicity fair-division work would support that regime, rather than collide with the mirror.

My weakest point is that the paper’s proof uses finite-agent marks and matchings. A continuous mass version of those steps is not automatic, and a type-level feasibility model may need additional work to ensure it represents the authors’ intended land allocations. The mirror also focuses on homogeneous site values. Still, it retains the defining question—how to allocate a multicake fairly when each recipient can manage only \(k\) pieces—and gives the authors a clear high-multiplicity computational problem to accept or reject.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 1’s polynomial-time claim is not a finite-input complexity result in the usual bit model. The paper permits arbitrary integrable value densities, and its algorithm asks agents to mark exact cuts; it does not give those valuations a finite encoding. The proponent supplies one by restricting to rational, uniform site values, but that restriction also strips away much of the paper’s interval geometry. In that model, for a fixed guarantee, an individual bundle’s minimum cost under dual site prices appears to reduce to fractional knapsack with a limit of \(k\) sites. The resulting configuration LP may therefore be a short tractability exercise, rather than a continuation of the paper’s matching and cutting ideas. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.08150v6)

That objection does not defeat the mirror. It only weakens the proponent’s narrow formulation: a richer finite type description could retain nonuniform densities and interval choices, and the beneficiary-household scenario is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 1 is still a named constructive algorithmic result, and the paper’s identical-valuation upper-bound example has a natural mass analogue. I found no fundamental identity, degeneracy, or prior-art collision that rules out a worthwhile population-continuous question.

So my negative case is weak. I cannot honestly defeat the sole anchor: Theorem 1 survives, though the proponent has not yet shown that their particular finite-vector version requires substantial new algorithmic work.
