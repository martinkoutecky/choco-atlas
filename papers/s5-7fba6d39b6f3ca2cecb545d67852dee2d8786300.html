The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity majority-profile mirror for computing the tournament equilibrium set.

There is, however, an important qualification. This paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting an algorithmic complexity result. Its named results are structural and axiomatic. The computational statement appears only as an unnumbered sentence in Section 4.2: computing the tournament equilibrium set is NP-hard, citing Brandt, Fischer, and Harrenstein. The underlying named result is the external paper *Recognizing Members of the Tournament Equilibrium Set is NP-hard*, whose precise claim is that deciding whether a specified alternative belongs to \(\mathrm{TEQ}(T)\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. citeturn10view0 Thus, strictly speaking, this paper has no eligible numbered computational anchor; I would not invent a theorem number. The paper’s own scope and results can be checked in its published manuscript. citeturn0search0

Taking that cited result as the closest available anchor, my lead problem would be:

\[ \mathrm{TEQ\text{-}Membership}_{\infty}. \]

An instance consists of candidates \(C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), a finite list of ranking types \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and a target candidate \(a\). Each \(\theta_i\) is a complete ranking of \(C\). For every pair \(b,c\in C\), define

\[ p_\mu(b,c)=\sum_{i:\,b\succ_{\theta_i}c}\mu_i. \]

We promise that \(p_\mu(b,c)\neq \tfrac12\) for every pair. The induced majority tournament \(T_\mu\) has

\[ b\succ_\mu c \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_\mu(b,c)>\tfrac12. \]

The question is whether

\[ a\in \mathrm{TEQ}(T_\mu). \]

Equivalently, the search version asks for the exact set \(\mathrm{TEQ}(T_\mu)\), where \(X\) is \(\mathrm{TEQ}\)-retentive if, for every \(x\in X\) with nonempty dominator set, the recursively computed \(\mathrm{TEQ}\) of that dominator set is contained in \(X\), and the answer is the union of inclusion-minimal retentive sets. The decision variable is therefore a candidate-membership bit, or in the search version a subset \(X\subseteq C\); there is no intervention on the population and no objective beyond exact winner-set computation.

This is a genuine population continuization. The agents are voters, a type is a complete ballot ranking, and \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of society casting that ballot. The natural regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballot types: for example, a national election in which millions of voters fall into a comparatively small set of preference classes. Formally, \(n\) voters with \(n_i\) copies of type \(\theta_i\) give the continuous society \(\mu_i=n_i/n\). Duplicating every type preserves \(T_\mu\) while making \(n\gg\tau\), so the high-multiplicity interpretation is substantive rather than cosmetic.

The mirror is especially plausible because the paper itself treats tournaments as majority relations generated by preference profiles. It is not replacing TEQ by a simpler winner rule; it preserves the complete TEQ recursion and merely represents the electorate by masses. The authors should recognise this as the high-multiplicity version of their computational question.

I would expect hardness to transfer, placing this problem in Class B. Given any tournament \(T\), McGarvey’s construction produces in polynomial time a preference profile whose majority tournament is exactly \(T\). Normalize that profile to a rational mass vector \(\mu^T\). Then

\[ T_{\mu^T}=T, \]

so an algorithm for \(\mathrm{TEQ\text{-}Membership}_{\infty}\) would decide membership in \(\mathrm{TEQ}(T)\). The population is continuous, but the reduction’s combinatorics live in the number of alternatives and in the tournament structure, not in individual voter multiplicity. This is precisely the programme’s expected hardness-transfer phenomenon.

The mirror covers only the TEQ-computation claim, not the paper’s stability, local-reversal-symmetry, or bipartisan-set characterizations. Further questions naturally arise: how should pairwise ties be treated; does hardness persist under a promised margin away from \(\tfrac12\); and what happens when the number of ballot types \(\tau\), the support size, or the tournament’s decomposition degree is parameterized?

The weakest point is also clear: after forming \(T_\mu\), all numerical population masses are discarded. Consequently this is a high-multiplicity encoding of the same tournament problem, not a population-sensitive problem of the sort that might become easier through fractional optimization. An opposing analyst could reasonably call it a reparameterization. My answer is that this still suffices for a valid continuous mirror under ChoCo: the programme explicitly includes Class B mirrors where the discrete hardness survives, and this one gives a precise, natural, high-multiplicity formulation rather than a merely verbal analogy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive under the stated anchoring rule: the proponent has not anchored the case on a named computational result of this paper. The paper’s only computational statement is an unnumbered sentence in Section 4.2 citing an external NP-hardness result for TEQ. The numbered theorems and corollaries concern stability, generators, local reversal symmetry, and axiomatic characterizations. Thus the proposed \(\mathrm{TEQ}\)-membership mirror is a mirror of Brandt, Fischer, and Harrenstein, not of a computational theorem proved in this paper.

Granting that external sentence as an eligible anchor, however, the negative case becomes substantially weaker. The proposed model is a legitimate high-multiplicity society: voter types are complete rankings, \(\mu_i\) is their population share, and a large electorate with repeated ballot types is entirely natural. It does not depend on voter identity, and the majority tournament remains nontrivial in the limit. The no-ties promise is also an ordinary genericity condition, not a fatal modelling defect.

The best substantive criticism is that \(\mu\) is only an encoding of the induced tournament. Once

\[ T_\mu=\bigl(C,\succ_\mu\bigr) \]

has been computed, TEQ discards all population information. McGarvey’s construction means that every finite tournament can be represented by a rational profile, so the continuous profile space adds no restriction to the abstract TEQ instances. This makes the proposed problem a redundant representation of tournament-TEQ membership rather than a population-sensitive optimisation problem.

That criticism is not, by itself, admissible under ChoCo’s rules: a Class B mirror is explicitly allowed, and the fact that hardness survives continuization is a result rather than an objection. It therefore cannot defeat the mirror merely by showing that the answer is unchanged.

The paper’s axiomatic results fare no better as population mirrors. Stability, local reversal symmetry, and the bipartisan-set characterization quantify over tournaments and alternatives, not over voters. Replacing a tournament by a weighted majority profile would simply provide another representation of the same quantified objects. Conversely, one could invent genuinely population-sensitive questions—such as the minimum mass transfer needed to make \(a\) enter \(\mathrm{TEQ}(T_\mu)\), or the continuous robustness radius of TEQ—but those would be new intervention and robustness problems, not computational results stated by this paper.

So the strongest honest case against is narrow: the submitted anchor is not actually a named computational result of the paper, and the paper contains no computational population question for which continuization is clearly its natural high-multiplicity form. But the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario cannot honestly be sustained. The TEQ mirror survives the modelling objections; it fails only the stricter requirement that the anchor come from a numbered computational result of this paper.
