What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Theorem 2, proved here, characterizes random choice rules satisfying Luce’s Choice Axiom: this holds exactly when choice is restricted by a rational choice correspondence \(\Gamma\), with alternatives in \(\Gamma(A)\) selected in proportion to \(e^{\alpha(a)}\); \(\Gamma\) is uniquely the support of the rule. Proposition 3 characterizes when that correspondence has a continuous utility representation, and Proposition 4 gives another characterization using WARP and conditional probabilities. These are structural results, not claims that a computational problem is in a complexity class. Proposition 5 gives a random-utility representation, also not an algorithmic result.

The paper’s probabilities can be interpreted as choice frequencies, but that interpretation does not itself make the society a distribution over voter or consumer types. Proposition 3’s continuity concerns the space of alternatives, not the population. A population model built from consumers with different choice rules could be a new research question, but it would not be a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. Under the requested standard, there is no anchor for which to state a per-anchor continuous problem or predict its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case offers no affirmative mirror anchor to defeat: it names Theorem 2 and Propositions 3–5 as results that are axiomatic or representational, not computational. That is the strongest objection here. The paper proves characterizations of random choice rules; it does not define a computational problem with an input, an output, and an algorithmic or complexity claim.

The best rescue would read \(p(a,A)\) as the limiting share of a continuum of consumers choosing \(a\) from menu \(A\). But with identical consumer types, those shares are just the probabilities already represented by \(p\). With heterogeneous types, the aggregate rule is generally a mixture, and Theorem 2 does not characterize such mixtures. Studying their recognition, identification, or manipulation could be worthwhile, but those would be new computational questions, not mirrors of this paper’s results.

Proposition 3’s continuity is about the topology of the alternative space \(X\); Propositions 4 and 5 concern conditional probabilities and random utility for a choice rule. Recasting those objects as population shares does not turn them into computational social choice results. A continuous consumer-choice project could grow nearby, but this paper supplies no computational result for it to mirror.
