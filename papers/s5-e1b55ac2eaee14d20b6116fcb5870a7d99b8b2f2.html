The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in the paper. It names no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. The numbered equations and the numbered properties of the proposed models are formulations and claims about the models; the application reports numerical allocations and stability values. None establishes that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. So I can’t honestly restate a paper result as an anchor or assign its mirror a class based on such a result.

The strongest positive candidate is an unanchored continuous river-bankruptcy allocation problem. Its types would be water-user groups with identical locations in the river network, access constraints, per-capita claim schedules, and any relevant entitlements or bargaining characteristics. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of claimants of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A plausible regime is a basin with many irrigation districts or water-user cooperatives, but relatively few distinct location-and-claim profiles. The decision variables are each type’s per-capita water allocation \(x_{t,h}\) over periods \(h\), and the river flows \(f_{e,h}\). Given a directed river network, rational hydrologic inputs and outlet requirements, require flow conservation at every node, \(0\le x_{t,h}\le c_{t,h}\), and feasibility of the outlet flow. For a proportional-rule version, one could minimize a mass-weighted analogue of the paper’s objective:
\[ \sum_h\left(z_h-\prod_{t\in T}\rho_{t,h}^{\mu_t}\right), \qquad \rho_{t,h}=\frac{x_{t,h}}{c_{t,h}},\quad z_h\ge \rho_{t,h}. \]
A solution is a feasible allocation and flow pattern minimizing that objective. This preserves the paper’s concern with proportional satisfaction under spatial and temporal access constraints while treating the claimant population as the continuous object.

That scenario is plausible but not secure. The paper explicitly generalizes network bankruptcy allocation beyond its river example, which helps the case that the underlying problem could survive a change in claimant scale. But its worked case has eight politically distinct provinces, and replacing them with many districts or cooperatives changes the negotiating parties. The type definition would need to preserve every feature the allocation problem uses, including network access and any differences in claims or power. Mass-weighting the rule’s objective is also a modeling choice that the authors might reject. I would cover the network-constrained allocation models, not claim to mirror the Iranian case or the BASI stability analysis.

The weakest point is that even this candidate is not anchored to a named computational result in the paper. The paper does not analyze the complexity of its optimization problems, so there is no grounded way to predict whether this mirror is tractable, inherits hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard. The proposal shows where a continuous-population question might be posed; it cannot supply the required complexity case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. It gives four nonlinear optimization formulations and reports their allocations, plus a stability index, but states no complexity classification or algorithmic bound. A complexity question for a continuous population would therefore be a new question about the models, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The proponent’s best candidate is a basin with many similar water-user groups sharing a small number of network locations and per-capita claim profiles. That is a plausible setting, but it exposes the central mismatch. Keep the paper’s actors—provincial authorities with region-wide claims and political power—and the example has eight claimants, not a population with meaningful mass. Split those authorities into farmers or local associations and the actors, claims, and acceptability analysis change. In particular, the paper’s BASI evaluates power among negotiating parties; it does not specify how that power should be distributed among individuals or mass types.

The type-based version does not resolve this cleanly. If type records include location, access, and claim schedules, groups with distinct network positions remain distinct. Groups that truly share a node and all relevant characteristics can be aggregated into their total claim and allocation in the paper’s existing network model. The proposed mass-weighted geometric objective also makes population proportions affect the fairness rule; the paper does not establish that rule as the continuation of its product over political claimants. It may be a sensible new fairness model, but it is an additional modeling choice.

The paper’s references to disaster relief, utilities, and fisheries make a broader network-bankruptcy project conceivable. That is the strongest way around the mismatch, but it broadens the subject beyond a mirror of this paper and still supplies no computational result to carry over. So the paper-specific negative case is strong: the offered candidate is not anchored to a computational result, and its population lift either collapses into aggregate claims or changes the actors and fairness model. The universal claim is weaker: a carefully specified high-multiplicity network-bankruptcy problem could be worth studying in its own right.
