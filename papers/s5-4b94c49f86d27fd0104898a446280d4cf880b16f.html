The case FOR (proponent)

The honest formal answer is that this paper has no qualifying anchor. In the supplied text there is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting a complexity, algorithmic, approximation, or parameterized result. Models PG1, PG2, and PG3 are statistical-model definitions; Gibbs sampling and EM are inference methods; and the RMSE, calibration, and AUC improvements are empirical experiments. The paper’s literature claims are not results proved here. Hence there is no compliant anchor, no defensible “lead” result, and no continuous mirror that can be presented as a continuization of a named computational theorem.

There is nevertheless a plausible, but explicitly unanchored, mirror of the paper’s central object. Model PG3 states that each submission has a latent score \(s_u\), each grader has a latent bias \(b_v\), and an observed grade satisfies

\[ z_u^v\sim N\!\left(s_u+b_v,\frac{1}{\theta_1s_v+\theta_0}\right). \]

Thus a grader’s reliability depends on her own submission score. The paper infers these latent quantities from the peer-grading graph and uses posterior uncertainty to reason about reallocating graders.

A continuous version could be called Continuous PG3 Type-Inference. Its instance would contain a finite type set \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_i\), and a grading degree \(d\), typically \(d=4\) or \(5\). A type would specify everything that is common within the cohort: language or rubric group, grading eligibility, workload constraints, and the latent behavioural parameters relevant to the model. There would be a mass \(\pi_{ij}\) of grading edges from grader type \(i\) to submission type \(j\), with

\[ \sum_j\pi_{ij}=d\mu_i, \qquad \sum_i\pi_{ij}=d\mu_j. \]

Observed grades would be represented by aggregate grade measures \(H_{ij}\), rather than by individually named edges. For latent type scores \(s_i\) and biases \(b_i\), put

\[ r_i=\theta_1s_i+\theta_0>0. \]

The posterior is determined by the same PG3 likelihood,

\[ z\mid(i\to j)\sim N(s_j+b_i,1/r_i), \]

together with the paper’s Gaussian priors on scores and biases. The computational problem is:

\[ \text{compute an }\varepsilon\text{-accurate posterior mean and credible interval for every }s_i,b_i, \]

or equivalently find the Bayes estimator minimizing posterior expected squared error,

\[ \min_{a,c}\; \mathbb E\!\left[ \sum_i\mu_i\bigl((a_i-s_i)^2+(c_i-b_i)^2\bigr) \mid H,\mu,\pi \right]. \]

This is a genuine population mirror: mass replaces named students, the grader–gradee relation becomes a coupling of type masses, and the self-score/reliability coupling from PG3 is retained. A plausible regime is a recurring MOOC with hundreds of thousands of students but perhaps tens or hundreds of repeated behavioural types formed by language, performance band, grading style, and engagement state.

I would expect the PG1 version, with fixed type reliabilities, to be Class A: its aggregated negative log-posterior is a quadratic program or linear-algebra problem in \(\tau\) type variables. Full PG3 is less clear. The score-dependent precision creates nonconvex terms such as

\[ (\theta_1s_i+\theta_0)(z-s_j-b_i)^2, \]

so it is a plausible continuum-specific-hardness candidate rather than an established tractable problem. Adding the paper’s proposed grader-allocation task would generate a further coupling-optimization problem over \(\pi\), with posterior uncertainty or fairness as the objective.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s actual experiments rely on individually varying students, sparse identities, and creative submissions, so repeated homogeneous types may be an artificial abstraction. A stronger scenario is a large recurring course with standardized rubrics and stable cohorts, but that is a new modelling choice. More importantly, this mirror is not anchored in a named computational result proved by the paper. It is a sensible ChoCo research question inspired by Model PG3, but under the programme’s formal rules the paper supplies no qualifying positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong here because the paper supplies no qualifying anchor at all. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a complexity, approximation, or parameterized result. PG1–PG3 are model definitions; Gibbs sampling and EM are inference procedures; and the RMSE, calibration, and AUC figures are empirical evaluations. The paper’s computationally interesting allocation question is explicitly left for future work. Thus the proposed continuous questions are new research problems inspired by the paper, not continuations of any named computational result.

The deeper modelling problem is that the paper’s object is not merely a population of graders with interchangeable behavioural types. Its latent variables are attached to individuals and submissions: \(s_u\) is the score of a particular submission, \(b_v\) and \(\tau_v\) belong to a particular grader, and in PG3 the grader’s own score \(s_v\) controls the reliability of that grader’s outgoing assessments. The peer graph also records which individuals graded which other individuals.

The proposed \(\mu_i\), \(\pi_{ij}\), and \(H_{ij}\) representation cannot preserve that structure unless all members of a type are genuinely interchangeable in every relevant respect. If a type \(i\) has a common score \(s_i\), bias \(b_i\), and reliability, then it no longer represents a cohort of distinct creative submissions. It represents repeated copies of one latent submission and one grading behaviour. The inference target has changed from estimating each learner’s score and grading ability to estimating a representative type parameter.

If types are instead kept broad enough to retain individual score variation, grading histories, temporal state, submission content, and graph position, then the type description becomes effectively individual-specific. Since scores and biases are continuous latent quantities, the number of distinct types will generally grow with the number of students. The resulting \(\mu\) is then a relabelling of the finite peer-grading instance, not a meaningful high-multiplicity regime. The marginal constraints

\[ \sum_j \pi_{ij}=d\mu_i, \qquad \sum_i \pi_{ij}=d\mu_j \]

preserve workload counts but not the incidence structure needed to connect a particular grader’s own score to that grader’s outgoing assessments.

The best rescue is a deliberately homogeneous recurring MOOC: standardized submissions, stable language or engagement cohorts, and exchangeable graders within each cohort. That is a coherent statistical model, but it is no longer a mirror of the paper’s central empirical problem. It studies inference for a finite-dimensional exchangeable random-effects model. Such a model may be useful in its own right, but its justification comes from the new scenario, not from a computational result in this paper.

The proposed PG1 anchor therefore does not survive as a ChoCo anchor. A quadratic type-level posterior may indeed be a clean calculation, but it is simply the consequence of replacing thousands of individual latent variables by a few shared parameters. The population mass is only a weight in a standard statistical estimation problem; it does not represent the paper’s individual grading task. This is not an objection that the answer is merely easy. It is an objection that the proposed problem has discarded the feature that made the paper’s problem what it was.

PG3 is no stronger. The term involving score-dependent precision may create a nonconvex inference problem, but that difficulty comes from the chosen nonlinear Bayesian model, not from continuization. If one retains individual scores, the difficulty is already present in the finite peer graph. If one aggregates them into types, one obtains a different model whose nonlinear moments are not determined by \(\mu_i\) and \(H_{ij}\) alone. Calling the result “continuum-specific hardness” would therefore overstate what has been established: it would be a new hardness question about Bayesian inference, with no named theorem in the paper to mirror.

Finally, the allocation proposal cannot supply the missing anchor. The paper does not solve optimal grader assignment; it reports a confidence-based simulation showing that some submissions might need additional grades and explicitly lists grader-to-submission matching as future work. A fractional allocation problem over type masses could certainly be formulated, but if its objective concerns individual confidence, the type aggregation again loses the necessary identities and posterior histories. If its objective is only average confidence by exchangeable type, it becomes a new optimal-design or transportation problem rather than a continuization of a result in the paper.

A contrived repeated-template course could make a population mirror mathematically coherent, so the universal claim is not a literal impossibility theorem. That is the weak point of any modelling-based negative case. But it would be a new exchangeable inference or allocation model, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results. Under the programme’s anchoring requirement, no proposed mirror survives.
