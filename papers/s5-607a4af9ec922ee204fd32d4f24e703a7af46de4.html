The continuous mirror question

Given two balanced populations represented by finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational type masses \((\alpha_a)_{a\in A}\) and \((\beta_b)_{b\in B}\), and a strict ranking of opposite-side types for each type, decide for specified \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\) whether there exists a stable mass matching \(x\geq 0\) with \(x_{ab}>0\). Here \(x_{ab}\) is the mass matched between \(a\) and \(b\), its row and column sums equal the input masses, and stability requires that for every type pair at least one side has zero mass currently matched to types it ranks below the other type.

The model it lives in

A two-sided continuum matching market with finitely many types and rational masses; the decision variable is a mass-allocation matrix \(x\), stability excludes type pairs with positive mass on both sides who prefer each other to their assigned types, and the query asks whether a specified pair occurs with positive mass in some stable allocation.

The objection that survived

Theorem 15 tracks a named woman's partners under realized individual rankings, while the mirror tracks positive type-pair mass under rankings of categories; the paper proves no limit identity connecting them.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 15's stable-partner enumeration task; it leaves aside the rank-gap, near-uniqueness, expected partner-count, and popularity-ratio results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can stable type-pair support be computed in time polynomial in the number of types and the mass bit length?
  • Does stable type-pair support coincide with the limit of cloned finite markets as the number of agents grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: Theorem 15 is the paper’s best computational anchor, though it is an algorithmic correctness result rather than an explicit complexity-class theorem. It is marked “Adapted from” Knuth et al., so it is cited background used by the paper, not a new result proved here. It says that, for an explicit finite matching market and a designated woman \(w^*\), the extended men-proposing deferred-acceptance algorithm outputs exactly her stable husbands: run ordinary deferred acceptance, then continue proposals while \(w^*\) rejects every proposal; among the proposals she receives, return those that are the best she has received so far. Each man proposes to each woman at most once, so this displayed algorithm runs in polynomial time. If an anchor must itself explicitly say “in P,” rather than give a correct polynomially bounded algorithm, then the paper has no qualifying complexity-class anchor.

A plausible mirror of that one result is Type-Level Stable Partner Support. An instance has two balanced populations, each represented by finitely many types with positive rational masses summing to \(1\). Agents within a type are identical for the matching problem: each type has a strict ranking of the types on the other side, with members of a type treated as equivalent. Each individual still receives one partner; the mass variables record how many agents of each type are matched to each other. Given a pair of types \((a,b)\), ask whether there exists a stable matching of the masses in which positive mass of type \(a\) is matched to type \(b\).

Precisely, a candidate matching is a nonnegative matrix \(x\), where \(x_{ab}\) is the mass matched between types \(a\) and \(b\), and the row and column sums equal the input masses. For each pair \((a,b)\), let
\[ r_A(a,b;x)=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'} \quad\text{and}\quad r_B(a,b;x)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}. \]
These are the masses on each side currently matched to options they rank below the other type. The matching is stable if, for every \((a,b)\), at least one of these two quantities is zero: there cannot be positive masses on both sides who would prefer each other to their assigned types. A YES solution is a stable mass matching with \(x_{ab}>0\); a NO answer means no such stable mass matching exists.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large hiring market in which many applicants share a qualification-and-location type and many vacancies share an employer-and-role type, with both sides ranking types rather than individual people. The population could be very large while \(\tau\), the number of distinct types, remains modest. That makes mass the natural unit for the aggregate question, and the query—whether a partner type occurs in some stable outcome—tracks Theorem 15’s stable-husband question. The category-based preference representation is the necessary modeling co-modification; it should be presented openly, since it gives indifference between individuals within a category.

My working expectation is Class A, if a polynomial-size stable-flow formulation or finite-type deferred-acceptance procedure can find and test partner support in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\). That is a research question, not a consequence of Theorem 15: the finite algorithm processes individual proposals, and its runtime bound in the number of people says nothing by itself about a binary-encoded mass input. Useful next questions are whether the continuous stable-partner support equals the limit of cloned discrete markets, and whether the same machinery can compute the mass of agents with multiple stable partners.

The mirror covers only Theorem 15’s underlying stable-partner enumeration problem. It does not mirror the paper’s main stochastic bounds—Theorems A–D on rank differences, uniqueness, and numbers of stable partners. My weakest point is that Theorem 15 is a classical subroutine, not the paper’s principal contribution, and the proposed type-level market replaces individualized random preference lists with rankings of categories. The authors might recognize it as a high-multiplicity version of stable-partner support, but may not accept it as a continuous form of the paper’s central stochastic questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 15 is a weak anchor for this programme. It is explicitly adapted from prior work, and its claim is the correctness of the extended deferred-acceptance procedure. That is a named algorithmic result, so I would not say there is no algorithm to mirror. But the paper’s own results concern expected rank gaps, core convergence, and counts of stable partners; none formulates a computational problem or analyzes its complexity. The proposed type-level support question is therefore a new problem adjacent to the paper, rather than a continuous version of one of its computational results.

The type-level question is coherent: a positive \(x_{ab}\) asks whether some stable mass allocation pairs a positive mass of type \(a\) with type \(b\). But it changes what Theorem 15 tracks. The theorem enumerates the stable husbands of a particular woman with a strict ranking over named men. Replacing named people with categories replaces those rankings with preferences over types, generally making members of a category interchangeable. The result is anonymous partner-type support, not enumeration of an individual’s possible partners under the paper’s random preference model.

The best repair would preserve the paper’s main subject: give each population type a preference-generating law, then ask for the limiting expected fraction of agents with multiple stable partners, or for a normalized rank-gap statistic. That is a sensible question. But realized random rankings make agents with the same generating law distinguishable in the matching; treating realized rankings as types can make the support grow with the population. Keeping only the generating laws keeps the type count small, but leaves a stochastic matching problem whose computational input and target must be specified anew. It does not recover Theorem 15’s type-level support problem or turn the paper’s probability bounds into computational results.

So I can argue that this anchor is not well grounded in the paper’s contribution, and that the proposed mirror changes the object being studied. I cannot honestly make that a strong case against every worthwhile mirror. A computational study of core convergence in a high-multiplicity stochastic market could be a natural extension the authors recognize; the paper’s mention of continuum matching makes the population regime plausible, though it is not itself a computational answer. My negative case is therefore limited: this is a weak paper-specific computational anchor, not evidence that no worthwhile mirror exists.
