The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, the paper's grouped four-alternative menu design, and rational masses \(\mu_{S,a}\) for menu-choice types \((S,a)\), promised to arise from a two-component Plackett–Luce model, recover \((\alpha,\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)})\) up to component swapping within \(\eta\) in parameter distance. Here \(\phi_S=\sum_{a\in S}\mu_{S,a}\), and the promise is \(\mu_{S,a}=\phi_S\sum_{r=1}^2\alpha_r\theta_a^{(r)}/\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j^{(r)}\), with \(\theta^{(r)}\) normalized and \(\theta_i^{(r)}\ge\epsilon\). What is the worst-case running time in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), mass bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\eta)\), including the effects of conditioning?

The model it lives in

The society is represented by rational masses \(\mu_{S,a}\) over observed menu-choice types in the paper's grouped design. The decision variables are the two mixture weights and normalized component parameters; the objective is to minimize the squared error between the conditional masses \(\mu_{S,a}/\phi_S\) and the corresponding two-component Plackett–Luce choice probabilities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's two-component consistency result for the grouped choice-\(2,3,4\) design. It leaves the non-identifiability results, the other identifiable data designs, and the arbitrary-\(k\) theory outside the computational question.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the worst-case complexity of recovering the parameters from exact rational masses?
  • How does recovery complexity depend on small mixture weights or nearly indistinguishable components?
  • Can the approach extend to mixtures with arbitrary \(k\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s \(2\)-component learning result. The named anchor is Theorem 4, proved here: for \(m\ge4\), if every component parameter satisfies \(\theta_i^{(r)}\in[\epsilon,1]\) for some \(\epsilon>0\), then Algorithm 1 is consistent when its moments use either ranked top-\(2\) plus \(2\)-way orders, or the paper’s grouped choice-\(4\) design. The algorithm estimates the structure frequencies from observed proportions and fits the mixture parameters by GMM. “Consistent” means its estimate converges in probability to the ground-truth parameter as the sample size grows, subject to the paper’s identifiability convention of ignoring component label switching.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Population \(2\)-PL Choice Recovery. Consider a large preference survey in which many respondents choose from small menus of alternatives, using the grouped choice-\(2,3,4\) design in the paper. A type is a complete observed response: the menu \(S\) and the chosen alternative \(a\in S\). Its mass \(\mu_{S,a}\) is the fraction of the population giving that response. With many respondents and relatively few menu-response types, the population can have \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of positive-mass response types.

An instance gives \(m\), the menu family, and rational masses \(\mu_{S,a}\), promised to come from a \(2\)-PL mixture with positive structure frequencies. Set \(\phi_S=\sum_{a\in S}\mu_{S,a}\) and \(q(a\mid S)=\mu_{S,a}/\phi_S\). The unknowns are mixture weights \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) and normalized component parameters \(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)}\), with \(\theta_i^{(r)}\ge\epsilon\). The task is to recover them, up to swapping the components, by minimizing
\[ \sum_{(S,a)\in E}\left(q(a\mid S)-\sum_{r=1}^2\alpha_r\frac{\theta_a^{(r)}}{\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j^{(r)}}\right)^2, \]
where \(E\) is the paper’s selected set of choice moments. On promised instances, a solution has zero residual. A computational version could ask for an \(\eta\)-approximation, with complexity measured in \(m,\tau\), the mass bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\eta)\).

The paper’s Theorem 2(c) supports this mirror by establishing identifiability for \(2\)-PL from choice-\(2,3,4\) data; it is support for the inverse problem, not a complexity result. For the restricted two-component case, I would expect tractability (Class A): the paper already supplies a consistent moment-fitting method, and the grouped design uses a bounded number of moments per group. But that expectation is not proved as a polynomial-time bound. Exact inversion, robust recovery when parameters are nearly indistinguishable, and the extension to arbitrary \(k\) are open computational questions.

The scope is the grouped choice-\(4\) branch of Theorem 4, not the paper’s non-identifiability results or its arbitrary-\(k\) theory. The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 4 proves statistical consistency, not a complexity classification. Also, the paper already defines a probability distribution over partial-order reports. The mirror is distinct only if the deterministic mass vector itself is treated as the computational input to be inverted; otherwise it risks restating the paper’s population-level statistical model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest case against starts with the anchors themselves. Theorem 4 proves statistical consistency, not a computational complexity bound. Its guarantee is that an estimator converges as the number of i.i.d. observations grows, under a parameter lower bound. It says nothing about worst-case runtime, bit complexity, or approximation guarantees. The paper’s claims of efficiency are supported by experiments, not a theorem classifying a computational problem.

The proposed population recovery task changes that target. Given exact rational masses for menu-response pairs, it asks us to invert the observable choice probabilities and recover latent mixture parameters. If those masses are empirical frequencies, this is a compressed representation of the sample: the paper’s GMM already computes its selected moments from frequencies. If they are exact population probabilities, sampling uncertainty has disappeared, and the task is deterministic inverse estimation. That may be a reasonable new problem, but it is not a continuous version of Theorem 4’s finite-sample consistency claim.

Theorem 2(c) does not supply a second computational anchor. It establishes identifiability: the parameter-to-distribution map is one-to-one under the stated choice data, modulo label switching. That is a uniqueness result, not a runtime result. With exact population probabilities, it answers whether recovery is unique; asking how fast to compute the recovery adds a new question. The paper’s chosen moments and GMM algorithm make that question concrete, but do not answer it.

There is also a type mismatch in the cleanest proposed mirror. A menu-response pair \((S,a)\) is a complete record for estimating the model, but it is an observed signal, not a voter’s underlying preference type. If we instead take complete rankings as the types, their population masses are not the partial choice frequencies supplied to the recovery algorithm. Supplying those ranking masses would give us the preference distribution directly; withholding them and supplying choice records returns us to the paper’s statistical observation problem. Neither formulation yields a clear new society-level decision problem of the kind ChoCo is built to classify.

These points defeat Theorem 4 and Theorem 2(c) as *anchors for the proposed mirror*. They do not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A better version would ask for the bit complexity or robust approximation complexity of recovering a two-component Plackett–Luce model from exact rational choice masses, with conditioning controlled as well as parameters bounded away from zero. The paper does not settle that question, and it could be useful. My negative case is therefore limited: the paper offers no named computational-complexity result to mirror, and the proposed recovery task is a new computational statistics problem rather than a clear continuation of its consistency theorem. I cannot honestly rule out that the stronger inverse-problem version is worth the programme’s time.
