What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror of the *setting*: imagine a very large all-play-all federation whose clubs fall into a small number \(\tau\) of complete audience-profile types. Let \(\mu_r\) be the fraction of clubs of type \(r\), and let \(b_{rs}\) be the audience for a type-\(r\) club hosting a type-\(s\) club. In the continuum limit, the equal-split allocation per club, scaled by league size, is

\[ q_r=\frac12\sum_s\mu_s(b_{rs}+b_{sr}), \]

and the total revenue per squared league size is \(\sum_{r,s}\mu_r\mu_s b_{rs}\). Thus the population of recipient teams—not the audience data or the money—is the continuous object. A league with many clubs and relatively few market or schedule types could make this a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

But that is an unanchored prospect, not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes the uniform rule using equal treatment of equals and aggregate monotonicity; Theorem 2 characterizes the equal-split rule using equal treatment of equals and team monotonicity. Theorems 3–5 characterize families of rules using other combinations of axioms. These are axiomatic characterization results, not named results about complexity, algorithms, or computational formulations. The paper contains no qualifying computational anchor, so there is no honest per-anchor prover question to state.

The positive case is therefore limited: the underlying revenue-sharing problem could admit a population-continuum analogue that authors might recognize, especially in a large federation with few complete club types. Its weakest point is decisive for this programme: the paper’s contributions are axiomatic, while computing allocations from the displayed rules is not the computational question its theorems answer. Presenting that as a continuous computational mirror would stretch the paper’s results beyond what they establish.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed many-club federation is a coherent high-multiplicity setting if clubs fall into a few complete types and audiences depend only on the pair of types. That is the strongest part of the case for a mirror. In less symmetric leagues, encoding each club’s opponent-specific audiences and schedule may make the types nearly unique, but the stylized setting is plausible enough that I would not reject it on that ground alone.

The decisive problem is what the paper contributes. Theorems 1–5 characterize allocation rules from equal treatment, additivity, and monotonicity axioms. None states a computational result. Replacing the finite team set with type masses and a pairwise audience kernel would create continuous versions of those axioms and characterizations; that remains axiomatic work, which ChoCo’s scope excludes. Computing allocations under the displayed rules would require only their aggregate formulas—finite sums for a finite type support, or integrals in a continuum model. That does not expose a computational question from this paper for ChoCo to chart. If arbitrary kernels make those integrals hard to compute, the difficulty depends on how the kernel is represented, a computational problem the paper does not formulate.

So the proponent’s population model does not supply an anchor: it supplies a possible new setting for axiomatic results. I cannot honestly claim that no author could find that extension worthwhile. The negative case is strongest and clearest within this programme’s scope: even the best plausible population model yields no continuous computational mirror of a named result in the paper.
