The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a possible computational extension, not one anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says each constrained sequential priority rule satisfies Pareto efficiency, consistency, and priority. Theorem 2, also proved here, says the constrained random arrival rule treats equals equally. Propositions 1–3, proved here, say that the constrained random arrival rule fails Pareto efficiency, population monotonicity, and balanced impact, while the constrained sequential priority rule fails resource monotonicity. These are axiomatic results, not complexity or algorithmic results. The paper notes that computing CRA requires averaging over \(n!\) orders, but gives no named result about the complexity of that computation. So there is no qualifying anchor, and no per-anchor mirror question to report.

There is still a plausible follow-up problem closely tied to the paper’s rules. Consider large creditor pools in which many claimants have identical claims and request the same subset of issue-specific resources. Let a type be \(t=(S_t,c_t)\), with \(S_t\subseteq\mathcal I\) the issues claimed and \(c_t\) the claim. Give each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(b_i\) be the per-capita supply of issue \(i\). The input is the finite list of types, their masses and claims, and the supply vector; one could require \(\sum_{t:i\in S_t}\mu_t c_t>b_i\) for each issue.

Give each claimant a uniform arrival time in \([0,1]\). If \(r_i(s)\) is the remaining per-capita supply at time \(s\), the paper’s sequential procedure suggests the award to an arriving claimant of type \(t\) is
\[ a_t(s)=\min\{c_t,\min_{i\in S_t}r_i(s)\}, \qquad r_i'(s)=-\sum_{t:i\in S_t}\mu_t a_t(s),\quad r_i(0)=b_i. \]
The output would be the expected per-claim award \(q_t=\int_0^1 a_t(s)\,ds\) for every type. The named problem could be Continuous Random Arrival Evaluation: given the rational instance and \(\varepsilon>0\), compute \(q_t\) for all types within additive error \(\varepsilon\). I would expect the approximation version to be tractable through numerical integration of this finite-dimensional system, while exact evaluation is a separate question.

This is a credible population mirror: the types are complete descriptions for the rule, mass represents claimant fractions, and random priority becomes a continuum of arrival times. A large pool of standardized claims gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The authors could recognize this as evaluating their CRA rule in that regime. Further questions include the convergence rate from finite CRA to this limit and whether the limit preserves any of the rule’s axiomatic properties.

The weak point is decisive for the requested anchoring: this paper studies axioms for finite claimant sets, not computation, and its factorial observation is motivation rather than a named computational result. The proposed mirror is therefore a natural next question, but it is not a continuous version of a computational result established in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Propositions 1–3 establish axiomatic properties; the factorial count is a brute-force description of computing CRA, not a complexity theorem. Continuizing those axioms would be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s scope.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s large pool of standardized claimants: types \(t=(S_t,c_t)\), mass \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(b_i\). That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not count high multiplicity itself against the mirror. But the proposed ODE has a scaling error. If \(r_i\) is remaining supply *per capita*, an individual’s award cannot be capped by \(r_i\): when \(r_i>0\), the finite society has \(n r_i\) units of that issue left. For one type, one issue, \(\mu=1\), and \(0<b<c\), finite CRA awards \(c\) to roughly a \(b/c\) fraction of claimants and zero to the rest, so the limiting expected award is \(b\). The proposed ODE instead gives \(b(1-e^{-1})\), leaving positive supply despite finite CRA exhausting it asymptotically.

The corrected fluid limit looks unusually simple. While an issue has positive per-capita stock, each arriving claimant whose requested issues all remain available gets their full claim. Issue \(i\)’s stock then falls at rate \(\sum_{t\text{ active}:i\in S_t}\mu_t c_t\). When an issue is exhausted, every type requesting it becomes inactive. There are at most \(m\) such events, so a direct scan computes each type’s limiting average award in polynomial time. That could be a useful fluid-limit observation, but it does not expose a substantial continuous-optimization or complexity question in this paper. Studying finite-population error bounds would be a new stochastic approximation project, not a mirror of a result the paper proves.

So the negative case is strongest on scope: the paper is axiomatic, and its only computational hook leads to a corrected limit that appears to collapse to a short event scan. It is not airtight. A finite-\(n\) approximation theorem for CRA could be worthwhile, and the authors might recognize that follow-up. But on the evidence here, that possibility does not supply a strong continuous mirror of the paper’s results.
