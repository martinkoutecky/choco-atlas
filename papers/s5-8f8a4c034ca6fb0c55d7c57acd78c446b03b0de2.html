The continuous mirror question

Given a distribution μ over τ receiving types, a known divergence matrix \(D\), and each type \(r\)'s limiting rate vector \(y_r\) and winning hypothesis θ_r^★, recover source-influence masses \(q_{sr}=μ_r x_{sr}>0\) satisfying \(B_r q_r=μ_r y_r\) and \(∑_s q_{sr}=μ_r\), where \(B_r=(\mathbf{1}_H e_{θ_r^★}^{\top}-I_H)D\); report the unique vector when identifiable, and otherwise report non-identifiability.

The model it lives in

Each type is a receiving cohort whose members share a statistical model and exposure architecture; μ_r is its population fraction. The unknowns are mass-level source influences \(q_{sr}=μ_r x_{sr}\), constrained by the cohort's limiting belief-rate data and mass normalization, with recovery or non-identifiability as the output.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s macroscopic topology learning problem. The paper gives named results about whether that inverse problem has a unique solution, though it gives no NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity classification. The results below are therefore identifiability and feasibility anchors, not claims that the authors proved a complexity bound.

Imagine a large platform with \(S\) sending communities and a receiving population divided into \(\tau\) cohorts. A cohort might be users with the same statistical model and the same recommendation or exposure architecture. Each cohort has mass \(\mu_r\), with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). Millions of people could belong to a small number of such cohorts. The assumption that cohort members share the same aggregate exposure pattern is consequential: if their patterns differ, they must be split into more types.

For each sending community \(s\), let \(f^{(s)}\) be its data distribution and \(L^{(s)}(\theta)\) its likelihood for hypothesis \(\theta\). Suppose the known input includes the divergence matrix
\[ d_{\theta s}=D[f^{(s)}\|L^{(s)}(\theta)]. \]
For each receiving cohort \(r\), the observations are its limiting log-belief rates \(y_r(\theta)\), and the unknowns are the source influence shares \(x_{sr}>0\), with \(\sum_s x_{sr}=1\). These shares define the mass-weighted influence \(q_{sr}=\mu_r x_{sr}\): how much of cohort \(r\)’s mass is attributed to each sending community.

Continuous Macroscopic Topology Recovery asks for the \(x_{sr}\), or equivalently the \(q_{sr}\), consistent with each cohort’s observed rates. Given its limiting winning hypothesis \(\theta_r^\star\), set
\[ B_r=(\mathbf{1}_H e_{\theta_r^\star}^{\top}-I_H)D. \]
A solution satisfies \(y_r=B_rx_r\) and \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}x_r=1\), with \(x_r>0\). The requested output is the unique influence vector for each cohort when one exists; otherwise the instance is reported as non-identifiable. This is an inverse computational question about a continuous population. Hypotheses remain finite, and time supplies the limiting data rather than being the object continuized.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3 (proved in this paper). It states that if \(H\ge S\) and the divergences \(d_{\theta s}\) are jointly absolutely continuous random variables, then for every receiving agent, with probability one, the minimizing hypothesis is unique and the associated matrix \(C_k\)—the rows of \(B_k\) augmented by the all-ones normalization row—has full column rank \(S\). The corresponding cohort problem is to recover source shares from those limiting belief rates under the same diversity condition. The theorem makes the answer almost surely unique; solving the resulting linear system is polynomial-time linear algebra in \(H\) and \(S\), and the cohort problems can be solved across \(\tau\) types. I would expect this mirror to be tractable, Class A. That runtime observation is a consequence of the formulation, not a complexity theorem stated in the paper.

Two results clarify the scope of that positive case. Lemma 1 (proved here) says that the topology-learning system has a unique positive solution exactly when \(\operatorname{rank}(C_k)=S\); in particular, \(H\ge S\) is necessary. Its continuous counterpart asks whether each cohort’s mass-weighted exposure vector is uniquely determined by its belief-rate data. It supplies the rank test for the recovery problem, rather than a separate source of hardness.

Theorem 2 (also proved here) gives a boundary case. For its structured Gaussian model—common unit-variance Gaussian likelihoods with distinct means, and distinct sending-community true means chosen from those likelihood means—with \(S\ge2\) and \(H\ge S\), it proves \(\operatorname{rank}(C_k)=2\). Thus influence shares are identifiable only when \(S=2\); for more sending communities, the continuous Gaussian instance is non-identifiable even if \(H\ge S\). The corresponding continuous problem is the same cohort recovery task under those Gaussian assumptions. For \(S=2\) it is tractable by the rank test; for \(S>2\) the obstacle is failure of uniqueness, not a hardness result. Theorem 3’s diversity condition shows how richer statistical models can remove that obstacle.

This is a plausible mirror of what the authors actually study: the paper explicitly targets aggregate influence from sending components to receiving agents, and its limiting-belief rates are the data used to infer that influence. The continuum changes the receiving side from individual agents to cohort masses while preserving the target quantity. It covers the paper’s topology-learning feasibility results, especially Lemma 1 and Theorems 2–3. It does not turn the social-learning dynamics of Theorem 1 into a computational problem, nor does it continuize hypotheses or time.

The weakest point is that this is a replicated cohort version of a problem the paper already formulates macroscopically for individual receivers. The rank condition and recovery calculation do not become harder or more revealing just because each cohort has large mass. And if users’ exposure patterns are highly personalized, the number of types may approach the population size, erasing the high-multiplicity regime. Still, where large cohorts really do share statistical and exposure structure, the mirror preserves the paper’s own inverse question and gives it a clean mass-level formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is at the programme’s scope gate: the three anchors are identifiability results, not computational-complexity results. Lemma 1 gives a rank criterion; Theorems 2 and 3 establish rank properties under particular statistical models. Once the divergence matrix and limiting belief rates are available, the recovery problem is a linear system for each receiver. With finitely encoded inputs, rank testing and solving those systems are polynomial-time linear algebra. The paper does not specify a computational model for exact access to its KL divergences, either; those values need not have finite rational encodings. Turning this into a complexity question would require adding assumptions about finite observations, input precision, unknown statistical models, or network design. Those might be worthwhile questions, but they are not continuizations of the results the proponent named.

The proposed cohort model makes that gap especially clear. To ensure that members of a cohort have the same observations and unknowns, a type must include the statistical model and the relevant exposure structure. Then each cohort has the same equation as an individual receiver, \(y_r=B_rx_r\). For positive mass \(\mu_r\), the proposed aggregate \(q_r=\mu_r x_r\) gives \(B_rq_r=\mu_r y_r\) and \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}q_r=\mu_r\): a rescaling of the same recovery problem. The mass does not enter the rank condition or couple cohorts. If exposure structures vary, they must be split into more types; in the extreme, the support can grow with the number of receivers. If they are identical, the mass simply records how many copies of the same inverse problem there are.

That answers Lemma 1 directly: its condition \(\operatorname{rank}(C_r)=S\) is unchanged by the mass rescaling, so the continuous version adds no population-level recovery question. Theorem 2 likewise remains a statement about the geometry of the Gaussian divergence matrix: the same rank deficiency persists for every cohort mass. Theorem 3 remains a generic full-rank statement about diversity in that matrix; repeating the system over \(\tau\) cohorts gives \(\tau\) separate rank tests, not a new computational landscape. These results may inform an analysis of cohort recovery, but their conclusions concern statistical identifiability, not the complexity of computing over a continuous society.

A more ambitious mirror could pool belief observations and ask for a distribution over exposure types, or estimate topology from finite, noisy data. But pooling changes what is observed from the paper’s per-receiver belief streams; finite-data estimation changes its limiting-data setup. Either could become a new problem, but neither rescues the three stated anchors as continuous computational results.

The negative case is strongest against a *worthwhile ChoCo computational mirror*, not against the possibility of a meaningful high-multiplicity scenario. Large platforms can plausibly have many users in repeated cohorts, so I cannot honestly rule out every useful continuous social-learning model. The point is narrower: for the named results, population mass is an external multiplicity attached to already separate inverse problems, while the paper’s results themselves do not chart a computational-complexity landscape.
