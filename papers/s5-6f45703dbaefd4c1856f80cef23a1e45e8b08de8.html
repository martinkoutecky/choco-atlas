The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(A\), a rational positional scoring vector with fixed tie-breaking, and a binary-rational society \(\mu\) supported on ranking types \(S\subseteq\mathcal{L}(A)\), let \(a=F(\mu)\). Does there exist a target \(b\in A\) and a nonnegative mass transfer \(x_{t,r}\), from true type \(t\in S\) to report ranking \(r\in\mathcal{L}(A)\setminus\{t\}\), such that each type sends at most its mass, the resulting report distribution \(\nu\) satisfies \(F(\nu)=b\), every type sending positive mass weakly prefers \(b\) to \(a\), and a positive amount of mass comes from types that strictly prefer \(b\) to \(a\)? Here \(\nu_r=\mu_r-\sum_{q\ne r}x_{r,q}+\sum_{t\ne r}x_{t,r}\), with \(\mu_r=0\) outside \(S\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with ranking types \(t\) and masses \(\mu_t\); decision variables \(x_{t,r}\) move mass from sincere reports to rankings that make \(b\) win. For each candidate \(b\), maximize the total transferred mass from types with \(b\succ_t a\), subject to mass limits, weak-preference eligibility for every sender, and the scoring inequalities making \(b\) the tie-broken winner. A positive optimum for some \(b\) is a strong group-manipulation witness.

The objection that survived

The anchor checks one arbitrary truth-table rule across all profiles, while the stated mirror asks about a particular society under a positional scoring rule; this is a genuine narrowing, though the paper’s manipulation-witness semantics also apply profile by profile.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the group-manipulation witness search associated with Theorem 5(iii), specialized to positional scoring rules over a supplied society. It leaves the modal completeness result, the audit and update theorems, and manipulation questions for other rule families aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the transfer problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length using a separation or pricing oracle over report rankings?
  • How should fixed tie-breaking and the requirement of strictly positive benefiting mass be encoded in an exact rational optimization algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for the group-manipulation problem underlying Theorem 5(iii). Theorem 5, proved in this paper in Section 7.5, says that when a resolute rule \(f\) is supplied as a full truth table, with preference comparisons precomputed, strong group strategy-proofness can be checked by direct enumeration in time \(O(nL^n(1+L)^n)\), where \(n\) is the number of voters, there are \(m\) alternatives, and \(L=m!\) possible strict rankings. The check ranges over true profiles, nonempty coalitions, and their joint reports. This is an enumeration bound, not a hardness result. The paper offers no comparable named hardness theorem for strategic manipulation; the NP-hardness in Remark 3 concerns satisfiability of the pure modal language.

A convincing regime is a large anonymous election: many people rank the same fixed set of alternatives, but the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings is much smaller than the population. A type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. Anonymity makes this aggregation appropriate: for a positional scoring rule, the outcome depends on reported ranking shares. The alternatives and winner remain discrete; it is the population that becomes continuous. When \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), the continuous score rule reproduces the corresponding finite election.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Strong-Group Manipulation for a Scoring Rule. The instance is a finite alternative set \(A\), a rational positional scoring vector with fixed tie-breaking, and a rational society \(\mu\) listed on support \(S\) of size \(\tau\). For any report distribution \(\nu\), let \(F(\nu)\) be the alternative maximizing its total positional score.

Write \(a=F(\mu)\). A solution is a target \(b\) and nonnegative masses \(x_{t,r}\), for true types \(t\in S\) and changed reports \(r\ne t\). They must satisfy
\[ \sum_{r\ne t}x_{t,r}\le \mu_t,\qquad \nu_r=\mu_r-\sum_{q\ne r}x_{r,q}+\sum_{t\ne r}x_{t,r}, \]
where \(\mu_r=0\) outside \(S\), and \(F(\nu)=b\). Every type that sends positive mass must weakly prefer \(b\) to \(a\), and a positive amount of transferred mass must come from types that strictly prefer \(b\) to \(a\):
\[ x_{t,r}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ b\succeq_t a, \qquad \sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\sum_{r\ne t}x_{t,r}>0. \]
The question is whether such a transfer exists; a solution gives the transfer itself. It represents a coalition that can divide a type’s mass fractionally while keeping everyone who deviates weakly better off and making a positive mass strictly better off.

For this scoring-rule mirror, I expect Class A. Fix \(b\); its winning conditions are linear inequalities on the scores of \(\nu\), and \(\nu\) is linear in the transfer variables. Maximize the strictly benefiting mass subject to those inequalities and the transfer constraints. There are \(\tau m!\) possible type-to-report variables, but the pricing problem for an implicit report ranking is a weighted assignment problem over candidates and positions. That is polynomial-time solvable, giving a route to an LP algorithm with column generation or separation. The solution \(x\) is also a directly checkable witness. This is the continuous optimization angle: the finite enumeration over reports becomes an LP whose pricing step can be studied.

This mirrors the manipulation-witness search behind Theorem 5(iii), at a given sincere society, rather than every part of the paper. It does not cover the modal completeness theorem or the public-update audit results. It also suggests follow-up problems: minimizing swap cost subject to such a manipulation, restricting the allowed report domain, or finding when efficient pricing fails for more general rules.

The weakest point is the gap between the anchor and the proposed algorithm. Theorem 5 handles arbitrary truth-table rules and checks all finite profiles; the LP case I have made is for anonymous positional scoring rules at one supplied society. Its tractability would be a new result for a natural subclass, not something established by the paper. A referee could ask whether that is sufficiently close to the authors’ problem. I think it is: the outcome, true preferences, joint deviations, and strong group-improvement condition are preserved, while the many-voter, few-ranking regime makes mass the natural unit of action.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 5(iii) supplies a weak computational anchor. Its input is a complete truth table with \(L^n\) rows, and its bound is a direct enumeration over true profiles, coalitions, and joint reports. It proves neither hardness nor a nontrivial algorithmic bottleneck. The proponent’s local question also changes the task: it asks whether a particular society admits a manipulation, while the theorem checks a rule across every profile. And an arbitrary table for one fixed electorate does not specify how the rule acts on fractional populations.

That objection weakens against the better mirror. For an anonymous scoring rule, there is a natural extension to rational report distributions, and asking whether *any* such society admits a profitable mass deviation restores the theorem’s universal flavor. The proposed positive-mass condition is a sensible group analogue, and the scoring constraints plus report-ranking pricing give a plausible continuous optimization problem. The paper itself studies manipulation and uses plurality and median examples, so this is close enough to its subject that I cannot dismiss it as an unrelated question.

My honest negative case is therefore limited: the paper does not establish that this is its intended computational problem, and Theorem 5(iii) alone gives little reason to prioritize it over other continuous manipulation questions. But that is a weak objection under ChoCo’s stated standard. The stronger scoring-rule mirror remains coherent and potentially valuable; I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
