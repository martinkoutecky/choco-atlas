The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing a computational result. Its strongest formal conclusion is that, among the rules it compares, the Talmud rule satisfies all six fairness properties in Table 2. The paper applies those rules to four SRES country groups, but gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result. So there is no qualifying anchor to mirror under your criteria.

The strongest plausible extension is a high-multiplicity version of its carbon-claims allocation problem. Imagine many emitting facilities, grouped into a small number of complete types—facilities with the same projected emissions claim and other relevant characteristics. Let \(T\) be the types, \(\mu_t\) the fraction of facilities of type \(t\), \(q_t\) their per-facility claim, and \(e\) the available budget on the same normalized, per-facility scale, with \(\sum_t \mu_t q_t>e\). The question would be: compute the per-type awards prescribed by the Talmud rule. The solution is an award \(x_t\) for each type; there is no optimization objective, because the paper studies fairness rules rather than minimizing a cost.

For \(e\le C/2\), where \(C=\sum_t\mu_tq_t\), find \(\theta\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t\min(q_t,\theta)=e\), and set \(x_t=\min(q_t,\theta)\). For \(e>C/2\), find \(\theta\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t\max(0,q_t/2-\theta)=e-C/2\), and set \(x_t=q_t/2+\max(0,q_t/2-\theta)\). With finite rational support, sorting the claims gives an exact, polynomial-time computation. The regime is plausible for a large population of similar facilities facing a shared carbon quota, though less directly grounded than the paper’s country-level scenario.

That is a candidate continuous question, not a mirror of a named result in this paper. It covers the calculation of a claims rule; it does not carry over the paper’s comparison of fairness principles as a proved uniqueness result. The weakest point is that the paper’s agents are four aggregate country groups, while the facility-level regime and its normalized budget are an extension. Without a named computational anchor, I would not claim the paper supplies a ChoCo complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its formal contribution is a comparison of claims rules against fairness axioms, followed by an application to four country groups. It states no complexity classification, algorithmic theorem, or computational problem. Table 2 reports which rules satisfy which properties; that is an axiomatic comparison, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The proposed facility model is a coherent high-multiplicity claims problem. I would not reject it on the ground that facilities cannot be grouped into types. But it changes the object from allocating a global budget among country groups to allocating permits among facilities. Computing the weighted Talmud allocation on rational type masses is a new application of the paper’s stated rule, not a continuous version of one of its computational results. Making the task richer—for example, by asking which rule to choose—would add an optimization or bargaining question the paper does not formulate.

A country-type version faces a related modeling choice. Keeping countries as the claimants preserves the paper’s unit of allocation but leaves a finite set of sovereign actors. Giving mass to residents, emitters, or facilities creates a different unit of entitlement and a new distribution problem. Either can motivate independent research, but neither supplies the missing computational anchor in this paper.

That is the defensible negative case, and its limit is real: I cannot establish that no worthwhile high-multiplicity claims problem could arise in a nearby carbon-permit setting. The paper itself, however, offers no computational result for such a mirror to extend.
