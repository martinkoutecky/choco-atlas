The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. It reports experimental findings, but contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. The results that \(NASH\) was often preferred in Experiment 1 and that \(\#Projects\) stood out in Experiment 2 are empirical findings, not anchors of the required kind. Proposition 3.7 is a result by Aziz et al. that the authors adapt; it is not a computational result proved in this paper. So, under the stated rule, there are no per-anchor continuous problems to supply.

The strongest follow-on mirror I can defend is a continuous-population version of the paper’s \(NASH\) participatory-budgeting problem. A type is a complete additive utility vector \(u_t:P\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that vector. Given projects \(P\), costs \(c\), budget \(\ell\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, set \(U_t(B)=\sum_{p\in B}u_t(p)\). The problem is to return a budget-feasible bundle
\[ B\in\arg\max_{B\subseteq P,\;\sum_{p\in B}c(p)\le\ell} \prod_{t\in T} U_t(B)^{\mu_t}, \]
with value zero if a positive-mass type receives zero utility. This is the continuous geometric-mean form of the paper’s \(NASH\) objective. When \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), its maximizers are exactly those of the finite electorate’s Nash product: the continuous objective is the discrete product raised to \(1/n\).

A plausible regime is a municipality with many residents but relatively few distinct benefit profiles across a fixed project slate—for example, repeated resident cohorts sharing the same estimated utility vector. Then \(n\) may greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\), while the projects remain indivisible. The mirror retains the paper’s PB bundle-selection problem and continuizes the population; it does not make the projects or outcomes fractional. The paper’s finding that \(NASH\) was commonly selected in Experiment 1 is motivation for studying this rule, but it is not a complexity result.

I would expect this optimization problem to be Class B: hardness transfers. With one type of mass \(1\), it reduces to maximizing additive utility under a project-cost budget, which contains 0–1 knapsack. That hardness is driven by the project choices, even when the population is homogeneous. This is a consequence of the proposed mirror, not a result established by the paper. Natural follow-up questions include how complexity depends on \(m,\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), and whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist.

The soft point is substantial: this is a credible computational continuation of the paper’s PB model and studied aggregation rule, but it does not mirror a named computational result of the paper. More fundamentally, computing the bundle selected by \(NASH\) does not answer the paper’s central empirical question of which aggregation method people consider appropriate. The experiments also use small instances, so the high-multiplicity municipal regime is plausible from the PB setting, not demonstrated by their data.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered experiments report survey choices, rankings, and statistical comparisons. Equations (1)–(3) define aggregation objectives; they do not establish their complexity. The BPJR algorithm is adapted from cited work, not proved as a computational result here. So the paper supplies no complexity claim whose continuous counterpart could fill the programme’s gap.

The proposed continuous \(NASH\) problem is nevertheless a coherent high-multiplicity model. If types are complete utility vectors with masses \(\mu_t\), then, when \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), maximizing \(\prod_t U_t(B)^{\mu_t}\) gives the same bundles as maximizing the discrete voter-by-voter Nash product: the continuous objective is its \(1/n\) power. Residents sharing a benefit profile is a plausible population regime, and keeping project bundles indivisible is consistent with continuizing the population. That makes this a credible new optimization problem, but not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. It asks which bundle optimizes a selected rule; the paper’s central question is which rules people consider appropriate.

The same gap applies to Experiment 2’s \(\#Projects\) and \(0/1\) utility findings. One could define approval-ballot types, assign them population masses, and optimize a bundle using either utility. Or one could model the mass of respondents preferring each criterion. The first again extends the paper’s setup without mirroring a complexity result; the second is a survey-aggregation question, not the computational complexity question this programme targets.

So the negative case is strong against calling any of these a continuous *mirror*. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could grow out of the paper: the \(NASH\) extension may be worth studying on its own. The paper gives no basis, though, for presenting that extension as the continuization of one of its computational results.
