The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result in this paper, so there is no qualifying anchor for a ChoCo mirror. “Efficient” here means maximizing expected total surplus, not running in polynomial time. The paper says that computing the efficient plan \(\chi^*\) is a stochastic dynamic programming problem, then assumes a solution exists.

The closest named results are all mechanism and equilibrium results, proved in the paper. Proposition 1 says that with private values, an efficient decision plan paired with Team Mechanism transfers makes truthful and obedient play a Bayesian Nash equilibrium and a within-period ex post equilibrium. Proposition 2 says that, under independent types, replacing a mechanism’s transfers with the specified balanced transfers preserves a truthful-obedient BNE. Corollary 1 concludes that the Balanced Team Mechanism has a truthful BNE under private values and independent types. Proposition 3 gives conditions under which a sufficiently patient finite-state Markov game has an efficient PBE. These are substantive results, but none classifies the computational complexity of finding a decision plan, mechanism, or equilibrium. Proposition 3’s reference to Puterman’s algorithm does not supply a complexity result for this paper’s problem.

One plausible *new* question inspired by the paper would use a distribution \(\mu\) over complete dynamic agent types—signal laws, utilities, and action sets—and ask for a dynamic allocation and transfer plan maximizing expected discounted surplus, subject to truthful-obedient incentives and period-by-period budget balance. But that would be an extension, not a computational problem anchored by a result here. Its weakest point is substantial: in an atomless population, an individual report may have no effect on aggregate decisions, so the paper’s per-agent incentive arguments may not survive the continuum limit. The paper itself distinguishes earlier continuum-agent models with predictable aggregates from the more general finite-agent setting it studies.

So the honest positive case is only that the paper suggests a possible research direction. Under the requested anchor rule, it contains no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the anchor rule: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 proves incentive compatibility given an efficient decision plan; Proposition 2 shows that balanced transfers preserve a truthful-obedient equilibrium under independent types; Corollary 1 applies those results; and Proposition 3 proves existence of an efficient PBE under conditions on a finite-state Markov game. “Efficient” means maximizing expected surplus, not running in polynomial time. The paper assumes an efficient plan exists, and Proposition 3’s use of Puterman supplies no complexity classification. None of these results specifies a computational problem and its complexity.

The best salvage would be to ask for the complexity of synthesizing an efficient, truthful-obedient, budget-balanced mechanism in a large population, with types describing agents’ signal processes and utilities. That could be a sensible new project. But it is not a continuous version of a result here: the paper studies implementation in a given stochastic environment, while the proposed question introduces a mechanism-synthesis problem over a population distribution. The full dynamic state and the agents’ history-dependent deviations remain central; a distribution over current types alone need not represent them. In an atomless model, an individual report also has no effect on aggregate decisions, so preserving the paper’s incentive problem would require additional structure, such as individual-level transfers or finite-population effects. Those choices may be worthwhile, but they define a new model.

This is not an argument that continuum-agent mechanism design is uninteresting or already solved. The paper itself points to continuum-agent models as a distinct regime, which supports the plausibility of that research direction. It is an argument that this paper provides no computational anchor for it. I would oppose a green verdict for a mirror of this paper’s results. The broader claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile continuous mirror is weaker: the material supports “no qualifying mirror is established here,” not a universal no-go.
