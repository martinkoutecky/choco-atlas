The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrower than the requested anchor-based case: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. It has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting membership in \(\mathrm{P}\), \(NP\)-hardness, parameterized complexity, or a comparable result. Its main findings are experimental and statistical.

The closest object is Table A4, “Ex-Post Pareto Efficiency.” The authors say that they formulate an integer program to maximize Pareto-improving trades using subjects’ reported preferences. It finds \(251\) trades, involving \(95\) students, under the Auction, versus \(44\) trades, involving \(23\) students, under A-CEEI; the differences are significant at \(p<0.001\). But Table A4 is an empirical computation, not a numbered theorem or complexity result. Appendix H gives the integer program in prose, not as a named result. I therefore would not honestly present it as a formal anchor.

If the programme permits this nearest computational object as a provisional anchor, the strongest mirror is the following.

Call it Continuous Pareto-Trade Capacity for Course Allocation. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over student types. A type includes the complete reported preference function used in the experiment—course values and pairwise adjustments—as well as eligibility and schedule constraints. The course menu is a finite set \(J\), and \(\mathcal S\) is the set of feasible schedules: at most five sections, no time conflicts, and at most one section from each course. The current allocation is a mass distribution \(a_{t,S}\), where \(a_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) currently holding schedule \(S\). Course capacities are per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\), so aggregate capacity scales with population.

A solution chooses mass transfers \(y_{t,S,S'}\ge 0\), meaning that mass of type \(t\) currently holding \(S\) is reassigned to \(S'\). Transfers must satisfy \(\sum_{S'}y_{t,S,S'}\le a_{t,S}\), every receiving schedule must be feasible, and reported utility must not decrease: \(r_t(S')\ge r_t(S)\). Aggregate course use after reassignment must respect capacity:

\[ \sum_{t,S}a_{t,S}\mathbf 1[j\in S] + \sum_{t,S,S'}y_{t,S,S'} \bigl(\mathbf 1[j\in S']-\mathbf 1[j\in S]\bigr) \le \kappa_j \]

for every course section \(j\). The objective is to maximize the number of course-seat exchanges,

\[ \sum_{t,S,S'} |S\setminus S'|\,y_{t,S,S'}. \]

A positive amount of mass must improve strictly, while no mass may be made worse off. The output is an optimal transfer plan and the resulting continuous allocation. Each infinitesimal student still receives an integral schedule; only the population and the number of students receiving each schedule are fractional.

This is recognisably the high-multiplicity version of the Appendix H calculation. A plausible regime would be a large MBA or continuing-education system using a common schedule template across many cohorts. Suppose tens of thousands of students fall into a few hundred complete preference types—career track, preferred meeting times, course values, pairwise complementarities, and eligibility constraints. Then \(N\gg\tau\), and the paper’s individual-level trade computation becomes a distributional allocation problem. The paper’s experimental subjects themselves are not such a regime, but the underlying course-allocation problem plausibly has one.

The expected complexity is mixed. If \(\mathcal S\) is explicitly listed, the continuous problem is a linear program and should be in Class A. If the number of sections is variable and schedules are generated implicitly, the pricing problem involves finding a feasible schedule with the best reported value subject to conflicts and pairwise adjustments. That can encode weighted independent-set or packing problems, so general hardness should transfer from the course-combinatorics side: Class B, not continuum-specific hardness. Restricted schedule structures or additive preferences could restore tractability through configuration-LP or separation methods.

The mirror covers only the paper’s ex-post Pareto-trade computation, not its central behavioural claim that students report preferences “accurately enough.” Modelling reporting accuracy would require an additional stochastic error kernel over types and would become a new behavioural model rather than a direct continuization of a named computational result. A natural follow-up would be to compare the continuous Pareto-trade capacity of A-CEEI and the Auction, and to establish the finite-to-continuous approximation error as the electorate grows.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper does not supply the required named computational result. The proposed mirror is mathematically credible and closely tracks the integer program the authors actually compute, but under the programme’s strict anchor rule it is an unanchored extension, not a fully admissible positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s opening concession is decisive under the programme’s rules: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Its contributions are experimental findings, survey comparisons, and statistical estimates—not a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, complexity classification, or algorithmic result.

The nearest candidate, Table A4, is not enough. It reports the output of an integer program applied to the experiment’s realized allocations: \(251\) detected Pareto-improving trades under the Auction and \(44\) under A-CEEI. That is an empirical diagnostic, not a computational result asserted by the paper. Treating every auxiliary optimization used in an empirical paper as an anchor would make the programme’s gate meaningless.

Even if Table A4 is accepted provisionally, the proposed mirror does not preserve the paper’s central object. The paper is fundamentally about whether true preferences can be accurately translated into reported preferences. Table A4 uses the reported utilities. A continuum over reported types therefore discards the distinction between \(u_i\) and \(\hat u_i\) that motivates the study. The stronger repair would define types as \(\theta=(u,\hat u,S,\text{eligibility},\ldots)\), including both true preferences and reporting errors. But that is a new population model of reporting-error distributions, not a continuization of the paper’s reported Pareto-trade calculation; the paper supplies neither such a regime nor a computational result about it.

The underlying course-allocation problem could certainly admit a sensible high-multiplicity story—large cohorts with repeated preference and eligibility types—and the proposed mass-transfer LP is mathematically credible. That is the honest weakness of the negative case: the allocation problem itself is continuizable. But it would be a worthwhile new ChoCo paper, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s results. Under the stated anchor requirement, there is therefore no admissible continuous mirror to grade.
