The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the required kind. Definitions 1–5 define agents, coalitions, hedonic games, and partitions; they do not establish complexity or algorithmic claims. Section 5 reports simulations with 20 agents over 100 steps and describes outcomes in Figures 1–4, but those empirical observations are not numbered theorems, lemmas, corollaries, or propositions asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or similar.

The most I can offer for the positive side is an unanchored research direction: in a large coalition-forming population with a small set of complete behavioral types, let \(\mu_q\) be the fraction of agents of each type, and study the aggregate coalition dynamics under the paper’s proposal, acceptance, and trust rules. That may be a sensible high-multiplicity model, but personalized trust and proposal histories could make the number of distinct types grow with the population. More importantly, the paper proves no computational result about those dynamics. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem—or expected complexity class—to state for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no named computational anchors to defeat: the paper states no theorem, lemma, or corollary about the complexity of a problem. Its results are simulation averages for a 20-agent system. The closest computational claim—that large systems make it impractical for agents to maintain a preference order—is informal; it defines no problem or algorithmic result. So there is nothing here whose complexity the continuous mirror could chart.

The proponent’s best direction is a large population with finitely many behavioral types, such as honesty and risk attitude. A stronger version could also specify a type-pair interaction kernel and aggregate trust state. But the paper’s mechanism depends on each agent’s interaction table, trust in particular partners, and history of proposals sent to or received from particular partners. Grouping agents by static type loses that relational state. Including it in the types makes the state space grow with the population and the history. Replacing it with an anonymous kernel and aggregate trust dynamics could yield a plausible mean-field model, but that changes the paper’s personalized negotiation process and invites analytic questions about the dynamics. To make it a computational-social-choice project, one would still need to introduce a new task—such as reachability or optimal intervention—that the paper does not formulate.

That is the strongest paper-specific case against a worthwhile mirror: its contribution is a protocol plus simulations, not a computational problem with a result to continuize, and its exact dynamics resist compression by population proportions. The universal claim is weaker. A deliberately exchangeable, finite-type coalition model could be a sensible continuous research direction. But it would be a new model and a new question, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
