The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and rational per-unit population contributions \(a_t>0\), let \(P(x)=\sum_t\mu_t x_t\), \(R(x)=\sum_t\mu_t a_t x_t/\sum_t\mu_t a_t\), and \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\) record each type's fraction of yes votes. Define \(F(x)=1\) iff \((P(x)\ge 11/20\land R(x)\ge 13/20)\lor(P(x)\ge 25/28)\). A weighted rule is \(u_{\lambda,q}(x)=1\) iff \(\sum_t\mu_t\lambda_t x_t\ge q\), for \(\lambda_t\ge0\). Find the minimum size of a monotone \(\wedge/\vee\) formula over weighted rules that agrees with \(F\) for every \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\), and give the formula and a proof of minimality.

The model it lives in

A continuum of interchangeable voting units with a finite set of types, where each type has mass \(\mu_t\) and a common per-unit population contribution \(a_t\). The decision profile is the mass fraction \(x_t\) voting yes; the task is to minimize the size of a monotone formula of weighted threshold rules that represents the dual-majority rule on every profile.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 1's robust Boolean-dimension result for the Lisbon-style rule. It leaves the paper's intersection-dimension bounds, co-dimension results, and optimization heuristics unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuous Boolean dimension for the Lisbon thresholds equal \(3\), or can one or two weighted rules suffice on the full cube?
  • Can the minimum formula size for this threshold family be determined in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the rational input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is for a narrow mirror of the paper’s Boolean-dimension result. The article has no numbered theorem asserting a complexity class such as NP-hardness or membership in P; its introduction cites the general exact-dimension hardness result from elsewhere. The best in-paper anchor for a continuous mirror is therefore Corollary 1, proved here from Propositions 2 and 3: for every relative population vector \(pop'\) within \(2.19\%\) in \(L_1\) distance of the paper’s 2014 EU population vector, the resulting discrete EU voting game has Boolean dimension exactly \(3\). Boolean dimension here is the minimum size of a monotone formula over weighted games. The paper’s three-rule expression gives the upper bound, and its robust dimension and co-dimension bounds rule out a formula of size at most \(2\).

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation of many voting units—municipalities, regions, or member organizations—with the same constitutional role and the same population contribution per unit within each of a small number of classes. The units are the agents; their class is their type. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of units of type \(t\), and \(x_t\in[0,1]\) the fraction of that type voting yes. The regime has many units but relatively few types. This is not the EU28 itself, where the 28 members have distinct population weights. It is a larger assembly with repeated, interchangeable units. The paper’s four-member blocking clause also needs a per-capita version: its natural analogue is that support from at least \(25/28\) of the units overrides the two majority tests.

Write \(P(x)=\sum_t\mu_t x_t\) for the share of units voting yes, and \(R(x)=\sum_t r_t x_t\) for the share of represented population voting yes, where \(r_t\) is type \(t\)’s share of the total population. The continuous rule is
\[ F(x)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bigl(P(x)\ge 0.55\ \land\ R(x)\ge 0.65\bigr) \ \lor\ \bigl(P(x)\ge 25/28\bigr). \]
A constituent weighted rule has the form
\[ u_{\lambda,q}(x)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_t\mu_t\lambda_t x_t\ge q, \]
with nonnegative type weights \(\lambda_t\) and quota \(q\).

The question I would hand to a prover is: Continuous Boolean Dimension for Dual-Majority Rules. Given rational type masses, population shares, and thresholds defining \(F\), find the minimum number of weighted rules needed as leaves of a monotone formula that agrees with \(F\) on every \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\), and provide the formula and weights, together with a proof that no smaller formula works. For the Lisbon thresholds above, the paper’s three-rule formula remains valid throughout the continuous domain, so the answer is at most \(3\). The substantive question is whether one or two weighted rules can represent it exactly over all fractional support profiles.

I expect this specified rule family to be Class A: the known three-leaf formula reduces the task to ruling out representations of size one or two, which looks approachable through polyhedral geometry and separation. That is a prediction, not a result of the paper; the target would be an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the rational input bit length. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem because it asks for the same minimum-formula parameter over weighted games, while requiring agreement on the full continuum of coalition masses.

This mirror covers Corollary 1’s Boolean dimension result only. It does not mirror the paper’s main intersection-dimension lower bound or its co-dimension results. Further questions include which threshold parameters make the continuous Boolean dimension drop below \(3\), and when the finite high-multiplicity game’s Boolean dimension agrees with the continuous value.

The weakest point is that Corollary 1’s lower bound is proved on the discrete coalition profiles, not on the whole cube. Its exact value does not automatically survive continuization: the continuous problem still needs a new lower-bound proof. Also, the paper’s population-perturbation robustness is not itself evidence that the EU28 is a high-multiplicity regime. The case rests on the proposed many-unit federation being a sensible setting for the same weighted-rule formula problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch in what the paper actually establishes computationally. Its named results give bounds for particular finite voting games and robustness under changes to the EU population vector. The general NP-hardness claim is cited from earlier work; the paper itself presents exact dimension as an open challenge and gives methods for bounding it. So Corollary 1 is a result about a finite rule’s Boolean dimension, not an algorithmic complexity result on a continuous society.

That makes the proposed mirror a step sideways: it asks for the minimum formula representing a threshold rule over every fractional type profile \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\). It is a precise question, but the paper’s computation concerns coalitions of 28 distinct member states. In the EU setting, a state is a constitutional decision maker with one indivisible vote; population affects its weight but does not create more voters of that state’s type. The proposed federation of repeatable units supplies the multiplicity by changing the setting.

That objection does not dispose of the mirror. A federation with many interchangeable member organizations and a few classes of population contribution is plausible. Its society can be represented by type masses, and the two majority tests and blocking clause have natural mass versions. The programme explicitly permits co-modifying the model when continuization requires it. The fact that the paper’s 28 states do not form a high-multiplicity population therefore weakens the connection to its case study, but does not rule out a sensible scenario.

Nor does the lower bound’s being proved only for discrete profiles defeat the question. It means the continuous version needs its own proof. The proposed three-threshold upper bound and exact-minimum question are recognizable as a continuous version of the paper’s Boolean-dimension problem. Generalizing that question to a family of rational type masses and quotas could yield a computational problem worth studying, even though the paper does not provide an algorithm for it.

So the honest negative case is weak. I can argue that the proponent has not yet connected their proposed continuous geometry to the paper’s computational challenge as tightly as the label “mirror” suggests, and that the concrete EU example itself has no meaningful multiplicity. But a more careful high-multiplicity federation model survives both objections. I cannot make a convincing case that no such scenario is worthwhile; the Corollary 1 anchor survives, with the caveat that its continuous lower bound and algorithmic significance remain to be established.
