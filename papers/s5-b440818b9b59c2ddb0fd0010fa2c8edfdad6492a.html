The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), Borda with fixed tie-breaking, a finite-support public-history distribution \(\bar\mu\) over partial orders \(Q_t\), and a queried homogeneous cohort of mass \(\delta\) sharing private \(P_t^{\mathrm{true}}\), current \(P_t\), and \(Q_t\), with \(P_t(c_j,c_k)\), compute either a truthful response or an order \(P_t'\) satisfying \(P_t'\models Q_t\cup\{(c_k,c_j)\}\), never producing a worse Borda winner than \(P_t\) for the cohort's common true ranking over any feasible mass-weighted completion and producing a strictly better winner for at least one completion, while minimizing \(d_{\mathrm{swap}}(P_t,P_t')\). Feasible completions assign variables \(y_{t,P}\ge0\) to complete rankings extending \(Q_t\), with \(\sum_{P\models Q_t}y_{t,P}=\bar\mu_t\); after a strategic response, move mass \(\delta\) from \(Q_t\) to \(\operatorname{cl}(Q_t\cup\{(c_k,c_j)\})\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support high-multiplicity crowdsourcing model in which public history types carry mass, private true and current rankings are shared within a queried cohort, each unit receives an integral comparison answer, aggregate Borda scores are fractional, and the cohort's distortion cost is \(\delta d_{\mathrm{swap}}(P_t,P_t')\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 2 takes the possible-winner information as given, while the positive-mass cohort requires a new public/private information model, common cohort utility semantics, and a proof that the interval characterization survives aggregate score changes.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the one-step interactive local-dominance response of Theorem 2 and uses Theorem 1 as its structural test; it leaves adaptive careful-center scheduling, convergence, and the empirical results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the aggregate possible-winner and cohort local-dominance problems be solved in time polynomial in \(m\), the number of public types, and the encoding length of the rational masses?
  • Does Theorem 1's interval characterization remain valid when a mass-\(\delta\) cohort changes aggregate possible-winner scores?
  • What public/private information structure makes hidden true rankings compatible with mass-level possible-world computation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow but genuine “yes”: this paper supports a continuous-population mirror of its interactive local-dominance response problem. I would not claim that the paper already studies such a mirror, nor that its optimal query-selection problem automatically becomes tractable.

The natural regime is a large crowdsourcing or MOOC peer-grading system of the kind explicitly mentioned by the authors. Suppose a platform repeatedly ranks \(m\) submissions under a common rubric. There may be millions of graders, but only \(\tau\) recurring behavioural types: graders with the same true ranking, the same current strategic ranking, the same accumulated comparison history, and the same utility order. At a given stage, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of graders of type \(t\), with \(\tau\ll n\). Histories must be included in the type: two graders with the same true ranking but different previous answers are not the same type.

Mass is not a fractional vote given to an individual. Each queried grader still answers either \(c_j\succ c_k\) or \(c_k\succ c_j\). The continuous object is the population: querying a cohort of mass \(\delta\) transfers that mass to a new public-history state. Aggregate Borda scores are fractional because they average integral answers.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. Under the paper’s “Common Givens”—a current preference order \(P_i\), an uncommitted query \(c_j\succ c_k\), the current possible-winner set \(PW_i\), and the transitive closure \(Q_i\) of previous answers—Algorithm 1, the Meta-Algorithm, runs in polynomial time in the number of voters and candidates. It finds a preference order \(P_i'\) satisfying both required conditions whenever such an order exists: \(P_i'\) must be locally dominant over \(P_i\), must remain consistent with \(Q_i\cup\{c_k\succ c_j\}\), and must have minimum swap distance among all such consistent orders. If no locally dominant minimum-distance manipulation exists, the algorithm leaves \(P_i\) unchanged.

The structural predicate used by that theorem is Theorem 1, also proved here: \(P_i'\) locally dominates \(P_i\) exactly when the possible winners retain their order under \(P_i'\), every interval between consecutive possible winners is at least as large as before, and at least one such interval becomes strictly larger. Thus the paper reduces strategic response computation to finite order and interval checks rather than searching over all \(m!\) rankings.

I would formulate the corresponding continuous problem as follows.

Call it Continuous Interactive Local-Dominance Response, or \(\mathrm{C\text{-}ILD\text{-}Response}_\infty\).

An instance contains:

  • a candidate set \(C\), a fixed lexicographic tie-breaking order, and the Borda rule;
  • a finite-support society \(\mu\) over types \(t\), where each type contains \(P_t^{\mathrm{true}}\), its current order \(P_t\), its public partial order \(Q_t\), and its mass \(\mu_t\);
  • a queried homogeneous cohort of mass \(\delta\leq\mu_t\);
  • an uncommitted pair \(c_j,c_k\), with \(P_t(c_j,c_k)\);
  • the public possible-winner set \(PW(\mu,Q)\).

The possible-winner set is defined exactly at the population level: \(c\in PW(\mu,Q)\) if there exists a measurable completion of every public partial order to complete rankings such that the mass-weighted Borda score of \(c\) is maximal. With finite support, this can be represented by variables \(y_{t,P}\) assigning fractions of each public-history mass to its admissible linear extensions, with

\[ \sum_{P\succeq Q_t} y_{t,P}=\mu_t. \]

The question is to return either a truthful response \(c_j\succ c_k\), leaving the current order unchanged, or a complete order \(P_t'\) such that:

\[ P_t'\models Q_t\cup\{c_k\succ c_j\}, \]

\(P_t'\) locally dominates \(P_t\) with respect to the possible worlds induced by \(PW(\mu,Q)\), and \(P_t'\) minimizes

\[ d_{\mathrm{swap}}(P_t,P_t') \]

among all orders satisfying those requirements. The solution also specifies the mass transition: \(\delta\) units move from the state \((P_t,Q_t)\) to the state \((P_t',\operatorname{cl}(Q_t\cup\{c_k\succ c_j\}))\). The distortion cost of that cohort is \(\delta\,d_{\mathrm{swap}}(P_t,P_t')\).

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. It retains Borda, partial preference elicitation, public possible winners, strategic answers, consistency under transitive closure, local dominance, and Kendall-swap minimisation. The only substantial change is that repeated, indistinguishable voters are represented by mass and queried as a homogeneous cohort. It is not continuity of the outcome space, time, or an axiom; it is continuity of the society.

I would expect the one-step problem to be Class A. Theorem 1 gives the local-dominance test through interval inequalities, and Theorem 2’s candidate scan can be run on a type representative rather than on every named voter. For a finite-support society, the weighted objective is separable across queried types, so the natural target is a running time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of the rational masses. The paper’s proof already claims that the necessary possible-winner computation is efficient in its model; under that same assumption, replacing repeated voters by type masses should remove dependence on \(n\).

There is one important qualification. The continuous version makes the possible-winner computation explicit as an optimization over fractional completions of partial orders. If exact separation for that aggregate problem is hard for unrestricted histories, then the difficulty may become a continuum-specific Class C phenomenon. That would not invalidate the mirror; it would be the substantive answer to the mirror. The theorem gives a strong reason to ask the question, not a proof that the mass-level version is already solved.

The paper’s Corollary 2 gives a useful supporting result rather than a second anchor: when both queried candidates belong to \(PW\), the voter answers according to her current order and \(P_i^{t+1}=P_i^t\). In the mirror, this yields a mass-level safe-query rule: querying a cohort on a pair inside the public possible-winner set produces no strategic profile change for that cohort. It naturally generates a further problem, \(\mathrm{C\text{-}Careful\text{-}Elicitation}_\infty\): choose adaptive cohort queries of masses \(\delta_1,\delta_2,\ldots\) so that a necessary winner is reached while minimizing \(\sum_r\delta_r\), subject to using safe queries whenever one exists. I would not claim that Theorem 2 settles this scheduling problem.

The weakest point is that the paper’s proof is explicitly about one named voter, while a positive-mass cohort can change aggregate Borda scores in a way that a single voter cannot. The continuous mirror therefore needs a fresh proof that batch local dominance behaves as expected, and it needs a careful treatment of hidden true rankings within a public-history class. The regime is also weak if every grader has an idiosyncratic history or ranking. But in a standardized, repeated crowdsourcing environment with recurring rubric-based cohorts, the high-multiplicity story is credible, and the resulting question remains unmistakably the paper’s strategic iterative-elicitation problem.

The paper contains no numbered NP-hardness or hardness-completeness theorem of its own, so there is no honest hardness anchor to prefer. The strongest positive case is consequently an algorithmic one: Theorem 2 supplies a concrete, proved polynomial-time discrete response result whose high-multiplicity population version is well-defined, nontrivial, and plausibly tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case does not survive as a faithful continuous mirror. Theorem 2 is the only serious computational anchor, but it is a conditional one-voter routine, not a population result.

Algorithm 1 already receives \(P_i\), \(Q_i\), and \(PW_i\) as inputs. Once those are supplied, neither \(n\) nor the rest of the electorate appears in the computation. Replacing voter \(i\) by a type \(t\) therefore changes no decision variable, constraint, or objective. It is merely the same finite scan with a different label. The population can matter only through computing \(PW\), but the proof simply assumes that computation is efficient. That is particularly damaging here: the paper itself notes that incomplete-information winner computations can be hard, and its discussion of necessary-winner query sets records hardness for non-plurality rules. Thus the proposed mirror must either treat \(PW\) as an oracle, making the population irrelevant, or add an aggregate possible-winner problem that Theorem 2 does not establish.

The atomless limit also destroys the strategic effect that makes the paper interesting. In the paper, one named voter can change the revealed profile and possibly the final winner. In a genuine continuum, one queried individual has mass zero. Reversing that individual’s answer changes neither the mass-weighted Borda scores nor the possible-winner set. The strict-improvement clause in local dominance consequently has no public consequence. Theorem 1 may still compare two private rankings syntactically, but its manipulation mechanism has disappeared.

The proposed repair—querying a positive-mass cohort—is not a harmless high-multiplicity translation. It turns a unilateral voter deviation into a coalition action. The paper defines utility, possible outcomes, and Kendall distance for one voter; it gives no semantics for whether a cohort maximizes common utility, average utility, worst-case utility, or something else. Theorem 1’s interval characterization and Theorem 2’s proof concern the former problem, not this batch-manipulation game. If the cohort mass is instead one clone of a population of size \(N\), its mass is \(1/N\) and the strategic effect vanishes as \(N\) grows. A fixed \(\delta>0\) avoids that limit only by changing the interaction model.

There is a further information mismatch in the proposed type construction. The proponent puts \(P_t^{\mathrm{true}}\), the current strategic order, and \(Q_t\) into the type. If the distribution \(\mu\) over such complete types is known to the voting center, then the center already knows the relevant true rankings and the elicitation problem is largely redundant. If \(\mu\) is hidden, it cannot simultaneously be the input used to define the public \(PW\). The suggested variables \(y_{t,P}\) occupy an incoherent middle ground: they fractionally split a complete type across different rankings, whereas the paper’s possible worlds contain integral rankings for individual voters. That is a new fractional-information model, not the paper’s high-multiplicity version.

Theorem 1 therefore supplies no independent rescue: it is a structural characterization conditional on the individual possible-winner set and individual history. Corollary 2 is even weaker. A safe query guarantees that one queried voter does not strategically alter her current order; it does not solve the mass-level query-scheduling problem. With zero-mass queries it has no aggregate effect; with positive-mass queries it becomes part of the new cohort game.

The honest limitation is that a deliberately designed cohort-elicitation model could be a worthwhile new research problem, especially for standardized crowdsourcing. But that concession is precisely a re-modelling of the strategic actor, information structure, and query operation. I would reject the claimed direct continuous mirror: the one-voter version is atomless and inert, while the nontrivial version is not what Theorems 1, 2, or Corollary 2 prove.
