The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no anchor to defeat. Their observation is right: Theorem 1 characterizes Nash equilibria, Proposition 3 proves existence, and Proposition 8 studies what happens as \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero. None states the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 8’s limit is a parameter limit, not a population limit. The proposed setting of many countries or institutions is a possible application, but it does not supply a computational question from the paper to mirror.

The strongest candidate would be: given a distribution over valuation types, compute the smooth multibidding mechanism’s equilibrium outcome. Grouping identical valuation vectors is sensible for a finite, high multiplicity instance. But taking that distribution as an atomless population changes the strategic problem at the heart of the paper. In the finite game, an individual’s bid affects both the project probabilities and their own payments. In an atomless model, one person’s bid no longer affects aggregate bids or probabilities. With unrestricted bids summing to zero, if project probabilities differ, the person can shift arbitrarily large bids toward lower probability projects and away from higher probability ones, making expected payments unbounded. Treating each type cohort as a single strategic player avoids that problem only by letting its members coordinate their bids; that is a different game from the paper’s unilateral Nash game.

One could instead keep the finite players and encode their counts as rational proportions. That preserves the original game and its unilateral deviations, but it does not create a new continuous computational result: the paper’s results remain equilibrium and welfare characterizations, not complexity claims. To get a computational question, one would have to add an input and an algorithmic task that the paper does not pose. There is also no evident exponentially large optimization problem or pricing problem here for continuous optimization methods to unlock.

The paper’s examples make large populations imaginable, especially many municipalities or institutions. That is the negative case’s weak point: a limit model of such participants could be worth studying in mechanism design. But the natural limit either loses the individual incentives that define this mechanism or requires a new strategic model. On the programme’s stated test—finding a computational result with a recognizable population mirror—this paper offers no worthwhile anchor.
