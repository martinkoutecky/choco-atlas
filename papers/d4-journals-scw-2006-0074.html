The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result, so it supplies no eligible anchor under your rule. Its closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which shows that a capacity-reporting game under the hospital-optimal stable rule can lack a pure-strategy equilibrium; and Theorem 2, also proved here, which establishes the same possibility under the intern-optimal stable rule. Each is demonstrated by a finite market with a best-response cycle. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 3 gives a welfare comparison between equilibria and larger capacity profiles; Theorems 4–7 establish equilibrium or truthful-reporting properties under restricted preferences. Those are equilibrium-theoretic results, not complexity results.

There is a plausible population-continuous question suggested by Theorems 1 and 2, though it cannot serve as a compliant anchor. Consider finitely many hospitals \(H\) and a finite set \(T\) of intern preference types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type is a complete preference ranking over hospitals and unemployment. Hospitals have type priorities and responsive preferences over assigned interns; normalize their true capacities to fractions of the population. Each hospital reports a capacity in \([0,\bar q_h]\). A matching is a flow \(x_{th}\geq 0\), with each type’s assigned mass at most \(\mu_t\) and each hospital’s total mass at most its reported capacity. Define the outcome by the hospital-proposing or intern-proposing deferred-acceptance rule for these divisible masses. The question is whether the resulting capacity-reporting game has a pure Nash equilibrium; a yes-instance’s solution is an equilibrium report vector and its matching flow.

A possible regime is a large cohort of resident applicants grouped into a modest number of shared preference types, competing for positions at a fixed set of hospitals. In that setting, mass rather than individual seats could be a sensible unit. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 motivate asking whether equilibrium failure persists in that regime, but they do not settle it: allowing fractional capacity reports and mass assignments changes the deviation space. The answer could differ from the paper’s finite examples.

That is the strongest honest positive case I can make: a credible high-multiplicity scenario and a concrete new equilibrium-existence problem. Its weakness is decisive for this assignment: the proposed question is an analytic extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result, because the paper reports no computational complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 show that pure strategy equilibria may fail to exist under the two stable matching rules. Theorem 3 compares outcomes when an equilibrium exists; Theorems 4–7 give equilibrium guarantees under preference restrictions. None asks for an algorithm or classifies complexity. The proposed continuous question—decide whether a mass-capacity game has a pure equilibrium, and return one when it does—would be a computational problem, but it is a new problem built from the paper’s equilibrium results, not a continuization of a computational result in the paper. The paper supplies no particular algorithmic question for the programme to carry across.

The strongest version of that new problem is coherent: model a large applicant cohort by rational masses of shared types, let hospitals report fractional capacities, and define outcomes through a mass version of deferred acceptance. But the paper’s strict hospital preferences distinguish named interns. Under those preferences, interns with different positions in even one hospital’s ranking are different complete types, so the paper’s instances generally have support growing with the number of interns. To keep support small, one must replace individual priorities with priorities over categories, such as score profiles. That may be a sensible model—especially in a school or residency market with repeated profiles—but it changes the preference structure that generates the paper’s strategic incentives. It is a plausible research extension, not a mirror established by these theorems.

The objection applies to both anchors: their shared continuous question concerns equilibrium existence and computation, and neither theorem makes that an algorithmic task. A better type-based model could make the question worth studying; the paper does not show that this is its computational contribution. I would not claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous study. The honest negative case is narrower: this paper does not supply a computational result to mirror, and the proposed equilibrium-computation problem is an additional research direction rather than a continuous mirror of one.
