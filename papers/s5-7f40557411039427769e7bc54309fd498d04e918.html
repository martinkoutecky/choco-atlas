The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and positive rational target shares \(\rho_t\) summing to \(1\), choose positive rational per-member weights \(a_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t=1\). For compatible replica sizes \(N\), form a weighted voting game with \(N\mu_t\) identical voters of each type, integer-scaled weights, and quota nearest half the total weight; let \(\Pi_t^N(a)\) be type \(t\)'s aggregate normalized Banzhaf power. Find weights minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}(\lim_{N\to\infty}\Pi_t^N(a)-\rho_t)^2\), or decide whether the target is attainable.

The model it lives in

A weighted voting game over a continuum of population types, with mass \(\mu_t\) assigned to type \(t\), common per-member weight \(a_t\), and type-level objective equal to aggregate normalized Banzhaf power. Under the proposed centered-quota limit, \(\Pi_t(a)=\mu_t a_t\), so the target is attained at \(a_t=\rho_t/\mu_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers centered-quota inverse Banzhaf design with aggregate power targets by type. It leaves aside free-quota design, finite-replica convergence rates, and the paper's multiple-game transformations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove the centered-quota limit \(\Pi_t(a)=\mu_t a_t\) for the stated replica construction, including the relevant lattice effects.
  • Determine convergence rates and how the limiting design changes when the quota is not centered at half the total weight.

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is Continuum Banzhaf Weight Design. It is the paper’s inverse design problem in a high-multiplicity regime: choose voting weights so that voter types receive prescribed shares of total Banzhaf power.

My anchor is Proposition 2.4 (Brams–Affuso), cited in the paper rather than proved there. For a weighted game \(v=[q;w_1,\ldots,w_n]\), it states that, for each voter \(i\), the polynomial
\[ B_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}(1+x^{w_j}) \]
has coefficients \(b_k^i\) counting coalitions of the other voters with total weight \(k\). Thus voter \(i\)’s number of critical coalitions is
\[ \eta_i(v)=\sum_{k=q-w_i}^{q-1} b_k^i. \]
The paper computes Banzhaf indices from these counts inside its weight-design algorithm. This is a computational formulation anchor, not a numbered complexity result: although the introduction reports that power computation is NP-hard, it does not give that claim as a numbered result. The paper also says the complexity of its design algorithm remains open.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance consists of a finite set of types \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and positive rational targets \(\rho_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\rho_t=1\). A type is a class of members identical for this voting model: each casts one yes-or-no vote, receives the same designed weight, and has the same target power share. Choose a nonnegative rational per-member weight \(a_t\) for each type, normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=1\). The objective is to minimize squared error between the target shares and the types’ shares of total normalized Banzhaf power.

Define those shares through finite replicas of the society. For each suitable \(N\), take \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), give each weight \(a_t\) (after a common scaling to integer weights), and use quota equal to half the total weight, rounded to an integer. Let \(\Pi_t^N(a)\) be the sum of the normalized Banzhaf indices of voters of type \(t\). The continuous objective is
\[ \min_{a:\,\sum_t\mu_t a_t=1} \sum_{t\in T}\bigl(\Pi_t(a)-\rho_t\bigr)^2, \qquad \Pi_t(a)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Pi_t^N(a). \]
The target has a direct finite interpretation: in the \(N\)-replica game, each voter of type \(t\) is meant to receive index \(\rho_t/(N\mu_t)\).

For bounded type weights and a quota centered at half the total weight, a local central-limit argument gives \(\Pi_t(a)=\mu_t a_t\): a voter’s pivotal probability is asymptotically proportional to their weight, while the common population-scale factor cancels when the type powers are normalized. This is my inference for the proposed mirror, not a theorem claimed by the paper. The optimum is therefore \(a_t=\rho_t/\mu_t\), so I expect this mirror to be tractable. The scaling normalization also fits the paper’s Proposition 3.3, which says that multiplying a game’s quota and all weights by the same positive factor leaves its power indices unchanged; I would treat that as supporting context, not a second anchor.

A plausible regime is a large mutual or association with many members in a small number of charter classes, where each class receives one common voting weight and the designer wants specified class-level influence. Here the number of voters grows while the number of types stays fixed or moderate. The paper itself studies designing weights for target power distributions and discusses population-based targets in its two-tier application, so the type-level target should be recognizable as the same institutional design question. The continuous object is the population; grouping members who have identical roles and assigned weights is exactly the high-multiplicity premise.

The mirror covers the inverse Banzhaf design problem and its power-counting kernel, not every result or application in the paper. It changes the unit of the target from an individual’s power in a finite game to a type’s aggregate share. That is the honest soft spot: in an atomless population, an individual is never pivotal, so an individual Banzhaf index would be zero. The type aggregate is the quantity that survives, and it corresponds exactly to summing the finite members’ indices. I think the authors could recognize that as their high-multiplicity design problem, provided the aggregate-power interpretation is stated explicitly.

Further questions include how quickly finite games approach \(\Pi_t(a)\), what changes when the quota differs from half the total weight, and whether analogous limits give useful design problems for multiple weighted voting games.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that Proposition 2.4 is a finite counting identity, not a complexity result about weight design. The paper’s own design algorithm has no complexity classification; it explicitly leaves that question open. In the proposed replica limit, the identity’s coalition-counting machinery disappears. Under the proponent’s regularity assumptions, the local central limit theorem gives
\[ \Pi_t(a)=\frac{\mu_t a_t}{\sum_s\mu_s a_s}. \]
With their normalization, this is \(\mu_t a_t\), so the target is met by \(a_t=\rho_t/\mu_t\). The mirror’s central computational question is therefore answered by a direct formula. The paper’s Penrose-law discussion points toward this asymptotic picture, though it does not establish the general target-share formula.

That is a reason to treat this as a low-priority mirror, not a decisive collision. The paper’s result is finite and combinatorial; the proposed continuous question asks what survives in a limit. A clean derivation of that limit could itself be a legitimate result, and a dull or tractable answer is still an answer. Nor does the replica construction fail merely because it needs an asymptotic definition: it gives aggregate type power a coherent meaning.

The better version—preserving integer weights, a weight budget, or other design constraints while taking the population to a continuum—also resists a categorical rejection. It may still reduce to a small optimization over types once the limiting power formula is known, but that is a genuine high-multiplicity design problem. The negative case cannot rule out its value without relying on the forbidden claim that a simple answer makes a question unworthy.

So the honest case against is narrow: the stated unconstrained mirror appears to replace the paper’s difficult finite computation with a known kind of asymptotic power calculation and a closed-form inverse. That makes it a weak computational programme target. But the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is not supported: the type-level design question is well-posed, and constrained versions could still be worth studying.
