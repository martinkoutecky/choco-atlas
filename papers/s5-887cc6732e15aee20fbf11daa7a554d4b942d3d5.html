The continuous mirror question

Given a rational polygonal jurisdiction \(\Omega\), a finite rational polygonal subdivision with nonnegative rational party densities \(\rho_A,\rho_B\) normalized so their total integral is \(1\), and integers \(d,q,r\), is there a partition of \(\Omega\) into \(d\) connected polygonal districts, each with at most \(r\) edges and total population \(1/d\), such that at least \(q\) districts have \(\int_{R_j}\rho_A > 1/(2d)\)?

The model it lives in

Treat residents as a population distribution over location and party preference, represented by rational densities \(\rho_A,\rho_B\) on a finite polygonal map. The decision variables are the boundaries of \(d\) connected districts; each must contain mass \(1/d\), and the objective is to reach at least \(q\) districts where party \(A\)'s mass exceeds \(1/(2d)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for Cell-Continuum WD, a continuous-population version of the paper’s decision problem about maximizing seats under geographic constraints.

Suppose a large jurisdiction is divided into a finite graph of adjacent census cells \(G=(V,E)\). In each cell, residents with the same deterministic party preference are interchangeable for districting purposes. The type set is therefore \(\{(v,A),(v,B):v\in V\}\), with rational masses \(\mu_{v,A},\mu_{v,B}\) summing to \(1\). A plausible regime is a city or state with millions of voters but many fewer supported cell-and-preference types: \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\le 2|V|\).

Given \(G\), those masses, a district count \(d\), and a target \(q\), ask whether the population can be divided into \(d\) equal-mass districts, each of mass \(1/d\), so that each district’s occupied cells are connected in \(G\) and at least \(q\) districts give party \(A\) a strict majority. A solution is a mass allocation \(x_{v,P,j}\ge0\), where \(P\in\{A,B\}\) and \(j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\), satisfying
\[ \sum_j x_{v,P,j}=\mu_{v,P},\qquad \sum_{v,P}x_{v,P,j}=1/d. \]
District \(j\) counts for \(A\) when \(\sum_v x_{v,A,j}>1/(2d)\). Mass can be divided among districts because an infinitesimal resident receives one district assignment, while a type-level total may be fractional. Strict majority supplies a natural tie rule for the continuum model.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: WD is NP-complete. WD asks, for a given geography of allowed districts, deterministic preferences of two parties, and an integer target, whether there is a districting that gives one party at least that many districts. The paper proves membership in NP and reduces from SET PACKING; its reduction applies in the paper’s general set-system geography, with fixed \(k\ge2\) and districts of size \(2k+1\). SET PACKING’s NP-completeness is cited from Garey and Johnson; Theorem 2’s redistricting result is proved here.

This is a credible mirror because it retains the central decision the authors study: assign a population to equal-size, geographically admissible districts to maximize the number won by a party. The population becomes continuous, while the geographic support and seat objective remain. The authors could reasonably recognize that as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime, though the cell graph is a particular geographic representation.

My lead prediction is hardness for continuum-specific reasons (Class C). The mirror still has combinatorial choices about which connected geographic supports receive enough \(A\)-mass to win. But Theorem 2 does not establish hardness for this fractional model: its reduction uses indivisible voters and arbitrary allowed set systems. Divisibility could defeat that encoding, so any hardness proof for Cell-Continuum WD would need to handle fractional allocations directly.

The main follow-up questions are whether the problem is hard for planar cell-adjacency graphs, and whether it becomes tractable with bounded graph treewidth or a fixed number of districts. One could also ask how far a continuous solution can round back to a finite election when the number of voters per type is large.

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s seat-threshold decision problem. It does not claim to mirror every result in the paper, including the pack-and-crack examples or the no-geography algorithm.

The weakest point is the gap between the theorem and the proposed geography: the theorem’s hardness result is for arbitrary set systems, while the paper’s connected-grid examples show pack-and-crack can fail but do not prove hardness for planar contiguity. The continuous mass allocation is another real change. Those limits prevent claiming hardness transfers; they do not erase the case that this is a recognizable, well-posed continuous version of the authors’ optimization question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the paper’s strongest anchor, and the stated cell model has a serious flaw. If contiguity means that the cells receiving positive mass must be connected, then infinitesimal allocations can make it irrelevant. Suppose the occupied cells form a connected graph. Choose \(\epsilon>0\) small enough that \(d\epsilon<\min_t\mu_t\) and \(\tau\epsilon<1/d\). Assign at least \(\epsilon\) of every positive-mass type to every district, then distribute the remaining mass to meet each district’s quota. Every district now touches every occupied cell, so every district is connected regardless of how the meaningful mass is allocated. The stated model therefore reduces to a statewide mass-allocation problem; it does not preserve the geographic constraint that drives Theorem 2.

The strongest repair is to represent party populations as densities over a map and require districts themselves to be connected regions of equal population. That avoids the infinitesimal-bridge problem, and the authors would likely recognize it as a question about optimal redistricting. Another repair is to assign whole census blocks, with continuous party masses inside them; that preserves graph contiguity but makes block indivisibility, rather than population mass transfer, carry the geography.

These repairs expose the limit of the negative case. Theorem 2’s reduction uses arbitrary set-system geographies, and its voters’ memberships in those sets can distinguish them all. Coarsening them into spatial types can lose that structure; keeping exact location can make the type space as large as the map. That is a real gap between the theorem and the proposed mirror. But it does not rule out a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a fixed map with many voters per geographic unit is a natural redistricting instance. Nor does the fact that the repaired model also optimizes district regions disqualify it under the programme’s scope, which allows co-modifications required by a continuous population.

So the best case against defeats the proponent’s *specific* cell formulation, but it does not defeat the better spatial mirror. I cannot make the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists honestly: this paper’s central problem is already about assigning a population to geographically constrained districts, and a population-density version is recognizable as that problem. The negative case is therefore weak against this anchor.
