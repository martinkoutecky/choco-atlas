The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its nearest named result is the unnumbered Arrow theorem in §2.1, proved here: for at least three candidates and every \(n\geq 1\), a voting rule \(f:\mathcal L(A)^n\to\mathcal L(A)\) satisfying Pareto and IIA must be a dictatorship; equivalently, \(\PIIA^n=\DICT^n\). That is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. The fixpoint proposition in §3.3 is also a proof result, not a complexity classification, and Banach’s theorem is cited background.

There is a tempting related model: let types be rankings and let \(\mu\) give their population shares. But the paper’s \(\Dis\) is a probability distribution over finite, labeled voter profiles, used to measure distances between voting rules and define voter force. It does not make the population continuous. Recasting Arrow’s theorem as an aggregation problem over \(\mu\) would change the role of voter identity and dictatorship, and would be an axiomatic extension rather than a computational mirror anchored in this paper.

So the strongest honest positive case is only that the paper offers a nearby subject for future work. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive obstacle is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Arrow’s theorem is an axiomatic characterization: it says that Pareto and IIA force a voting rule to be dictatorial. The fixpoint result is part of that proof, and Banach’s theorem is background. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The supplied case is right on this point; its references to probabilities and fixpoints do not supply a missing computational anchor.

The strongest candidate mirror is an Arrow theorem over a population distribution on rankings: take types to be rankings, and let \(\mu\) record their population shares. But a natural aggregation rule on these distributions, with mass-based Pareto and IIA, is still an axiomatic question. To preserve the paper’s notion of dictatorship, one must also decide what a dictator means in an atomless population: each individual has zero mass, so the paper’s identity-based condition does not transfer directly. Replacing it with a positive-mass bloc or another form of influence defines a new axiom, not a computational mirror of this theorem.

The fixpoint construction does not provide a stronger computational anchor. Its metric is the probability that two rules disagree on a finite, labeled profile; its force is defined separately for each named voter; and its map transfers the votes of least-force voters to a most-forceful voter. The distribution weights profiles—it does not make the society continuous. A continuum reformulation would need new definitions for force and for the transfer operation, and its central question would still be whether axioms imply a form of dictatorship, not the complexity of computing anything.

One could add computation by asking, for example, whether a succinctly encoded rule satisfies Pareto and IIA, or by optimizing a measure of its violations. But then the encoding and objective create the computational problem; the paper does not identify or classify it. Making the voter population continuous would not, by itself, make that an algorithmic consequence of Arrow’s theorem.

So I see no worthwhile *computational mirror* in this paper. That is a strong assessment, not a proof that no imaginable related project exists: one could study computational questions about aggregation over population shares. But such a project would need its own computational anchor, and the paper’s probability distribution, force, and fixpoint argument do not provide one.
