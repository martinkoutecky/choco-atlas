The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result that can serve as an anchor under your criteria. Its numbered results are probabilistic comparisons and limits. The closest are Theorem 1, proved here, which says free delegation makes \(A\) less likely to win than conventional voting for every \(m\geq 1\) and \(p>1/2\); Theorem 2, also proved here, which gives the same inequality for capped delegation when \(c>1\); and Theorem 3, proved here, which says equal weights maximize the probability of a correct decision in the common-value setting. None classifies the complexity of evaluating or optimizing a computational problem. The NP-completeness claim about delegation-game equilibria appears only as an unnumbered citation in related work and concerns a different problem.

There is a plausible population-level setting: a large blockchain community with many delegators and voters, grouped into a small number of complete types, such as delegation status and preference or signal. Type masses could be the fractions of the community, and a delegation policy could transfer mass from delegators to voter types. But the paper’s central effects depend on random assignment of indivisible votes to named voters. A deterministic mass-transfer model changes that concentration; preserving it would require additional randomness or a distribution over societies. That makes this a possible follow-on model, not a faithful continuous mirror of the paper’s numbered findings.

So I can’t give the requested prover-ready continuous problem per anchor without inventing a computational result the paper does not establish. The strongest positive case is only that the application has a credible high-multiplicity population. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is right: a large blockchain community can be a sensible high-multiplicity population. That supports using type masses to describe such a community. It does not, by itself, supply a computational problem to study. The paper defines random voting procedures and proves statements about their winning probabilities; it does not ask for an allocation, delegation policy, or other object to compute. Nor does it specify an encoding for its arbitrary distribution \(F\) or a precision requirement for computing those probabilities. The cited NP-completeness result concerns equilibrium existence in a different network game. There is no named computational result here for ChoCo’s complexity programme to mirror.

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are the strongest apparent anchors, but their strict probability comparisons are driven by finite-population fluctuations. In a continuum with voter mass split into fractions \(p\) and \(1-p\), independent preference draws average out. Random delegation also averages out: mass is distributed in proportion to the recipient types, and a fixed \(p>1/2\) leaves a positive majority margin. The win probabilities therefore converge to \(1\) under both conventional and delegated voting; the strict comparisons disappear. This is consistent with the paper’s own limiting results: as the number of delegators grows, delegation’s probability converges to the conventional one, and as the electorate grows, the majority wins with probability \(1\). A capped mass model has the same problem: symmetric capacity treatment preserves the type proportions in the limit.

One can keep a nontrivial comparison by shrinking \(p-1/2\) as the population grows, or by retaining a random aggregate population composition. The first gives a fluctuation or central-limit question about asymptotic probabilities, not a computational question from this paper. The second introduces common shocks or random type masses; it changes the independence setup on which Theorem 1 rests, and the paper’s counterexample shows that the direction of its result need not survive alternative uncertainty models. Neither gives a natural complexity problem without adding a new objective and an input model.

Theorem 3 fares no better. With independent signals and diffuse weights, the probability of a correct decision tends to \(1\) for any regular positive weighting scheme, so equal weights cease to be distinguishable by limiting accuracy. Retaining an influential voter with nonvanishing weight preserves a finite identity-level feature, but that voter is an atom, not part of a continuously divisible population. Optimizing weights under new constraints, or optimizing an error exponent, could be interesting work; those would be new optimization problems rather than mirrors of the theorem as stated.

So the negative case is not that a blockchain community lacks a plausible multiplicity regime, nor that continuous analogues cannot be written. It is that the natural population limit washes out the effects these results compare, while preserving them requires fluctuation analysis or extra modelling choices that yield no evident computational target. That is a strong case against spending ChoCo’s time on a mirror of this paper, though not a proof that no carefully designed follow-on problem could be worthwhile.
