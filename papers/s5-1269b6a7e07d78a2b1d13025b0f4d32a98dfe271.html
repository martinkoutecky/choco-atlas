The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give an anchor under your rules. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition of its own asserting a computational complexity result. Its numbered material is algorithms, equations, and empirical results. It cites the exponential communication bound from [25] and discusses an incentive guarantee from [7], but neither is a named result proved here. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor continuous question to state.

The strongest positive case is still a plausible population regime. Imagine repeated spectrum auctions with hundreds of bidders drawn from a modest catalog of firms with identical valuation schedules, budgets, and other relevant constraints. A type would be that complete bidder description; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of bidders of type \(t\). With licenses or supplies scaled per capita, the number of bidders could be large while the support size \(\tau\) stays modest. The paper’s 50-bidder experiments support the relevance of large cohorts with shared structure—for example, with 50 national bidders and 196 items, MT-MLCA-F reports efficiency \(0.761\), against \(0.728\) for the baseline. But the MRVM classes in those experiments share only a broad structure: random parameters distinguish bidders, so “local,” “regional,” and “national” cannot simply be counted as three complete types.

As an unanchored research question, I would propose *Type-Mass Query-Limited Allocation*. An instance gives \(m\) items, a finite set \(T\) of observable cohorts with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita item supplies \(\rho_j\), a query cap \(q\), and for each cohort a finite hypothesis class of possible common valuation functions \(v_t:\{0,1\}^m\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\). The actual valuation in each cohort is private but can be queried by bundle. The question is whether an adaptive query policy, asking at most \(q\) distinct bundle values per cohort, can guarantee an \(\alpha\)-efficient allocation for every valuation profile in the supplied classes. Its output is a mass allocation \(z_{t,x}\ge0\), with \(\sum_x z_{t,x}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,x:j\in x}z_{t,x}\le\rho_j\); each unit of mass still receives an integral bundle. The per-cohort query cap is an explicit co-modification: an answer from one representative serves the whole cohort of exact clones.

My tentative expectation for this question is hardness transfers in the unrestricted version: the \(2^m\) bundle menu and the query-selection choices remain, even though allocation over mass is continuous. Whether hardness persists when \(\tau\) is small is the more interesting follow-up; singleton cohorts preserve individual-bidder instances, but do not establish hardness for the high-multiplicity regime.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s method shares information across bidders with *similar* valuations, while this mirror makes each cohort’s valuations identical and its membership observable. That changes the information structure and the query accounting. If the authors require the mirror to retain unknown similarities between distinct bidders, this candidate may not count as their problem. And because the paper offers no qualifying complexity anchor, this is a reasoned research direction, not a continuous counterpart to one of its named theorems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the proponent supplies no qualifying anchor. This paper proves no complexity result for preference elicitation or auction allocation. It defines a query-limited optimization problem, presents Algorithms 1 and 2, and reports experiments on SATS instances. The exponential communication bound is cited from earlier work. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous counterpart the programme can classify. A complexity study of a continuous auction model would begin a new line of work, rather than mirror a result in this paper.

The proposed *Type-Mass Query-Limited Allocation* is a plausible research question, but it changes the paper’s information model. It assumes observable cohorts of exact clones, with known masses and a query answer that applies to every member of a cohort. The paper studies bidders whose valuations are related but distinct, keeps bidder-specific models, and uses ID features to help those models capture their differences. The proposed worst-case guarantee over finite hypothesis classes is also a new objective; the paper’s contribution is an empirical comparison of learning architectures.

A faithful high-multiplicity model would make a type a complete valuation description. If many bidders share that type, the model has one valuation-learning task per cohort, and the paper’s transfer between distinct tasks largely disappears. If the model retains the paper’s bidder-specific differences, those differences belong in the types, and the support may grow with the number of distinct bidders. Scaling item supplies with population size can make a mass allocation well-defined, as the proponent proposes, but it also requires a repeated-resource auction scenario beyond the paper’s tested setup.

That is a strong case against treating this paper as an anchor for the continuization programme. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: a large population of exact-clone bidders with per-capita supplies could be a sensible setting, and the paper’s motivation makes it adjacent to the authors’ interests. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: this paper provides no computational theorem to mirror, and its plausible continuous extension would require substantial new modelling.
