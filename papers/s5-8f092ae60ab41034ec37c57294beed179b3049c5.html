The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule. The supplied text has no visible theorem numbers, and none of its named results classifies a computational problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The main theorem in §3.1 is the strongest result to build from, but it is a probabilistic performance theorem: with one woman choosing an optimal strategy in a uniformly random balanced market, almost surely a \(1-o(1)\) fraction of women receive partners in their top \(O(\log^{2+\epsilon} n)\), the expected average women’s rank is \(O(\log^4 n)\), and the average men’s rank is at least \(\Omega(n/\log^{2+\epsilon} n)\) almost surely. Those are asymptotic bounds on outcomes, not a computational result. The prose observation that the woman’s best stable match can be found by running Gale–Shapley with the sides reversed is not a named result.

There is a plausible research lead: represent a large matching market by masses of agents with shared preference types, and let a distinguished woman choose a truncation threshold to improve her match and affect the women’s average rank. A recurring-cohort market could make the type model plausible. But preserving the paper’s single strategic agent is the hard part. In a continuum, she has vanishing mass; giving her positive mass makes her a cohort. Also, the paper ranks named individuals, while a type-level continuum model needs to specify preferences over types and how matching flows implement deferred acceptance. I would not claim the authors would recognize that reformulation as their theorem’s continuous computational analogue.

So there is no anchor here for which I can responsibly state the requested prover-ready continuous problem or predict a complexity class. The paper’s results concern average-case behavior in random finite markets, rather than the computational questions that the continuization programme targets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its main theorem bounds matching ranks in a random finite market: one woman chooses an optimal strategy, and the other agents’ complete rankings are drawn uniformly. The theorem says nothing about the complexity of finding an outcome or strategy. The paper does say that the woman’s best stable match can be computed by running Gale–Shapley with the sides reversed, but that is an aside, not a complexity result the paper develops.

The best repair would represent men and women as masses over preference types and study the strategic woman’s effect on average ranks. That runs into the paper’s defining feature. If she remains one person, her share is \(1/n\), which vanishes in the continuum; a hybrid model could retain her as an atom, but that is no longer a purely continuous society. If she becomes a positive-mass cohort, the model is about coordinated group manipulation, not the influence of one optimally acting woman. It also needs new matching primitives: the paper ranks named individuals, while a finite type model would typically rank classes and decide how to match indistinguishable agents within each class. Under the paper’s uniformly random rankings over named agents, repeated complete preference types are rare, so those classes would have to be introduced by changing the market regime.

One could formulate computational questions for either repaired model, and a recurring-cohort interpretation might be plausible. But those would be new problems, not continuous versions of a computational result in this paper. A continuum analysis of the rank bounds would instead ask about limiting stochastic matching dynamics—the kind of analytic question the programme distinguishes from its computational agenda.

That makes the negative case strong under ChoCo’s mirror criterion, but it does not establish that no worthwhile continuous matching project could be built from these ideas. The narrower conclusion is that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for one.
