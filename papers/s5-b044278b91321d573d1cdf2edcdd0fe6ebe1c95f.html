The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous computational mirror from this paper: it contains no named computational result. It studies finite-agent exchange economies and proves existence, uniqueness, and equivalence results for solution concepts; it does not analyze algorithms or complexity.

The closest results are Theorem 3.3, which under Assumptions (A.1)–(A.5) says the \(\gamma\)-core and extreme core coincide with the unique \(\gamma\)-stable set, and Theorem 3.4, which under (B.1)–(B.4) says the \(\delta\)-core and Pareto-optimal allocations coincide with the unique \(\delta\)-stable set. These are substantive mathematical results, but neither asserts that a problem is tractable, hard, or has any other computational complexity. The paper gives no algorithmic input representation or complexity measure from which such a claim could be read.

A type-mass version of the exchange economy is conceivable: types could encode complete preferences and endowments, \(\mu_t\) could denote each type’s population share, and blocking could redistribute the coalition’s endowment mass or status-quo allocation mass. But the paper defines utilities over the consumption vector of a finite set of named agents, not over a distribution of types. Building that continuous model would require additional assumptions about how externalities depend on the population profile. It would be a proposed extension, not a computational mirror anchored in a result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about one narrow point: the paper contains no complexity theorem, so there is no computational result to translate literally. But that does not establish that no worthwhile computational mirror exists. Theorems 3.3 and 3.4 identify precise solution sets; asking how to find or recognize their members is a natural computational question about the paper’s central objects.

The stronger anchor is Theorem 3.4. Consider a large exchange economy with finitely many types, where a type records an agent’s preferences and endowment, and \(\mu_t\) is its rational population share. A natural externality model lets an agent’s utility depend on their own bundle and on type-level average consumption, \(U_t(x;q_1,\ldots,q_\tau)\). A coalition can select positive masses of types and redistribute their status-quo resources; those resources scale with the coalition’s mass, so the deviation remains meaningful in a continuum. With an appropriate representation of the utilities, one can ask how hard it is to find an allocation in the \(\delta\)-core, or to decide whether a proposed allocation belongs to it. Theorem 3.4 makes these questions especially crisp: under its assumptions, the \(\delta\)-core is exactly the Pareto-optimal set and the unique stable set. Computing one representative allocation is a finite-output task even though the set itself may be infinite.

This is population continuization: \(\mu_t\) represents shares of society, while the paper’s commodity bundles remain outcomes. It also preserves the paper’s central concern with externalities in a plausible high-multiplicity regime—many consumers can share preferences and endowments while caring about consumption across types. The authors study existence and uniqueness of allocations with these properties, so computationally finding or checking such allocations is a recognizable follow-on question. The result need not already be algorithmic for that question to be worthwhile.

Theorem 3.3 is a weaker route: its exclusive-commodity assumption is awkward to lift when many agents share a type, so I would not rest the case on \(\gamma\)-dominance. The \(\delta\) result does not have that particular obstruction. Nor does the need to specify how externalities depend on a population profile rule out the mirror; it identifies a modeling choice, and aggregate type consumption is a natural one. The honest limit is that this would be a computational extension, not a complexity result already present in the paper. That defeats the claim of a direct mirror, but it does not support the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
