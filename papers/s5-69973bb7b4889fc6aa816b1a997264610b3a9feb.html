The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), school types \(j\) with rational per-capita capacities \(q_j\), and a feasible proposed allocation \(x_{t,j}\), is the allocation popular against every feasible reassignment of the same population? Let \(z_{t,j,k}\) be the mass of type \(t\) reassigned from its current destination \(j\) to destination \(k\), require \(\sum_k z_{t,j,k}=x_{t,j}\) and \(\sum_{t,j}z_{t,j,k}\le q_k\) for each school type \(k\), and maximize \(\sum_{t,j,k} z_{t,j,k}(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t k])\); the allocation is popular exactly when this maximum is at most \(0\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity HA model with applicant types carrying mass \(\mu_t\), school types with per-capita capacity \(q_j\), and a proposed fractional allocation \(x\). Reassignment variables \(z\) move mass between destinations, and their objective is the total vote margin; a maximum-cost flow tests whether any reassignment has positive margin.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers HA popularity recognition. It leaves the paper’s dual-certificate equivalence proofs, the HAT and SMI results, and extensions to ties or two-sided preferences unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s graph-structural characterization be lifted to applicant-type masses and shown equivalent to the optimization-based criterion in this model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is for the HA popularity-recognition problem. The paper contains no numbered theorem giving a new complexity classification, and it gives no hardness result. The usable computational anchor is Theorem 2.5, cited from KMN11 and McC08, not proved in this paper: for a house-allocation instance with strict applicant preferences, define \(w_M(a,h)=2\) if \(a\) prefers \(h\) to their assignment in \(M\), \(1\) if \(h=M(a)\), and \(0\) if \(a\) prefers their assignment in \(M\) to \(h\). The theorem says that \(M\) is popular exactly when it is a maximum-weight \(A\)-perfect matching under \(w_M\). Since maximum-weight bipartite matching is polynomial-time solvable, this gives a polynomial-time test for whether a proposed matching is popular. The paper’s own contribution for HA is the equivalence proof: in particular, Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 construct the dual certificate that connects its graph-structural characterization to this optimization view.

My lead question is Continuous HA Popularity Recognition. There are applicant types \(t\in T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type includes each applicant’s complete relevant information: acceptable schools and their strict ranking. There are school types \(j\in H\), each with rational per-capita capacity \(q_j\); repeated interchangeable seats are represented by capacity. Each type has its own last-resort destination \(d_t\). The input also includes a proposed feasible allocation \(x_{t,j}\), including any mass at \(d_t\): \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\), and each school’s allocated mass is at most its capacity.

The question is whether that allocation is popular against every feasible reassignment of the *same population*. For each type \(t\), destination \(j\) in the proposed allocation, and permitted new destination \(k\), let \(z_{t,j,k}\) be the mass reassigned from \(j\) to \(k\). Require \(\sum_k z_{t,j,k}=x_{t,j}\), and require each school’s total new allocation to be at most \(q_k\). Maximize the vote margin
\[ \sum_{t,j,k} z_{t,j,k} \bigl(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t k]\bigr). \]
The proposed allocation is popular exactly when this maximum is at most \(0\). A “no” answer can include a positive-margin reassignment; a “yes” answer can include an optimality certificate. Although \(x\) and \(z\) are fractional masses, each person still receives one destination. Within a type, the mass currently assigned to \(j\) is the cohort whose votes are compared against its reassignment.

The regime is large-scale school placement: many pupils, but a moderate number \(\tau\) of distinct preference-and-eligibility types, and a moderate number \(r\) of school types. Applicants share a type only when they agree on all information the model uses. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime when many seats at each school are interchangeable; scaling their capacities with the population is part of the model.

The expected result is tractability. The recognition problem is a maximum-cost bipartite flow: the left side has one node for each positive-mass incumbent cohort \((t,j)\), the right side has school destinations, and edge weights encode the vote comparison. Its size is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), and the input bit length \(L\). There is also a direct discrete-to-continuous dictionary. For rational input, scale by a common denominator to obtain an integer high-multiplicity HA instance. The reassignment flow has an integral optimum at that scale, so allowing fractional mass does not create a new winning deviation: popularity agrees with popularity in the replicated discrete instance.

This is a narrow mirror of the HA result, not a claim about the paper’s HAT or SMI sections. The authors should recognize it as their underlying question: does any feasible reassignment win a strict majority of the applicants? The mirror preserves that question and its one-sided electorate. Its weakest point is also clear: the anchor is a polynomial-time characterization cited from earlier work, not a new hardness result or a new complexity theorem of this paper. The mirror therefore supports a clean high-multiplicity formulation and dictionary, but it does not probe whether continuization dissolves discrete hardness. Further questions include whether the paper’s graph-structural characterization has a useful mass-level counterpart, and whether the same construction extends to ties or to the two-sided SMI model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the proposed anchor misses what this paper contributes. Theorem 2.5 is a prior characterization, credited to KMN11 and McC08 in the preliminaries. The paper’s own results in Sections 3–5 establish equivalences between known graph and optimization characterizations, with dual certificates; they do not give a new complexity classification for popularity recognition. Continuous HA recognition therefore mirrors a background tool more directly than the paper’s main contribution.

A closer mirror would ask whether those two characterizations remain equivalent for type masses and per-capita school capacities. That is a plausible question, but the proponent’s flow formulation does not answer it: it tests popularity without supplying a mass-level structural characterization or deriving one from the other. Still, this objection is about the paper’s novelty, not the mirror’s legitimacy. The programme asks for computational questions, and the continuous recognition problem is precise, computational, and recognizable as the applicants’ majority-vote question. Scaling school capacities is expressly allowed as a co-modification; grouping applicants by preferences and splitting their mass among current assignments preserves the information the vote comparison needs. The exact replication argument also makes the discrete–continuous dictionary unusually clean.

I would not claim that existing “fractional popular matching” work already answers it: that work studies lotteries over whole matchings, which is outcome-space continuity rather than a continuum of applicant types ([Huang and Kavitha, “Popularity, Mixed Matchings, and Self-Duality”](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.2020.1063)).

So the negative case is weak. It can say the anchor is peripheral to this paper’s contribution, but it cannot establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Under the programme’s stated test, the HA popularity-recognition mirror survives.
