The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), symmetric type-pair valuations \(v_{ab}\), and a fixed team-size limit \(s\ge3\), choose nonnegative densities \(\lambda_S\) for multisets \(S\) of at most \(s\) types, subject to \(\sum_S q_a(S)\lambda_S=\mu_a\) for each type \(a\). A type-\(a\) member of \(S\) receives utility \(u_a(S)=\sum_b(q_b(S)-\mathbf 1[a=b])v_{ab}\); require every assigned member to have nonnegative utility. Maximize the population Nash welfare \(\exp(\sum_S\lambda_S\sum_a q_a(S)\log u_a(S))\), with value \(0\) if no feasible assignment gives every positive-mass agent positive utility.

The model it lives in

A bounded-team hedonic society with type masses \(\mu_a\), symmetric valuations \(v_{ab}\), and team configurations \(S\) of at most \(s\) members. Real-valued densities \(\lambda_S\) cover each type mass exactly; each person still receives an integral team, and the objective is the mass-weighted geometric mean of utilities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8’s hardness result for Nash-welfare maximization with fixed coalition size at least \(3\). It leaves the paper’s unrestricted-coalition results, approximation results, and stability results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • When \(n\mu_a\) is integral for every type, how much welfare can be lost by rounding an optimal continuous assignment to integer team counts, especially when the optimum is near zero?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a bounded-team, high-multiplicity ASHG. Imagine a large organization forming project teams of at most \(s\) people, for a fixed \(s\ge 3\). People fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of role types; two people of the same type have identical valuations toward every type, and valuations are symmetric. The input is the rational mass \(\mu_a\) of each type and the type-pair valuation matrix \(v_{ab}\). This is a plausible regime when many people repeatedly fill the same roles and teams are small by design.

The anchor is Theorem 8, proved in the paper. It says: for any finite \(A\subseteq\mathbb Q\) with \(|A|\ge2\) and \(\max A>0\), maximizing Nash welfare is NP-hard in symmetric ASHGs whose pairwise valuations lie in \(A\), even when every value in \(A\) is allowed to occur arbitrarily often and every coalition is restricted to size at most any fixed \(s\ge3\). The paper proves this result by reducing from PartitionIntoTriangles for \(s=3\), and from PartitionInto\(K_s\) for larger fixed \(s\). [Theorem 8 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19030) is my sole anchor and the lead. The mirror covers this bounded-coalition hardness result, not the paper’s unrestricted-coalition results.

Here is a continuous problem precise enough to hand to a prover. Call it HM-Bounded-Team Nash Welfare. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_a\ge0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric valuation matrix \(v_{ab}\in A\), and a fixed bound \(s\ge3\). A team configuration \(S\) is a multiset of between \(1\) and \(s\) types; write \(q_a(S)\) for the number of its members of type \(a\). A type-\(a\) member of \(S\) receives utility
\[ u_a(S)=\sum_{b\in T}\bigl(q_b(S)-\mathbf 1[a=b]\bigr)v_{ab}. \]
A solution assigns a nonnegative coalition density \(\lambda_S\) to each configuration, subject to
\[ \sum_S q_a(S)\lambda_S=\mu_a \qquad\text{for every }a\in T. \]
Only configurations giving every member nonnegative utility are allowed. Thus \(\lambda_S\) is the number of teams of configuration \(S\) per unit population; it need not be integral, but every member still receives an ordinary, integral team.

If all agents receive positive utility, the objective is to maximize
\[ \mathcal{NW}(\lambda) =\exp\!\left( \sum_S\lambda_S\sum_{a\in T}q_a(S)\log u_a(S) \right). \]
This is the population geometric mean of utilities. If positive utility cannot be given to everyone, the optimum is \(0\), as with the all-singleton partition in the finite model. For a discrete population with \(n_a\) people of type \(a\), any partition maps to this model by setting \(\mu_a=n_a/n\) and \(\lambda_S\) equal to the number of teams of configuration \(S\) divided by \(n\). The continuous problem relaxes those coalition densities to real values.

I expect this mirror to be tractable for fixed \(s\) and fixed finite \(A\): there are \(O(\tau^s)\) configurations, and maximizing log Nash welfare is a linear objective in the \(\lambda_S\). The theorem’s hardness uses arbitrary individual interaction graphs; with few complete types, the relevant interactions reduce to a type-pair matrix and the continuous model can split each type’s mass across team configurations. This is the kind of population aggregation the authors should recognize as their underlying optimization problem in a high-multiplicity regime. It does not make the outcomes fractional: teams remain bounded-size groups, while fractions describe how much of each type receives each team configuration.

The main follow-up is whether the continuous optimum approximates the finite optimum as the population grows: how well can an optimal \(\lambda\) be rounded to integer team counts while respecting the type totals, especially near zero welfare? Other natural questions are whether hardness returns when \(s\) grows, or when the number of types grows with the population.

My weakest point is the gap between the paper’s hard instances and this regime. Its reduction uses an arbitrary graph on named agents, which will generally require \(\tau\) close to \(n\); it does not establish hardness when many agents share a modest number of types. So the claim is deliberately limited: Theorem 8 has a compelling continuous-population analogue for repeated-role, bounded-team societies, and that mirror appears to turn its combinatorics into a tractable configuration optimization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The size-bounded hardness theorem is a real computational result, so the decisive objection that there is no result to mirror is unavailable. The strongest negative case is that its hardness comes from arbitrary interactions among named agents: the reduction encodes triangle packing in a graph. In a repeated-type society, those interactions must be summarized by a type-pair valuation matrix. With a modest number of types, that replaces the arbitrary graph by a much smaller description; with team size fixed at \(s\), there are only \(O(\tau^s)\) team configurations. The proposed continuous model then has a direct configuration optimization. It therefore studies a tractable regime that leaves the theorem’s hard instances behind.

Allowing \(\tau\) to grow does not fully close that gap. One can represent an arbitrary graph by giving each vertex its own type, but then the instance has no repeated population types. The continuous densities also permit fractional mixtures of teams, which can erase the exact-cover obstruction that drives the discrete reduction. So the theorem itself gives little evidence that its hardness persists in a genuine high-multiplicity regime.

That is the best objection I can make, and it does not defeat the mirror. Repeated roles in large organizations are a plausible high-multiplicity setting; the type model preserves each person’s utility calculation and the bounded-team constraint comes from the paper’s own result. The configuration optimization and the question of rounding it back to finite populations are computational questions the authors could recognize as theirs. Under the programme’s criteria, the anchor survives. The negative case is weak: it shows that the mirror shifts attention away from the paper’s hard instances, but it cannot establish that the resulting continuous question is not worth pursuing.
