What the mirror covers

The only proposed anchor is Theorem 2.2.1.3.1; the empirical regressions and the decision-market procedure do not provide a named computational result in this paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is Theorem 2.2.1.3.1, proved in this paper: under Arrow’s axioms plus the learning and equilibrium axioms, consensus across individuals is necessary and sufficient for logically consistent social preferences in the second round. It is a named structural theorem, but it does not establish a computational result such as tractability or hardness. The paper’s mentions of computational intractability and the decision-market algorithm either refer to other work or are not stated as numbered computational results. The empirical regressions are not complexity results. So this paper supplies no eligible computational anchor for the requested mirror.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large online consultation about environmental projects. A type could specify a participant’s complete ranking of the projects and how they update it after a social announcement; \(\mu_t\) would be the share of participants of each type. That makes the population regime plausible, though the paper’s eight-member board is not itself a high-multiplicity case.

One could extend Theorem 2.2.1.3.1 to such a society by replacing “all individuals” with “almost every type under \(\mu\).” But that would be an axiomatic restatement about consensus and consistency, not a computational problem the paper studies. Turning it into an optimization or complexity question would require adding a decision variable or objective absent from the paper. That is the weak point, and it defeats the positive case under the programme’s scope: I would not present a continuous computational problem as a mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

A large online consultation gives the strongest population story: many respondents could share a complete type consisting of their project preferences and how they respond to a social announcement. A distribution \(\mu\) over those types is sensible, even though the paper’s eight-person board is not a high-multiplicity population. But this does not make Theorem 2.2.1.3.1 a computational result.

In that theorem, the learning and equilibrium axioms already determine the key implication. For each pair of goods, a weak social preference in round two entails a strict social preference in round one, which entails that every individual weakly prefers it in round two. Replacing “every individual” with “every type in the support of \(\mu\)”—or with “\(\mu\)-almost every type”—leaves the proof as an axiomatic argument. The masses do no computational work. Checking whether the resulting preferences satisfy the conditions is possible, but it is a direct check of the axioms, not a complexity question about a social choice problem.

The best computational extension would ask for a round-two distribution of preferences, or for an announcement that maximizes consensus or project value. The paper does not specify a behavioral rule that computes updates from the announcement: AL and AE constrain which preferences are allowed. Choosing an objective and optimizing announcements would therefore add the central computational problem from outside the paper. Allowing richer strategic or probabilistic updates would require a new model as well.

The empirical findings do not supply a stronger anchor. A large consultation could estimate convergence or compare valuations of complex and simple goods, but that would turn the exercise into statistical inference over a larger sample. The paper’s score is described as a weighted average of guesses, and its decision-market algorithm is attributed to prior work; the paper gives no complexity theorem for computing it. Its remarks about computational intractability and tractability likewise state no formal problem, algorithm, or complexity bound.

So the population can be made continuous in a plausible setting, but the strongest mirror of the paper’s named theorem remains an axiomatic restatement. A computational mirror with a substantive objective would be a new research question attached to this paper’s subject, rather than a continuous version of a computational result it establishes.
