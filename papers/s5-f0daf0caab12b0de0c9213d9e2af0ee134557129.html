The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible computational anchors in this paper. Its named results are axiomatic characterizations: for example, Theorem 1, proved here, says the weighted mixed value is the unique mixed allocation rule satisfying mixed component efficiency and weighted mixed fairness. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 give two other uniqueness characterizations. None asserts a complexity classification or studies an algorithm for a computational problem.

Proposition 1, also proved here, expresses the pseudogame of a unanimity game using the minimal connection set-graphs of a coalition. It is a useful formula, but the paper does not turn it into a computational formulation or analyze its running time. So it does not provide an anchor under the stated criteria.

The model’s weights \(\lambda\) are positive weights on the finite players and links; they are not population masses. The player set \(N\), graph \(\gamma\), and coalition game over \(2^N\) remain finite. A continuous-population version would need a new model of mass distributed across network roles and a new allocation rule for that continuum. That could be a research direction, but it would change the paper’s axiomatic allocation question into a different problem rather than mirror a named computational result. I therefore have no defensible lead mirror or per-anchor continuous problem to propose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the strongest objection: this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorems 2–3 characterize the weighted mixed value by uniqueness; Proposition 1 gives a formula for the pseudogame. None studies an algorithm, a decision problem, or its complexity. The formula may suggest an interesting question about the cost of computing the value, but that would be a new computational project, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The best attempt at a mirror would model a large communication network as masses of player types and link types, perhaps using a graphon, then ask how to compute payoff densities for the weighted mixed value. But a player’s complete type here must capture not just their weight, but their network position and their role in the coalition game. For the paper’s arbitrary graph and arbitrary TU-game worth function, those details can distinguish almost every player. Grouping them by weight alone loses information about connectivity and coalition value; including all their relationships in the type can leave a support as large as the original network. A graphon and a measurable continuum game could preserve more structure, but they would require new definitions of connectivity, coalition worth, and the continuum allocation rule. That would be a substantial new model, with no computational theorem in this paper to carry across.

A simpler scenario—many copies of the same communication network—does permit repeated types. Yet if the copies are independent, the continuous allocation is just the finite allocation repeated at scale. Connecting the copies to create new network behavior requires the graphon-style redesign, and again produces a new question rather than a mirror of the paper’s characterizations.

So the paper offers no eligible anchor, and its most promising computational reinterpretation does not make the article a computational contribution. The proponent’s absolute claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile adjacent project is too strong: large social networks with repeated roles are plausible. But the honest negative case is strong for the narrower question ChoCo asks: this article supplies no named computational result whose continuous-population mirror would advance the programme.
