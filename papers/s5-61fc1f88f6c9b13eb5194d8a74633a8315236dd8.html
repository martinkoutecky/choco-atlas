What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; the axiomatic aggregation results and the discussion of probability aggregation remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its only numbered theorem, Theorem 1, says that when \(|V|\ge3\), no causal-judgement aggregation rule satisfies universal domain, acyclicity, unbiasedness, and non-dictatorship together. The authors derive it from Dietrich and List (2006). It is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a result about whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally difficult. The paper also discusses a reduction in the number of probability values to aggregate, but gives no named complexity result for that.

There is a possible population setting: many forecasters or analysts assessing the same finite set of variables, with types given by a shared causal DAG and conditional-probability table, and mass representing the fraction of the group with each complete view. But the paper’s famine example is a small expert panel, and turning its theorem into a continuous-population aggregation question would still be an axiomatic question. Replacing the paper’s aggregation task with an optimization problem over DAGs would introduce a new objective. So I can’t give a continuous computational problem per anchor without departing from the paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility theorem about satisfying four conditions on causal-judgement aggregation. The paper’s other results are also about aggregation properties, compatibility, and the number of probabilities experts must report—not complexity or algorithms. A continuous version of Theorem 1 would remain an axiomatic question, outside the programme’s scope.

A large crowd of forecasters with shared causal graphs and conditional-probability tables is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But replacing counts with masses would not create a computational target: threshold aggregation amounts to summing the mass supporting each causal edge, and probability pooling amounts to weighted averaging once a DAG is selected. The paper gives no complexity result for these operations. Its reduction in the number of probabilities to report is useful, but it is not a result about computational complexity.

The best attempt to create an algorithmic mirror would be to ask for an acyclic aggregate DAG that optimizes agreement with the population, perhaps while trading off sparsity and disagreement. That could lead to a substantive computational problem. But the paper specifies no such objective; its concluding challenge is to devise aggregation rules that balance competing goals, with the appropriate balance depending on the decision problem. Choosing an objective and solving it would add a new research question rather than continuize one of this paper’s results.

The proponent raised no computational-result anchors, and that is the decisive weakness for the mirror. The honest limit to this negative case is that no one can rule out worthwhile computational social choice research inspired by the paper. But under ChoCo’s stated remit, that would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
