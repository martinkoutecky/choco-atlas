The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is qualified: the paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying named computational anchor. The supplied text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, approximation, or a related computational classification. The only proposition mentioned is “Kurz et al. (2019, Prop. 3),” an external structural result about Copeland symmetry, not a result proved in this paper. Its statements that evaluating \(I(\cdot)\) is “not difficult” for up to five alternatives and that the computations use Monte Carlo are not named complexity results.

Subject to that formal defect, my lead mirror would be Continuous Weighted-Committee Bloc Influence.

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\) alternatives. A type is \(t=(\rho,\omega)\), where \(\rho\) is a strict ranking of \(C\) and \(\omega\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\) is the voting weight per member of that type. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite type set \(T\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of committee members of type \(t\). Thus the weighted mass of type \(t\) is \(\mu_t\omega_t\). The winner \(r_\infty(\mu)\) is computed exactly as in the paper: weighted Borda or plurality scores, weighted pairwise margins for Copeland or Schulze, and weighted first-round and runoff scores for plurality runoff, with the paper’s lexicographic tie-breaking.

Choose a focal type \(q=(\rho_q,\omega_q)\) and a perturbable bloc mass \(0<\lambda\leq\mu_q\). Changing that bloc’s preference to \(\rho'\) produces

\[ \mu^{q\to\rho'} = \mu-\lambda e_{(\rho_q,\omega_q)} +\lambda e_{(\rho',\omega_q)}. \]

The decision problem is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous Weighted-Committee Bloc Influence} \]

Given \((C,T,\mu,r,q,\lambda,\theta)\), determine whether

\[ J^r_q(\mu,\lambda) = \frac{1}{m!-1} \sum_{\rho'\neq\rho_q} \mathbf 1\!\left[ r_\infty(\mu)\neq r_\infty(\mu^{q\to\rho'}) \right] \geq \theta. \]

Equivalently, one may ask for the exact value of \(J^r_q\), or which rule \(r\) among Borda, plurality, plurality runoff, Copeland, and Schulze maximizes it. This directly continuizes the paper’s central object, equation (4): outcome sensitivity to preference changes, with an individual player replaced by a positive-mass voting bloc.

The natural regime is a large party congress, trade-union convention, shareholder assembly, or professional association with thousands of delegates but only a small number of recurring ranking-and-weight profiles. Delegates may belong to constitutional voting classes, regional constituencies, or institutional blocs. Then \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of distinct \((\rho,\omega)\) types. This is precisely the paper’s own group-and-voting-share interpretation, not an artificial reinterpretation of anonymous voters.

The high-multiplicity bridge is exact. A finite profile with \(n_t\) members of type \(t\) maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); conversely, rational \(\mu\) can be expanded by clearing denominators. All weighted scores and pairwise margins are merely rescaled, so the winner is unchanged. The continuous model therefore asks the same substantive question at mass resolution: how much of a recurring constituency must change its preference before the collective decision changes?

For explicitly listed types and fixed perturbation support, this problem is plausibly Class A: weighted winner determination is polynomial, and \(J^r_q\) can be evaluated by checking each listed destination type. If the destination rankings are represented implicitly as all \(m!\) permutations, exact influence becomes a counting-over-permutations problem; that is a worthwhile further boundary question, especially for Borda and Schulze, although this paper proves no hardness result for it.

The serious weakness is that the paper measures the influence of one named player, whereas an atomless population gives an individual zero mass and hence zero direct influence. The proposed bloc perturbation is therefore a genuine co-modification. It is defensible because the paper repeatedly treats players as groups, shares, and voting blocs, but a strict referee could say that bloc influence is a new problem rather than a literal continuum limit of \(I_i\). More fundamentally, because the paper has no qualifying named computational result, this is a substantively credible mirror but not a compliant anchor-based ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is formal: this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. It has no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition proving a complexity classification, algorithm, approximation result, or parameterized result. Equation (4) defines an influence index; equations (8), Tables 3–4, and the figures report enumerations and Monte Carlo comparisons. The cited “Prop. 3” belongs to an external paper. Thus there is no named computational result here for ChoCo to mirror.

Even if one treats the index itself as an anchor, the proposed bloc construction does not preserve it. The paper’s \(I_i\) measures the effect of changing exactly one named player’s preference while holding every other player fixed. Under a \(K\)-fold high-multiplicity expansion, that player’s weighted mass is \(O(1/K)\). For any generic profile with a positive winner margin, sufficiently large \(K\) makes the player unable to change the outcome, so the individual influence converges to zero. Exact ties are knife-edge exceptions. The continuous limit therefore destroys the paper’s central object: individual pivotality.

The proponent avoids this collapse by changing a positive mass \(\lambda\) of a type simultaneously. That is defensible as a new bloc- or coalition-influence measure, but it is not the high-multiplicity limit of \(I_i\). The “exact bridge” only shows that weighted winners are preserved when rational masses are expanded; it does not show that the paper’s expected single-player sensitivity is preserved. Choosing \(\lambda\) is an additional modelling decision: \(\lambda\to0\) gives the degenerate individual limit, while fixed \(\lambda\) studies a coalition. Asking for the smallest \(\lambda\) that changes the winner would be a new intervention or robustness problem, not a continuization of the paper’s index.

The paper’s impartial-culture expectation creates a second obstruction. Its index averages over all finite profiles \(P\in\mathcal P(A)^n\), and finite sampling fluctuations are precisely what create pivotal events. In a high-multiplicity population, those fluctuations disappear and the empirical profile converges to its deterministic distribution. To retain them one must reintroduce finite-\(N\) noise, a prior over societies, or a central-limit scaling. Those are stochastic or smoothed models, not a continuous population alone. Averaging only over destination rankings, as \(J_q^r\) does, does not reproduce the paper’s a-priori index.

There is also no genuinely new population object in the proposed winner calculation. For every rule studied—Borda, plurality, runoff, Copeland, and Schulze—the outcome depends only on aggregate weighted mass assigned to each ranking. If

\[ z_\rho=\sum_{t:\,\rho_t=\rho}\mu_t\omega_t, \]

then \(r_\infty(\mu)\) depends on \(z\), not on how that mass is partitioned among many agents. The population has therefore been quotiented away; what remains is the same continuous weighted-profile geometry already used in the paper’s simplex plots. The proposed \(J_q^r\) adds a bloc perturbation to that geometry, but does not expose a computational role for high multiplicity.

The honest weakness of this negative case is that a paper on continuous bloc sensitivity could be worthwhile in its own right. But that would be a new research programme, with a new object, new perturbation scale, and new probabilistic semantics. The faithful limit of this paper’s actual contribution is zero individual influence; the nondegenerate alternative is coalition power; and the aggregate winner model is already weighted-profile analysis. None is a worthwhile continuous-population mirror of the paper’s named results.
