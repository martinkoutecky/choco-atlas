The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow, and it misses your anchor rule: this paper has no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic bound. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are mathematical results about a solution concept; neither says that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorem 2 is the closest foothold, but calling it a computational anchor would overstate what the authors prove.

Theorem 1, proved here, says that \(MS(M)\) is nonempty and \(m\)-stable, and that if the strict core is nonempty, then \(MS(M)\) is contained in the strict core. Its proof uses the prior result that \(m\)-stable sets exist. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the min-max characterization: with \(s(x)=|\{y\in X:y\gg x\}|\) and \(g(x)=|\{y\in X:x\gg y\}|\), \(MS(X,P)=\arg\max\{g(y):y\in\arg\min_{x\in X}s(x)\}\). That is a characterization of what the finite selector chooses, not a complexity result about computing it.

The strongest plausible *follow-on question* is about the high-multiplicity limit of that selector. Take a fixed finite set of good classes \(G\). A type \(t\) consists of a weak order over \(G\) and an endowed class \(e(t)\); let \(\mu_t\) be its population share. Class \(g\) has supply \(\sigma_g=\sum_{t:e(t)=g}\mu_t\). At each scale \(n\) for which \(n\mu_t\) is integral, make a finite exchange market with \(n\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), each owning a distinct indivisible good in class \(e(t)\). Compute the paper’s finite \(MS\)-set, and record the aggregate assignment \(p^x_{t,g}\): the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) receiving a good in class \(g\). These aggregate assignments lie in the transportation polytope with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(\sigma_g\). Fractional entries describe type-level averages; each individual still receives one indivisible good.

Call \(L(\mu)\) the set of limits of those aggregate assignments from \(MS\)-allocations as \(n\) grows through admissible scales. A precise problem is: given rational \(\mu\) and a rational feasible assignment \(p\), decide whether \(p\in L(\mu)\)—that is, whether for every rational \(\varepsilon>0\) and every \(N\), some admissible \(n>N\) has an \(MS\)-allocation whose aggregate assignment is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(p\). This keeps the paper’s market, weak dominance, and \(MS\) selector intact while asking what its selected outcomes look like in a society of repeated types.

A plausible regime is a large housing exchange cooperative with many residents, a fixed set of unit classes, and recurring complete rankings and endowment classes. The number of agents grows while the supported types stay fixed or grow much more slowly. The authors could plausibly recognize the question as the large-market behavior of their solution concept. Theorem 1 at least implies that \(L(\mu)\) is nonempty: each finite market has an \(MS\)-allocation, and the aggregate assignment vectors lie in a compact polytope, so a sequence has a convergent subsequence.

The weakness is substantial: the paper’s selection compares cardinalities of sets of allocations. In a continuum, those cardinalities do not give a useful replacement for the finite counts. Taking limits of the finite selected sets avoids inventing a new selector, but it makes the question an asymptotic extension rather than a direct continuous version of a result the paper establishes. The limit-membership problem has no justified tractability or hardness prediction from these theorems; I would leave its classification open. So this is a credible research direction, but it is not an anchor-compliant positive case under the stated rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper establishes no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves existence and stability properties of \(MS(M)\); Theorem 2 characterizes the selector by minimizing and maximizing counts over allocations. Neither gives an algorithm, running time, or complexity classification. Asking how hard it is to compute \(MS\) would be a new computational problem built from the paper’s concept, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed large housing market is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many residents can share preference and endowment types, with repeated units in a fixed set of classes. That is not a reason to reject the model. The deeper difficulty is that the paper’s selector is defined by cardinalities of sets of allocations. Theorem 2’s \(s(x)\) and \(g(x)\) count allocations reachable through dominance. In a continuous allocation space, cardinality stops distinguishing outcomes. Replacing those counts with volumes would require choosing a measure on the allocation space; that choice is not supplied by the population distribution and would change the selector. It would make outcome-space measure part of the model.

The strongest proposed rescue is to retain the finite selector and take limits of its aggregate assignments, as in \(L(\mu)\). This is a coherent asymptotic question, and Theorem 1’s nonemptiness plus compactness makes nonemptiness of the limit set straightforward. But membership in \(L(\mu)\) asks about selected outcomes across arbitrarily large finite replications. It preserves the finite allocation-counting rule rather than producing a continuous \(MS\)-selector. Studying that asymptotic behavior could be worthwhile on its own; it is not a computational result established by this paper, nor does the paper provide a reason to expect a continuous optimization formulation.

So the negative case is strongest on the programme’s stated criterion: there is no named computational result here whose complexity landscape can be continuized. It is weaker as a claim that *no* useful follow-on research could come from the paper. The limit question is plausible, but it is a new asymptotic study of a finite selector, not a continuous computational mirror of the paper’s results.
