The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the paper asserting a computational result such as NP-hardness or polynomial solvability. So, strictly, it offers no anchor of the required kind, and it does not support a complexity classification of the discrete problem.

The closest computational anchor is MQ1, which the paper formulates and uses in its matching procedure and experiments. It maximizes \(\lambda_1\sum_{b,l}u_l(b)x_{bl}-\lambda_2\sum_{b,l}w_{bl}\), with binary assignment variables \(x_{bl}\) and blocking-pair variables \(w_{bl}\). Its constraints give each lender at most one borrower, require every borrower’s requested amount to be covered by assigned lender budgets, and use the paper’s C1 inequalities to penalize blocking pairs. The paper reports solving MQ1 with Gurobi, but does not prove a complexity result for it; the model has binary variables, despite the experiments section calling it a linear program.

My lead, conditional on accepting MQ1 as a computational anchor, is a mass version of this static matching problem. Consider a lending platform with many lenders funding a small batch of projects. A lender type records the lender’s budget, utility for each project, and ranking of projects. The platform has finitely many types \(T\), each with rational population mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Borrowers rank the lender types according to their profiles and terms. Let \(q_t\) be the per-lender budget of type \(t\), and let \(d_b\) be project \(b\)’s requested funding per lender in the population. The instance also gives the weights \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\).

The question is to find a maximum-utility mass assignment that funds every project while accounting for blocking pairs. Let \(y_{bt}\) be the fraction of the whole lender population of type \(t\) assigned to project \(b\), and \(z_{bt}\) the mass of type-\(t\) lenders counted as blocking with \(b\). An optimal solution maximizes

\[ \lambda_1\sum_{b,t}u_t(b)y_{bt}-\lambda_2\sum_{b,t}z_{bt} \]

subject to

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_b y_{bt}\le \mu_t &&\forall t,\\ &\sum_t q_t y_{bt}\ge d_b &&\forall b,\\ &0\le z_{bt}\le\mu_t,\quad y_{bt}\ge 0 &&\forall b,t,\\ &d_b y_{bt} +d_b\sum_{b':\,b'\succ_t b}y_{b't} +\mu_t\sum_{t':\,t'\succ_b t}q_{t'}y_{bt'} \ge d_b(\mu_t-z_{bt}) &&\forall b,t. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\succ_t\) is type \(t\)’s ranking of projects, and \(\succ_b\) is project \(b\)’s ranking of lender types. The last line is the type-aggregated, population-scaled version of C1. A solution is an optimal feasible assignment and corresponding blocking mass. With explicit type support, this is an LP of size polynomial in the number of projects, the number of lender types, and the input bit length. I would expect this stated mass problem to be tractable, in Class A.

The regime is a platform with a very large pool of retail lenders and a small set of simultaneous projects. Many lenders use the same investment template, budget tier, and project preferences, so the number of lenders \(N\) is much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). Project requests scale with the pool, making per-lender demand \(d_b\) the meaningful quantity. This preserves the paper’s central decision—assigning lenders to projects while weighing lender utility, funding requirements, and blocking pairs. The paper’s experiment with independently sampled utilities does not establish such a regime; the case relies on the plausible alternative of standardized lender profiles.

The mirror covers MQ1’s static assignment question, not the UCB rounds, uncertain rewards, or regret analysis. Those concern sequential learning and uncertainty, rather than a continuous lender population. The natural follow-up questions are whether the aggregate C1 constraint is the right continuum interpretation; whether solutions can be rounded to large finite lender pools with controlled funding and blocking error; and whether a less aggregated, pointwise stability version remains tractable.

My weakest point is that the LP’s tractability comes with a substantive change: it averages C1 over each lender type. It also treats lenders with identical profiles as interchangeable, allowing borrower indifference within a type, whereas the paper assumes strict individual preferences. The authors might accept both as natural consequences of a high-multiplicity market, but the paper does not establish that they would. So this is a concrete and plausible mirror of MQ1, not a result the paper itself anchors in the required theorem format.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no complexity theorem to mirror. Its experiments report solving MQ1 with Gurobi, but that is not a complexity result, and the paper itself leaves the justification of its stability constraints out. There is also no single settled objective: MQ1 optimizes lender utility, while the hindsight benchmark later optimizes combined borrower and lender utility. So the proposed mirror has no clearly established computational result as its anchor.

The stronger objection to that mirror is that its small type support does not follow from the paper’s preference model. Each borrower strictly ranks individual lenders. Those ranks affect C1, so they belong in a lender’s complete type; with even one borrower, the strict ranks distinguish every lender. Standardizing budgets and lender preferences would not make \(\tau\) small. The proposed LP instead has borrowers rank lender *types*, effectively making them indifferent among lenders within a type. Its aggregate blocking constraint then captures type level blocking, not the paper’s individual blocking relation. That is a substantive change to what stability means, not merely a fractional restatement of the stated model.

But this objection has a limit. A platform where borrowers rank lender profiles or categories, and many lenders share each profile, is plausible. In that regime, the mass assignment and blocking question may be a sensible new problem, and the paper’s matching objective gives it a recognizable connection. The paper does not establish that this is its own problem, and the proponent’s particular LP needs a clearer justification; still, I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. The negative case is strongest as a claim that this paper does not yet anchor the proposed complexity study, not as a case that no continuous mirror is worth pursuing.
