The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates and a support \(S\) of \(\tau\) complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_R\) summing to \(1\), compute the candidates that can rank first under some legal tie order in ranked pairs applied to \(w_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{R\in S}\mu_R(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_R b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_R a])\). The decision version asks whether a specified candidate belongs to that winner set.

The model it lives in

Use complete rankings as types and their rational masses as the population. Compute pairwise margins from those masses, then determine which candidates can win under at least one legal ordering of tied ranked-pairs edges; there is no optimization objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s PUT-RP problem: compute which alternatives can win under some legal resolution of ranked-pairs ties.

There is an anchor limitation. This text has no numbered theorem asserting NP-completeness or another complexity classification. It states that PUT-RP is NP-complete in the abstract and introduction, citing earlier work. The strongest named computational anchor is Theorem 2, which the paper cites from Zavist and Tideman and uses to build and run its ranked-pairs ILP. It is a characterization, not a new complexity result.

Theorem 2 says: for a profile \(P\) and strict ranking \(R\), let \(G_R\) contain exactly the nonnegative weighted-majority-graph edges consistent with \(R\). Define \(\mathrm{IW}(i,j)\) as the maximum, over paths from \(i\) to \(j\), of the minimum edge weight on each path. Then \(R\) is an outcome of ranked pairs if and only if, for every \(i \succ_R j\), \(\mathrm{IW}(i,j) \ge w_{(j,i)}\). The paper uses this cited characterization to formulate an ILP for checking whether a specified alternative can be a PUT-RP winner, and reports experiments with that formulation.

Here is the corresponding continuous problem, Continuous PUT-RP. An instance consists of candidates \(A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}\) and a support \(S\) of \(\tau\) complete rankings, each with a positive rational mass \(\mu_R\), where \(\sum_{R\in S}\mu_R=1\). For every pair \(a,b\), compute the population margin
\[ w_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{R\in S}\mu_R\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_R b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_R a]\bigr). \]
Run ranked pairs on these margins, grouping equal margins into tiers and allowing every legal order of edges within each tier. The task is to return
\[ W_\mu=\{a\in A:\text{$a$ is top under at least one legal tie resolution}\}. \]
The decision version asks whether a specified candidate belongs to \(W_\mu\). The decision variable is the tie resolution; there is no optimization objective. A solution to the search problem is the exact winner set.

The types are complete rankings because that is all this rule uses: people with the same ranking are indistinguishable to PUT-RP. A plausible regime is a large election with many repeated ballot types—say, hundreds of thousands or millions of voters but a much smaller number of distinct rankings. That scenario is credible for elections with a constrained ballot menu, though the paper’s small synthetic profiles and Preflib experiments do not establish that its tested instances have this shape.

The connection is exact for rational masses. Choose a common denominator \(q\); a discrete profile with \(q\mu_R\) voters of each ranking has margins \(q\,w_\mu(a,b)\). Positive scaling preserves margin ties, tier order, and the path inequalities in Theorem 2, so it preserves the PUT-RP winner set. Conversely, any finite profile maps to its ranking proportions. Thus this is a sensible high-multiplicity mirror, and Theorem 2 carries over by scaling its weights. The continuous question does not change what PUT-RP asks; it asks the same question on a population represented by type masses.

I expect hardness transfers (Class B). The paper reports PUT-RP as NP-complete, and every discrete profile maps to a rational-mass instance with the same winners. So that hardness does not disappear through normalization. The excerpt does not give the cited reduction, however, so it cannot establish whether the hardness is driven specifically by the number of candidates or say much about parameterized complexity. Natural follow-up questions are whether winner computation admits useful bounds in \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\), and whether an exact anytime or output-sensitive algorithm can exploit profiles with few winner candidates.

This mirror covers the paper’s ranked-pairs winner computation and its Theorem 2-based ILP; I would not stretch it to cover STV here. Its weakest point is that PUT-RP depends only on pairwise margins, and rational normalization gives an exact discrete-profile correspondence. An author may regard the mirror as a compact high-multiplicity representation rather than a substantively new model. The positive case is that this is precisely a population continuization with a clear regime and a well-defined complexity question, even if the answer may turn out to match the discrete one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor has a paper-attribution problem. Theorem 2 is a characterization cited from Zavist and Tideman, not a result this paper proves, and it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s own Theorem 1 concerns the graph structure used by its search algorithm; it is independent of how voters are represented. The NP-completeness claims appear in the introduction with citations, not as numbered results established here. So the proponent has not found a paper-owned complexity theorem to continuize.

That is the strongest objection, but it does not defeat the underlying PUT-RP mirror. A better formulation takes complete rankings as types, assigns each a rational mass, computes the pairwise margins, and asks which candidates can win under some legal order of tied edges. This is well-defined for a plausible high-multiplicity election regime. It does not lose relevant individual identity: PUT-RP uses rankings only through their aggregate margins. If the masses have common denominator \(q\), multiplying them by \(q\) gives a discrete profile with the same margin ordering, ties, and PUT-winner set. That exact correspondence is a thin source of novelty, but the programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity questions as worthwhile and hardness transfer as a meaningful outcome.

The strongest substantive case against is that this mirror offers little of the continuous-optimization opportunity that motivates ChoCo. PUT-RP depends only on the \(O(m^2)\) pairwise margins. The type masses can be aggregated into those margins and then discarded; the hard part is the same search over ranked-pairs tie resolutions. The model introduces no mass-transfer choice, fractional objective, or pricing problem, and its discrete complexity carries over directly. That makes it a poor candidate for discovering new continuous algorithms.

But this is a case for low expected payoff, not for there being no worthwhile mirror. The prompt rules out dismissing a well-posed question because its answer may be unchanged or its hardness may transfer. The continuous population is sensible here, and the problem is genuinely about computational winner determination. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor on the stated standard: the attribution to Theorem 2 is weak, but Continuous PUT-RP itself survives. The negative case is therefore weak, and a no-mirror verdict would require stretching the allowed objections.
