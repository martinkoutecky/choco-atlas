The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named \(P\), \(NP\)-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result. Its strongest named algorithmic anchors are approximation bounds. My lead is the social-cost lower bound, because its dependence on the number of agents creates a sharp high-multiplicity question.

A natural regime is a city choosing one public facility, with many residents but relatively few distinct residential locations. A type is a location \(x\in\mathbb R^d\); residents at the same location have identical distance costs under the paper’s fixed strictly convex norm. The society is a finitely supported distribution \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{x_j}\), with rational masses summing to one. Here \(\tau\) can stay small as the population grows.

The facility remains a point or a lottery over points. A mass mechanism \(F\) maps each such society to a lottery \(F(\mu)\) with finite expected distance. It is unanimous if \(F(\delta_x)=\delta_x\), and translation-invariant if shifting every location by \(a\) shifts the output lottery by \(a\). For group-strategyproofness, allow a coalition to move any amount of mass from each true location to reported locations. Writing \(q_{j,y}\geq0\) for the mass moved from \(x_j\) to \(y\), the resulting society is
\[ \mu'=\mu-\sum_{j,y}q_{j,y}\delta_{x_j}+\sum_{j,y}q_{j,y}\delta_y, \qquad \sum_y q_{j,y}\leq\mu_j. \]
No such deviation may make every participating unit of mass strictly better off, meaning that for each \(j\) with \(\sum_yq_{j,y}>0\),
\[ \mathbb E_{Z\sim F(\mu')}\|x_j-Z\| \;\geq\; \mathbb E_{Z\sim F(\mu)}\|x_j-Z\|. \]
This makes the mass model a direct high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s location game; fractional coalitions are permitted as part of that relaxation.

Lead: Corollary 4.9, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(n\geq3\), no translation-invariant group-strategyproof mechanism can achieve a social-cost approximation better than \(n/2-1\). The continuous question is: for fixed \(d\) and norm, what is the best worst-case per-capita social-cost ratio of a translation-invariant, group-strategyproof mass mechanism? Its objective is
\[ \mathbb E_{Z\sim F(\mu)} \left[\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\|x_j-Z\|\right], \]
compared with \(\inf_z\sum_j\mu_j\|x_j-z\|\). An instance of the existence question specifies the norm and a target ratio \(\alpha\); a solution is a mechanism defined over all rational-mass societies, with proofs of its properties and guarantee, or an impossibility proof. I expect no finite uniform ratio. Restricting \(F\) to empirical societies of \(n\) agents gives a finite mechanism whose ratio is the same after normalizing social cost by \(n\). Corollary 4.9 then forces its worst-case ratio to be at least \(n/2-1\) for every \(n\). A single mass mechanism must work for arbitrarily large \(n\), so no fixed \(\alpha\) can suffice. This is an approximation impossibility inherited from the discrete lower bound, rather than a running-time classification.

Second: Corollary 4.8, also proved in this paper. It states that for every \(n\geq3\), a translation-invariant group-strategyproof mechanism cannot achieve a maximum-cost approximation better than \(2\). The corresponding continuous question is to find the best worst-case ratio for
\[ \mathbb E_{Z\sim F(\mu)} \left[\max_{j\leq\tau}\|x_j-Z\|\right], \]
against \(\inf_z\max_j\|x_j-z\|\). The same instance and solution format apply. The discrete lower bound transfers, so a ratio below \(2\) is impossible. There is also a stronger obstacle for a mechanism based only on \(\mu\): it is anonymous by construction. The paper’s Corollary 4.7 rules out anonymous, unanimous, translation-invariant, group-strategyproof mechanisms for \(n\geq3\). Any finite approximation guarantee forces unanimity at a single-location society, since the optimum there is zero. Thus the natural distribution-only mirror admits no finite max-cost ratio either.

The authors should recognize the model’s facility, distance utilities, randomized outcomes, group-strategyproofness, translation invariance, and approximation objectives. The strongest objection is also clear: the original mechanisms can use agent identities, while a society represented only by \(\mu\) erases them and imposes anonymity. That is consequential in this paper, where anonymity is impossible under the other properties. I think the mirror remains worth asking precisely because a population distribution naturally suppresses identities; the resulting impossibility is informative about the boundary of continuization. A role-augmented model could preserve public priority classes, but it would need a convincing high-multiplicity account of those roles.

This case covers the paper’s approximation bounds for group-strategyproof facility location in strictly convex spaces. It does not mirror the full mechanism characterization, and it does not continuize the outcome space. The next useful questions are whether either objective has a finite approximation on restricted societies such as support size two, and whether a fixed number of public role types can retain meaningful priority without making the number of types grow with the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to continue. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize mechanism classes; Corollaries 4.8 and 4.9 give distortion bounds. None asks how hard it is to compute an outcome or solve an encoded intervention problem, or supplies a complexity parameter for such a question. A mass version of the two corollaries can be well-posed mechanism design, but it does not by itself create the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

Corollary 4.9 is the stronger proposed anchor. A single rule \(F(\mu)\) over rational-mass societies would impose consistency across electorate sizes: the same \(\mu\) must get the same output whether it represents \(n\) people or a multiple of \(n\). The paper’s mechanisms are defined on labelled, fixed-size electorates and can choose dictators by identity. Thus the proposed no-uniform-ratio result follows only after adding cross-size consistency and erasing those identities; it is not a direct high-multiplicity translation of the paper’s mechanism class. Its unbounded ratio is driven by a vanishingly small, labelled dictator cohort. That is a sharp singularity in the distortion objective, but it creates no algorithmic work over the support: the bad examples already use just two locations.

Corollary 4.8 has a further problem. Maximum cost gives a type at arbitrarily small positive mass the same influence as a large cohort. As that mass tends to zero, the support-based maximum can jump when the type disappears. One can define an essential supremum or impose a minimum mass, but those choices either preserve the rare-type discontinuity or change the objective. Meanwhile, a distribution-only rule is anonymous, and the paper’s Corollary 4.7 rules out anonymous, unanimous, translation-invariant, group-strategyproof mechanisms for \(n\ge3\). That result does not make a mass question unworthy simply because it settles it; it shows that this proposed mirror changes the admissible mechanism class in exactly the way the paper’s results make consequential.

The best rescue is to add finitely many public role types—perhaps facility users from a few constituency classes—so the rule can distinguish cohorts while compressing repeated agents within each class. That could be a sensible new model. But the paper has no such roles: its dictators are particular indexed agents. Keeping individual identities as types makes the support grow with the population; replacing individuals with privileged cohorts changes who can deviate and how mass represents strategic power. Either route asks a new mechanism-design question, without a computational anchor in this paper.

So I would reject both corollaries as ChoCo computational mirrors, while conceding that the blanket claim “no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario” is stronger than the evidence supports. A role-based population model might be worth studying on its own. The negative case is strongest when stated narrowly: it is not a faithful, computationally anchored continuization of this paper’s results.
