The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no named computational result of the kind the anchor rule asks for: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, and so on, and it does not compute with an LP or other formulation. The closest substantive anchor is Theorem 1 (proved here), but it is a structural theorem, not a computational one.

Theorem 1 says that in an initially \(\chi\)-stable, monotone, symmetric FS production game, a partition with one team \(G_1\) of size less than \(t\) and all other teams of size exactly \(t\) remains stable under the stated safe-training conditions: every employee in \(G_1\) receives no more training than every employee outside it; and the employees outside \(G_1\) can be ordered by nondecreasing training so that the order is consistent with the partition—teams are not split apart. That is a meaningful result about which training patterns preserve team stability, but it is not an algorithmic result.

The strongest positive case I can make is a computational follow-on to that theorem: Mass-\(\chi\) Stability-Preserving Training. Consider a large firm with workforce mass \(\mu\) over a small finite set of employee types. A type records baseline ability, training cost, and allowed training levels; many employees share each type. The firm has a specified team partition, represented by type-mass vectors \(g^j\), with one team smaller than the task size and the others task-sized. Training is a mass allocation \(x_{j,t,r}\): how much of type \(t\) in team \(j\) receives training level \(r\). Given a budget \(B\), choose that allocation to maximize post-training production while keeping the specified partition \(\chi\)-stable.

To make this a continuous model, let the task size be a fraction \(\theta\) of the workforce. For a coalition with ability-mass vector \(z\), define production as the total ability among its highest-ability mass \(\theta\lfloor |z|/\theta\rfloor\), where \(|z|\) is its total mass. This carries over the paper’s “best \(d\cdot t\) employees” production rule while allowing fractional mass of a type. Define the continuous Shapley wage density by the limit of the per-employee Shapley wages in finite replicas of the given type distribution, and use the paper’s \(\chi\) correction to compute wages within each team. Stability means that for every coalition mass vector \(z\leq\mu\), at least some positive mass in \(z\) does not gain by forming the two-team structure \(\{z,\mu-z\}\).

The motivating regime could be a large firm or consortium with many employees in a few repeated qualification and cost classes, organized into large divisions whose staffing requirements scale with the workforce. The number of employees is large compared with the number of types; team assignment and training are choices over masses. The authors would likely recognize the training-and-stability question. The link to Theorem 1 is especially direct for a uniform baseline-ability population and the theorem’s one-small-team structure: its ordered training condition supplies a concrete family of stability-preserving continuous policies.

I would treat the full optimization problem as unresolved, tentatively leaning Class C: stability imposes conditions across all coalition mass vectors, and the paper gives no separation method for checking them. Further questions are whether the finite-replica \(\chi\) wages have a well-defined, path-independent limit; whether their stability constraints admit efficient separation; and how the continuous optimum relates to discrete high-multiplicity instances. This proposal covers Theorem 1’s training/stability question only; it does not mirror the paper’s other claims.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s Shapley value is defined through permutations of a finite workforce, and neither its continuous extension nor the proportional scaling of task size is established by the authors. If that extension is not accepted as a natural version of their model, the proposal becomes a new cooperative-game problem rather than their continuous mirror. That caveat is why I would present this as the best research direction suggested by the paper—not as a qualifying mirror anchored in one of its computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes training patterns that preserve \(\chi\)-stability under restrictive assumptions; Corollaries 1–3 give further structural claims about stability and wages. None poses an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity result. That is the strongest case against treating this paper as a ChoCo anchor.

The proposed *Mass-\(\chi\) Stability-Preserving Training* problem supplies the missing computational task by adding a training budget and an objective to maximize production. Those additions are plausible, but the paper gives no training costs, budget, or firm objective to ground them. Its training vector is an input, and it explicitly leaves the firm and its owners outside the model. The computational problem would therefore be a new optimization model motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

A more faithful mirror would ask which distributions of training preserve a given team structure in a large workforce. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many employees can share ability, training options, and team conditions. But Theorem 1’s stated conditions translate into restrictions on how training levels are ordered across teams; they do not supply a computational question. Adding costs or an objective could make one, but would again move beyond the result being mirrored.

I would not argue that a continuous workforce is implausible here. The firm scenario is credible, and a carefully designed computational follow-on might be worth studying. The negative case is weaker at that broader level: this paper alone does not establish that no worthwhile continuous research can grow from its model. It does establish that the proposed anchor is a structural theorem, and that the proponent’s optimization problem gets its computational content from assumptions the paper does not make.
