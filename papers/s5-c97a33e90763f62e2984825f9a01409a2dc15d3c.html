Why no mirror

The population model is plausible, and the paper itself motivates budget additivity through crowds of like-minded traders, so the proposed population question survives the recognition test. But the named anchors establish an aggregation property and its geometric condition; they do not assert that an object can be computed, give a computational formulation used to compute it, or prove hardness. Adding a terminal-price computation question cannot supply the missing computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: a continuum of like-minded traders whose budget-weighted influence is represented by mass. One qualification up front: this paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest anchors are named results about the operational effect of sequential trading. If the anchor requirement is read as requiring a complexity classification, the paper has no qualifying anchor.

The population regime is plausible when many participants share the same forecast and fall into a small number of budget tiers—for example, employees trading on one company forecast using a common model-based estimate. A type is the complete pair \((\mu,\beta)\): the trader’s belief \(\mu\) about payoff vectors and their budget density \(\beta\). Let \(\rho_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\rho_t=1\). Mass \(\rho_t\) carries aggregate budget \(\rho_t\beta_t\). This is a fixed-total-budget high-multiplicity scaling: in a population of \(N\) people, a person with budget density \(\beta_t\) has budget \(\beta_t/N\). The intended regime has \(N\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct belief-and-budget types. If budgets are individually unique, that compression disappears.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. In full: if the acute angles condition holds for every face \(X\subseteq\Omega\), then the prediction market is budget additive on \(\widetilde M\). That means that for a belief \(\mu\in\widetilde M\), an arbitrage-free initial state \(q_0\) with price in \(\widetilde M\), and any two successive optimal actions by traders with that same belief and budgets \(B,B'\), the second state is also an optimal action from \(q_0\) with budget \(B+B'\). The price after the sequence equals the price after the combined-budget action. The theorem’s cost-function assumptions and the definition of \(\widetilde M\) are those in Section 4.

The resulting continuous problem is Continuous Budget Aggregation under Acute Angles. An instance consists of a finite payoff set \(\Omega\), a cost function \(C\) satisfying the paper’s assumptions, an initial state \(q_0\), a common belief \(\mu\), and a finite-support distribution of budget densities \((\rho_t,\beta_t)\). The acute angles condition is promised. A population slice of mass \(ds\) and type \(t\) makes a myopic optimal trade under belief \(\mu\) and budget \(\beta_t ds\). The question is to compute the market’s terminal price after the population trades, for any order of the slices; a solution is that price vector and a state \(q^*\) attaining it.

The predicted answer is characterized by one optimization:

\[ \bar B=\sum_t\rho_t\beta_t,\qquad q^*\in\arg\max_q U(q,\mu;q_0) \quad\text{subject to}\quad U(q,\omega;q_0)\ge-\bar B\ \ \forall\omega\in\Omega. \]

The output price is \(\nabla C(q^*)\). Repeated application of Theorem 5.2 gives the same terminal price for every finite partition and order of the population, so it also defines the continuum endpoint. This is a Class A candidate: once the population has been aggregated, the remaining task is the paper’s finite-dimensional convex optimization problem. That is a prediction about the mirror, not a complexity bound proved by Theorem 5.2. A polynomial-time claim would need a specified encoding of \(C\), \(\Omega\), and the requested precision.

A concrete second problem is Continuous Budget Aggregation for Affinely Independent Log-Partition Markets, anchored by Theorem 5.6, also proved here. The theorem states that if \(\Omega\) is affinely independent, then the acute angles assumption holds for the log-partition cost. Given such an \(\Omega\), \(C(q)=\log\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}e^{q\cdot\omega}\), a common belief, initial state, and budget-density distribution as above, compute the terminal crowd price. Theorem 5.6 supplies the condition needed to apply Theorem 5.2, so the solution is again the price from the single optimization with budget \(\bar B\). This is a restricted version of the lead problem, but it connects the mirror to the paper’s concrete prediction-market setting and its discussion of log-partition markets.

The fit is unusually recognizable to the authors: the paper itself motivates budget additivity by saying that a crowd of like-minded traders can have the same effect as one trader with the combined budget. The mirror makes that crowd a population measure with budget density. It keeps the paper’s market state, trader objective, outcome-wise loss constraint, and sequential decisions. Only the population becomes continuous; the outcome space remains finite.

The weakest point is also clear. The paper already proves the finite sequential aggregation fact, so the continuum endpoint follows with little additional work. The mirror therefore poses a sensible population-limit question, but it is unlikely to uncover new population-driven hardness. And the common-belief condition is restrictive: this case says nothing about crowds with heterogeneous beliefs, where trading order may matter.

The scope here is only that same-belief crowd effect, especially under the acute-angles condition and its log-partition case. It does not mirror the paper’s entire geometric analysis. Further questions include whether aggregation is stable under small differences in belief, what happens when a population contains several belief types, and what the exact or approximation complexity is for explicitly represented market makers and outcome sets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 5.2 characterizes when sequential optimal trades by traders with the same belief compose into an optimal trade with the combined budget. Theorem 5.6 supplies a geometric condition for that property in log-partition markets. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity bound. The proponent’s “compute the terminal price” question adds a computational task that the paper does not analyze.

The proposed population is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: traders share a belief, and types record budget density. But its distribution enters the proposed optimization only through \(\bar B=\sum_t \rho_t\beta_t\). The remaining task is to solve the paper’s finite-dimensional trader program. For an arbitrary differentiable convex cost function \(C\), the paper gives no computational representation of \(C\), so exact or approximate running time is not even defined. Choosing an explicit encoding for \(C\) and the payoffs could yield a worthwhile optimization problem, but that would be a new computational study of the trader program, not a complexity mirror of either theorem.

Theorem 5.6 does not supply a separate opening: it establishes a sufficient geometric condition that lets Theorem 5.2 apply. Its continuous-population version therefore inherits the same gap between a structural aggregation statement and an algorithmic result.

A stronger alternative would let the population contain traders with different beliefs. That would make the distribution over types matter beyond a single budget total. But then a continuous model needs a rule for how those traders act over time or in what order they trade. Different choices define different market dynamics, and the paper’s same-belief additivity results do not select among them. Such a study might be valuable, but it would introduce a new model and lack a computational result here to mirror.

This negative case has a substantial weakness: the paper itself motivates budget additivity with the effect of a crowd of like-minded traders. The proposed fixed-total-budget scaling is coherent, and the authors would likely recognize the resulting population question. I can make a strong case that the two anchors do not support a *computational* mirror; I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
