What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror addresses only the private-signal optimum subject to sincere voting being a BNE; it leaves the behavioral-voter and public-signal results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time classification, or parameterized-complexity result. Its best computational anchor is Proposition 6.4, which gives a linear program for the sender’s optimal private signal subject to sincere voting being a Bayes–Nash equilibrium. The proposition is proved in the paper; Theorem 6.5, also proved there, solves that LP. This is an optimization anchor, not a complexity theorem.

Here is a continuous mirror of that problem. Consider a referendum with a nonatomic electorate \([0,1]\), a quota \(\rho\in(0,1)\), and two states \(X,Y\). Every receiver has the paper’s same preferences: the outcome should match the true state. The common prior is \(p=\Pr(X)\in(0,\tfrac12)\), and the sender wants outcome \(x\). Thus the electorate has one complete receiver type, with mass \(1\).

Call the problem Mass-Quota Equilibrium Persuasion. An instance gives rational \(p\) and \(\rho\). A signal specifies, for each state \(\omega\), a probability measure \(\nu_\omega\) over \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), the fraction of the electorate sent message \(x\). After drawing \(\alpha\), the sender assigns \(x\) to a measurable subset of mass \(\alpha\) and \(y\) to everyone else; a uniformly random rotation of an interval of length \(\alpha\) is one way to make each receiver’s marginal message probabilities identical. Each receiver sees only their own message and votes sincerely, with the paper’s tie rule. The question is to choose \(\nu_X,\nu_Y\) and the associated allocation rule to maximize the probability that at least mass \(\rho\) votes \(x\), subject to sincere voting being a BNE. An answer is an optimal signal and its expected sender payoff.

For straightforward signals, the relevant persuasion constraint is
\[ p\int\alpha\,d\nu_X(\alpha)\ \ge\ (1-p)\int\alpha\,d\nu_Y(\alpha), \]
so that an \(x\)-message leads to an \(x\)-vote. The corresponding condition for \(y\)-messages is automatic when such messages occur and \(p<\tfrac12\). In this atomless model a single receiver has zero mass and cannot change the outcome, so sincere voting is a BNE. The equilibrium requirement is therefore present, as in the paper’s problem, but its finite-electorate pivotality constraint has no bite in the limit.

Proposition 6.4 states that for finite \(n\) and \(k\le n-1\), the BNE-constrained optimum is the LP
\[ \max\ p+(1-p)r_{k+1} \]
subject to
\[ r_0,r_{k+1}\ge 0,\qquad r_0+r_{k+1}=1,\qquad p-(1-p)\frac{k+1}{n}r_{k+1}\ge 0. \]
Theorem 6.5 gives its optimal signal: in state \(X\), send \(x\) to all \(n\) receivers; in state \(Y\), send \(x\) to exactly \(k+1\) receivers with probability
\[ r^*_{k+1}=\min\!\left\{1,\frac{np}{(k+1)(1-p)}\right\}, \]
and send \(x\) to none otherwise. Its value is
\[ \min\!\left\{1,\frac{n+k+1}{k+1}p\right\}. \]
With \(k/n\to\rho\), the LP’s constraint becomes \(p\ge(1-p)\rho r\). The continuous optimum is attained by \(\nu_X=\delta_1\) and
\[ \nu_Y=r\delta_\rho+(1-r)\delta_0,\qquad r=\min\!\left\{1,\frac{p}{(1-p)\rho}\right\}. \]
Its value is
\[ \min\!\left\{1,p\left(1+\frac1\rho\right)\right\}. \]
This is Class A: the problem reduces to a small LP, and an optimal signal has finite support and rational data for rational \(p,\rho\). The mirror keeps the paper’s correlated private recommendations, state-matching receiver payoffs, sender objective, and quota rule. It changes the integer electorate and quota into a mass distribution and quota fraction, as the continuous-population question requires.

The regime is a large referendum electorate whose members face the same policy exposure and share the same state-matching payoff and prior. That gives \(n\) large and type support \(\tau=1\), with heterogeneous messages arising from the sender’s intervention rather than from idiosyncratic receiver types. The paper itself studies homogeneous finite electorates, so this is a particularly credible high-multiplicity setting. The result also matches the paper’s finite formula: the extra \(1/n\) of receivers needed to avoid pivotality disappears as the population grows.

The strongest objection is that the paper already computes this limit from its finite-\(n\) formula, and its own discussion says the cost of the BNE constraint vanishes when the quota is a fixed population fraction. So the continuous optimum is recognizable but unlikely to open a new complexity landscape on its own. The BNE condition also loses its strategic force in an atomless electorate. The useful follow-on questions are whether this mass formulation remains tractable with several receiver types, type-dependent priors or payoffs, and multiple proposals—and how its solutions relate quantitatively to finite electorates.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The large-referendum scenario is credible: this paper really does study homogeneous voters, so high multiplicity is not the problem. The sharper objection is that Proposition 6.4’s equilibrium constraint is built around an individual voter being pivotal. In a nonatomic electorate, changing one voter’s action changes the vote share by zero—even when the share is exactly at the quota. Every action is therefore a best response, and sincere voting is automatically a BNE under every signal. The constraint that defines Section 6 has vanished, along with the swing voter’s curse that motivates it. Proposition 6.4 is a named optimization result, but it is not a complexity classification; its two-variable LP is solved explicitly by Theorem 6.5.

The strongest faithful rescue is to mirror the paper’s behavioral-voter problem instead. With homogeneous voters and anonymous signals, summarize a signal by the random fraction \(\alpha\) receiving recommendation \(x\). Sincere voting imposes a condition on the conditional means of \(\alpha\); the sender’s success depends on whether \(\alpha\) reaches the quota. This is a sound continuous-population question, but in this model it reduces to a one-dimensional moment problem with an optimal signal supported on a few points. Its natural parameters are just the prior and quota fraction. There is no growing type space or pricing problem from which to chart a computational landscape. The paper’s exact finite-\(n\) formulas already give the corresponding large-population regime; that fact alone does not invalidate the mirror, but it leaves little algorithmic work for the programme to do.

A version that preserves meaningful strategic pivotality would need to retain finite voter granularity, or specify a finite-size model of margins near the quota. Either way, pivotality then depends on microscopic fluctuations that an ordinary population-mass limit discards. Treating positive-mass blocs as strategic players instead leaves a finite collection of voters, not a high-multiplicity electorate. Adding heterogeneous voter types could make a richer information-design problem, but this paper assumes homogeneous preferences and a common prior; that extension changes its primitives rather than selecting another regime of the same problem.

So the negative case is strongest against the proposed equilibrium-constrained anchor: its defining constraint degenerates in the continuum. It is weaker against a mirror of the behavioral persuasion result. That mirror is well-posed and faithful, and its simple answer is not by itself a reason to reject it; the case against is that, for this paper’s homogeneous model, it appears to offer a solved scalar optimization rather than a worthwhile complexity programme.
