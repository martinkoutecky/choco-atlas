The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying case for a continuous mirror from this paper: it contains no named computational result about complexity or algorithms to anchor one.

The closest formal result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. In its four-agent experiment, the core allocation under SP-RSD, OSP-RSD, and TTC gives one agent her first choice, two agents their second choices, and one agent her third choice. That is a finite outcome characterization, not a result about solving a computational problem. The paper’s Results 1–5 concern experimental behavior and outcomes. Its “Theorem AS” and “Proposition Li” are cited theoretical claims about mechanism equivalence and obvious strategy-proofness, not computational results.

A possible extension would group applicants by complete preference type, give each type mass \(\mu_t\), and scale object supplies with population size. But RSD and TTC depend on finite random queues, and an individual’s report becomes nonpivotal in an atomless population. Letting a positive mass manipulate reports would instead pose a coalition-manipulation problem. I don’t think the authors would recognize that as a continuous version of the individual behavior their experiment studies.

So there is no honest prover-ready continuous problem to state per qualifying anchor, nor a tractability or hardness prediction to attach to one. Aggregating many repetitions of the paper’s four-person experiment could yield continuous population frequencies, but that would aggregate experimental observations, not continuize the computational social-choice problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “CASE FOR” appears to argue against a mirror: it raises no continuous mirror anchors for me to defeat. On the requested negative case, its strongest point is also the one that survives scrutiny: this paper does not establish a computational result for ChoCo to continuize.

The paper’s own Proposition 1 describes the efficient allocation in one four-agent preference profile. Results 1–5 report experimental behavior, welfare, and regression findings. The cited “Theorem AS” and “Proposition Li” concern mechanism equivalence and obvious strategy-proofness, not complexity or algorithms. So there is no named computational problem here whose continuous version could reveal a tractability or hardness boundary. One could invent a new computational question about assignment under a distribution \(\mu\), but that would be a research question prompted by the paper, not a mirror of one of its results.

The strongest candidate is to scale preference-type masses and object supplies together, then compute welfare under RSD or TTC. That is a plausible high-multiplicity assignment model; multiplicity itself is no objection. But it does not continue the paper’s central evidence about whether boundedly rational individuals recognize dominant strategies. At large scale, the allocation process is governed by aggregate type masses and priority positions. To predict reporting behavior, the model would still need a behavioral rule linking cognitive types and the mechanism to reports. The experiment does not supply such a rule for arbitrary \(\mu\); it measures behavior in one small, highly structured market. Adding one would make the computation an analysis of that new behavioral model.

The other apparent route—letting a continuum of copies of the four-person experiment generate population frequencies—preserves the experiment by repeating finite markets. It makes the sample larger, not the society continuous. A continuous-population version of Proposition 1 is possible, but it would extend an allocation characterization, not create the computational question ChoCo is meant to chart.

This is a scope argument, not a proof that no useful continuous assignment model can be built. A high-multiplicity study of RSD or TTC may be worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror, and the most plausible alternatives either add a new behavioral model or aggregate repeated experiments.
