The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror, but this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor the positive case. Theorem 2 proves an existence bound; Theorem 3 proves a worst-case lower bound on committee size. Theorem 1 and Corollary 3.1 are also existence results. Theorem 4, attributed to Nguyen and Song (2024), and Theorem 5 establish equilibrium existence. None asserts that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. Algorithm 1 uses an SLEO, but the paper does not give a complexity analysis for computing one.

The closest honest mirror is Minimum \((t,\alpha)\)-Undominated Committee. An instance is a candidate set \(A\), an integer \(t\), a rational \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), and a finite support \(T\subseteq S_A\) of complete rankings, with rational masses \(\mu_\pi\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Find a minimum-cardinality set \(C\subseteq A\), \(|C|\geq t\), such that for every \(a\notin C\),
\[ \sum_{\pi\in T:\,|\{c\in C:c\succ_\pi a\}|<t}\mu_\pi\leq\alpha. \]
Here a type is a complete ranking, and its mass is its fraction of the population. A plausible regime is a large paper-review or proposal-selection platform with many reviewers but relatively few distinct rankings. The paper’s Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are useful context for this problem: they give, respectively, an upper bound on the committee size sufficient for every election and a lower bound showing some elections require large committees. They are not computational anchors, and neither classifies the search problem’s complexity.

The mirror is close to the paper’s subject: it keeps the same candidates, rankings, comparison against the \(t\)-th best selected member, and mass threshold. The weakness is that the paper asks for a universal existence bound, not the complexity of finding a minimum committee for a given society. Its finite-election guarantees also already cover every rational distribution after scaling to a finite electorate, so the continuous formulation may be natural without yielding a new computational question. Under the requested anchor rule, I can’t make a stronger positive case from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed Minimum \((t,\alpha)\)-Undominated Committee problem is well formed: a large review platform with many reviewers sharing a relatively small set of rankings gives a plausible population regime, and its objective depends on type masses rather than reviewer identities. That makes it a sensible high-multiplicity problem. It does not, however, mirror a computational result in this paper.

Theorems 1–3 establish existence and worst-case committee-size bounds. Theorems 4–5 establish equilibrium existence. None classifies the complexity of finding a committee, and Algorithm 1 gives no running-time analysis for computing its SLEO. Theorem 3’s lower bound concerns how large a committee some election requires, not the complexity of finding one. So the strongest version of the proposed mirror—minimize \(|C|\) for a support-compressed society with binary rational masses—would introduce a new algorithmic problem. It may be worth studying on its own, but the paper supplies no computational result to anchor it.

The closest population restatement of the paper’s actual question, the smallest committee size guaranteed for every society, adds little: rational mass vectors correspond to finite elections by scaling voter counts to a common denominator. That translation does not create the missing complexity result. The paper’s use of random incomes, prices, fractional allocations, and dependent rounding does not change this point: its voters remain individually indexed.

This is a narrow negative case, not an airtight claim that no worthwhile continuization exists. The high-multiplicity regime is plausible, and the instance-specific committee problem could be a useful ChoCo project. But it survives as a new computational question, not as a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
