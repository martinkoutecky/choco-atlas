The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 4

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4 — survived: Fresh respondents sampled from a RUM-generated ranking distribution reproduce the winner-feedback law, so the PAC interview question remains the paper’s problem.
  • Theorem 6 — survived: Its Plackett–Luce lower-bound instances give a respondent-population sampling lower bound, though not a computation lower bound for known masses.
  • Theorem 7 — survived: A fixed-ranking respondent population matches the top-(\(m\)\u0029 feedback law in the Plackett–Luce subfamily; the opponent shows the broader general-RUM translation can fail.

The continuous mirror question

Given sampling access to a population law \(\mu\) over complete rankings \(\pi\in S_n\), with rational type masses and generated by an iid RUM with latent utilities \(\theta\) and minimum-advantage sensitivity \(c\), each round choose \(S\subseteq[n]\), \(|S|=k\), and observe a fresh respondent’s top-ranked item in \(S\). What is the minimax expected number of interviews needed to return an item \(i\) with \(\theta_i\ge\max_j\theta_j-\epsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and does Seq-PB attain the rate \(O\!\left(\frac{n}{c^2\epsilon^2}\log\frac{k}{\delta}\right)\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(\pi\) of the \(n\) items, with population masses \(\mu_\pi\). The learner has sampling access to \(\mu\), adaptively chooses a size-\(k\) subset, observes the sampled respondent’s favorite item in it, and minimizes expected interviews while identifying an item within \(\epsilon\) of the best latent utility with confidence \(1-\delta\).

The objection that survived

The results bound interview rounds with sampling access to an unknown population law; they give no \((\tau,L)\)-time result for a learner handed the explicit type masses.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s winner-feedback upper bound and Theorem 6’s sample lower bound; Theorem 7 transfers for fixed-ranking Plackett–Luce populations, but its general-RUM top-\(m\) feedback need not match one respondent’s ranking. It does not cover computation from an explicitly supplied mass vector.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the PAC guarantee extend to arbitrary rational ranking populations with a suitable stated relation between pairwise margins and latent item values?
  • Can the general-RUM top-\(m\) feedback process be represented by stable respondent types without restricting to Plackett–Luce?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is best-item learning from a high-multiplicity respondent population. Take a market research panel with \(M\) people and \(\tau\) distinct preference types, where \(M\gg\tau\). A type is a complete ranking \(\pi\) of the \(n\) products; two respondents share a type when they make the same choice from every subset. Its mass \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of the panel with that ranking. For ChoCo’s finite representation, take the masses to be rational. The learner has sampling access to this society: each round it chooses \(k\) products, then a respondent drawn from the population with replacement returns their favourite among them. In the top-\(m\) version, the respondent returns their top \(m\) in order.

For the paper’s RUM instances, the respondent rankings are generated by ordering utilities \(X_i=\theta_i+\zeta_i\). The ranking law \(\mu\) therefore reproduces the paper’s feedback probabilities exactly. The learner does not know \(\theta\), and seeks an item \(i\) with \(\theta_i\ge\max_j\theta_j-\epsilon\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\), while minimizing expected interview rounds. This preserves the paper’s active subset testing and its latent best-item objective. The population regime is plausible for large panels or customer cohorts with concentrated preference clusters: millions of respondents, but many fewer distinct rankings.

My lead is Theorem 4, “Sequential-Pairwise-Battle: Correctness and Sample Complexity,” proved in this paper. It says that for an iid RUM subset-choice model whose minimum advantage satisfies \(\mathrm{Min\text{-}AR}(i,j)\ge 1+\frac{4c\Delta_{ij}}{1-2c}\), Seq-PB is an \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-PAC algorithm under winner feedback, with sample complexity \(O\!\left(\frac{n}{c^2\epsilon^2}\log\frac{k}{\delta}\right)\). In the population mirror, the question is: given \(n,k,\epsilon,\delta\), a respondent population whose ranking law has this sensitivity to the latent utility gaps, and access to sampled respondents, can we identify an \(\epsilon\)-optimal product with that many interviews? The answer is tractable in the PAC sense: the paper supplies the adaptive elimination policy and bound. This is my strongest anchor. [Theorem 4 in the paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/aadirupa-saha20a/aadirupa-saha20a.pdf)

Theorem 6, “Sample Complexity Lower Bound for RUM\((k,\theta)\) model,” also proved here, gives the matching boundary for that same winner-feedback problem. For \(\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac14]\), \(\delta\in(0,1]\), and any \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-PAC algorithm, it says there exists a RUM instance with \(\mathrm{Min\text{-}AR}(i,j)\ge1+4c\Delta_{ij}\) for which the algorithm’s expected sample complexity is at least \(\Omega\!\left(\frac{n}{c^2\epsilon^2}\ln\frac{1}{2.4\delta}\right)\). Its proof is in the paper and uses a change-of-measure argument, including a Plackett–Luce subfamily. In the mirror, this asks for the worst-case interview cost of any policy over the stated respondent populations. It predicts an essentially matching minimax rate, up to the \(\log k\) factor in the upper bound. This is a sample-complexity lower bound, not a claim of NP-hardness. [Theorem 6 in the paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/aadirupa-saha20a/aadirupa-saha20a.pdf)

A separate, worthwhile mirror is Population-RUM-Top-\(m\)-PAC, anchored by Theorem 7, “mSeq-PB(Alg. 2): Correctness and Sample Complexity,” proved here. Its instance and objective are the same, except each sampled respondent returns the top \(m\) of the queried \(k\)-set. Under the same minimum-advantage sensitivity condition as Theorem 4, the theorem guarantees an \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-PAC policy with sample complexity \(O\!\left(\frac{n}{mc^2\epsilon^2}\log\frac{k}{\delta}\right)\). The population question is whether the extra ranking information reduces the required interviews by a factor of \(m\); the theorem says yes, with tractable PAC complexity. [Theorem 7 in the paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/aadirupa-saha20a/aadirupa-saha20a.pdf)

These anchors cover the paper’s winner-feedback result, its worst-case lower bound, and its top-\(m\) extension. They do not turn its experiments or its proposed future directions into claims about a continuous population. For a ChoCo version with rational masses, I would ask whether the proofs extend from RUM-generated ranking laws to finite rational ranking distributions satisfying the same minimum-advantage condition. The paper proves the RUM-generated subfamily; its elimination arguments appear to use that structure through the advantage condition.

The weak point is that this is a sampling-access mirror. The paper does not give the learner an explicit list of types and masses; if it did, direct computation from that list could bypass the interview problem. More fundamentally, a RUM’s random utility draws already induce a distribution over rankings, so interpreting each draw as a respondent is a close mathematical bridge and could be seen as a change in meaning more than a new model. The positive case holds when the respondents are a genuine high-multiplicity cohort and sampled interviews are part of the problem. It is much weaker as a mirror of ChoCo’s explicit-profile computational setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has a plausible high-multiplicity story: a large panel with repeated preference rankings. The difficulty is that it changes what the paper’s theorems are about. The paper gives the learner sampled feedback from an unknown RUM; it does not take a population’s support and masses as input. Its bounds count interview rounds, not the time to compute from a continuous society described by \((\tau,L)\). For winner feedback, a respondent drawn from a ranking distribution can reproduce the RUM’s observations exactly. But then the panel is an interpretation of the paper’s existing sampling law: the transcript distribution, algorithm, and bound are unchanged. That is not a new continuization result about a high-multiplicity input.

Theorem 4 is the strongest anchor, and the most convincing panel version is still a sampling-oracle problem. If the population is restricted to rankings generated by the paper’s RUM, Seq-PB already gives the stated PAC guarantee from sampled respondents. If instead the continuous society is explicitly supplied as masses over rankings, the theorem does not say how to compute from those masses or how to recover the paper’s latent \(\theta\). For an arbitrary ranking distribution, that latent objective may not even be defined. One could pose a different problem, such as finding the item with best expected rank, but that would be a new learning objective rather than this theorem’s mirror.

Theorem 6 has the same boundary. Its lower bound is on how many samples a policy needs to distinguish hidden RUM parameters. It is not a lower bound on computation from a society whose type masses are known. Making the panel large does not supply the learner with more information per interview in the theorem’s model; making the masses explicit removes the hidden-sampling premise on which the change-of-measure argument depends. So the bound remains useful for a panel-interview question, but it establishes no complexity boundary for ChoCo’s explicit-mass model.

Theorem 7 has an additional translation problem. A stable ranking respondent returns the top \(m\) items from one fixed ranking of the queried set. The paper’s general-RUM top-\(m\) feedback is defined as successive winner draws from the remaining set. Those are not automatically the same joint response law for a single persistent ranking; they coincide naturally in the Plackett–Luce case, but the paper’s claim covers more general RUMs. A mirror could change the feedback to one fixed ranking per respondent, or assign each respondent a complete stochastic response plan for every subset and history. The first narrows the theorem’s setting; the second makes the population a representation of the sampling oracle again, with no computation over population masses in the result.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here. A real panel can have many repeated rankings, and the winner-feedback experiment is observationally equivalent to sampling rankings from a distribution. That is the weak seam in the negative case: if ChoCo expands to include sampling-access PAC learning, Theorems 4 and 6 make a credible and useful panel story. For the programme as stated, though, these anchors concern statistical information from an unknown stochastic model, while the continuous mirror would need to make an explicitly represented society the computational object. The paper supplies no such result, and converting its sampling law into a respondent population does not supply one.
