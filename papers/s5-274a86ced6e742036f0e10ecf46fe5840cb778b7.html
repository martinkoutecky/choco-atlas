The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive mirror is Mass-\(\mathrm{FB}\)-GFT Posted-Price Approximation. The paper has no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity result; its relevant anchors are approximation theorems. Theorem 2 is the best fit here. It is proved in this paper: if the buyer’s downward-closed feasibility constraint is \((\delta,\eta)\)-selectable, then \(\mathrm{FB}\leq O(\log(1/r)/(\delta\eta))\cdot \mathrm{CFPP}\), where \(\mathrm{CFPP}\) is the best GFT achievable by a constrained fixed posted-price mechanism and \(r\) is the minimum, over items, probability that the buyer’s value is at least the seller’s cost. In other words, the paper proves a logarithmic approximation to first-best GFT using a truthful, ex-post weakly budget-balanced posted-price mechanism.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. There is a finite set of seller-item classes \(A\), with rational masses \(\rho_a\) summing to one. A seller’s complete type is \(t=(a,s)\): the item class \(a\), including its role in the buyer’s feasibility constraint, and the seller’s cost \(s\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of sellers of that exact type. Each unit remains an individual seller with an integral trade decision; a type-level allocation \(x_t\in[0,1]\) records the fraction of that mass that trades. The single buyer has a private value coordinate for each unit, drawn from the class’s public value law \(F_a\); sellers’ costs follow the class’s public cost law \(G_a\). Specify a downward-closed feasibility family over measurable bundles, with capacities scaled per capita—for example, a total capacity of \(\kappa\) units of mass. Assume it has a continuous analogue of \((\delta,\eta)\)-selectability.

The first-best benchmark is the expected maximum per-capita surplus, \(\mathrm{FB}_\infty=\mathbb{E}[\sup_{X\in\mathcal F_\mu}\int_X(b(u)-s(u))\,du]\). The question is to return buyer and seller prices by public class, plus any allowed randomized subconstraint, whose induced mechanism gives at least an \(O(\log(1/r)/(\delta\eta))\) approximation to \(\mathrm{FB}_\infty\). Here \(r\) is the smallest class-level probability of trade under the input value and cost laws. The output is the price rule and its induced allocation; each individual unit still trades or does not trade. Fractional mass represents the average across indistinguishable sellers, not a fractional item.

This is a plausible mirror of the paper’s problem: the objective remains gains from trade, the same buyer and seller incentives remain relevant, and the same feasibility constraint governs what can be traded. A natural regime is a large exchange or procurement platform with many sellers in a few repeated listing classes—say, many sellers with the same service category and cost type—so the number of sellers is much larger than the number of distinct types. The population is genuinely the seller side of the market; the public prior alone is not being relabeled as a continuum.

I expect the approximation question to be Class A for finite-support inputs and selectable mass constraints. The paper’s quantile-price construction and its optimization over a feasibility polytope give a concrete route to an integral version; the paper also discusses efficient computation of its approximate mechanism. That expectation is a research hypothesis, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 2. Natural follow-up questions are whether the factor can depend on the number of types \(\tau\) rather than the number of sellers, how rounding \(N\mu_t\) affects the guarantee, and which continuous feasibility constraints admit efficient selection schemes.

The mirror covers the paper’s first-best approximation result for selectable constraints, not its second-best theorem or its general downward-closed guarantee. Its weak point is that the buyer remains a single, multidimensional agent, so only the seller-item population is continuized. Also, the paper’s selectability argument is finite-ground-set machinery; extending it to a mass constraint needs an actual proof. Those are real limits, but they leave a recognizable question about the authors’ own GFT objective and mechanism class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but the proposed mirror does not yet preserve the paper’s central model. The paper has one strategic buyer with a private value coordinate for each individually identified item, and a separate strategic seller for each item. Item identity matters through both that buyer coordinate and the feasibility family. If the family is arbitrary, it can distinguish every item, leaving one seller-item type per seller. Then the population has no high-multiplicity compression.

The best repair is to restrict to repeated classes with symmetric feasibility and identical value and cost laws. That creates a plausible large-market model, but it forces a choice the mirror has not resolved. Keep the paper’s independent buyer values across copies, and the buyer’s private type becomes a growing, ultimately infinite-dimensional vector; finite class masses do not describe it, and incentive compatibility must be specified over that function space. Instead make copies share one class value, or replace their realized values by an aggregate distribution, and the model changes: it removes the paper’s independent item-level coordinates or turns the buyer’s private vector into a cross-sectional law. Either may be a worthwhile model in its own right, but neither is a direct continuization of the result as stated.

There is a separate gap between the anchor and ChoCo’s target. Theorem 2 proves an approximation ratio for a mechanism family; it does not classify the complexity of an optimization problem. The appendix’s computation discussion still uses a finite ground set and finite OCRS machinery. The proposed question assumes a continuous selectability analogue and a mass-feasibility family, but supplies neither a finite encoding nor a computational problem whose input length and parameters can be analyzed. The new work would first have to define those objects and show how the buyer’s functional type and the feasibility rule can be represented. That is substantial model-building, not yet a computational mirror of Theorem 2.

This does not make the mirror impossible. In particular, a large procurement market with repeated, genuinely interchangeable units could motivate a continuous model, and prior high-multiplicity work supports taking that regime seriously. My negative case is weaker there: once the authors accept the symmetric class restriction and specify the buyer’s information and the mass constraint, a recognizable GFT question may survive. The objection is that the proposed mirror has not yet shown that it can retain the paper’s distinctive single-buyer, multidimensional problem while producing a finite, computationally meaningful continuous instance.
