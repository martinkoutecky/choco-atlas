What the mirror covers

Proposition 1 computes equilibrium timing; Propositions 2–5 characterize welfare-optimal timing and comparisons; Proposition 6 establishes fee implementation. The proposed group aggregation does not turn these into a computational question over a continuous population sharing a network.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying computational anchor from this paper. It has numbered propositions, but Proposition 1 characterizes equilibrium adoption timing; Propositions 2–5 characterize efficient timing and compare it with equilibrium; and Proposition 6 shows that a fee can implement the efficient timing. These are game theoretic and welfare results. None asserts a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. Proposition 6 is the closest fit, but an implementation existence result for the paper’s two player game is not, by itself, a computational result.

The most charitable continuous analogue would be a large cohort of independent two person platform adoption groups. A group’s type would be its members’ value and preference types; mass would be the fraction of groups of each type pair. Each pair would play the paper’s two period adoption game. One could ask for a common timing fee and equilibrium strategies that maximize aggregate expected payoff. That is a concrete question, and the paper’s fee result motivates it. But it aggregates copies of the finite game; it does not make the agents within a network externality continuous, or produce a complexity question anchored by a result in this paper.

So the positive case is limited: the paper’s type heterogeneity and fee scheme suggest a natural aggregation exercise, but there is no named computational result to mirror. Claiming a stronger case would require inventing a new result or changing the paper’s strategic problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes equilibrium timing; Propositions 2–5 characterize efficient timing and its relation to equilibrium; Proposition 6 proves fee implementation. These are results about a fixed Bayesian game and its welfare properties, not complexity bounds or algorithms. The other analyst is right to treat Proposition 6 as the closest anchor, but it does not supply a computational problem by itself.

Replicating the two-player game across many adoption groups gives a plausible continuous distribution over *groups*, not a continuous population participating in one shared network. Each group’s strategic problem remains the original finite game. With group types given, one can apply the paper’s characterization group by group and aggregate payoffs. A single fee shared across groups would create a new fee-design problem; the paper’s result does not establish that problem, and with a scalar fee and finitely many group types it offers no evident source of the algorithmic challenge ChoCo is looking for.

The stronger candidate is a large society of consumers choosing a shared platform. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, but it changes the strategic mechanism the propositions analyze: an individual in a continuum has measure zero, so cannot sway the aggregate by adopting early. One could instead let whole type blocs act together, but then those blocs are the strategic players, and their size and coordination become new features of the model. Either route may motivate a worthwhile project, but its computational questions would come from the enlarged model, not from a continuous mirror of the paper’s two-player results.

This is a strong objection to the proposed anchors and to their direct extensions, not a proof that no useful scenario exists. The paper itself points toward many-player platform adoption, and that makes an absolute claim that no high-multiplicity regime is sensible hard to defend. The negative case is strongest on programme fit: the paper offers rich strategic and welfare results, but little computational substance for a continuous-population complexity study to inherit.
