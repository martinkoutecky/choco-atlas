The case FOR (proponent)

The closest result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: there is no anonymous or non-dictatorial, propositionwise aggregation function that, for every profile of complete, weakly consistent and deductively closed individual views, makes the priority-to-the-past process both decisive and invariant to the decision path. That is a strong result about collective path dependence, but it is not a computational complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. The paper contains no named result asserting that a problem is hard, tractable, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified. So I cannot honestly offer a compliant anchor.

The strongest candidate mirror would be Weighted Judgment Agenda Sensitivity. An instance gives the paper’s finite proposition set \(X\), a target proposition \(\phi\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) over a listed set of types. Each type \(t\) is a complete individual judgment function on \(X\) satisfying completeness, weak consistency and deductive closure. Define the collective attitude by weighted majority: \(\delta_\mu(\psi)=1\) exactly when the mass accepting \(\psi\) exceeds \(1/2\), with ties rejected, as in the paper’s majority rule. Apply the paper’s priority-to-the-past process to any ordering \(\Omega\) of \(X\). The question is whether there are two orderings \(\Omega_1,\Omega_2\) such that \(\phi\) is accepted under \(\Omega_1\) and \(\neg\phi\) under \(\Omega_2\). A solution is such a pair of orderings, or a determination that none exists. Its natural parameters are the number of propositions, support size \(\tau\), and the bit length of the masses.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization deciding on a modest set of interconnected policies, where many members share the same complete judgment package and only a relatively small number \(\tau\) of packages occur. Mass then means the fraction of members with each package. This is faithful to the paper’s collective model: people of the same type are indistinguishable for the decisions it represents. It is also a genuine population mirror, rather than continuity in the outcome or noise model.

The main positive evidence is that the paper explicitly studies aggregation over individual views and sequential decisions over propositions; the mirror preserves both. But Theorem 4 only establishes an impossibility about aggregation rules, not the computational complexity of testing agenda sensitivity. Moreover, for rational masses, clearing denominators reproduces a finite electorate’s weighted-majority outcomes exactly. That makes the mirror a sensible high-multiplicity formulation, but the paper provides no named computational result showing that continuization changes the tractability of this problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorems 1–8 characterize consistency, path dependence, aggregation impossibilities and escape routes; none classifies the complexity of an input/output problem or gives an algorithm. The proponent acknowledges this, then proposes *Weighted Judgment Agenda Sensitivity* as a new computational problem. That is a reasonable extension, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

It is also the best version of the proposed mirror, and it exposes the limit of the negative case. A large organization whose members share a small number of judgment packages is a credible high-multiplicity scenario. Given rational masses, weighted majority gives a well-defined collective attitude, and asking whether some two agendas yield opposite verdicts is coherent. Theorem 1 even supplies a characterization for the unweighted model. So I cannot honestly argue that no population-continuous question can be formulated here.

What weakens the case for spending ChoCo’s time on this particular mirror is that it adds a computational layer the paper does not study. The masses are used only to calculate each proposition’s majority tally. The question then concerns the resulting collective judgment set and the logical conditions for agenda sensitivity; it does not optimize or transfer mass, model a population intervention, or analyze a computational task posed by the authors. A proposed “minimum mass of members who must misreport” would be closer to a ChoCo optimization problem, but it requires adding a manipulation cost or distance between judgment types that the paper does not define.

So the negative case is strong under the requested anchor standard, but weak as a universal claim. The paper offers no named computational result for which a continuous mirror can be assessed. Its best candidate is a plausible new extension, and the material here does not establish that the extension is worthless—only that it should not be presented as mirroring a computational result of this paper.
