What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorem 2, also stated as Theorem 11 in the appendix; it leaves the Serial Dictatorship and Random Serial Dictatorship bounds, the TAP+ characterization, and the experiments unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is a nonatomic traffic-assignment problem, anchored on the paper’s deterministic lower bound. Theorem 2 states that no deterministic strategyproof TAP mechanism can have approximation ratio below \(3-\varepsilon\), for any \(\varepsilon>0\). The paper proves this result; its appendix repeats the proof under the number Theorem 11. This is an approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness result.

A plausible regime is a large commuting population with relatively few origin–destination pairs: many drivers share an origin, destination, route costs, and report options, so the support size \(\tau\) is far smaller than the number of drivers. Let each type \(t=(o,d)\) have rational mass \(\mu_t\), with total mass \(1\). Give each road segment a rational per-capita capacity \(q_e\) and travel cost \(w_e\); for a cohort of size \(N\), its physical capacity is \(Nq_e\). Scaling capacity with the cohort is a co-modification of the model, and is plausible for a growing city or a managed traffic cohort whose infrastructure scales with demand.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Nonatomic Strategyproof TAP. Given a directed network \(G=(V,E)\), rational capacities \(q_e\), rational costs \(w_e\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many reportable origin–destination types, determine the best deterministic strategyproof approximation ratio and compute a mechanism attaining it. A mechanism maps each reported population distribution to a feasible mass routing \(x_{t,P}\), where \(x_{t,P}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to simple path \(P\). Feasibility and social cost are

\[ \sum_P x_{t,P}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,P:e\in P}x_{t,P}\le q_e,\qquad \mathrm{SC}(x)=\sum_{t,P}x_{t,P}w(P). \]

Each driver still travels one integral path; \(x\) describes how a type’s mass is divided among paths. The mechanism can implement those shares by partitioning a cohort or by drawing a route for each driver. To retain the paper’s route deviations in the nonatomic model, a driver may switch to a true-destination route with spare capacity; the route allocated to that driver remains available when it is truthful.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, with optimal ratio \(1\). For every reported distribution, compute a minimum-cost splittable multicommodity flow. It is a linear program with polynomially many edge-flow variables in \(|V|, |E|,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\); its solution decomposes into integral paths for individual drivers. At an optimum, no positive mass can be using a more expensive path while a cheaper path has spare capacity: shifting a small amount of mass would lower the LP objective. Thus truthful assignments are already cost-minimizing among available routes. In the atomless model, one driver’s report changes zero mass, so it cannot change the aggregate routing or create a cheaper available true-destination route. The exact optimum is therefore strategyproof under this interpretation.

This covers Theorem 2’s question—whether truthfulness forces a loss in social-cost approximation—but not the paper’s finite-population bounds for Serial Dictatorship or Random Serial Dictatorship, its TAP+ characterization, or its experiments. The key follow-up is how the exact flow solution rounds to finite populations, and whether its guarantee persists when capacities do not scale with population.

My weakest point is also the main modeling risk: the atomless limit removes the one-driver effect on capacity that gives finite TAP much of its strategic difficulty. Route feasibility can be discontinuous at a saturated edge: one unused seat may matter to an individual even when its share of the population tends to zero. If the authors require the mirror to preserve that tagged-driver effect, the nonatomic LP question may be too weak, and the \(3-\varepsilon\) lower bound may not dissolve. I still think this is a worthwhile mirror because it isolates exactly the high-multiplicity question: whether the paper’s approximation loss comes from population granularity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the one anchor, repeated as Theorem 11 in the appendix. It proves a mechanism-design impossibility: even an unrestricted deterministic strategyproof mechanism cannot beat a certain social-cost approximation ratio. That is a result about the price of truthfulness, not a complexity result about computing a mechanism. The paper gives no running-time or complexity analysis for finding these allocations. A question about computing the best truthful mechanism could be added, but it would extend the paper’s agenda rather than continuize a computational result it establishes.

The commuter example makes repeated origin–destination types plausible, so I would not reject the mirror for lack of multiplicity. The deeper problem is what happens to individual incentives when the population becomes atomless. If capacities and demand both scale with population, one driver’s report changes the reported mass by zero. Any mechanism that depends only on the mass distribution therefore returns the same allocation after a unilateral misreport. Strategyproofness becomes automatic, regardless of whether the allocation is a minimum-cost flow. The \(3-\varepsilon\) bound then disappears because the capacity effects that make a single driver strategically consequential have disappeared too. That is a degeneration of the property being studied, not a substantive improvement in a computational trade-off.

The route-deviation model makes the limit still less direct. In the paper, an agent’s assigned route is feasible after that agent is removed from the flow; the residual capacity can make another route available as well. Removing an atomless individual changes no edge load. A path saturated by the aggregate flow remains saturated, including the path assigned to that individual. A splittable flow LP can describe total mass on paths, but it does not by itself say which routes an individual can take after allocation. The proposed model patches this by declaring the assigned route available when truthful. That convention may define a game, but together with zero influence from reports it yields the claimed truthfulness largely by construction. Keeping the driver as a tagged unit so that removing them frees capacity restores a meaningful deviation test, but also restores an atom and its denominator-dependent, discrete capacity effect.

The best nearby alternative is a high-multiplicity *finite* TAP: encode counts of identical origin–destination types compactly, retain integral edge capacities, and let one driver’s report change a type count by one. That is a sensible problem, and the high-multiplicity setting supports its plausibility. But it is not the proposed atomless mirror. A continuous flow relaxation might help bound its social cost; it does not automatically preserve the unilateral incentive constraints that Theorem 2 studies. Treating a whole type cohort as one strategic player would preserve a nonzero effect, but would ask about coordinated group reports rather than the paper’s individual truthfulness.

So the case against this anchor is that its faithful atomless version makes the paper’s central incentive condition vacuous, while repairs that retain individual capacity effects leave the continuum or change the strategic question. The negative case is not airtight: finite high-multiplicity traffic assignment could still support worthwhile algorithmic work, and a continuous relaxation might be useful in that work. That is a case for studying a neighboring problem, though, not strong evidence that Theorem 2 has a worthwhile continuous-population mirror.
