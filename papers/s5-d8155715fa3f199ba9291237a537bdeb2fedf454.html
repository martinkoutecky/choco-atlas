The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(A\) and position types \(R\) with rational masses \(\alpha_a,\beta_r\) summing to the same total, a fixed allowed-pair graph \(E\), rational costs, and an optimal transportation plan with optimal dual potentials, costs incident to one type \(a_0\) are updated. Determine the complexity of returning a new exact optimal plan and dual potentials; in particular, can this be done with one shortest-path computation in \(O(|E|+(|A|+|R|)\log(|A|+|R|))\) arithmetic operations, with bit complexity parameterized by the input length?

The model it lives in

Applicants and positions are grouped into complete types with masses \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_r\). A plan \(x_{ar}\ge 0\) assigns mass along allowed pairs \((a,r)\in E\), satisfies \(\sum_r x_{ar}=\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ar}=\beta_r\), and minimizes \(\sum_{(a,r)\in E}c_{ar}x_{ar}\). After the costs in one applicant-type row change, return an optimal plan and feasible optimal dual potentials.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 3.1's local matching update and Theorem 4.8's EF1 guarantee; they do not establish the paper's running-time bounds for mass instances or address Theorem 4.9's EFX guarantee.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one cohort-row cost update be handled by a single shortest-path computation, with complexity independent of the encoded mass denominators?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest direct mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s dynamic assignment problem. The paper contains no named hardness result, so the relevant anchors are algorithms. Theorem 3.1, proved here, says that in a balanced bipartite graph with a minimum-weight perfect matching and a potential, if edge weights change only around one vertex, a new minimum-weight perfect matching and potential can be found in \(O(m+n\log n)\) time.

Call the continuous version Type-Local Transport Reoptimization. There are finitely many applicant types \(A\) and position types \(R\), with rational masses \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_r\), each summing to \(1\). Types include all attributes used by the matching problem, and a person of any type still receives one integral match. The allowed matches form a bipartite graph \(E\), with rational cost \(c_{ar}\) for each allowed pair. An assignment is a nonnegative mass plan \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_r x_{ar}=\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ar}=\beta_r\); its cost is \(\sum_{(a,r)\in E}c_{ar}x_{ar}\).

The input includes an optimal plan and feasible optimal dual potentials. Costs incident to one applicant type \(a_0\) are then updated. The task is to return an optimal plan for the new costs and corresponding feasible optimal dual potentials. The output is the exact mass sent between each pair of types. This is a continuous matching problem in its own right, with the transportation LP as its optimization model.

The regime is credible in a labor market with large cohorts of applicants who share qualifications and cost profiles, matched to large cohorts of positions grouped by role. The total number of people can be very large while \(|A|+|R|\) is modest. A change in the cost of matching one applicant type to roles—for example, after a qualification or hiring-cost change—gives the local update. The model changes the population into masses and allows fractional aggregate flows; each person’s match remains integral. That is the co-modification needed to express high multiplicity, not continuity of the outcome space as the programme’s goal.

I expect the underlying reoptimization problem to be tractable: it is a rational min-cost transportation problem, solvable in polynomial time in \(|A|+|R|\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length. The sharper question that genuinely mirrors the paper is whether locality permits an update close to the theorem’s single-shortest-path bound, or whether changed costs can force several rounds of rerouting. Further questions include how many augmentations suffice independently of the encoded masses, and whether a similarly fast update works with more general capacity or balance constraints.

A second, more specifically fair-division mirror is Mass-EF1 Allocation. Theorem 4.8, also proved here, says that Algorithm 2 finds an EF1 allocation for \(n\) agents and \(m\) items in \(O(mn^2)\) time, for the paper’s nonnegative monotone valuations. The paper’s theorem does not give a named hardness result for this problem.

For a precise continuous instance, take finitely many valuation types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and finitely many item kinds with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\). A permitted bundle \(b\) is an integral vector of item counts, and each type \(t\) has a monotone valuation \(v_t(b)\). Let \(x_{t,b}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\). Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,b} b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\) for each item kind. The solution is such an allocation whose positive-mass classes are pairwise EF1: for every two classes \((t,b)\) and \((u,d)\) with positive mass, either \(v_t(b)\ge v_t(d)\), or there is an item \(j\) in \(d\) such that \(v_t(b)\ge v_t(d-e_j)\). Thus each person still receives an integral bundle, while a type’s average bundle can be fractional.

A plausible regime is a large population of households with a small number of shared valuation profiles, receiving repeated copies of standardized goods. The normalized supplies \(q_j\) say how many copies are available per person. This preserves the paper’s fairness question at the individual level and lets the aggregate allocation distribute different integral bundles across members of one type.

My initial expectation is tractability when the finite bundle set is manageable, at least parameterized by the number of type-bundle classes \(Q=|T||\mathcal B|\). One can enumerate candidate supports, reject supports containing an incompatible pair, and test the remaining mass and supply constraints by linear programming. Whether this can be made polynomial in the natural input parameters, especially with an implicit bundle set, is a further question. The mirror covers Theorem 4.8; I would not claim that its \(O(mn^2)\) bound automatically becomes a bound polynomial in the number of types.

The local transport mirror is my lead: it preserves the paper’s exact optimization objective and update locality, and its continuous version is plainly well-posed. The EF1 mirror brings the paper closer to fair division in computational social choice and is a credible high-multiplicity question, though its algorithmic prospects are less settled.

The soft spot is that the transport mirror’s basic polynomial solvability follows from standard min-cost flow machinery; what is not established is that Theorem 3.1’s fast local repair survives aggregation into masses. For Mass-EF1, the converse concern applies: grouping agents by type is natural, but the paper’s algorithm does not by itself show how to compress its computation to depend on the number of types rather than the population. Those limits leave meaningful computational questions without pretending that either paper theorem already answers its continuous counterpart.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper’s speedups depend on details that population aggregation changes. The paper does have named computational results, so the “no result to mirror” argument does not apply.

For Theorem 3.1, locality means that costs incident to one vertex change. If that vertex represents one person in a continuum, the update affects zero mass. If it represents a cohort, then the update changes costs for the cohort’s whole row in a capacitated transportation problem. The old proof’s single matching exchange and shortest path do not directly cover that case: a fractional flow update may require several reroutings. This makes the promised update bound uncertain. But a cohort-wide tariff change in a large hiring market is a plausible scenario, and the resulting row-update problem is precise. The mismatch weakens the direct correspondence; it does not defeat the better mirror.

The fixed-goods version of Theorem 4.8 has a real degeneration: as the population grows while the number of indivisible items stays fixed, almost everyone receives nothing. The proposed per-capita supply model repairs that problem. It is a substantial co-modification, but repeated standardized goods and valuation types make it recognizable as a high-multiplicity fair-allocation problem. Requiring EF1 between every pair of positive-mass type-and-bundle classes also gives a coherent individual fairness condition. Clearing the rational denominators turns such an instance into a finite replicated instance; the remaining question is whether it can be solved with complexity governed by type count and input bit length rather than the expanded population. The paper’s algorithm does not answer that question, but that gap is exactly what a mirror could investigate.

There is a modeling cost for the general monotone-valuation version: valuations over bundles of repeated item kinds need an explicit or succinct representation, especially as the denominator grows. A restricted version with additive valuations or a bounded bundle set avoids that problem, though it mirrors less of Theorem 4.8. The same tradeoff applies to the EFX extension in Theorem 4.9.

So the defensible negative case is about research priority: Theorem 3.1 becomes a capacitated-flow sensitivity problem, and Theorem 4.8 needs a new resource model and a type-level fairness formulation. I can’t honestly turn those objections into a claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. The cohort-update problem and the per-capita Mass-EF1 problem both remain plausible, well-posed candidates under the programme’s stated scope.
