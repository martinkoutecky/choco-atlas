The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) item categories, \(\tau\) types with known MAX-SUM-ALL read-once valuations \(v_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-person supplies \(b_i\), compute the exact maximum welfare and a maximizing allocation. Each person receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq[n]\); the allocation assigns mass \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) of type \(t\) to bundle \(S\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:i\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_i\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), with complexity measured in \(n\), \(\tau\), the formula sizes, and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A configuration LP over type and integral-bundle pairs \((t,S)\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is assigned population mass, each type receives total mass \(\mu_t\), and each item has per-capita supply \(b_i\). Its objective is aggregate welfare \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the welfare-allocation problem for MAX-SUM-ALL read-once preferences anchored by Theorem 6. It leaves the value-query elicitation results, approximate elicitation, and Toolbox-DNF allocation results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact optimal welfare and a finite rational mass allocation be computed in time polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), total formula size, and \(L\), using Theorem 4 for pricing and optimization-separation?
  • How does the population optimum compare with Theorem 6's two-agent hardness when members of the same type may receive different bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 6, proved in this paper’s Appendix D. It says that, given two agents with known MAX-SUM-ALL read-once valuations, it is NP-hard to find an item allocation whose welfare is more than one half of the optimum. The proof reduces SAT with a gap: the constructed instance has optimum \(2\) if the formula is satisfiable and at most \(1\) otherwise. This is the paper’s clearest hardness anchor.

The most credible high-multiplicity setting is a large program distributing bundles of capacity-limited goods or service entitlements to many customers. Suppose customers fall into a small number \(\tau\) of repeated preference profiles—for example, standardized household or subscription profiles—and each profile’s read-once formula fully captures its members’ preferences, including the item values. The population may contain millions of customers while \(\tau\) is in the tens or hundreds. The available quantity of each good grows with the population. That makes the population the continuous object while retaining the paper’s welfare objective: allocate goods to maximize the sum of agents’ valuations.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover, Continuous Read-Once Welfare Allocation. An instance gives \(n\) item categories, a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) MAX-SUM-ALL read-once valuation formulas \(v_t\) with rational leaf values, rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-person supplies \(b_i\) for each item \(i\). A person still receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq[n]\); the continuous variable represents how much mass of a type receives each bundle. Set \(x_{t,S}\) to that mass. The constraints and objective are

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq[n]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni i}x_{t,S}\le b_i \quad(i\in[n]),\qquad x_{t,S}\ge 0, \]

\[ \text{maximize}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}x_{t,S}v_t(S). \]

The requested solution is the exact optimal per-person welfare and a maximizing allocation, given as a finite list of bundles with their rational assigned masses. Its complexity should be measured in \(n,\tau\), the total formula size, and input bit length \(L\). The variables describe different people receiving different whole bundles; they do not give fractional bundles to individuals.

I expect this problem to be Class A: tractable. Its configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables, but its pricing problem for type \(t\) at item prices \(p_i\ge0\) is \(\max_S(v_t(S)-\sum_{i\in S}p_i)\). The paper’s Theorem 4, proved here, supplies the relevant algorithmic tool: given one known MAX-SUM-ALL read-once valuation and one known additive valuation, a welfare-maximizing allocation can be found in polynomial time. Assigning the additive party the prices of the items it receives is equivalent to this pricing problem up to the constant \(\sum_i p_i\). Thus each type has polynomial-time pricing, and the LP’s dual can be solved by the standard optimization–separation machinery. The theorem’s role here is technical support for the single continuous problem, rather than a second anchor.

The connection to Theorem 6 is direct but does not preserve its hardness gap. Given its two agents with valuations \(f\) and \(g\), set \(\mu_f=\mu_g=1/2\) and \(b_i=1/2\) for every item. Any discrete split \(A,[n]\setminus A\) gives a feasible continuous allocation by assigning mass \(1/2\) of type \(f\) to \(A\) and mass \(1/2\) of type \(g\) to its complement; the continuous welfare is half the discrete welfare. But the continuum can distribute different bundles among members of the same type, so its optimum may be higher. The SAT gap therefore does not automatically transfer. That is the interesting question: the paper’s two-agent hardness may dissolve when the same social-welfare problem is posed for a high-multiplicity population.

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s welfare-allocation problem for read-once preferences, not the paper’s value-query elicitation results, approximate elicitation, or Toolbox-DNF allocation. It raises follow-on questions about rounding rational mass allocations to finite populations with bounded welfare loss, robustness to changes in type masses and supplies, and whether other preference classes also admit efficient pricing.

The weakest point is the change from two agents sharing a fixed set of single items to a large population sharing per-person supplies. That is a meaningful extension, not a literal limit of the theorem’s instance. I think the authors would recognize it because the underlying decision remains allocation of goods to agents to maximize the sum of their valuations, with the same read-once preferences. Still, the scenario must be defended as a real high-multiplicity market, and the continuous result should be presented as a new population version of their problem—not as a consequence of Theorem 6.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 6 does not itself describe a large population. It allocates one copy of each item between two possibly very different agents. If we simply replicate those agents while keeping the original supplies fixed, each person’s share of the goods vanishes as the population grows. To get a nondegenerate limit, we must scale supplies with population size, changing the setting into a market for repeated customer types.

That is a real change, but it does not defeat the proposed mirror under ChoCo’s scope: per-capita supplies may scale with the population, and the scenario may differ from the paper’s own. A market serving many customers with a small number of standardized preference profiles is plausible. Each person can still receive an integral bundle; the continuous variables describe how much of each type receives each bundle. The objective remains maximizing aggregate welfare.

In that model, the configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables, but its pricing problem for type \(t\) is \(\max_S(v_t(S)-\sum_{i\in S}p_i)\). Theorem 4 solves precisely this form of read-once valuation minus an additive cost. So the continuous welfare problem has a credible polynomial-time route through pricing and LP duality. That makes this a strong Class A mirror. Theorem 4 is supporting machinery here, not a separate anchor.

The skeptical residue is about contribution size: if Theorem 4 makes the continuous result an immediate corollary, it may be a modest extension. But tractability through pricing is exactly the kind of result the programme counts, and the fact that Theorem 6’s hardness need not survive the population version is not a valid objection.

I can’t honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case is weak: the proposed setting changes the theorem’s original market, but it is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the same welfare-allocation problem, and it yields a well-posed computational question.
