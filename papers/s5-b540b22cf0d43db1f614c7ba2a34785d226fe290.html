Why no mirror

Theorem 1 has computational character under this rubric: it gives a concrete finite rule for selecting a core committee, though no running-time bound. Each weighted voter is described for this problem by a disapproval set and weight, so aggregating equal types gives exactly the proposed distribution μ_H; conversely, any rational distribution of that form is a paper instance. The proposed mirror therefore restates the paper’s own population model and fails \(b\)-already.

What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and its finite selection rule; it gives no polynomial-time search bound, and the verifier grids are bounded checks. The domain with \(|H|\le2\) leaves larger-disapproval cases open.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity, in \(m,\tau,L\), of finding a core committee by maximizing \((e(B),j(B))\) over \(|B|=m-k\), and can it be done faster than exhaustive search?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Co-rank-two Hare-Core Search. Its fit is unusually direct: the paper’s weighted voters can be aggregated into a distribution over approval types.

Let \(C\) be a candidate set, \(2\le k\le |C|\), and let \(\mu\) be a rational probability distribution over disapproval types \(H\subseteq C\) with \(|H|\le2\). A type \(H\) approves \(C\setminus H\); its mass \(\mu_H\) is its share of the population. Given this society, find a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) such that every nonempty target set \(S\subseteq C\) has gainer mass strictly below its Hare quota:

\[ \sum_H \mu_H\, \mathbf 1\!\left[ |(C\setminus H)\cap S|>|(C\setminus H)\cap W| \right] <\frac{|S|}{k}. \]

A committee satisfying these inequalities is the solution. The natural regime is a large membership organization choosing \(k\) representatives from \(m\) candidates, where each member objects to at most two candidates. Many members may share the same objection set, so the number \(N\) of members can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of types. This is the paper’s near-consensus domain; it makes no claim that the domain is empirically universal.

The anchor is Theorem 1 (Co-rank-two core theorem), proved in this paper. It states that for every \(m\ge k\ge2\), every approval election with positive rational voter weights summing to one and at most two disapprovals per voter has a nonempty Hare core, with no bounds on candidates, seats, or voter types. More specifically, when \(m>k\), every committee whose missing set \(B\), of size \(m-k\), maximizes the pair \((e(B),j(B))\) lexicographically is in the core. Here \(e(B)\) is the weight of two-disapproval voters whose disapproval set meets \(B\), and \(j(B)\) is total weighted disapproval incidence with \(B\). A fixed tie-break makes the rule single-valued. The theorem gives a selection rule, though the paper does not claim it can be implemented in polynomial time.

This is a sensible continuous analogue because the core condition, quota, and committee decision are unchanged; only the population is expressed directly as masses by type. But the formal model is already present in the paper: aggregating voters with the same disapproval set gives \(\mu_H\), and its normalized rational weights are precisely rational type masses. The result therefore applies to the stated mirror directly. That makes the model fit especially persuasive, while also making this likely a novelty collision rather than a new continuization result.

For the theorem’s existence question, the answer is settled: every valid instance has a core. For the associated search problem, I would not assign a tractability class from this paper. Its explicit rule can be implemented by enumerating missing sets, but it supplies no polynomial-time bound; the maximization over candidate sets retains combinatorics in \(m\). Further questions include the search complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), whether a core can be found without computing that maximizer, and whether the result extends beyond two disapprovals.

The weakest point is that Theorem 1 is not a named complexity classification of the kind the prompt asks for: it establishes nonemptiness and gives a finite selection rule, not that core search is in P or hard. The positive case is therefore strongest as evidence that this weighted high-multiplicity model is natural and exact. As a fresh continuous-complexity contribution, it is weak: the paper already proves the central statement for rational population masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is an exact model collision. Theorem 1 is the only anchor raised, and its weighted approval elections already have the ChoCo population representation. For each disapproval type \(H\), set \(\mu_H=\sum_{i:H_i=H}w_i\). Then the mass of voters who gain from any target \(T\) against committee \(W\) is exactly
\[ \sum_H \mu_H\,\mathbf 1\!\left[|(C\setminus H)\cap T|>|(C\setminus H)\cap W|\right]. \]
The quota is unchanged, and the theorem’s objectives \(e(B)\) and \(j(B)\) depend on the voters only through these same type masses. Conversely, any rational distribution \(\mu\) in the proposed mirror is a paper instance with one weighted voter per supported type. Splitting or merging voters changes nothing.

That makes the large-membership-organization story plausible, but it does not create a new continuous model: the paper already allows arbitrary positive rational weights summing to one, with no bound on the number of types. This is not an objection based on a separate high-multiplicity result, nor on the claim that the answer is already known. The paper’s own input and the proposed continuous input are the same up to aggregation.

There is also no named computational-complexity result here to continuize. Theorem 1 proves core nonemptiness and supplies a finite selection rule; it does not establish a running-time bound for finding the maximizing missing set. The bounded verifier grids do not provide one either. One could study the complexity of computing a core or optimizing \((e,j)\) as a function of \(m,\tau,L\). That might be worthwhile algorithmics, but it is a question about the weighted problem the paper already formulates—not a new result made possible by continuizing its population.

A new intervention problem, such as changing masses to create or destroy a core, would add a different computational task. It would not rescue the proposed mirror of Theorem 1. The negative case is strongest on this narrow point: the population-by-type interpretation is sensible, but it is already present in the paper, while the paper supplies no computational classification for a continuous mirror to extend.
