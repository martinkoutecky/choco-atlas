The continuous mirror question

Given a society with distribution \(\mu\) over two complete agent types, each with a finite conflict-free argument set and a confident strategy, run one dialogue between fresh representatives. If the dialogue accepts a plan \(P\), must \(P\) belong to \(\mathrm{GE}_{\langle A_{t_1}\cup A_{t_2}\cup\{P\},R\rangle}\), the grounded extension formed from the arguments held by the society's two types?

The model it lives in

A two-cohort society specified by \(\mu_{t_1},\mu_{t_2}>0\) with \(\mu_{t_1}+\mu_{t_2}=1\), where each type shares a complete agent profile. The decision is the paper's pairwise dialogue; the property is that any accepted plan is grounded against the union of the types' arguments. Cohort masses do not change that union.

The objection that survived

Repeated pairwise meetings update agents' arguments, so a fixed distribution over initial types cannot represent the paper's full multi-agent extension; this mirror is limited to a one-shot two-cohort dialogue.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4's accepted-plan soundness for two homogeneous cohorts and Proposition 1's termination guarantee for a finite batch of independent type-pair sessions. It leaves multi-type collective agreement, evolving shared states, and plan-generation complexity open.

Open questions for a prover

  • For \(\tau>2\), under what interaction schedule and state-update rule can pairwise dialogues yield one plan sound with respect to \(\bigcup_t A_t\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: this paper supports a high-multiplicity mirror of its pairwise plan-dialogue protocol, not a continuous complexity landscape for planning. It gives no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar. Its best anchors are protocol guarantees, so they fall short of the programme’s usual complexity anchors.

A plausible regime is a very large deployment of cooperative planning agents in standardized settings, such as repeated logistics cells. Let a type be an agent’s complete profile for this paper: its finite argument set, preferences over arguments, planning beliefs and action theory, shared goal, and dialogue strategy, including any tie-breaking choices. Agents of one type are exact copies; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the deployment with that profile. A fleet could have millions of agents but only a small support of types. The paper itself says its two-agent results carry over to more agents by conducting pairwise dialogues. That makes a type-level reduction recognizable as an extension of its setup.

Lead anchor: Proposition 1, proved here. It states that an iterated dispute between agents with finite argument sets always terminates. In the paper’s proof setting, the agents begin with finite, consistent argument sets and follow confident strategies. The argument is that each dispute terminates, and each failed defence reveals arguments the proponent did not previously know; finite argument sets therefore prevent an infinite sequence of failed disputes.

A continuous version is Type-Compressed Pairwise Dispute Execution. Its instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete agent types, a shared planning goal and action vocabulary, and a schedule of pairwise plan dialogues. For each scheduled type pair, run the paper’s exact iterated-dispute protocol between representatives of those types. A solution is the terminal transcript for each pair, including an accepted proposal when a dialogue accepts one, or the terminal no-agreement outcome otherwise. The question is whether this finite batch of type-pair dialogues terminates. For \(\tau\) supported types, the batch has at most \(\tau^2\) ordered type-pair sessions, regardless of the population size \(N\). Proposition 1 gives termination for each session, hence for the finite batch.

I would expect this termination question to have a positive answer on every valid instance. That is a termination guarantee, not a polynomial-time result: the paper gives no bound on the number of moves or total transcript length. In ChoCo terms, it suggests a tractable direction for type-compressed protocol execution, but does not establish a P classification for generating the transcripts.

Secondary anchor: Proposition 4, proved here. It states that if an iterated dispute terminates with both agents accepting a plan proposal \(P\), then \(P\) belongs to the grounded extension of the argumentation framework formed from both agents’ initial arguments and \(P\), assuming initially conflict-free argument sets and confident strategies.

Its mirror is Two-Cohort Plan-Agreement Soundness. The instance has two positive-mass types, \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), with finite initially conflict-free argument sets, a shared goal, and the other profile components above. Run the plan dialogue between representatives of the two cohorts. The output is the terminal transcript and, if accepted, the plan \(P\). A valid accepted output must satisfy \(P\in\mathrm{GE}_{\langle A_1\cup A_2\cup\{P\},R\rangle}\). Proposition 4 supplies that soundness guarantee directly. I would expect the property to carry over exactly for two cohorts; it says nothing about whether an acceptable plan exists, how hard it is to find one, or whether pairwise acceptance across three or more types implies membership in the grounded extension of the whole society’s argument framework.

These mirrors cover the paper’s pairwise dialogue termination and accepted-plan soundness results. They do not cover the complexity of planning, the existence of a group-wide accepted plan, or any optimization over persuasion or communication. The paper supplies no hardness anchor, so there is no basis here for claiming either transferred hardness or continuum-specific hardness.

The weakest point is that mass is passive: dialogue behaviour depends on type profiles, while \(\mu_t\) does not affect the outcome. The support-level compression is a plausible high-multiplicity reading of the paper’s proposed pairwise extension, but it does not by itself create a new population-dependent computational problem. I would present it as a modest mirror of the protocol results, not as a strong contribution to the programme’s central complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors are computationally relevant, but neither gives the kind of population-dependent problem ChoCo is meant to continuize. Proposition 1 proves that a particular two-agent dialogue terminates when the argument sets are finite and the agents use confident strategies. It gives no bound on moves, transcript length, or running time. Proposition 4 is a conditional soundness guarantee: if those two agents accept \(P\), then \(P\) is grounded with respect to their combined arguments. The paper leaves plan generation to a standard planner. So there is no complexity classification here; the named results are properties of a bilateral protocol.

The proposed type-compressed batch is the strongest direct reading of Proposition 1, but it has no population object beyond the list of types. For each pair of types, it runs an ordinary dialogue between representatives. The transcript depends on the two profiles, not on their masses: changing \(\mu_t\) while keeping the support fixed changes nothing about the sessions. The result is a finite table of pairwise transcripts, not a computational question about a continuous society. One could weight that table to report the fraction of random pairs that accept a plan, but that statistic is not a result or objective in the paper, and its dialogues remain isolated dyads.

There is a more population-aware possibility: let agents meet repeatedly, and study how much of the population comes to accept a common plan. But the paper’s protocol changes the participants’ argument sets as they learn from each dispute. After meetings, agents who began with the same profile can have different knowledge, so a fixed type-pair table no longer represents the population. A continuous model would need an encounter schedule or rates, rules for updating the distribution of knowledge states, and a society-level objective. If dialogues are instead independent, there is no transfer of beliefs or agreement across pairs. Either way, this is a new interaction or allocation problem, not a mirror of Proposition 1’s termination result.

Proposition 4 has the same boundary. Applied to every positive-mass pair of types, it gives the pairwise guarantee pointwise; \(\mu\) contributes no semantic content. A stronger demand—that one plan be sound against the arguments of the whole society—is a different property. Under grounded semantics, an argument’s relevance depends on whether it is present, not on how much mass holds it. A type with arbitrarily small positive mass can therefore affect the collective framework just as much as a large type. Weighting arguments to avoid that jump would change the paper’s acceptance semantics.

The best alternative I can see is to use pairwise outcomes to match cohorts, maximizing the mass paired around an accepted plan. That could be worth studying, but it adds a pairing decision and an objective absent from the paper; it can also produce separate pairwise agreements rather than one plan accepted by the society. The negative case is weakest here: large deployments of planning agents could make a population interaction model sensible. But the paper supplies no result about that model. Its anchors support a bilateral protocol over agent states; turning them into a continuous-population problem requires introducing the population-level computation first.
