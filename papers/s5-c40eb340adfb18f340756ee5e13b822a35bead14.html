The continuous mirror question

Given projects \(\mathcal P\), lecturers \(\mathcal L\), supported complete preference types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita project capacities and lecturer lower and upper quotas, decide feasibility and compute both a maximum-mass feasible allocation with lexicographically maximum rank profile \((q_1,\ldots,q_R)\) and one with lexicographically minimum reverse profile \((q_R,\ldots,q_1)\).

The model it lives in

Use allocation variables \(x_{t,p}\geq 0\) for the mass of type \(t\) assigned to acceptable project \(p\), and unassigned mass \(u_t\geq 0\), with \(\sum_{p\in A_t}x_{t,p}+u_t=\mu_t\). Enforce project and lecturer bounds, maximize total assigned mass, then optimize the rank-profile coordinates lexicographically; each unit of mass receives one whole project, while a type's mass may be split across projects.

The paper's own placement examples do not establish that complete preference types remain few as cohorts grow or that project and lecturer quotas scale with cohort size.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5.5, proved in this paper. It states that for an \(\mathrm{SPA\text{-}l}\) instance, one can obtain both a constrained greedy maximum matching and a constrained generous maximum matching, if either exists, in \(O(n_1^2 R m_2)\) time. Here \(n_1\) is the number of students, \(R\) the maximum preference rank, and \(m_2\) the total length of the students’ preference lists. The paper has no named hardness result, so this is an algorithmic mirror rather than a test of whether discrete hardness disappears.

A plausible setting is a large centralized placement scheme with tens of thousands of students and a common project catalogue. Repeated preference lists or ranking templates could leave hundreds or thousands of distinct lists, so the number \(\tau\) of types is much smaller than the population. A type is the student’s complete weak ranking of acceptable projects, including which projects are tied; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students with that ranking. The exact ranking matters because it determines both acceptability and profile rank. Project capacities and lecturers’ lower and upper quotas are given as fractions of the cohort. Each unit of mass receives at most one whole project: fractional values describe how much of a type goes to each project, not a divisible project or lottery for an individual.

Lead mirror — Continuous SPA-L Profile Optimization. An instance gives a finite project set \(\mathcal P\), lecturers \(\mathcal L\) with their offered-project sets, supported types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita project capacities \(c_p\), and rational lecturer quota bounds \(d^-_\ell,d^+_\ell\). For each acceptable type-project pair, choose an allocation mass \(x_{t,p}\geq 0\), and choose unassigned mass \(u_t\geq0\), with
\[ \sum_{p\in A_t}x_{t,p}+u_t=\mu_t. \]
Require \(\sum_t x_{t,p}\leq c_p\) for every project and
\[ d^-_\ell\leq \sum_{p\in P_\ell}\sum_t x_{t,p}\leq d^+_\ell \]
for every lecturer. If these constraints are feasible, return both: an allocation maximizing total assigned mass and then lexicographically maximizing the rank profile \((q_1,\ldots,q_R)\); and an allocation maximizing total assigned mass and then lexicographically minimizing the reverse profile \((q_R,\ldots,q_1)\). Here \(q_r\) is the mass assigned to rank \(r\). If the quota constraints are infeasible, report that. These are the continuous constrained greedy and constrained generous maximum matchings, respectively.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves the acceptable pairs, one-project-per-student rule, project and lecturer capacities, lecturer lower quotas, maximum-cardinality priority, and both profile objectives. It replaces individually named students by supplies at preference-type nodes. The corresponding network sends mass from types to acceptable projects, then through lecturers, with lower and upper bounds on lecturer flow. Maximize flow first, then optimize the rank coordinates in sequence. This gives a polynomial-time LP or flow formulation in \(\tau\), the number of supported type-project pairs, \(|\mathcal P|\), \(|\mathcal L|\), \(R\), and the rational input bit length. The expected direction is tractable.

There is also an exact bridge for rational instances. Choose a common denominator \(D\) for the masses and quota fractions and scale every quantity by \(D\). Type supplies and capacities become integers; network-flow integrality gives an optimal integral flow, which corresponds to assigning whole students. Conversely, any such discrete assignment gives a feasible allocation of mass. Thus the profile objectives agree after normalization, and replication of a cohort does not change the type-level problem. This makes the model a credible high-multiplicity formulation, not an outcome-space or lottery variant.

The mirror covers the paper’s constrained greedy and generous maximum-matching results, with zero lower quotas recovering the ordinary \(\mathrm{SPA}\) case. Its weakest point is that the paper already gives polynomial-time network-flow algorithms for the discrete problem. This mirror therefore does not show a hard problem becoming tractable, and its algorithmic novelty may be modest: the paper’s core matching and profile structure survives aggregation quite directly. The positive case is strongest as a precise type-compressed formulation of a genuinely large-population placement problem, with a clean exact correspondence to replicated discrete instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to Theorem 5.5 is that the proposed high-multiplicity setting is not established by the paper’s own applications. A type must preserve a student’s exact acceptable projects and their ranking, including ties. In a personalized project allocation, those lists may be mostly unique, so the support size \(\tau\) could grow with the cohort and leave little compression. And if the catalogue and lecturer capacity stay fixed as the cohort grows, per-capita capacity tends to zero: the normalized limit assigns vanishing mass. That makes this a conditional mirror, whose usefulness depends on finding instances where repeated preference lists and capacity growth are both credible.

But this does not defeat the stronger version of the mirror. The paper’s objective depends on rank counts, not on who receives each project. Aggregate students by complete preference type, send each type’s mass along acceptable project edges, and impose project and lecturer bounds, including lower quotas. Maximize assigned mass first, then optimize the rank profile lexicographically. This is the same matching question over a continuous population, and it has a direct flow formulation.

For rational inputs, clearing denominators gives integer type supplies and quota bounds. The resulting network flow has an integral optimum, so the continuous allocation corresponds to assigning whole agents in a replicated finite instance. The type-compressed network can still be useful when the population is huge but \(\tau\) is small; the fact that the paper already gives a polynomial discrete algorithm does not erase that computational question. The constrained greedy and generous objectives both survive, and zero lower quotas recover the ordinary case.

So the negative case is limited to whether this regime is common enough to justify ChoCo’s attention, and whether an essentially standard flow formulation offers enough beyond the paper’s existing algorithm. Those are fair reservations, but they do not rule out a plausible regime or a well-posed computational mirror. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor: Theorem 5.5 remains a strong candidate for a green result.
