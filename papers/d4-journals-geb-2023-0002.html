The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no anchors, and that is the strongest negative case here: the paper’s results are not computational results. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–4 describe stability, efficiency, and incentives of matching rules. Theorem 2 characterizes which rules are Pareto-efficient; Theorem 3 characterizes which are strategyproof; Theorem 4 restricts individual manipulation. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. That leaves no result in this paper for the programme to mirror.

There are plausible high-multiplicity settings for school assignment, admissions, or refugee placement. The paper also discusses coarse priorities, which could let many students share a type. But that only makes a continuous population model sensible; it does not turn these characterizations into computational questions. One could study their analogues for a continuum of students, with capacities scaled per capita and assignments represented by masses. That would be a continuous mechanism-design or axiomatic analysis, outside this programme’s stated scope. Adding an algorithmic objective—such as finding a manipulation or optimizing an assignment—would pose a new problem rather than mirror a result established here.

The individual incentive results have a further obstacle in an atomless model: one student’s report changes zero mass, so their deviation cannot affect the aggregate outcome. The resulting strategyproofness question is vacuous unless deviations are given positive mass; treating a whole type as a coordinated bloc would instead ask about coalition manipulation. Neither rescues a mirror of Theorems 3 or 4.

So the negative case is strong against a *computational mirror of this paper*. It does not show that continuous school matching is uninteresting; it shows that any interesting continuous-computation project here would need a new problem, not one anchored in this paper’s named results.
