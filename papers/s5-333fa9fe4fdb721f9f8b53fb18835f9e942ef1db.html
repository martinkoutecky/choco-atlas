The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result in the sense required for an anchor. Theorem 1 proves that student-proposing DA is locally non-bossy; Theorem 2 characterizes DA through axioms; and Theorem 3 proves stability and strategy-proofness on the preferences-over-colleagues domain. None asserts a complexity classification for a computational problem. The paper describes the DA algorithm, but does not give a named result about the complexity of computing its outcome.

A high-multiplicity school-choice scenario is imaginable, but it would not supply the missing anchor. In the paper’s model, types would have to capture both students’ preferences and their positions in each school’s strict priority order; those positions distinguish students rather than creating repeated types. Grouping students by preferences alone would discard information the model uses. Resolving that would require a substantive change to the priority model, and the paper’s results would not establish the computational claims for it.

So I can’t state a continuous problem per qualifying anchor without inventing one. The paper offers meaningful results about incentives and characterization, but no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves local non-bossiness of DA; Theorem 2 characterizes DA through axioms; and Theorem 3 establishes stability and strategy-proofness on a richer preference domain. These are results about incentives and axioms, not the complexity of a computational problem. The paper describes the DA algorithm, but does not make its running time or another computational task a result. The proponent’s case identifies no theorem that clears that bar.

The best attempt to build an anchor from Theorem 1 would be a mass-robustness question: how much of a large student population must misreport preferences to change the composition of a school while remaining assigned there? That is a plausible population-level computational problem, perhaps for a large district with capacities scaled to enrollment. But it changes the theorem’s question. Theorem 1 concerns one named student changing an exact roster while keeping her own school. A positive-mass cohort’s coordinated deviation is a different incentive property; the paper does not characterize it. It may merit study, but it is a new problem rather than a computational mirror of this result.

One could try to make that scenario more faithful by grouping students into types that share both preferences and school priorities. The strict priority orders in the paper make this difficult: priority position is part of the information the mechanism uses, so distinct positions distinguish students. Ties or priority bands could create repeated types, but then the model must specify how schools select within a band and how capacities scale. Those are plausible modeling choices, not objections to high multiplicity itself. They still do not supply a computational result from this paper to mirror.

Theorem 2 may look more promising because it studies changes in the population. But its population-monotonicity axiom compares assignments of the same named students across subsets of a fixed population. A type-level version needs a new way to track those agents—such as fractional assignments to a type—and the theorem remains an axiomatic characterization either way. Theorem 3 is harder to carry over: preferences over colleagues concern who a student is assigned with. Replacing peer identities with a distribution over peer types changes the preference domain; in a nonatomic population, a single student also cannot change that aggregate distribution. A positive-mass group deviation again asks a different question.

So the negative case is strongest at the paper’s actual results: none gives the computational anchor the programme is meant to continuize, and the most natural population-level reconstructions replace their individual incentive questions with new cohort or robustness questions. The universal claim is less airtight: a model of large cohorts, priority bands, and peer-composition robustness could be worthwhile if the authors recognize it as their problem. But that would be a new computational agenda built around this paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
