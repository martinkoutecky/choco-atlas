What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns the opinion-expression equilibrium in Theorem 1; it does not cover the organizational signaling or recommender-system simulations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the opinion-expression game in Section 3. A plausible regime is a large online discussion about one policy issue: millions of users, but relatively few recurring opinion and parameter profiles. In the paper’s baseline model, a type can be the user’s opinion \(o\in[0,1]\); if users differ in moderation costs or other payoff parameters, those belong in the type too. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users of type \(t\). Each type chooses rhetorical intensity \(\gamma_t\in[0,1]\), with silence represented by zero. The question is to characterize or compute a symmetric equilibrium of the mass population under random matching and the paper’s expression utilities. This is a recognizable population-level version of the authors’ rational-silence question. It covers the baseline game, not the organization and recommender simulations.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in Appendix A. It says that for an arbitrary prior opinion distribution \(f\), with equal support for approval and disapproval, an ex-ante symmetric Bayesian Nash equilibrium in which everyone chooses zero rhetorical intensity exists if and only if
\[ \mathbb{E}_f[v(o)] < \frac{\alpha}{1-\hat n(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{in}})}, \]
where \(v(o)=o\) for \(o\geq 0.5\) and \(v(o)=1-o\) otherwise, \(\alpha\) is the expression cost, \(\hat n\) is the estimated approval share, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{in}}>1\) is the in-group boost.

A precise candidate mirror is Continuous Rational-Silence Equilibrium. An instance gives a finite set of opinion types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, exactly half the mass on each side of \(0.5\), and rational game parameters in the paper’s stated ranges. The society consists of \(N\) users with \(N\gg |T|\), and each type’s mass chooses an equilibrium rhetorical intensity under the paper’s payoff and matching model. The decision question is whether an ex-ante symmetric equilibrium with \(\gamma_t=0\) for every type exists; a yes-solution is that all-zero strategy. For these instances, the stated theorem supplies a direct test by computing \(\sum_t\mu_t v(o_t)\). I would expect this restricted decision question to be tractable.

The weakness is substantial: Theorem 1 is an equilibrium characterization, not a named computational-complexity result. The paper contains no numbered result asserting a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar, so it supplies no qualifying computational anchor under the requested standard. Further, its theorem already takes expectations over a distribution of opinions. A continuum-mass reading is natural, but risks restating the paper’s existing model rather than creating a distinct continuous mirror. Thus there is a plausible population interpretation, but not an anchor-ready ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it does not supply a computational result: it characterizes when an all-silent symmetric equilibrium exists. The paper states no complexity problem or algorithmic bound. Turning that condition into a finite-input decision problem gives a weighted sum, \(\sum_t \mu_t v(o_t)\), followed by the theorem’s threshold comparison. That is a reasonable formalization of the theorem, but it adds no computational question for ChoCo to chart.

More fundamentally, the population distribution is already inside the paper’s model. Opinions are drawn from an arbitrary distribution \(f\), agents are randomly matched from the community, and the equilibrium analysis takes expectations over \(f\). The proposed finite type set and rational masses make that distribution explicit; they do not create a new continuous society. Theorem 1 already gives the all-silent test at the distribution level. This is a collision with the paper’s own model, rather than a case where earlier high-multiplicity work merely supports the mirror.

A stronger computational variant could ask for type-contingent equilibria or optimal signaling policies. But the paper’s equilibrium analysis already works with a distribution over opinions, and its organizational model already has belief states, signals, and a policy-optimization procedure. Making those procedures into exact complexity questions could be legitimate algorithmic game theory; it would not establish a new population continuization, and it would not mirror a named computational result in this paper. Likewise, adding an election or other collective-choice mechanism to study how silence affects a winner would supply a new problem that the paper does not analyze.

The negative case is strongest on novelty and fit, not on whether the population story is sensible. Millions of users grouped by opinion and payoff-relevant attributes are a plausible mass population, and the model’s random matching makes aggregate proportions meaningful. I cannot rule out worthwhile computational work built around that setting. But for this paper, the population is already represented distributionally, while the one named theorem is an analytic characterization rather than an unaddressed computational anchor.
