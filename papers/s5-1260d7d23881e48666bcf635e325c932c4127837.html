The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many worker types \(T\) with rational shares \(\mu_t\), chore classes \(J\), and additive costs \(c_t(j)\), an online adversary reveals chore-class mass increments whose total supply per worker is \(q_j\). A causal policy assigns each increment by moving worker mass from states \((t,B)\) to \((t,B+e_j)\), so each worker has an integral bundle. Determine the least \(\alpha\) for which a deterministic policy guarantees, for every type with positive mass, \(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\mid t}\sum_{j\in J}c_t(j)B_j\leq \alpha\,\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)\), where \(\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)=\inf_{\pi:\,\mathbb E_{B\sim\pi}[B_j]=q_j\ \forall j}\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\pi}\sum_{j\in J}c_t(j)B_j\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of workers with type shares \(\mu_t\), each receiving whole chores and retaining an integral bundle \(B\); online decisions are causal mass flows between bundle states, and performance is measured against each type’s limiting MMS for total per-worker supply \(q\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.1’s adversary may force only one worker above MMS, whose mass can vanish in the continuum; the proponent acknowledges that transfer to fixed support is unproved.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s deterministic online competitive lower bound as a question about a high-multiplicity population; it leaves the finite-\(n\) bound itself and randomized algorithms outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(|T|\) and \(|J|\), what is the optimal deterministic competitive ratio?
  • Does the finite MMS benchmark converge to \(\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)\) along every large-multiplicity sequence?
  • Can an adversary force a positive mass of workers above the proposed ratio when the type support is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a high-multiplicity online workforce: a large organization repeatedly assigns indivisible tasks to many workers, with a fixed set of worker profiles. Workers share a type when they have the same cost for every task class the problem uses. If there are \(N\) workers but only \(\tau\) such profiles, with many workers of each type, their type shares \(\mu_t\) are a natural description of the society. This is a plausible regime for recurring work among employees with the same training and working conditions.

My lead mirror is Online MMS Chore Assignment\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite set of worker types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and task classes \(J\). Type \(t\) has an additive cost \(c_t(j)\) for each class \(j\). Tasks arrive online; the policy learns each task’s costs when it arrives and must assign it immediately, without knowing the remaining stream.

Represent the population at each time by a distribution over states \((t,B)\), where \(B\in\mathbb N^J\) is an integral bundle of chores already assigned. On an arrival of class \(j\), the policy chooses a mass flow from states \((t,B)\) to \((t,B+e_j)\). Thus each worker who receives a chore gets a whole chore, and every individual bundle remains integral; fractional values describe only the share of the population in each state. Arrivals and chore counts are normalized per capita, as in the large-\(N\) limit where \(N\) workers receive \(N\) times the normalized chore mass.

If \(q_j\) is the final chore supply of class \(j\) per worker, define the limiting MMS benchmark for type \(t\) by
\[ \mathrm{MMS}_t(q)= \inf_{\pi:\ \mathbb E_{B\sim\pi}[B_j]=q_j\ \forall j} \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\pi} \sum_{j\in J} c_t(j)B_j, \]
where \(\pi\) ranges over distributions on integral bundles. This is the asymptotic version of partitioning the chores among many workers and minimizing the largest bundle cost as evaluated by type \(t\). The question is to determine the best worst-case competitive ratio of a deterministic causal mass-flow policy: for every allowed arrival stream, what is the least \(\alpha\) such that almost every type-\(t\) worker finishes with cost at most \(\alpha\,\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)\)? A solution is either such a policy or an adversarial stream showing that no policy can achieve the proposed ratio.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It states that for \(n\) agents, no deterministic online chore-assignment algorithm has competitive ratio better than \(n\) against the MMS benchmark. The proof strengthens the earlier lower bound of \(2\): for every \(\epsilon>0\), it constructs an adaptive arrival scenario on which any deterministic algorithm fails to be \((n-n\epsilon)\)-MMS. This is an online algorithmic impossibility result, not an NP-hardness result. The mirror therefore asks about competitive guarantees, rather than claiming a P-versus-NP result.

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous analogue: it retains the online revelations, irrevocable assignments, additive costs, and MMS comparison. The mass-flow description changes the population representation while preserving whole chores and whole individual bundles. The conjecture I would test first is Class A for fixed support: when \(\tau\) and the number of recurring chore classes are fixed, can mass-flow or configuration methods give a finite competitive guarantee? The paper’s theorem motivates the question but does not settle it. Further questions include whether the limiting MMS formula agrees with finite MMS along all large-multiplicity sequences, and whether a lower bound survives when \(\tau\) grows with \(N\).

The weak point is that Theorem 3.1’s ratio grows with the number of agents, and the paper does not establish that its construction still works when many agents share each of a fixed number of types. In particular, the theorem’s \(n\)-agent lower bound does not itself transfer to this fixed-support limit. That is a real gap in the positive case, but it also makes the mirror a useful question: it isolates whether the lower bound comes from online uncertainty that survives continuization or from having many separately targetable agents.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case rests on a real mismatch in Theorem 3.1’s guarantee. The theorem is a genuine computational result: it proves an online competitive lower bound, so it cannot be dismissed as merely axiomatic. But failure is witnessed by one agent whose burden is too high. In the proposed mirror, the guarantee is for almost every worker, and the MMS benchmark uses an essential supremum. A worker who represents mass \(1/N\) in a finite workforce can disappear as \(N\) grows. The lower-bound event that drives the paper’s result can therefore vanish in the proposed limit.

The natural repair is to require the guarantee for every worker. A distribution over types and bundle states cannot express that condition: it forgets how assignments behave on sets of measure zero. A pointwise assignment map over individually labeled workers could retain it, but then the model must track individual histories as well as population masses. That weakens the case that this is the paper’s problem in a continuous-population form. The other repair, a guarantee for almost every worker, is meaningful for some workforce settings, but it changes the paper’s all-agent fairness objective. The lower bound gives no reason to expect that its central obstruction survives this change.

The workforce scenario itself is plausible, and the paper’s theorem does not settle the fixed-support question. Its adversary uses up to \(n\) individually targetable agents; when many workers share a fixed set of cost profiles, that construction does not directly transfer. This is the proponent’s strongest surviving point: the fixed-support online question might still be worth studying on its own. So the honest case against is limited. The proposed mirror loses the theorem’s worst-agent content, while a pointwise version loses the mass-only representation; but neither shows that every continuous mirror would be uninteresting. The universal negative claim is weaker than the specific objection to this anchor.
