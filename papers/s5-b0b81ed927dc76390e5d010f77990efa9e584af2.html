The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a mirror of the paper’s turnout comparison. For each fixed agenda, let a voter type be an ideal point \(w\in\{-1,1\}^n\), and let the society be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types. A large electorate can have many people sharing each complete type, with \(\mu_w\) recording its population share.

Given party positions \(V=(v_1,\ldots,v_m)\), retain the paper’s ballot choices and its objective: each type chooses a ballot \(x\) minimizing \(\|Vx-w\|_2\). For plurality, \(F_M=\{0\}\cup\{e_j\}\); for approval, \(F_A=\{0\}\cup\{|S|^{-1}\sum_{j\in S}e_j:\varnothing\ne S\subseteq[m]\}\). Abstention is \(x=0\). Define \(q_R(w;V)=1\) when some non-null ballot under rule \(R\) gives a strictly smaller distance than abstention, and \(0\) otherwise. The Continuous Participation-Mass problem takes \(n,m\), a rule \(R\), the party positions, and a support-list representation of \(\mu\); it asks for the exact participating share
\[ P_R(\mu,V)=\sum_w\mu_w q_R(w;V). \]
Its decisions are ballots, its per-type objective is distance to the voter’s ideal point, and its population-level quantity is participating mass.

A plausible regime is a large election in which citizens with the same issue positions are interchangeable for the model’s purposes. For fixed \(n\), the number of possible types is \(2^n\); the electorate can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types actually present. Uniform mass over all \(2^n\) ideal points gives the paper’s symmetric case.

The strongest named result for this mirror is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It assumes that every coordinate of every party position and voter ideal point is independently \(+1\) or \(-1\), each with probability \(1/2\). When the number of issues is \(n\) and the number of parties is \(m=n\), it proves that the probability a voter prefers voting to abstaining under plurality tends to \(0\) as \(n\to\infty\), while the corresponding probability under approval tends to \(1\).

Theorem 2 is not a computational-complexity result of the kind the programme asks analysts to anchor on: it establishes a probabilistic turnout limit, not membership in P, hardness, or another complexity classification. I cannot present it as a qualifying complexity anchor. It is nevertheless a close population-level anchor. With uniform \(\mu\), averaging \(q_R(w;V)\) over all types gives the share \(P_R(\mu,V)\). Averaging that share over random party positions equals the paper’s probability for a randomly drawn voter. Thus Theorem 2 implies that expected plurality participation mass tends to \(0\), while expected approval participation mass tends to \(1\). Since the masses lie in \([0,1]\), Markov’s inequality further implies convergence in probability to those limits. That last step is an inference from the theorem, not a result stated by the authors.

For this particular random, uniform regime, the expected direction is tractable in the programme’s broad sense: the turnout-mass limit is established analytically, and the mass itself concentrates at the stated extremes. The unrestricted exact Participation-Mass problem is a further computational question; the paper supplies no complexity result for it. It generates questions about exact or approximate mass computation for sparse rational \(\mu\), and about the complexity of finding a closest approval ballot for each type. Those questions preserve the paper’s ballot choices and distance objective, rather than replacing them with a continuous relaxation of approval ballots.

The mirror covers the turnout comparison in Theorem 2, not the paper’s entire model. Its plausibility comes from keeping the authors’ individual decision problem intact and aggregating its choices by type mass—an aggregate their random-voter analysis already studies. Its weakest point is also clear: the theorem’s limit varies the number of issues and parties, not the electorate size, and the mass formulation does not turn the paper’s result into a computational-complexity result. So this is a credible continuous-population reading of the paper’s turnout result, but a weak foundation for ChoCo’s central complexity claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only anchor raised, and it is a turnout limit, not a computational result: it gives no complexity classification or algorithm. The strongest version of its proposed mirror is still easy to state. For fixed party positions \(V\), let \(q_R(w;V)\) indicate whether type \(w\) prefers some non-null ballot under rule \(R\), and let
\[ P_R(\mu,V)=\sum_w \mu_w q_R(w;V) \]
be the participating share. A large electorate with repeated ideal points is a plausible regime, and this quantity is recognizably about the paper’s turnout question.

The problem is that the population does no work in this model. Each type chooses independently; there is no constraint linking types, no population-level intervention, and no election outcome whose calculation depends on how their ballots combine. Given the per-type choices, \(\mu\) only supplies weights for the sum. Any computational difficulty in finding the best approval ballot is already present for a single voter. Asking for \(P_R\) over a support list batches those same individual calculations; it does not expose a new population-level computational structure of the kind the programme studies. Adding a decision to alter party positions, campaign to types, or change the electorate would create a different problem, rather than continue the paper’s question.

For the uniform distribution over \(\{-1,1\}^n\), even the aggregate reformulation adds no question to Theorem 2: the paper’s probability for a random voter is the expected participating share over random party positions. The proposed concentration inference is valid, but it converts that probability statement into mass language; it does not supply a computational question. An arbitrary sparse \(\mu\) would be a genuine extension, but the theorem does not anchor its computational interest. Theorem 1 does not repair this: with positive mass on every type, “everyone votes” is the same coverage condition as before; replacing it with an almost-everywhere condition changes the question.

So the negative case is strong against this being a ChoCo complexity mirror, but it cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The population interpretation is coherent and the turnout mass is meaningful. The case is weaker precisely there: someone could study that aggregate as a new computational problem. What the paper does not provide is a computational result for that mirror, or a population-dependent decision structure that makes it a compelling continuization of the paper’s own problem.
