The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper. Theorem 3.1, proved here, says the Felsenthal Owen index is the unique coalitional extension of the Felsenthal index satisfying non-negativity, the quotient-game property, and PELS. Theorem 3.3, also proved here, uniquely characterizes it through efficiency, the null-player and symmetry properties, two transfer properties, IIC, and ILSE. Those are axiomatic characterizations, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Section 4 reports calculations for the IMF, but gives no numbered computational result of that kind. I would not present either theorem as a computational anchor.

The best positive lead I can make is therefore a precise follow-on to the paper’s computation of the Felsenthal Owen index, rather than a mirror anchored in one of its named results. Call it the High-Multiplicity Felsenthal Owen Power Limit. An instance consists of a finite set of member types \(T\), a finite set of a priori unions \(K\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a positive rational voting weight \(w_t\) for each type, its union \(k(t)\), and a rational quota \(\alpha\). A type records the attributes relevant to the game: here, voting weight and union. For each sufficiently divisible replication factor \(r\), form a finite game with \(r\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), in the same union, and quota \(\alpha r\sum_t\mu_t w_t\).

Let \(\Psi_i^{(r)}\) be member \(i\)’s Felsenthal Owen index in that finite game. The requested output is the vector
\[ \Phi_t=\lim_{r\to\infty}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}\Psi_i^{(r)} \]
for all \(t\), if the limits exist; otherwise, determine that they do not. The entries are aggregate type power and sum to \(1\). The coalition variable behind the index is the selected mass \(x_t\) from each type, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\); within a union, least-size winning coalitions correspond to minimizing \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to the relevant quota being met. The finite index’s equal weighting of distinct least-size coalitions must be respected in taking the limit; it cannot simply be replaced by a uniform distribution over continuous coalitions.

A plausible regime is a large member-based organization with stable constituencies or chapters, many members, and relatively few combinations of voting weight and union membership. Then the population is large compared with the support size \(\tau\), while the paper’s two-level coalition structure and its notion of power remain intact. The IMF data in the paper do not themselves exhibit this regime particularly well: 188 countries across 25 constituencies are not a large population with many repeated member types. But the authors could plausibly recognize the proposed limit as asking how their index behaves for large weighted voting games with a priori unions.

My tentative expectation is tractability when the number of unions and \(\tau\) are moderate: least-size coalition selection has a linear optimization structure, while the limiting frequencies among equally weighted finite coalitions may require an additional entropy calculation. The resulting questions include whether the limit always exists, whether it is independent of the replication subsequence and quota rounding, and how quickly finite indices approach it. These are proposals, not results of the paper.

The weak point is decisive for the requested anchoring standard: this is a reasonable continuous-population research question, but the paper has no named computational result for it to mirror. Its finite index averages over discrete coalitions, and deriving the limiting type-level index adds a new asymptotic problem. That makes the lead plausible as a follow-on, but it cannot honestly be claimed as a continuous mirror of a numbered computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 3.3 are axiomatic uniqueness characterizations. Section 4 reports finite calculations. None states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result, so there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous mirror. The proponent is right about that.

The proposed limit is the strongest follow-on I can see: replicate rational type masses, compute each finite game’s Felsenthal Owen index, and take aggregate power by type. It is coherent, and the uniform lottery over least-size coalitions gives a natural way to define the limit through finite replications. But that asks a new asymptotic question—about existence, convergence, and the limiting distribution induced by coalition counts. It does not continue a computational result established in this paper. The paper defines an index and characterizes it; it does not claim a complexity bottleneck that this limit would resolve.

There is a further gap between that proposal and the paper’s general model. The paper allows arbitrary simple games, whose winning coalitions need not be determined by a finite list of voter types. Restricting the limit to weighted voting games supplies that missing structure, but makes the project a follow-on about a narrower model rather than a mirror of the paper’s results.

That is a strong case against calling this paper a continuous-computational mirror. It is not proof that the proposed limit would be worthless on its own: a large organization with repeated member types could make it a sensible, perhaps useful research question. The honest negative case is strongest on the anchoring criterion; it is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise from the paper.
