The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_a,\mu_b\) with \(\mu_a+\mu_b=1\), two complete additive valuation types over a finite good catalogue \(G\), and \(\tfrac12\) copies of every good per unit population, compute a polynomial-size mass allocation \(\rho_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_B\rho_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{B\ni g}\rho_{t,B}=\mu_t/2\), and \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(B'\setminus\{g\})\) for some \(g\in B'\), for every type \(t\) and supported bundles \(B,B'\).

The model it lives in

Two complete valuation types carry masses \(\mu_t\); each good has per-capita indivisible-copy supply \(\tfrac12\); decision variables \(\rho_{t,B}\) assign type mass to integral bundles; the objective is polynomial-time construction of equal-division marginals whose supported bundles are EF1.

The objection that survived

In a literal nonatomic model, a measure-zero agent's report cannot affect the mass allocation, so truthfulness is vacuous; a finite-replica lift must also specify how arbitrary off-type deviations are represented.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the polynomial-time two-agent EF1 mechanism and its mass decomposition, with a structural mass analogue of Theorem 3.3; it does not cover the general-agent mechanisms, EFX-style impossibilities, or Pareto-optimality results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a finite-replica implementation preserve dominant-strategy truthfulness against arbitrary off-type reports while retaining a polynomial description in the number of types and valuation bit length?
  • For more than two valuation types, characterize when equal-division mass marginals admit an EF1-supported decomposition.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s two-agent mechanism, with a useful three-type boundary. This paper contains no NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity theorem; its named computational anchors are mechanism-existence, impossibility, and polynomial-time construction results.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper. It states that for \(n=2\), there exists a truthful randomized mechanism \((F_=,D)\) whose every realized allocation is EF1, and that the distribution of EF1 allocations can be produced in polynomial time. The equal-division rule \(F_=\) gives each agent probability \(1/2\) of every item; Proposition 3.2, originally due to Kyropoulou et al. and reproved here, supplies the polynomial-time decomposition into two complementary EF1 allocations.

Here is a genuine continuous-population version.

Take a continuum of households of total mass \(1\), with two complete valuation types \(T=\{a,b\}\), occurring with masses \(\mu_a,\mu_b\), where \(\mu_a+\mu_b=1\). Each type has an additive valuation \(v_t(g)\) over a finite catalogue \(G\) of indivisible goods. There are \(1/2\) copies of every good per unit population. Thus, in a finite realization with \(N\) households, there are \(N/2\) copies of each good: goods remain indivisible for every household, while their aggregate supply scales with population.

A solution is a mass allocation \(\rho_{t,B}\), where \(\rho_{t,B}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) households receiving the integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}\rho_{t,B}=\mu_t, \]

\[ \sum_{B\ni g}\rho_{t,B}=\frac{\mu_t}{2} \qquad\text{for every }t\in\{a,b\},\ g\in G, \]

and every bundle in the support must be EF1 relative to every other supported bundle, for both valuation types:

\[ \forall t,\ B,B'\in\operatorname{supp}(\rho),\quad \exists g\in B'\ \text{such that}\ v_t(B)\ge v_t(B'\setminus\{g\}). \]

The objective is feasibility and construction: find such a \(\rho\), with a finite polynomial-size description, or certify that none exists. This is not fractional allocation of goods. Each household receives an integral bundle; only the population mass assigned to each bundle is fractional.

Theorem 3.1 gives a direct solution. Proposition 3.2 produces a partition \(G=X\mathbin{\dot\cup}Y\) such that both \((X,Y)\) and \((Y,X)\) are EF1 for both valuation types. Set

\[ \rho_{a,X}=\rho_{a,Y}=\frac{\mu_a}{2}, \qquad \rho_{b,X}=\rho_{b,Y}=\frac{\mu_b}{2}. \]

The equal-division marginal is truthful because every household receives expected share \(1/2\) of every good, so its expected utility is always

\[ \frac12\sum_{g\in G}v_t(g), \]

independently of its report. The partition can be found in polynomial time by the same \(2\)-regular bipartite-graph edge-colouring argument used in the paper.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large population of households belongs to two stable preference cohorts, while the number of valuation types remains constant and the number of households is much larger than the catalogue size. The authors should recognise it as their mechanism with anonymous replicas of the two agent roles. The only co-modification is the necessary per-capita scaling of item copies; the population, not the goods, is the continuous object.

The paper’s Theorem 3.3 gives a second, complementary mirror. It is proved in the paper and states that for three agents there is a valuation profile for which the equal-division fractional allocation is not EF1-realizable. The profile has four goods and valuations

\[ \begin{array}{c|cccc} & g_1&g_2&g_3&g_4\\ \hline v_1&2&1&4&4\\ v_2&1&2&4&4\\ v_3&4&4&2&1 \end{array} \]

and no lottery over EF1 integral allocations has marginal probability \(1/3\) of assigning every good to every agent.

Its continuous mirror is the same mass-allocation problem with three valuation types of masses \(1/3\), per-capita supply \(1/3\) of every good, and equal-division marginals

\[ \sum_{B\ni g}\rho_{t,B}=\frac{1}{9} \]

for every type \(t\) and good \(g\). Require, again, that every pair of supported integral bundles be EF1 for every type. The answer is no. If such a \(\rho\) existed, independently sampling one supported bundle for each of the three types would produce a lottery over three-agent allocations with equal-division marginals, and every sampled allocation would be EF1. That contradicts Theorem 3.3.

So the first mirror is a Class A-style tractable continuous problem: two valuation types, equal-division truthfulness, and EF1 mass decomposition are solvable by a polynomial-time construction. The second is a transferred obstruction: the failure already occurs with \(\tau=3\) types and four goods, so population multiplicity does not remove it. The combinatorics lives in the type profile, not in the number of named households.

This case deliberately covers only Theorems 3.1 and 3.3, not the paper’s general \(n\)-agent mechanisms, tri-valued Pareto impossibility, or the \(EFX\)-style impossibility results. The main weakness is that the lead theorem is originally stated for two agents, whereas the mirror has arbitrarily many households. The defence is that the households are high-multiplicity replicas of two complete types, and all fairness constraints remain pointwise at the individual-bundle level. A related semantic issue is that unilateral deviations by a measure-zero agent can become vacuous in a literal continuum model; the clean interpretation is therefore the finite-replica/high-multiplicity one, with the mass allocation as its normalized limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest against the proposed mirrors’ claim to preserve the paper’s computational content. It is not strongest against high multiplicity in fair division itself.

For Theorem 3.1, the proposed \(\rho\) is essentially the paper’s lottery \(D\) rewritten as population mass. The paper produces two complementary allocations \(X,Y\), each with probability \(1/2\). Writing

\[ \rho_{t,X}=\rho_{t,Y}=\frac{\mu_t}{2} \]

merely tensors that lottery with the type mass \(\mu_t\). The algorithm still takes exactly two valuation vectors and constructs exactly the same partition \(X\mathbin{\dot\cup}Y\). The population size, and even the values of \(\mu_a,\mu_b\), do no computational work.

More seriously, the proposed continuum loses the strategic content of truthfulness. An individual has measure zero, so changing her report cannot affect \(\mu\) or the mass allocation. Truthfulness is then vacuous. If instead an entire positive-mass type is allowed to misreport, that is a coalitional deviation, not the dominant-strategy condition in the paper. A finite-replica interpretation restores individual deviations, but then a single deviator creates an exceptional valuation type that the two-type summary no longer represents. Restricting reports to the two preselected types avoids this problem only by changing the valuation domain.

Scaling goods per capita does not repair this mismatch. It gives a sensible replicated economy, but the equal-division lottery can then be implemented by deterministically assigning half the replicas \(X\) and half \(Y\). That is a valid high-multiplicity allocation, but it is not the paper’s question about whether randomization rescues individual truthfulness for two agents. Keeping a fixed number of goods instead makes the goods’ mass vanish as the population grows, so the fairness problem degenerates. Thus the anchor faces a structural choice: preserve the strategic problem and lose the continuum summary, or preserve the mass model and lose the paper’s strategic problem.

Theorem 3.3 is not a clean rescue. The proponent’s argument treats the quantities \(\rho_{t,B}\) as though bundles assigned to different types could be sampled independently. They cannot: \(\rho\) records only type-level marginals, not the joint allocation or correlation between cohorts. A genuine population allocation needs a coupling over complete allocations.

If that coupling is represented by

\[ \pi(B_1,B_2,B_3), \]

with each marginal having equal-division item probabilities and every supported triple being EF1, then Theorem 3.3 transfers immediately. But that is exactly the paper’s original statement: \(\pi\) is a lottery over three-agent allocations. The population has disappeared from the mathematics. With many replicas, sampling one agent from each positive-mass type again induces precisely such a three-agent lottery, provided fairness is ex post and individual rather than merely type-average. The obstruction is therefore a finite representative obstruction, not a computational phenomenon caused by a continuous society.

The alternative is to use only the marginal \(\rho\), permit correlations that are not represented, or replace individual EF1 by an average or type-level condition. The first is not a well-defined ex-post allocation model; the latter changes the paper’s fairness notion and moves toward outcome/probability continuization rather than population continuization.

So the two anchors do not establish a worthwhile mirror as stated. The first is a lottery encoded as mass and makes truthfulness vacuous; the second either collapses to the paper’s finite convex-hull obstruction or requires a different fairness model. I would not claim the universal negative case is airtight: a genuinely new problem with variable \(m\), variable \(\tau\), rational type masses, and a joint high-multiplicity allocation polytope could be worthwhile. But that would be a new fair-division programme, not a continuous mirror supplied by Theorems 3.1 and 3.3.
