The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is the paper’s multi-unit extension, but it does not meet the required anchor test. Theorem 4—proved in this paper—characterizes the mechanisms for \(m\) homogeneous indivisible objects: a mechanism is envy-free, budget balanced, Bayesian incentive compatible, and order additive if and only if it allocates the objects to the \(m\) highest-valued agents and uses the stated double-cumulation transfers. Winners pay \(-\frac{n-m}{n}\bigl(v_m-\int_{v_{m+1}}^{v_m}F(w)\,dw\bigr)\); non-winners receive \(\frac{m}{n}\bigl(v_m-\int_{v_{m+1}}^{v_m}F(w)\,dw\bigr)\). Theorem 3 proves the corresponding characterization for one object; Theorems 1 and 2 establish its necessity and sufficiency. These results are proved here. None asserts a complexity classification or an algorithm with a stated running time. Propositions 1 and 2 are structural characterizations, relying on cited work, not computational results either.

There is a plausible population limit behind Theorem 4. Consider a large market with finitely many valuation types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and a supply of \(\rho\) indivisible objects per person, for rational \(0<\rho<1\). Agents of a type share the same valuation and other relevant characteristics. A mechanism assigns each individual either zero or one object, assigns total mass \(\rho\), and makes transfers summing to zero. It must be envy-free, incentive compatible for an individual whose report has zero effect on the aggregate distribution, and anonymous. At a cutoff valuation \(q\), the mechanism can split the mass of that type to meet supply exactly. Assign recipients the transfer \(-(1-\rho)q\) and non-recipients \(\rho q\). The transfers balance, and both groups are indifferent at \(q\); agents above or below the cutoff prefer their designated bundle.

That is a precise, computable candidate problem: given \((T,\mu,\rho)\), find and output a cutoff allocation and transfer rule satisfying those conditions. Sorting the support and accumulating its rational masses finds the cutoff; the expected direction is tractable. It is a natural limit of Theorem 4: when \(m/n\to\rho\) and the relevant order statistics approach \(q\), its winner and non-winner transfers approach the two amounts above. The regime is credible for a large allocation market with repeated valuation categories and supply scaling with population. Theorem 4’s existing multi-unit result makes that scenario more recognizable to the authors than a continuum built from the one-object case.

The weakness is decisive under your instructions: this candidate is an exploratory extension, not a mirror anchored to a named computational result in the paper. Also, the paper’s distribution \(F\) describes uncertainty about the valuations of finitely many named agents; it does not make the society itself continuous. The proposed limit changes that population model and its incentive condition. So the honest positive case is that Theorem 4 motivates a plausible, tractable continuous-population problem—not that the paper supplies a qualifying computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent already concedes: Theorem 4 is not a computational result. It characterizes mechanisms for a finite set of agents with i.i.d. private valuations drawn from \(F\). It gives no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity claim. Theorem 3 makes the same kind of characterization for one object; Theorems 1 and 2 establish its necessity and sufficiency. Propositions 1 and 2 describe envy-free, budget-balanced allocations, but do not supply computational results either. There is therefore no named computational result here for ChoCo to mirror.

The multi-unit limit is nevertheless a coherent population model. If \(m/n\to\rho\), a plausible limit has a cutoff \(q\) with \(F(q)=1-\rho\): allocate to the top \(\rho\) mass, split any mass at the cutoff, and give winners transfers \(-(1-\rho)q\) and non-winners \(\rho q\). This respects the proposed co-modification of supply, and the finite transfers in Theorem 4 converge to these amounts when the adjacent order statistics approach \(q\). I would not dismiss this mirror as incoherent or claim that the limit is invalid.

But its natural question is a limit characterization of a mechanism, not a computational question from the paper. If the task is to construct that cutoff mechanism from a finite rational support, sorting and accumulating the masses gives it directly. That may be a correct algorithm, but it is a newly posed construction task with no complexity problem or computational bottleneck in the paper to anchor it. To get a harder algorithmic problem, one would need to add an objective, constraints, or costs that Theorem 4 does not study. The paper’s open question about mechanisms outside the order-additive class is a possible research direction, but it too is a mechanism-characterization question unless given a separate computational formulation.

The single-object theorem is a weaker route: keeping one object as \(n\) grows makes supply per person vanish. The multi-unit extension repairs that issue, so it is the strongest anchor and still fails the computational test. The negative case is strong against the proposed *paper-anchored* mirrors, but it does not prove that no useful continuous mechanism-design question could ever be developed from the paper.
