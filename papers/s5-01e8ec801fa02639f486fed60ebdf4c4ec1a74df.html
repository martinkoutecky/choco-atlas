The continuous mirror question

Given \(C\), a target \(p\in C\), a rational coalition mass \(\alpha\), a distribution \(\nu\) of total mass \(1-\alpha\) over the \(\tau\) supported rankings of the nonmanipulators, and a choice of whether revoting is allowed, does there exist an allocation \(x\) of coalition mass \(\alpha\) over rankings of \(C\) that makes \(p\) an overall Veto winner? Veto winners are the candidates with minimum veto mass; if the first-round winner set \(W\) has more than one candidate, restrict nonmanipulator rankings to \(W\) and run Veto again. Without revoting, restrict the coalition’s rankings from \(x\) to \(W\); with revoting, allow a new allocation \(y\) of mass \(\alpha\) over rankings of \(W\). The instance masses are binary rationals; the question is feasibility.

The model it lives in

Represent the population as fixed nonmanipulator mass \(\nu\) over complete ranking types plus a coordinated coalition of mass \(\alpha\). The decision variables are the coalition’s ballot-mass distribution \(x\), and, when revoting is allowed, its runoff distribution \(y\); there is no cost objective. The first-round Veto winner set is determined by the combined veto masses, and the runoff uses rankings restricted to that set.

The objection that survived

For fixed \(m\), enumerating possible first-round winner sets and solving the corresponding linear programs is polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), so any hardness must use growing \(m\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.20’s Veto-CUCM-runoff and Veto-CUCM-revoting hardness results. It leaves ordinary Veto-CUCM and the paper’s other voting rules and realizability results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For each mode, what is the complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\); can the search over first-round winner sets be avoided?
  • Do the revoting and no-revoting continuous variants have different complexity?
  • When does continuous feasibility imply discrete feasibility for a finite coalition, with a bounded increase in coalition size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror here is the Veto manipulation result, because the paper studies exactly the two-stage decision that carries over to a society represented by mass: can a coordinated group make \(p\) win, given that tied first-round winners advance under the same rule?

The anchor is Theorem 4.20, proved in this paper. It states that both Veto-CUCM-runoﬀ and Veto-CUCM-revoting are NP-complete. The first asks whether an unweighted coalition can cast ballots that make \(p\) win, with any tied Veto winners advancing to a Veto runoff on the same ballots restricted to those candidates. The second asks the same question when the manipulators may change their ballots in the runoff. The theorem’s proof reduces from Exact Cover by 3-Sets. The paper contrasts these results with ordinary Veto-CUCM, which is in P.

A natural regime is a large membership vote over competing candidates or proposals. Most members’ complete rankings fall into a modest number of recurring ballot types, while a coordinated bloc has a shared goal and the ability to choose its ballots. The population may be large relative to its number \(\tau\) of distinct nonmanipulator rankings. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that every election has a useful continuum limit. It also preserves the paper’s central assumptions: ballots are anonymous, the coalition seeks one designated winner, and it knows the other ballots.

I would give a prover the following problem, which covers both parts of Theorem 4.20:

Continuous Veto Same-System Runoff Manipulation. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a target \(p\in C\), a rational mass \(\alpha\) of manipulators, and a distribution \(\nu\) over complete rankings of \(C\) for the nonmanipulators, with total mass \(1-\alpha\). The distribution is listed by its \(\tau\) positive-mass rankings and binary rational masses. The instance also specifies whether manipulators may revote.

A solution in either mode assigns the manipulator mass across rankings: a distribution \(x\) over rankings of \(C\), with total mass \(\alpha\). In the revoting mode, it also assigns a distribution \(y\) of that mass over rankings of the first-round winners if a runoff occurs. The question is whether some such assignment makes \(p\) an overall winner. In the no-revote mode the same \(x\) is restricted to the runoff candidates. In both modes, nonmanipulator ballots are restricted to the candidates who advance, and the runoff uses Veto again. “Winner” retains the paper’s co-winner meaning: tied candidates are winners.

Equivalently, Veto selects the candidates with minimum veto mass. If \(W\) is the first-round minimum-veto set and has more than one member, those candidates advance; the chosen distributions must then make \(p\) a minimum-veto candidate in the runoff. The solution is a ballot-mass distribution, not a cost-minimizing transfer: the underlying paper problem is a feasibility question. The main size parameters are \(m=|C|\), \(\tau\), and the bit length \(L\); the manipulator distribution need not be restricted to the nonmanipulator support.

This is a direct population mirror. A discrete instance with \(N\) voters and \(k\) manipulators maps to \(\alpha=k/N\), with each nonmanipulator ballot mass equal to its count divided by \(N\). Discrete successful manipulations therefore give continuous ones. The reverse implication is not automatic: the continuous coalition can split its mass among rankings in fractions that no fixed finite coalition can realize. That is precisely the computational question worth asking.

I would expect hardness, if it persists, to be continuum-specific, rather than transferred from Theorem 4.20. The theorem’s X3C construction relies on discrete manipulators making indivisible ballot choices. A divisible coalition may split mass and destroy that reduction’s exact-cover constraint. But there is still a discrete choice in the continuous problem: which candidates tie for the first-round minimum and enter the runoff. For any fixed first-round winner set, the mass-allocation question has a linear formulation; choosing that set as part of the solution may retain hard combinatorics. My provisional prediction is therefore Class C for both the no-revote and revoting versions, based on the winner-set choice—not on Theorem 4.20’s hardness carrying over. An algorithm exploiting that structure could overturn the prediction.

The follow-on questions are concrete: classify the two modes as \(m,\tau,L\) grow; determine whether fixing \(m\) leaves a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau,L\); and establish when continuous feasibility implies discrete feasibility with a controlled increase in coalition size. The revoting mode also asks whether the extra freedom changes the complexity, as it does in some of the paper’s synthetic systems.

My weakest point is that Theorem 4.20 motivates the mirror but does not establish hardness for it. Fractional mass can erase the reduction’s key constraint, and the paper’s construction does not establish hardness in a regime with many voters but fixed or small \(\tau\). Still, the mirror preserves the authors’ actual manipulation question—making a target win through a same-system runoff—and asks what changes when a large, homogeneous coalition is represented by mass. That seems like a question the authors could recognize as theirs. It covers only Veto-CUCM-runoff and its revoting companion; it makes no claim about the paper’s other voting rules or realizability constructions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that Theorem 4.20’s NP-hardness gives little evidence about the continuous problem. Its X3C reduction relies on indivisible manipulators: each one vetoes a particular set candidate, and the construction forces those choices to form an exact cover. Divisible mass can split among candidates, replacing exact cover with fractional covering constraints. The paper proves no hardness result that survives that change. So the proponent’s Class C prediction is conjecture, not a consequence of the anchor.

For the best continuous formulation, though, that weakness does not defeat the mirror. Let \(W\) be the first-round winner set containing \(p\), and let \(q_c\) be the nonmanipulators’ first-round veto mass for candidate \(c\). For each fixed \(W\), the manipulator mass needed to make exactly \(W\) tie for the minimum veto score is described by linear constraints. In the runoff, the nonmanipulator veto mass for \(c\in W\) is computable from the \(\tau\) rankings by restricting each ranking to \(W\). With revoting, the manipulator mass can be assigned freely among runoff veto choices. Without revoting, it can be represented by at most \(m^2\) variables recording each ballot’s initial veto choice and its veto choice after restriction to \(W\). Each fixed-\(W\) feasibility test is therefore an explicit linear program. Enumerating \(W\) gives a \(2^{m-1}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) algorithm for either variant.

That structure makes the proposed hardness prediction especially uncertain: the exact-cover gadget may vanish, and the remaining search is over candidate sets. It also gives polynomial time for fixed \(m\). But this is only evidence against the proponent’s predicted classification. An exponential dependence on \(m\), or a better way to select \(W\), is still a computational question about the continuous-population version. The programme explicitly treats the alternative count as a parameter and welcomes continuum-specific hardness; that search cannot itself disqualify the mirror.

The other grounds against the anchor are weaker. The paper has a named computational result, and its anonymous-ballot model does not track who receives what. A large membership vote with recurring rankings and a coordinated bloc is at least a coherent high-multiplicity regime. Exact ties may be exceptional in some elections, but the mirror asks a worst-case feasibility question, and tied co-winners are central to the paper’s own model. Retaining discrete manipulators to preserve the reduction would make a hybrid problem, not a better continuous-population mirror. Existing analytic work on continuum electorates would not answer this computational question.

So the honest negative case is weak. Theorem 4.20 does not support the suggested Class C outcome, and the Veto structure offers a useful fixed-\(m\) algorithm. But I cannot show that the authors’ underlying manipulation question ceases to be meaningful when the population and coalition become masses. The continuous mirror survives; what fails is the proponent’s confidence about where its hardness would come from.
