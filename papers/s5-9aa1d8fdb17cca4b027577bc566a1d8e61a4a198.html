The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes the equity concepts, Proposition 2 gives conditions that implement them, Proposition 3 proves an incompatibility, and Proposition 4 characterizes two first-best cases. None states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result.

Proposition 3 is the closest anchor the other analyst identified, but its content is structural: PE and RE together would require the population sum of selected council members to be nonrandom, contrary to the paper’s assumptions. Replacing the finite list of countries with a distribution over types might produce a continuous version of that characterization. It would not turn the proposition into a computational question.

The strongest rescue is the second-best design problem suggested in the conclusion. One could give a mass distribution over complete polity types and optimize allocation probabilities and voting weights to reduce deviation from the equity benchmarks. That might be a worthwhile new project, and the authors might recognize it as an extension. But the paper does not specify the design problem’s realpolitik constraints or ask for an optimization algorithm; those would have to be added. A continuous population would make the new problem’s input more scalable, but would not make it a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The Banzhaf calculations and simulation in Section 3 do not supply another anchor. They use probability over council allocations and repeated finite elections; those are not a continuous population. The citizens’ populations enter as country-level parameters, while countries remain the council’s decision-making units.

So the negative case is strong on the paper’s actual results: none survives as an anchor for a continuous computational mirror. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise in any related scenario is harder to establish. A high-multiplicity council-design problem could be worthwhile on its own; I would classify it as a follow-on, not a mirror of this paper.
