The continuous mirror question

Given per-dollar subprojects \(D\), budget \(B\), feasible ground-truth sets \(g\subseteq D\) with \(|g|=B\), and report types \(r\subseteq D\) with \(|r|\le B\) occurring with rational masses \(\nu_r\), where \(P(r\mid g)\propto\exp(|g\cap r|)\), compute \(g^\star\in\arg\max_{|g|=B}\sum_r\nu_r\log P(r\mid g)\), using the paper's consistency and tie-breaking rules.

The model it lives in

A participatory-budgeting society is a finite-support distribution \(\nu\) over complete Knapsack report types. The decision variable is a feasible ground-truth allocation \(g\); the objective is expected log-likelihood, equivalently expected overlap \(\sum_r\nu_r|g\cap r|\), optimized by mass-weighted per-dollar scores and top-\(B\) selection.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the fractional Knapsack Voting aggregation, its overlap and welfare interpretation, and the Noisy Knapsack MLE theorem. It leaves individual strategy-proofness, partial strategy-proofness, integral implementation, value-for-money comparisons, coalition manipulation, and the empirical studies untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact MLE equivalence and polynomial-time algorithm be formalized for binary-encoded rational capacities without expanding capacities into individual dollar subprojects?
  • What concentration and rounding guarantees relate the finite empirical electorate to the continuous mass solution?
  • How should positive-mass coalition manipulation and robustness be defined when individual nonatomic strategy-proofness is vacuous?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a Class A mirror for the paper’s fractional Knapsack Voting result. The continuous object is the electorate, not merely the project allocations: the paper already allows fractional allocations, and I would retain that feature rather than mislabel it as the continuization.

The paper contains no named theorem giving an NP-hardness, P, FPT, or similar complexity classification. The honest anchors are therefore its named structural results, especially Theorem 2.2 and, secondarily, Theorem 2.5. Both are proved in this paper.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2.2: “The truthful dominant strategy equilibrium for Knapsack Voting is welfare-maximizing under \(\ell_1\) costs.” Its content is that when voters truthfully report their preferred budget allocations, the Knapsack Voting outcome maximizes aggregate welfare measured by the sum of voters’ \(\ell_1\) losses. The proof uses Lemma 2.1, also proved here, which identifies overlap welfare with \(\ell_1\) welfare when all allocations spend the same budget.

The corresponding problem is:

Continuum Knapsack-Welfare. An instance consists of projects \(P=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), capacities \(c_p\), budget \(B\), a finite set of voter types \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Type \(t\) has ideal allocation \(a_t=(a_{t,p})_{p\in P}\), with \(0\leq a_{t,p}\leq c_p\) and \(\sum_p a_{t,p}=B\). Find a feasible outcome \(w\), with \(0\leq w_p\leq c_p\) and \(\sum_p w_p=B\), maximizing \(W_\mu(w)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{p\in P}\min\{a_{t,p},w_p\}\). Equivalently, find \(w\) minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\lVert a_t-w\rVert_1\).

A solution is the exact allocation \(w^\star\), with the paper’s deterministic tie-breaking rule if there are several optima.

This is genuinely the high-multiplicity version of their problem. A finite election with \(N\) voters and \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) induces \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and the continuous objective is exactly the discrete welfare divided by \(N\). Conversely, every rational \(\mu\) can be expanded into a finite electorate. No preference information has been discarded: a type is a complete ideal allocation, and its mass is the fraction of residents with that allocation.

The problem is tractable. It has the LP formulation with variables \(w_p\) and \(z_{t,p}\): maximize \(\sum_{t,p}\mu_tz_{t,p}\), subject to \(z_{t,p}\leq w_p\), \(z_{t,p}\leq a_{t,p}\), \(0\leq w_p\leq c_p\), and \(\sum_pw_p=B\). Thus the mirror is solvable in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input precision. In the paper’s per-dollar representation, one instead aggregates the masses into scores \(q_j=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf 1[j\in S_t]\) and selects the \(B\) highest-scoring dollar subprojects. Consistency of the types makes the resulting allocation consistent.

The relevant regime is a city-wide participatory-budgeting election with many residents but relatively few recurring preference profiles: for example, neighborhood, school, age, or community cohorts whose members have the same ideal allocation. One might have \(N\) in the tens or hundreds of thousands, \(m\) in the tens, and \(\tau\) in the hundreds or low thousands. This is not artificial relative to the paper: its own motivating example explicitly discusses “one voter (or a homogeneous pool of voters).” The shared public budget and project costs are common to everyone; the type is the ideal allocation, which is exactly what determines \(\ell_1\) and overlap utility.

A secondary anchor is Theorem 2.5: “The Knapsack rule returns the maximum likelihood estimate under the Noisy Knapsack Vote Model.” The paper’s model has a ground-truth allocation \(S^\star\) of size \(B\), and each reported vote \(S_i\) is drawn independently with probability proportional to \(\exp(|S^\star\cap S_i|)\) for \(|S_i|\leq B\), and probability zero otherwise. The theorem proves that the maximum-likelihood ground truth is the size-\(B\) set maximizing total overlap with the observed votes, which is precisely what Knapsack Voting computes.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuum Noisy-Knapsack MLE. The observed electorate is a distribution \(\nu\) over report types \(r\). For each feasible ground truth \(g\) of size \(B\), define \(P(r\mid g)\) by the paper’s Noisy Knapsack Vote Model. Find \(g^\star\) maximizing \(\sum_r\nu_r\log P(r\mid g)\). A solution is the exact maximum-likelihood ground truth, again with fixed tie-breaking.

For rational \(\nu\), this is exactly the normalized finite-sample likelihood problem. The normalizing constant is independent of the ground truth in the paper’s model, so the objective reduces to expected overlap, and the same aggregate-score algorithm solves it. This is also Class A. It is a secondary anchor rather than an independent hardness frontier, since computationally it coincides with the welfare problem.

The authors should recognize both mirrors as their problem: the projects, budget constraint, Knapsack aggregation, overlap utility, \(\ell_1\) interpretation, and likelihood model are unchanged. Only the electorate is represented by masses. The fractional outcome is not being introduced as the supposed novelty; it is retained from their own model so that the population continuization can be stated exactly.

The main limitation is important. The paper’s strategy-proofness result does not survive in a substantively interesting form for an entirely nonatomic population: one individual has zero mass and cannot affect the outcome, so individual truthfulness becomes vacuous. I would therefore not claim a meaningful continuous analogue of Theorem 2.1’s strategic content. The positive case rests on the welfare and maximum-likelihood problems, not on that part of the theorem.

Further questions naturally arise: robustness of \(w^\star\) to small mass changes, approximation when the type distribution is sampled, positive-mass coalition manipulation, and integral implementation when projects cannot be fractionally funded. The mirror covers the paper’s fractional Knapsack Voting and noisy-MLE results, but not its empirical timing studies, value-for-money comparisons, or group-strategyproofness counterexample.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case begins with a basic mismatch: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 2.1–2.5 establish strategy-proofness, welfare characterizations, partial sincerity, and a maximum-likelihood interpretation. None states a complexity classification, an algorithmic running time, or even a computational problem whose input size is analysed. The proposed mirrors therefore introduce new optimization questions around the paper rather than continuize one of its computational results. Under ChoCo’s remit, that is a serious defect.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 2.2 is mathematically legitimate, but it does not repair the mismatch. The theorem’s key identity is already

\[ \sum_{v\in V}|S_v\cap S| = \sum_{j\in S}\#\{v:j\in S_v\}. \]

Replacing the finite count by a mass,

\[ q_j=\sum_t \mu_t\mathbf 1[j\in S_t], \]

and then selecting the \(B\) largest \(q_j\)'s is simply the same score-tallying argument with normalized weights. The proposed LP is a valid formulation, but it is a planner’s reformulation of the mechanism’s existing aggregation rule, not a computational result asserted by the paper. The population distribution is compressed immediately into the finite vector \(q\); the theorem contains no population-dependent computational obstacle for continuization to expose.

More importantly, the theorem’s strategic content has been removed. In a nonatomic society, an individual voter has mass zero and cannot change any \(q_j\), so truthfulness is weakly dominant only vacuously. If one instead gives a cohort positive mass and lets it deviate, the problem becomes coordinated coalition manipulation. That could be an interesting new problem, such as finding the minimum manipulating mass, but it is not a mirror of the paper’s individual strategy-proofness theorem. It requires new assumptions about whether a type may split, which agents coordinate, and what deviations cost. Thus the faithful continuum loses the substantive strategic claim; the non-vacuous version changes the problem.

The maximum-likelihood anchor is weaker still. The paper’s Noisy Knapsack theorem concerns a finite i.i.d. sample. Given an empirical distribution \(\nu\) of reported votes, the normalized log-likelihood is

\[ \sum_r \nu_r\log P(r\mid g) = \sum_r \nu_r |g\cap r|-\log Z = \sum_{j\in g}q_j-\log Z, \]

where \(Z\) is independent of \(g\) in the paper’s model. Hence the proposed “continuum MLE” is exactly the same weighted score tally as the welfare formulation. It is not a distinct computational question. Treating \(\nu\) as an unknown population law instead would lead to estimation and sampling questions, but those return to finite observations and go beyond the theorem’s MLE statement. Allowing an arbitrary continuous measure would additionally require an oracle or other representation for the input; finite support gives only the same weighted tally.

There is no valid objection that participatory-budgeting voters lack a high-multiplicity regime. A city-wide election with recurring neighbourhood or community preference profiles is entirely plausible, and the paper itself mentions homogeneous pools. Nor is fractional project allocation the problem: retaining the paper’s fractional outcome space is perfectly legitimate. The objection is narrower: after those choices, the only faithful mirrors are an elementary mass-weighted restatement of the mechanism and a population-limit restatement of its likelihood proof. Neither mirrors a computational result of the paper, and the strategic theorem—the paper’s most substantive named result—degenerates under a genuinely nonatomic population.

This negative case is not airtight. If ChoCo counts any new LP characterization of a named structural theorem as sufficient, then the proponent has a valid Class A mirror. But under the programme’s stated computational bar, I would reject this paper as a priority mirror: its high-multiplicity version is sensible, yet the paper supplies no computational complexity result whose continuous population version would create the intended research problem.
