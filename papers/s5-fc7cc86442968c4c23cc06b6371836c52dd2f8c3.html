The continuous mirror question

Given finite facilities \(J\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\), per-person allocation costs \(g_{tj}\), capacity shares \(q_j\), and opening costs \(F_j\), choose \(y_j\in\{0,1\}\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) minimizing \(\sum_j F_jy_j+\sum_{t,j}g_{tj}x_{tj}\), subject to \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{tj}\le q_jy_j\). Require no mass to prefer an open undersubscribed facility, and no nonzero improving assignment circulation \(z_{tjk}\ge0\) with \(\sum_k z_{tjk}\le x_{tj}\), supported only on destinations \(k\succ_t j\), and balanced inflow and outflow at each facility. Find an optimal stable pair \((y,x)\), or report infeasibility.

The model it lives in

A finite set of facilities and a finite support of applicant types, each type carrying mass, a strict ranking, and an allocation-cost vector. Facilities have open-or-closed decisions, per-capita capacities, and opening costs; assignments distribute each type's mass across facilities. Minimize opening and allocation costs subject to capacity and mass-circulation cyclic stability.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the reduced cyclic-coalition-stable CFLCP formulation, model (10), and its location and allocation objective. It leaves the customer-stable and pairwise-stable variants and the maximum Pareto-optimal CHA formulation aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(|J|\), does the discrete stable CFLCP embed into this mass model strongly enough to transfer hardness, or is there an efficient exact algorithm?
  • When rational type masses and capacities are scaled to finite cohorts, does the mass-circulation stability condition admit a stability-preserving rounding guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror in the paper’s cyclic-coalition-stable facility-location problem. Imagine a large regional school-admission round: the district chooses which candidate schools to open and assigns applicants, while each school has capacity proportional to the cohort. Applicants in the same type have the same strict school ranking and the same allocation-cost vector—for example, because they come from the same neighborhood and applicant category. The regime is one where the cohort size \(N\) is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of such types. Applicants must be numerous enough that mass shares are meaningful, and the stability assumption is most plausible if assignments can be reconsidered before they are finalized.

My anchor is Proposition 8, proved in this paper. It says that in the reduced cyclic-coalition-stable formulation \((\mathrm{CFLCP})^R_{\mathrm{CC}}\), the integrality constraints on the assignment variables \(x_{ij}\) can be relaxed while retaining the formulation’s intended solutions. With the facility-opening and fill-order variables fixed, the assignment constraints have a totally unimodular matrix; the additional constraints (10i) append rows with a single nonzero coefficient, preserving total unimodularity. The right-hand side is integral, so the assignment polytope has integral vertices. This is a formulation result, not a claim that the full facility-location problem is polynomial-time solvable: opening and fill-order variables remain binary. The paper gives no named theorem asserting a complexity class for this problem.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it CC–CFLCP\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite candidate set \(J\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Type \(t\) includes a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of the facilities and the per-person allocation costs \(g_{tj}\). Each facility \(j\) has capacity share \(q_j\) and opening cost \(F_j\), expressed in per-capita units. For an \(N\)-person version of the paper’s instance, these can be \(q_j=c_j/N\) and \(F_j=f_j/N\). A solution chooses open facilities \(y_j\in\{0,1\}\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), with
\[ \sum_{j\in J}x_{tj}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}x_{tj}\le q_j y_j. \]
It minimizes
\[ \sum_{j\in J}F_jy_j+\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in J}g_{tj}x_{tj}. \]
Here \(x_{tj}\) is the share of type \(t\) assigned to \(j\); it does not give an individual a fractional school.

The allocation must also be cyclic-coalition stable. There must be no positive mass assigned to a facility \(j\) that would prefer an open, undersubscribed facility \(k\): formally, no \(t,j,k\) with \(x_{tj}>0\), \(k\succ_t j\), \(y_k=1\), and \(\sum_s x_{sk}<q_k\). Nor may a positive mass coalition strictly improve by exchanging assignments while preserving the number of people at every facility. Precisely, rule out every nonzero collection \(z_{tjk}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_k z_{tjk}\le x_{tj}\), supported only on open destinations \(k\) preferred to \(j\) by type \(t\), and satisfying, for each facility \(j\),
\[ \sum_{t,k}z_{tjk}=\sum_{t,k}z_{tkj}. \]
Such a circulation decomposes into improving exchange cycles; a two-facility cycle captures a blocking pair. A solution is an optimal pair \((y,x)\) satisfying these constraints and stability conditions, or a report of infeasibility. Since the support and all input data are finite and rational, the question is concrete in \(m=|J|\), \(\tau\), and bit length.

Proposition 8 makes this mirror especially plausible: the paper already proves an exact fractional-assignment property inside its discrete cyclic-stability computation. But the proposed question is about optimizing over a continuous population, not about relaxing an encoding. It covers only the paper’s reduced cyclic-coalition-stable CFLCP, model (10); it does not claim to mirror the customer-stable or pairwise-stable variants, or the separate maximum Pareto-optimal CHA formulation.

My expectation for unrestricted \(m\) is hardness transfer, if the corresponding discrete problem is shown hard: the facility-opening decisions and stability structure remain, and masses \(1/N\) offer a route to embedding finite cohorts. Proposition 8 supports the possibility that fractional assignments need not help once the discrete facility and fill-order choices are fixed. But that route is not a proof of an embedding, and the paper supplies no named hardness result for this exact problem. With \(m\) fixed, enumerating facility and fill-order choices followed by assignment optimization suggests a tractable slice; whether that can be improved to polynomial time with variable \(m\) is a natural further question. Another is whether rounding type masses to an \(N\)-person cohort preserves stability and gives an additive cost guarantee.

The weakest point is the stability semantics. The paper defines exchanges among named, indivisible customers; the mirror treats type mass as divisible and interprets blocking coalitions as improving mass circulations. That seems faithful for a large cohort whose members are interchangeable within type, but Proposition 8 does not prove the continuous model equivalent to the discrete one or settle its complexity. Fixed opening costs also normalize to \(f_j/N\), so the mirror retains their intended trade-off by expressing costs per capita. These are real modeling choices for authors and a referee to assess, but they do not make the population-continuous question artificial.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against starts with the anchor: Proposition 8 is not a complexity result for CFLCP. It says that, once facility openings and fill order are fixed, the assignment variables in one formulation can be relaxed to continuous variables without losing integral solutions. That is a useful polyhedral fact about a subproblem, but it says nothing about the complexity of the full problem. The paper’s main contributions are stability definitions, formulations, and computational comparisons. So Proposition 8 is weak evidence that a population-continuous mirror would answer a question this paper itself established.

There is a stronger version of the proposed mirror: a large school cohort divided into types with identical rankings and allocation costs, per-capita capacities, and a cyclic-stability condition that rules out improving mass circulations. That is coherent. The paper’s definition of a blocking coalition naturally suggests the circulation condition, and treating a type as a complete description avoids losing relevant individual data. Scaling opening costs per capita also gives a consistent objective. Those choices answer the most obvious objections to the proponent’s formulation; I cannot honestly claim they make the mirror artificial.

The narrower skeptical point is that Proposition 8 does not establish that the aggregated mass model inherits its integrality property or any useful complexity result. Its proof concerns assignments of individually indexed customers after discrete facility and order decisions have been fixed. A continuous-population model would need its own analysis of stability and of the resulting optimization problem. The article offers no named complexity theorem to carry over, and its formulation results do not by themselves show that population multiplicity is the source of computational difficulty. That makes this a less direct ChoCo target than a paper whose results explicitly chart complexity across discrete and high-multiplicity regimes.

The maximum Pareto-optimal CHA formulation at the end presents the same difficulty for a negative case: a large cohort of applicants with shared preference types gives a sensible mass-matching question, but the paper states a formulation rather than a complexity result about it. Still, that does not defeat the model-level mirror. The honest negative conclusion is therefore limited: the proponent overstates what Proposition 8 anchors, but the broader school-admissions mirror remains plausible and worthwhile to investigate. I cannot support the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror.
