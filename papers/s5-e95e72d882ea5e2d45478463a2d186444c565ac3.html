The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(C\), a finite support \(S\subseteq\mathcal{L}(C)\) with rational masses \(\mu_V\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), an effective sample size \(\rho>0\), and fixed rational \(\varphi\in(0,1)\), define a posterior on ground-truth rankings by \(\pi_{\mu,\rho}(W)\propto\varphi^{\rho\sum_{V\in S}\mu_V\operatorname{Kendall}(V,W)}\). For each nonempty decision set \(D\subseteq C\), let \(R_{\mu,\rho}(D)=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{L}(C)}\pi_{\mu,\rho}(W)\left(1-\mathbf{1}[\operatorname{top}(W)\in D]/|D|\right)\). Determine a Bayes-risk-minimizing \(D\), or decide whether a given \(D\) is optimal and whether \(R_{\mu,\rho}(D_1)\le R_{\mu,\rho}(D_2)\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types, with effective sample size \(\rho\). The posterior over Mallows ground-truth rankings is proportional to \(\varphi^{\rho\sum_V\mu_V\operatorname{Kendall}(V,W)}\); choose a nonempty winner set minimizing the paper's posterior expected top-loss.

What the mirror covers

Covers only the Mallows model with uniform prior: Theorem 3's risk-comparison and optimal-decision hardness. It leaves the Condorcet-model algorithm, normative properties, and asymptotic results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the fractional-mass version admit useful algorithms or structural results beyond the hardness inherited from its integral-count subfamily?
  • Can hardness be shown when the ranking support size \(\tau\) is bounded or otherwise small relative to the number of alternatives?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is Mallows High-Multiplicity Bayesian Decision. Consider a crowdsourced ranking task on a fixed slate of alternatives: many people submit rankings, and the system must choose a set of winners. A voter’s type is their complete ranking \(V\). Two people share a type exactly when the model treats their reports identically. In the regime of interest, the number \(N\) of reports is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings observed.

Represent the society by its type distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_V\) is the fraction of reports of ranking \(V\), and include an effective sample size \(\rho\). The paper’s finite-profile likelihood has a natural extension to this representation: for a possible ground-truth ranking \(W\), assign weight \(\Lambda_{\mu,\rho}(W)=\varphi^{\rho\sum_V\mu_V\operatorname{Kendall}(V,W)}\). Normalize these weights to obtain the posterior under the uniform prior. When \(\rho\mu_V\) is an integer for every type, this is exactly the posterior from the finite profile containing \(\rho\mu_V\) copies of \(V\), since the Mallows normalization factor is common to all \(W\). Rational masses outside that subfamily give the corresponding fractional-count extension.

A decision is a nonempty set \(D\) of alternatives. Its loss at \(W\) is the paper’s loss, \(\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{c\in D}\mathbf{1}[c\ne\operatorname{top}(W)]\); its objective is to minimize posterior expected loss. The underlying optimization problem is to find a minimizing \(D\). Its decision versions ask either whether the risk of \(D_1\) is at most that of \(D_2\), or whether a given \(D\) is optimal. These are precise questions about the society distribution as input; they do not make the outcome space continuous.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. For every \(\varphi\), under the uniform prior for the Mallows model with fixed dispersion, both the better Bayesian decision problem—given two decisions and a profile, compare their Bayesian risks—and the optimal Bayesian decision problem—given a decision and a profile, ask whether it minimizes Bayesian risk—are \(\mathrm{P}_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{NP}}\)-hard. The paper proves this with a reduction from Kemeny winner, whose \(\mathrm{P}_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{NP}}\)-completeness it cites from Hemaspaandra, Spakowski, and Vogel (2005). I would hand a prover the distribution-input versions above, including both query forms, as the continuous mirror of this result.

I expect hardness to transfer. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the alternatives and the Kemeny ordering problem, rather than in a need to distinguish many individual voters. For a profile \(P\) with \(n_V\) occurrences of each ranking, set \(\mu_V=n_V/n\) and \(\rho=n\); this maps it directly to a distribution-input instance with the same Bayesian risks. The conversion is polynomial in the explicit profile size. The theorem therefore gives a hardness lower bound even on the integral-mass slice of the mirror. It does not show that every rational-mass extension has exactly the same complexity.

This is a plausible instance regime for the authors’ problem. Their paper already studies ranking aggregation in crowdsourcing and meta-search, where many reports can repeat and the alternatives can be a fixed, modest slate. The input distribution here is the realized composition of reports, not the Mallows model’s latent distribution over reports and not the posterior over the true ranking. That distinction matters: the paper’s model treats votes as i.i.d. samples, but its computational input is still a finite profile. Replacing that input by type masses asks what the same Bayesian decision problem means when the realized society is represented by its aggregate composition.

The mirror covers only the Mallows, uniform-prior, top-loss Bayesian decision problems in Theorem 3. It says nothing about the paper’s normative-property or asymptotic results, and does not try to continuize those questions. The paper’s polynomial-time result for the Condorcet model, Theorem 4, is useful context, but I would not make it a second anchor: the hardness theorem is the stronger basis for this case.

The weakest point is that the hard instances need not have small support \(\tau\), so the reduction itself may not demonstrate the practical compression benefit of a large society with few types. Also, fractional effective counts extend the paper’s finite-sample likelihood rather than reproducing an ordinary finite profile. The exact finite-population slice avoids that modeling leap and already supplies the hardness result; the fractional extension is a further question for the programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3 computes from a finite statistical sample, whose size carries information the proposed society distribution omits. If \(n_V\) reports have ranking \(V\), then the posterior weight of a ground truth \(W\) is proportional to
\[ \varphi^{\sum_V n_V\,\operatorname{Kendall}(V,W)}. \]
Writing \(\mu_V=n_V/n\) does not preserve that posterior by itself: profiles \(P\) and \(P^q\) have the same type distribution, but their likelihood weights are raised to different powers, so their Bayesian risks can differ.

Adding the effective sample size \(\rho\) repairs the definition. But when \(\rho\mu_V\) is integral, the resulting instance is exactly a finite profile encoded by its ranking counts. Theorem 3’s hardness then transfers because the original problem is embedded in the new encoding. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity formulation, but it gives no evidence that the theorem’s hard instances have small support \(\tau\), or that the continuous representation opens a new algorithmic route. The hardness lift may amount to re-encoding the same sample computation.

For nonintegral \(\rho\mu_V\), the proposed fractional exponent is a power-likelihood extension. It is mathematically coherent, but it is no longer the posterior of the paper’s stated i.i.d. sampling model. One could give it a population-level interpretation, but that interpretation and its connection to the authors’ decision problem would need to be established; Theorem 3 does not settle its complexity.

This is the best negative case I can make, but it does not defeat the anchor. Repeated rankings in crowdsourcing are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and an effective sample size is meaningful there. The paper explicitly treats crowdsourcing and studies computations on profiles, so the authors could reasonably recognize the count-based version as their problem. Since the programme treats high-multiplicity work as supporting evidence, the fact that the integral slice is an exact encoding is not enough to rule the mirror out. I do not see an honest basis for claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists here.
