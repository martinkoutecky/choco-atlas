The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of outcomes \(K\), role-specific type sets \(T_1,T_2\) with one side having two types, and rational masses \(\mu(t_1,t_2)\) on supported case types, determine whether nonnegative masses \(x^k(t_1,t_2)\) exist such that \(\sum_{k\in K}x^k(t_1,t_2)=\mu(t_1,t_2)\) for each supported pair and \(\sum_{t_{-i}}x^k(t_i,t_{-i})=\mu_i(t_i)Q_i^k(t_i)\) for each role, type, and outcome.

The model it lives in

A population of repeated two-person cases, grouped by their complete type profile \((t_1,t_2)\), with mass \(\mu(t_1,t_2)\). Each case receives one outcome in \(K\); \(x^k(t_1,t_2)\) is the mass of cases in that cell assigned outcome \(k\). The objective is feasibility of the requested role-and-type outcome frequencies \(Q_i^k(t_i)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's implementability criterion. It leaves Proposition 1's lattice-polyhedron structure and the paper's prospective optimization and incentive-compatibility questions outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What rounding or integrality guarantees connect the mass-feasibility model to finite cohorts in which each case receives one outcome?
  • Which incentive constraints or optimization objectives preserve the paper's flow structure for case cohorts?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is narrow: mirror Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It is an exact characterization of the underlying implementability decision problem, though the paper does not state a complexity-class result such as “in P.” I would not present it as a proved polynomial-time theorem.

Consider a large mediation service handling many standardized, separate two-person cases. Each participant’s type completely describes their relevant preferences or utilities over the finite set \(K\) of compromise outcomes. For example, a type might encode a private risk attitude or a package valuation. A case has a pair of types \(t=(t_1,t_2)\). Let \(\lambda(t)\) be the fraction of cases in that cell, with rational masses, and assume the paper’s conditions: independent type distributions and one side having exactly two possible types. The regime is a very large number of cases but a modest number \(\tau\) of supported type pairs. This is not a claim about one bargain involving a continuum of people: each case remains a two-person bargain, and the population consists of many such cases.

For each type pair \(t\), let \(q^k(t)\) be the fraction of those cases assigned outcome \(k\), with \(q^k(t)\ge 0\) and \(\sum_{k\in K}q^k(t)=1\). The requested reduced form \(Q_i^k(t_i)\) is the fraction of cases in which a participant on side \(i\), with type \(t_i\), receives outcome \(k\), averaged over counterpart types. The question is whether there is a rule \(q\) realizing all the requested side-and-type frequencies. There is no optimization objective: the output is either a feasible rule or a certificate that none exists. Equivalently, the decision variables can be the masses \(x^k(t)=\lambda(t)q^k(t)\), which split each type-pair cell among outcomes while preserving its total mass.

Theorem 1 says that \(Q\) is implementable if and only if the paper’s conditions (4), (5), and (cut2) hold. In full, for \(K_*=K\setminus\{k_0\}\), the non-slack outcomes must have nonnegative interim probabilities and consistent ex ante totals:
\[ Q_i^k(t_i)\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{t_1\in T_1}Q_1^k(t_1)\lambda_1(t_1) = \sum_{t_2\in T_2}Q_2^k(t_2)\lambda_2(t_2) \quad (k\in K_*). \]
They must also satisfy, for every \(G\subseteq K_*\), \(E_1\subseteq T_1\), and \(E_2\subseteq T_2\),
\[ \sum_{k\in G}\left[ \sum_{t_1\in E_1}Q_1^k(t_1)\lambda_1(t_1) - \sum_{t_2\in E_2}Q_2^k(t_2)\lambda_2(t_2) \right] \le \lambda(E_1\times E_2^c). \]
Here \(k_0\) is the slack outcome; the input is an interim allocation rule, so the probabilities across all outcomes for each side and type sum to one. The theorem is proved in the paper, using a multiflow formulation and a transformation to a classic flow problem. The inequalities are an exact characterization, not a claim that checking the displayed family directly takes polynomial time.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: its mass-splitting formulation has one variable per supported type pair and outcome, with cell-mass and interim-marginal constraints, so it is a linear feasibility problem of size polynomial in \(|K|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. That observation is about the stated continuous cohort problem; I am not attributing a formal “in P” result to Theorem 1. The mirror covers the paper’s implementability result, not its broader mechanism-design applications or incentive-compatible optimization.

The authors have a strong chance of recognizing the question: it asks exactly whether prescribed interim outcome frequencies can be generated by feasible outcomes at each pair of private types. The population reading gives the type probabilities a cohort interpretation—fractions of cases in each cell—while keeping the same two-person allocation question. Further questions include whether the flow structure survives correlated type-pair masses, how to round a feasible mass assignment for a finite cohort that must assign one outcome per case, and how implementability interacts with incentive constraints or an optimization objective.

The weak point is that the paper itself presents \(\lambda\) as a prior over uncertainty in a single two-person bargaining problem, not as the composition of a large case population. Also, lotteries already make its allocation probabilities fractional. So this is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario for their problem, but the population interpretation adds little new mathematics. Its case rests on that scenario being a legitimate application of their implementability question, not on a claim that the paper already studies population-scale computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1, and the proponent’s repeated-case scenario is its strongest version. But that scenario exposes the main problem: its continuous object and feasibility question are already present in the paper. The paper takes a distribution \(\lambda\) over type profiles and asks whether outcome probabilities \(Q\) can be generated by lotteries \(q^k(t)\). In a continuum of repeated cases, let \(\lambda(t)\) be the mass of cases of profile \(t\), and \(x^k(t)=\lambda(t)q^k(t)\) the mass assigned outcome \(k\). Those are the paper’s own variables after its change of variables. Theorem 1 already characterizes exactly when the requested side-and-type frequencies can be realized. Replacing prior probabilities with cohort fractions gives the model a population story without changing the object or the question.

That is a narrower objection than saying repeated cases are implausible. A large mediation service could have many standardized cases, and role and type can be part of a complete case description. The issue is that this plausible scenario does not yield a worthwhile *new* continuous mirror: the paper’s result already applies to its type-profile distribution and allocation masses. This is a collision with the paper’s own treatment, not an objection that the answer happens to be the same.

Reading “population” as the people in one large bargaining society does not rescue the mirror. The paper allocates one public alternative for each pair of agents at each type profile. Turning many agents into one society would require specifying how they are paired, whether an outcome applies to the whole society or separately to each pair, and how feasibility couples those decisions. Keeping separate pairwise bargains preserves the paper’s model; joining them into a collective decision changes it. The paper does not provide a named computational result for that new problem.

The paper does have a named, computationally relevant implementability characterization, so “there is no computational result to mirror” would be too strong. Still, Theorem 1 is a structural characterization, not a complexity theorem, and the paper itself leaves polynomial-time optimization and implementation as further questions. A new optimization problem with costs, incentives, or a collective allocation rule might be worthwhile, but it would need its own objective and model; it does not follow as a continuous mirror of this anchor.

So the negative case is strongest for the proposed implementability mirror: a case-population interpretation is sensible, but the paper already supplies the relevant distributional feasibility result. It is weaker as a universal claim about every possible extension. The paper leaves room for new computational questions, though they would be extensions of its model rather than continuizations of a result that currently lacks a population counterpart.
