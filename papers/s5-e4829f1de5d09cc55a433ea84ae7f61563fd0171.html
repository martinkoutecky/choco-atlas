What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the weighted-welfare optimization primitive used in the proofs. It does not cover the paper's EF1 and fPO existence guarantees or its category-sensitive fairness result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its main theorems are existence results, and Section 7 explicitly leaves polynomial-time computation of EF1 and fPO allocations open. The best computational anchor I can defend is Lemma 18, which the paper uses to justify its weighted-welfare LPs.

Lemma 18 (Total unimodularity), proved here: The constraint matrices for the exactly balanced polytope \(P_{\mathrm{eq}}\), the balanced polytope \(P_{\mathrm{bal}}\), and the padded category-assignment polytope are totally unimodular. Their right-hand sides are integral, so the polytopes are integral: every linear objective over each polytope has an optimum at an integral allocation. The paper uses this result in its proofs; the consequence is polynomial-time solvability of those weighted-welfare optimization problems by linear programming.

My lead mirror is High-Multiplicity Balanced Weighted Welfare. Consider a large course-allocation system with a finite set \(T\) of student types and a finite set \(G\) of seat types. A type records a student’s complete valuation vector over the seat types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the population mass of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); let \(q_g\) be the per-student supply of seat type \(g\); and let \(v_{tg}\geq 0\) be type \(t\)’s value for one seat of type \(g\). All these inputs, along with positive welfare weights \(\alpha_t\), are rational. Assume total supply \(Q=\sum_g q_g=r+\sigma\), where \(r\) is an integer and \(0\leq\sigma<1\).

A bundle is still an *integral* bundle of seats. Write it as \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{G}\), where \(B_g\) counts seats of type \(g\), and allow bundles with \(\sum_g B_g\in\{r,r+1\}\). For each type \(t\) and allowed bundle \(B\), let \(y_{t,B}\geq0\) be the mass of type-\(t\) students assigned that bundle. The question is: find a feasible assignment of bundle mass maximizing weighted welfare, subject to

\[ \sum_B y_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad(t\in T), \qquad \sum_{t,B} y_{t,B}B_g=q_g \quad(g\in G), \]

with objective

\[ \max \sum_{t,B} y_{t,B}\alpha_t\sum_g B_gv_{tg}. \]

The output is an optimal, finitely supported distribution over integral bundles for each type. Fractional mass describes how many students of a type receive each bundle; it does not give any student a fractional seat. Exact balance is the case \(\sigma=0\). Category restrictions can be represented by limiting the allowed bundles, with zero-valued padding where needed.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a university may have many thousands of students, while students’ full valuation vectors cluster into a much smaller set of program, year, and requirement types. Course-seat supplies scale with enrollment. The aggregate bundle masses are fractional, as they should be, even though each student’s bundle remains integral. The paper’s weighted-welfare optimization is central to its proofs, so the authors should recognize this as a continuous version of a problem they actually use.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. Its configuration LP may have many bundle variables, but pricing for a fixed type is just maximizing an additive reduced value over bundles of size \(r\) or \(r+1\); sorting the reduced values gives a polynomial-time pricing routine. This mirrors the optimization structure behind Lemma 18, though it does not claim that the paper’s EF1-and-fPO allocation can be computed this way.

The scope is deliberately limited to the weighted-welfare optimization subproblem. Theorem 1’s balanced EF1-and-fPO existence guarantee is not a computational anchor, and I would not present this LP mirror as a solution to it. The natural next question is whether this continuous society admits a measurable assignment that is EF1 for almost every pair of students and fPO against feasible fractional reassignments—and, if so, whether such an assignment can be found efficiently.

The weak point is that weighted-welfare optimization is already tractable in the finite model. This mirror demonstrates a sensible population-continuous problem and a tractable aggregate formulation, but it does not show discrete hardness dissolving. A strict reading of the requested anchor rule might also reject Lemma 18 as an algorithmic consequence rather than a named complexity result; if so, the paper has no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Lemma 1 are existence results; Lemma 18 proves total unimodularity and integrality. Polynomial-time solvability of the finite weighted-welfare LP follows by standard linear programming, but the paper does not state or classify that as a computational problem. Its explicit questions about computing EF1 and fPO allocations are left open. Under ChoCo’s computational gate, Lemma 18 is a proof tool, not a result in the complexity landscape.

The course-seat model is the strongest version of the proposed mirror. It has a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and scaling supplies with the population is a sensible co-modification. But it mirrors only the weighted-welfare primitive, not the paper’s EF1-and-fPO guarantee or a computational result about finding such an allocation. In the stated model, its configuration variables can also be collapsed to \(x_{tg}=\sum_B y_{t,B}B_g\): bundle integrality is represented by \(r\mu_t\leq\sum_g x_{tg}\leq(r+1)\mu_t\), while supplies impose \(\sum_t x_{tg}=q_g\). The objective is linear in these marginals, giving a compact transportation LP. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity optimization problem, but it does not capture the paper’s KKM and price-interlacing argument.

A continuous EF1-and-fPO question would be closer to the paper’s main results: one could ask whether a distribution over integral bundles for each type can satisfy EF1 across assigned bundles and be fractionally Pareto optimal. But that would be a new existence or search problem, not a mirror of a computational theorem in this paper. The proposed “almost every pair” fairness condition would also need a precise formulation before it could serve as the computational target.

This is the strongest negative case: the sole proposed anchor fails the programme’s computational-result criterion, and the closer fairness question would be new work rather than a mirror. The case is weak against the broader claim that *some* continuous follow-up could be worthwhile. The course-seat model is coherent, and I cannot honestly rule it out on modelling grounds. Calling its likely tractability a reason not to study it would be the forbidden “the answer is easy” objection.
