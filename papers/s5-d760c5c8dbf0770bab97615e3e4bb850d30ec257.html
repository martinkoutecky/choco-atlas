The continuous mirror question

Given a finite value set \(V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_\tau\}\) and rational masses \(q_i\ge 0\) with \(\sum_i q_i=1\) in each of two populations, one of buyers and one of sellers, pair the populations independently so pair type \((v_i,v_j)\) has mass \(q_iq_j\). Find a common price \(p\ge 0\) maximizing per-unit aggregate welfare \(W_q(p)=\sum_{i,j}q_iq_j\left[v_j+(v_i-v_j)\mathbf{1}\{v_j\le p\le v_i\}\right]\).

The model it lives in

The society consists of buyer and seller types indexed by reservation value, each with mass \(q_i\); encounters are paired independently. A single posted price \(p\) determines each encounter's integral trade, and the objective is aggregate welfare per unit mass of pairs.

The objection that survived

The population interpretation is a repeated market of independent bilateral trades, so it adds no distinct population-level computation beyond the paper's distributional price calculation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3 and Theorem 7 for symmetric, full-information welfare. It leaves the single-sample result, asymmetric welfare results, and gains-from-trade results outside the proposed core.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large, symmetric exchange program made up of many small bilateral trades. There are many buyers and sellers, each trading over one indivisible item. A type is a role and a reservation value: buyer of value \(v\), or seller of value \(v\). For a finite value set \(V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_\tau\}\), let \(q_i\) be the fraction of each role-population with value \(v_i\). The populations share the same distribution \(q\), and buyers and sellers are paired independently, so pair type \((v_i,v_j)\) has mass \(q_iq_j\). Here \(\tau\) is the number of distinct values, while the number of agents can be very large.

The mechanism posts one price \(p\) across the whole market. Each pair trades if the seller’s value is at most \(p\) and the buyer’s value is at least \(p\). Aggregate welfare per unit mass of pairs is
\[ W_q(p)=\sum_{i,j}q_iq_j\left[v_j+(v_i-v_j)\mathbf 1\{v_j\le p\le v_i\}\right], \]
and first-best welfare is
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_q=\sum_{i,j}q_iq_j\max\{v_i,v_j\}. \]
Each encounter still gives each agent an integral allocation; mass and welfare are fractional only in the aggregate. The common price preserves the paper’s fixed-price mechanism and its incentive properties.

My lead is Continuous Symmetric Posted-Price Welfare Optimization: given the value types and their rational masses, find a price maximizing \(W_q(p)\). Its anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper: when buyer and seller values are drawn from the same distribution \(F\), a welfare-maximizing fixed price is the mean, \(p^*=\mathbb E[S]=\mathbb E[B]\). For the finite-mass input above, the answer is \(p^*=\sum_i q_i v_i\), computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. I expect this mirror to be tractable. It keeps the authors’ actual objective—welfare from a DSIC fixed-price trade—and replaces their ex ante valuation distribution with realized proportions in a large cohort.

A second, distinct question is Continuous Symmetric Posted-Price Guarantee. The instance is the class of admissible symmetric valuation distributions, or equivalently the large-market value-mass profiles; the task is to find the largest \(\alpha\) for which a price-selection rule \(p(F)\) guarantees \(W(p(F),F)\ge \alpha\,\operatorname{OPT-W}(F)\) for every \(F\), and to give such a rule with a matching worst-case example. Its anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here: the optimal worst-case ratio over fixed prices is exactly \((2+\sqrt 2)/4\). The rule \(p(F)=\mathbb E[S]\) achieves that guarantee, and the paper gives a sequence approaching the bound. Finite rational type-mass profiles can approximate the sequence, so the same boundary is relevant to the high-multiplicity input regime. I would also place this mirror in Class A: the price rule is easy to compute, and the theorem’s barrier is a sharp approximation limit for the mechanism class, not a computational-hardness result.

The paper contains no named theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time, W[1]-hard, or similar. These anchors are its named optimization and approximation results; the mirror therefore offers a tractable algorithmic case, not a hardness-transfer case. Its scope is the symmetric, full-distribution welfare results above. I would leave the paper’s sample-only and asymmetric settings outside the core case, though an asymmetric extension would naturally ask what guarantees are possible from separate buyer and seller type masses, and how those change when the price setter knows only one side’s distribution.

The weak point is that the paper itself has exactly two agents. Its distributions describe uncertainty about their values, while this mirror treats the same masses as the realized composition of a large market. That makes the aggregate formulas line up cleanly, but the authors could see the cohort as repeated independent bilateral trades rather than their original problem. I think the scenario is still credible: a large platform can run many independent trades over standardized goods, with buyers and sellers grouped into a relatively small number of reservation-value tiers and one common price. The mirror is strongest as a population-scale version of their policy question, not as a claim that the paper already studies a many-agent market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that this paper supplies no computational result for a continuous-population programme to mirror. Its theorems concern the welfare guarantees of fixed-price mechanisms when a buyer and seller draw values from known distributions. They prove approximation ratios and identify good prices; they do not analyze the complexity of an input-dependent intervention on a population.

The repeated-market scenario is coherent, so I cannot rule it out on multiplicity grounds. But with independent buyer–seller pairs and one shared price, expected welfare across \(N\) pairs is just \(N\) times the paper’s \(W(p;F)\). Interpreting the value probabilities as cohort proportions preserves the formulas while adding no population-level decision. For Proposition 3, the proposed task of finding the best price from rational masses is a computational wrapper around the result \(p^*=\mathbb E[S]\). The paper already identifies that price; the cohort interpretation contributes no new algorithmic question.

Theorem 7 has the same limitation. Its minimax is over distributions \(F\), not over high-multiplicity instances requiring computation. Recasting the near-worst-case distributions as value-mass profiles preserves the approximation ratio, but does not turn the distributional guarantee into a complexity problem about a society. The stronger asymmetric version has the same issue: choosing a price from two known finite-support populations remains a pricing calculation, while restricting information to one side turns the question into one about information and guarantees.

A market with capacities, cross-pair constraints, or strategic changes to the value distribution could create a genuinely computational population problem. Those changes would also alter the fixed-price welfare question that anchors this case. They would need new results of their own, rather than a continuous mirror of the paper’s theorems.

So the negative case is about value, not impossibility: the proposed population reading is sensible, and both anchors transfer cleanly. What fails is the claim that they open a computational landscape. The paper studies an analytic guarantee for a scalar price, and treating repeated trades as a continuum leaves that structure intact.
