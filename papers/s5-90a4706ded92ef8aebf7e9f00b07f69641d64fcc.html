The continuous mirror question

Given a scale \(N\) and rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\) on finite support \(A\) of request types \(a=(u_a,t_a,c_a,p_a)\), emergency departments \(S\), initial workloads \(\bar\ell_s\), carry-over factors \(\alpha_t\), quality values \(q_{s,p}\), and convex piecewise-linear waiting costs \(w_s(n)=\max_h\{a^s_h n+b^s_h\}\), let \(E_a=\{s:f_{p_a}\in F_s,\ d(u_a,v_s)\le t_{\max}(p_a)\}\). Choose \(x_{a,s}\ge0\), with \(x_{a,s}=0\) for \(s\notin E_a\) and \(\sum_{s\in E_a}x_{a,s}=\mu_a\), and workloads \(\ell_{s,0}=\bar\ell_s\), \(\ell_{s,t}=\alpha_t\ell_{s,t-1}+N\sum_{a:t_a=t}x_{a,s}\), to minimize \(\sum_{a,s\in E_a}x_{a,s}(d(u_a,v_s)-\gamma q_{s,p_a})+N^{-1}\sum_{s,t}w_s(\ell_{s,t})\). Determine its complexity, including whether the generalized-flow tractability result of Theorem 6.5 extends under the mass-scaled counterpart of condition (6.33).

The model it lives in

A finite-support continuous-demand allocation model: each request type carries mass that can be split among eligible emergency departments; aggregate workloads evolve over time and incur convex piecewise-linear waiting costs, alongside per-capita travel and service-quality terms. Its explicit formulation is a linear program using epigraph variables for the waiting costs.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(MIP_0\) tractability result in Theorem 6.5 under the counterpart of condition (6.33), with Theorem 6.3 as a related integrality question; it leaves the full workload-balance model and the other thesis chapters aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass formulation admit a strongly polynomial generalized-flow algorithm under the exact counterpart of condition (6.33), beyond polynomial-time solvability as a linear program?
  • When \(\mu_a=n_a/N\), can continuous optimal allocations be rounded to assignments of finite requests with explicit bounds on per-capita objective loss?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Chapter 6’s assignment of first-aid requests to emergency rooms. The thesis already studies regional routing of thousands of requests across a fixed set of facilities; its computational experiment reports about 4,000 requests per day on average. A natural high-multiplicity regime is regional planning in which requests are represented by their shares across origin district, time slot, triage code, and pathology. Those are the attributes that determine eligibility and cost in the model. Many requests can share a type, while the number of supported types can remain much smaller than the number of requests.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.5, proved in this thesis. It concerns the workload-balance-free model \(MIP_0\): requests must be assigned to an emergency department that offers the required treatment and can be reached within the pathology’s travel-time limit. The objective accounts for travel, convex piecewise-linear waiting costs at each department, and the service-quality term. The theorem states that, if condition (6.33) holds—roughly, the travel-cost advantage between eligible departments is at least the largest relevant difference in waiting-cost slopes—an optimal solution to \(MIP_0\) can be computed in strongly polynomial time. The thesis proves this by relating the model to generalized min-cost flow.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, Continuous First-Aid Request Allocation. An instance gives a finite support \(A\) of request types \(a=(u_a,t_a,c_a,p_a)\), with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to one; a population scale \(N\); emergency departments \(S\), their locations and treatment capabilities; travel times and pathology deadlines; quality values \(q_{s,p}\); waiting-cost functions \(w_s(n)=\max_h\{a^s_h n+b^s_h\}\); carry-over factors \(\alpha_t\); and the quality weight \(\gamma\). For each type, let \(E_a\) contain exactly the departments that offer its treatment and meet its travel deadline.

Choose masses \(x_{a,s}\geq 0\), with \(x_{a,s}=0\) for \(s\notin E_a\) and \(\sum_{s\in E_a}x_{a,s}=\mu_a\). Define each department’s workload recursively by
\[ \ell_{s,t}=\alpha_t\ell_{s,t-1}+N\sum_{a:t_a=t}x_{a,s}. \]
Find a feasible allocation minimizing the per-capita version of the thesis’s objective:
\[ \sum_{a\in A}\sum_{s\in E_a}x_{a,s}\bigl(d(u_a,v_s)-\gamma q_{s,p_a}\bigr) +\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{t\in T}w_s(\ell_{s,t}). \]
A solution is the mass routed from each supported request type to each eligible department, together with its objective value. The complexity question is whether this can be solved in polynomial time in the support size, department and time-slot counts, piecewise-linear descriptions, and rational input bit length, under the continuous analogue of condition (6.33).

I expect this mirror to be tractable under that condition. The thesis’s generalized-flow argument is a strong reason to expect a continuous mass-flow formulation to retain tractability, though Theorem 6.5 itself is about the thesis’s finite-request model and does not by itself prove the continuous extension. The question is recognizably the same routing problem: requests still go only to suitable departments, and the objective still trades travel, waiting, and service quality. The population-level variables simply allow a type’s demand to be split as mass.

This scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the Chapter 6 allocation problem and its \(MIP_0\) variant, which drops the workload-balance surcharge. The theorem does not establish tractability of the full model with threshold-penalty variables. A natural follow-up is whether the mass version of the full model preserves the assignment-integrality result of Theorem 6.3, also proved in the thesis: relaxing the request-to-department assignment variables preserves the optimum, and an optimal integral assignment can be recovered in strongly polynomial time. Another follow-up is the discrete-to-continuous dictionary: how well a finite request instance is represented by type masses, and what rounding or recovery guarantees hold as \(N\) changes.

The weakest point is that these “agents” are request episodes, not stable citizens. Aggregation is persuasive only when requests within a type really are indistinguishable for routing—especially in deadline, treatment eligibility, and quality. If those attributes vary substantially within a district-time-triage-pathology cell, the type set must be refined, and its size may grow enough to weaken the high-multiplicity case. The thesis’s centralized allocation is also presented as an offline benchmark for remote triage, rather than a policy already in operation. Still, the authors themselves formulate that allocation question and evaluate its system-wide value, so the continuous version would be a credible extension of their problem.

The thesis mentions that kidney exchange with cycles of length at least three is NP-hard, citing Abraham et al. (2007), but does not give that claim as a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition. I therefore would not use it as a required anchor here. It also seems a weaker mirror: transplant pairs are indivisible, and compatibility structure is central to that problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a scope objection. Chapter 6 assigns request episodes to emergency rooms. Its agents are not a stable population of people making choices: they are events that arrive at different places and times, with clinical categories and deadlines. A continuous model of their shares would be a fluid demand-allocation problem. Even a stronger version, with resident risk groups generating request rates, would model demand flows rather than a society choosing among alternatives. That may be outside ChoCo’s intended subject.

But this does not defeat the proposed mirror. The thesis’s model already represents requests by district, time slot, triage code, and pathology; those attributes determine eligibility and cost. Many episodes can share them, and the paper reports thousands of requests per day. So the type space need not grow with the request count. Calling episodes “events” rather than “people” does not make their aggregate mass meaningless for the routing problem the authors study.

Theorem 6.5 is therefore a strong surviving anchor. Its continuous version can route each type’s mass to eligible departments and track aggregate workloads over time. The paper’s convex piecewise-linear waiting costs and linear assignment conditions provide a direct formulation; the scale \(N\) and per-capita objective are sensible co-modifications. The theorem itself does not prove the continuous result, but that is a reason to ask the question, not a reason the mirror fails.

Theorem 6.3 also survives. Its integrality result is a bridge between individual requests and fractional assignments, and it shows that request identity can disappear without changing the finite model’s optimum. It does not by itself settle aggregation into rational type masses, but the authors’ own flow formulation makes that extension natural. Treating the result as a collision or as evidence against the mirror would mistake a useful bridge for a disqualifier.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that this is better described as continuous healthcare demand allocation than as continuized social choice. I cannot honestly argue that no plausible high-multiplicity scenario exists, or that the named results fail to support a worthwhile mirror. The decisive uncertainty is whether ChoCo includes this kind of administrative allocation problem in its remit; on the model and instance regime alone, the proponent’s anchor stands.
