The continuous mirror question

Given finite companies \(C\), complete applicant types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), rankings \(r_{\theta j}\), scores \(s_{\theta j}\), category map \(g(\theta)\), capacities \(u_j\), and category lower quotas \(\ell_j^g\) satisfying \(\sum_j\ell_j^g\le\mu_g\), \(\sum_g\ell_j^g\le u_j\), and \(\sum_j u_j\ge1\), find or report infeasibility of an aggregate assignment \(x_{\theta j}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_jx_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta j}\le u_j\), \(\sum_{\theta:g(\theta)=g}x_{\theta j}\ge\ell_j^g\), and, whenever \(g(\theta)=g(\phi)\), \(s_{\theta j}>s_{\phi j}\), and \(x_{\phi j}>0\), \(\sum_{k:r_{\theta k}\le r_{\theta j}}x_{\theta k}=\mu_\theta\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity matching market in which complete preference-and-score profiles carry mass \(\mu_\theta\); \(x_{\theta j}\) is assigned mass, capacities and category quotas constrain \(x\), and within-category envy is the support condition inherited from cloned individual applicants. The objective is feasibility or an infeasibility certificate.

The objection that survived

The aggregate envy condition is discontinuous: an arbitrarily small \(x_{\phi j}>0\) constrains all mass of a higher-scoring type, and the paper supplies no denominator-independent algorithm for this formulation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's existence and construction of a complete within-type envy-free matching, with Theorem 2 providing an equivalent stability interpretation. It leaves the integer-programming formulations, empirical applications, multiobjective variants, Conjecture 3, and the unnumbered HRTL hardness discussion largely untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow Class A mirror of Theorem 1. It is not a hardness-based case: the paper’s explicit NP-hardness statement for HRTL appears only in unnumbered prose and is attributed to earlier work, so I would not use it as an anchor under the programme’s rule.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper:

If every company is acceptable to every student, the total lower quota required for each applicant type is no greater than the number of applicants of that type, and the sum of type-specific lower quotas at each company is no greater than that company’s upper quota, then a complete within-type envy-free matching exists and can be found efficiently.

The theorem’s proof is constructive: it computes a stable matching separately within each applicant type using artificial company capacities, merges those matchings, and then increases artificial capacities until all applicants are assigned. The paper does not give a formal polynomial bound in an input encoding, so “efficiently” is the theorem’s own level of precision.

The continuous problem I would mirror is Continuous Complete Within-Group Envy-Free Allocation.

There is a finite set of companies \(C\) and a finite set of complete applicant types \(\Theta\). A complete type \(\theta\) specifies the applicant’s entire preference ranking \(r_{\theta j}\) over companies, the score \(s_{\theta j}\) received from each company, and a distributional category \(g(\theta)\). The category \(g(\theta)\) is the paper’s nationality or demographic class; it is not itself the complete type. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_\theta)_{\theta\in\Theta}\), where \(\mu_\theta\) is the mass of applicants of complete type \(\theta\).

Each company \(j\) has a per-capita upper capacity \(u_j\), and each category \(g\) has a lower quota \(\ell_j^g\) at company \(j\). We assume

\[ \sum_j \ell_j^g\le \mu_g \quad\text{for every }g, \qquad \sum_g \ell_j^g\le u_j \quad\text{for every }j, \]

where \(\mu_g=\sum_{\theta:g(\theta)=g}\mu_\theta\), together with the necessary aggregate-capacity condition \(\sum_j u_j\ge1\). The latter appears to be implicit in the paper’s use of “complete matching.”

The decision variable is an aggregate assignment \(x_{\theta j}\ge0\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_j x_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta \]

for every complete type, the company capacities

\[ \sum_\theta x_{\theta j}\le u_j, \]

and the category lower quotas

\[ \sum_{\theta:g(\theta)=g}x_{\theta j}\ge \ell_j^g. \]

Within-group envy-freeness is imposed in the direct mass analogue of the paper’s binary constraint: whenever \(g(\theta)=g(\phi)\), \(s_{\theta j}>s_{\phi j}\), and \(x_{\phi j}>0\), all mass of type \(\theta\) must be assigned to \(j\) or to a company that \(\theta\) ranks above \(j\):

\[ \sum_{k:r_{\theta k}\le r_{\theta j}}x_{\theta k}=\mu_\theta. \]

The question is to find such an \(x\), or report that none exists. There is no objective beyond feasibility in the anchored theorem. Every infinitesimal applicant still receives one integral company; \(x_{\theta j}\) is only the aggregate mass assigned there. For rational masses, multiplying by a common denominator recovers an ordinary finite matching of cloned applicants.

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for national school admissions, resident allocation, or large internship markets: many applicants share complete preference templates, score bands, and demographic categories, while the number of distinct complete types \(|\Theta|\) is much smaller than the population size \(N\). The original CEMS instances with \(25\) or \(40\) students are not themselves convincing continuum regimes, but the same mathematical problem arises plausibly with hundreds of thousands of applicants and a relatively small catalogue of repeated preference-and-priority profiles.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. The proof of Theorem 1 is already organized by distributional categories and company capacities, rather than by named individuals. Its continuous analogue should be attackable through compressed stable-matching, flow, or LP machinery whose complexity depends on \(m\), \(|\Theta|\), and the bit length of the rational masses, not on the common denominator or on \(N\). Establishing that denominator-independent bound would be the genuinely new continuous-computation result.

The paper’s Theorem 2, also proved here, provides useful structural support but I would not count it as a second computational anchor:

Under the assumptions of Theorem 1, a complete matching is within-type envy-free if and only if it is stable with type-specific scores.

In the mirror, this says that the same allocation question can equivalently be phrased as finding category-company score offsets \(\delta_{g,j}\) such that \(x\) is weakly stable under adjusted scores \(s_{\theta j}+\delta_{g(\theta),j}\). This is a valuable bridge to standard stable-matching machinery, but Theorem 2 itself is not a complexity classification.

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and the equivalent interpretation supplied by Theorem 2. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s IP formulations, its numerical CEMS applications, or its unnumbered HRTL NP-hardness discussion. That HRTL statement is a natural follow-up: does divisibility of applicant mass make exact distributionally constrained stability tractable, or does hardness survive through the finite set of preference and score types?

Other resulting questions include whether type-specific upper quotas remain tractable, whether minimum cross-category envy becomes a linear or convex optimization problem, whether the paper’s Conjecture 3 extends to three or more categories, and how much quota violation is necessary when the continuous stable region is empty.

The weakest point is that the anchor is only informally algorithmic. If the programme requires a numbered theorem with an explicit \(P\)-membership or polynomial-running-time statement, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Even accepting Theorem 1, the continuous version must prove that its support-based envy constraints admit a compact, denominator-independent algorithm; that does not follow automatically from the paper’s finite proof.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case turns on the fact that Theorem 1 is not really a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. It gives no decision problem, encoding, running-time bound, or dependence on the population size versus the number of types. “Can be found efficiently” is an informal claim supported by a finite Gale–Shapley construction. Thus the proposed continuous problem would have to supply essentially all of the computational content itself; it would not be a mirror of a complexity theorem in the paper.

There is also a serious type mismatch. In the paper, \(T^k\) is a coarse distributional category such as nationality. Applicants of the same \(T^k\) may have completely different preferences \(r_{ij}\) and company scores \(s_{ij}\). The within-type envy constraints compare those individual data. Consequently, the proposed \(\Theta\) cannot consist merely of the paper’s applicant types. It must refine each category into complete preference-and-score profiles. If many applicants share such a profile, the result is a legitimate high-multiplicity model—but it is then essentially a compressed market of cloned applicants, not the continuous population object suggested by the paper’s terminology. If they do not share profiles, the compression disappears.

The proposed mass analogue also introduces a nontrivial new definition:

\[ x_{\phi j}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{k:r_{\theta k}\le r_{\theta j}}x_{\theta k}=\mu_\theta . \]

This is not a linear aggregate version of the paper’s IP constraint. An arbitrarily small positive mass of a lower-scoring profile at \(j\) imposes an all-or-nothing restriction on the entire higher-scoring mass. The condition is support-sensitive and discontinuous at \(x_{\phi j}=0\). One can adopt it, but then the continuous problem is a new disjunctive matching problem whose existence and algorithms are not supplied by Theorem 1. Alternatively, one can define blocking in terms of positive-measure coalitions; that is more naturally nonatomic, but it is no longer the paper’s within-type envy-freeness, and the theorem’s proof does not transfer automatically.

The obvious stronger proposal—per-capita quotas for a large school-choice or resident-allocation market—is coherent. With complete repeated profiles and rational masses, cloning gives a meaningful high-multiplicity interpretation. But this concession exposes the weakness of the paper as an anchor: the interesting result would be a new denominator-independent algorithm for a compressed stable-matching problem, not a continuous reformulation of a numbered computational theorem. Theorem 2 does not repair this, since it is only an equivalence between two solution concepts, not a complexity or algorithmic result. Likewise, minimizing rank or cross-type envy would produce potentially worthwhile new optimization problems, but they are not results established by this paper.

So the strongest defensible negative verdict is that the paper does not itself provide a qualifying ChoCo anchor. Its applications are small and its theorem is an existence/construction statement with no formal complexity content; the plausible high-multiplicity formulation must be invented and analyzed almost from scratch. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: the national-school or resident-allocation version is a sensible candidate. If “found efficiently” is accepted as a sufficient computational anchor, however, the negative case is weak and the proponent’s Class A mirror survives.
