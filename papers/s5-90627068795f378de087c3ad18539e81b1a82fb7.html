Why no mirror

The finite-category replica market is a coherent continuous extension of stable allocation. But the paper’s unnumbered theorem gives a distributional identity for rank multisets, not a named computational guarantee under the stated criterion; the algorithm and runtime discussion do not supply the required anchor. Bit (a) therefore fails.

fails bit a — no named computational result to mirror

What the mirror covers

The proposed extension asks about core-stable assignments in a finite-category replica market. It does not transfer Knuth’s rank-distribution identity or the priority-to-shuffle correspondence for distinct labeled goods.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a core-stable aggregate assignment be found in time polynomial in the number of types and the input bit length?
  • Can stability be characterized or tested efficiently from the aggregate assignment \(x\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational anchor. Its §4 “Theorem” is unnumbered and proves that, for independent uniformly random preference lists, the rank multiset in the unique stable allocation has the same distribution as the rank multiset produced by uniform hashing. The paper derives \(\mathbb E[\sum_i r_i]=(n+1)H_n-n\), and notes that its algorithm’s running time is essentially proportional to that sum. Those are substantial probabilistic and algorithmic observations, but they are not a named complexity result asserting a complexity classification. The §2 algorithm computes the stable allocation, but it too is not such an anchor. So the required anchor-backed list is empty.

There is a plausible continuous problem nearby. Fix a finite set \(G\) of good categories. Let each type \(t=(e,\succ)\) specify an endowed category \(e\in G\) and a complete ranking \(\succ\) of \(G\); let rational mass \(\mu_t\) describe the society, with supply \(\nu_g=\sum_{t:e=g}\mu_t\). The task is to output an aggregate allocation \(x_{t,g}\geq0\), with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(\nu_g\), that has no positive-mass blocking coalition: selected agents can pool their endowed goods and reassign them so everyone weakly improves and some positive mass strictly improves. This models many traders sharing a bounded number of complete types, while each person still receives one indivisible copy of a good. It mirrors the paper’s stable-allocation problem, and the authors could plausibly recognize the connection.

I would tentatively expect this finite-type mass problem to be tractable, perhaps through a type-level top-trading-cycles method. But that is a new algorithmic question, not a result of Knuth’s paper. The random-rank theorem does not transfer directly: its market has \(n\) distinct goods for \(n\) traders, and its random rankings are not a high-multiplicity type regime. That mismatch is the weak point, and it prevents this plausible model from becoming an honest anchor-based positive case under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no result of the kind the proposed mirrors are supposed to anchor. Its main theorem proves a distributional identity for random preference lists; the later formulas give rank statistics under that distribution. The algorithm computes the stable allocation, and the paper relates its running time to the rank sum, but it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result about a continuous population. On the stated standard, there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The proponent’s best alternative is a society with finitely many good categories, where a type records an endowed category and a ranking of the categories. That is a coherent replica-market question, and it avoids the mistaken objection that fractional type-level allocation is incompatible with indivisible goods. But it does not carry over Knuth’s result. In the paper, each trader owns a distinct good and ranks all \(n\) labeled goods. If types include that endowment and ranking, no type repeats: each has mass \(1/n\). Creating repeated types requires replacing distinct goods with categories that have multiple interchangeable copies. Then the paper’s random permutations, rank statistics, and priority-to-shuffle correspondence are no longer the objects being analyzed. The resulting continuum core problem may be worthwhile, but it is a new computational question suggested by the setting, not a continuous version of the paper’s theorem.

That is the limit of the negative case. The category model is plausible, and the authors might recognize it as a natural replica version of their stable-allocation problem. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. The case against is strongest if the programme requires a paper’s computational result to supply the anchor; it is weak if a recognizable computational problem from the paper’s setting is sufficient.
