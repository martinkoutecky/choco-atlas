The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid anchored case for a continuous computational mirror. The paper contains no named computational result: it gives no complexity classification, algorithm, running time, approximation guarantee, or parameterized result.

The closest result is Theorem 3.1, proved here. It says that when the attribute vector measures are nonatomic, preferences are irreflexive, continuous, monotone and convex, and positive endowments hold, a competitive equilibrium exists; its price measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the sum of the attribute measures. Corollary 3.2 then establishes a nonempty weak core under those assumptions, and Theorem 4.4 establishes a weak group-envy-free, weakly Pareto-efficient allocation. These are existence results, not computational anchors.

A scenario with many landholders sharing complete preference, endowment and cost types could make a high-multiplicity version of land exchange sensible. But the paper’s continuum is the *commodity*—measurable pieces of land—while its set of agents is finite. Turning those agents into a continuous population and asking how to compute an equilibrium would pose a new computational problem; none of the paper’s named results anchors it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for a mirror has no computational anchor. Theorem 3.1 proves that an equilibrium exists; Corollary 3.2 derives weak-core nonemptiness from that equilibrium; Theorem 4.4 proves that a fair allocation exists. None gives an algorithm, complexity bound, approximation guarantee, or other computational result to continuize. The paper’s assumptions also allow arbitrary measurable spaces, nonatomic vector measures, and preference correspondences, without specifying finite encodings or how to measure computational error. Its existence proof invokes equilibrium-existence machinery; it does not pose a pricing problem or another algorithmic task.

The strongest rescue would be to model many landholders as a distribution over complete types, give land characteristics and preferences finite rational descriptions, and ask for an approximate equilibrium or fair allocation. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity computational-economics project. But it requires choosing the input representation, preference restrictions, and approximation notion that the paper does not supply. The possibility of many similar landholders supports the regime; it does not turn an existence theorem into a computational result. Likewise, computing a weak-core allocation is not a separate anchor here, since Corollary 3.2 follows from the equilibrium result.

So the negative case is strong on the question ChoCo asks: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous-population project could ever be built around land exchange is harder to defend. A finite-type equilibrium-computation project might be worthwhile, but on the evidence here it would be a new computational study inspired by the paper, not a mirror of one of its results.
