What the mirror covers

The proposed LP covers welfare allocation over merchant-type masses. It leaves the paper's truthful implementation results and its implementability characterizations without a continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly name a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 4, proved here, says that when each \(g_o\) is component-wise convex, a truthful mechanism can select an outcome maximizing \(\sum_i v_i(o)+g_o(\vec p(o))\). Theorem 5 gives the matching necessity for deterministic truthful mechanisms. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 give the corresponding simple-model results; Theorem 2 proves an impossibility for a quality-threshold objective. These are results about truthful implementation and welfare objectives. None classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized, and the paper makes no complexity claim about computing the maximizing outcome. So none meets your anchor rule.

The strongest positive lead I see is an unanchored extension of Theorem 4’s multi-slot setting. Imagine a platform assigning deals across many locality-day placements. Its merchant population consists of many franchise outlets or standardized merchants. A type \(t\) specifies the complete information relevant to the allocation: valuation \(v_{tj}\) and purchase-state distribution \(p_{tj}\) for each placement \(j\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of merchants of type \(t\); let \(\kappa_j\) be placement capacity; and let \(a_{tj\omega}\) be the consumer value of state \(\omega\) for type \(t\) in placement \(j\).

One precise problem is Continuous Daily-Deal Cohort Allocation: given these rational inputs, choose masses \(x_{tj}\geq 0\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to placement \(j\), to maximize

\[ \sum_{t,j} x_{tj}\left(v_{tj}+\sum_{\omega}a_{tj\omega}p_{tj\omega}\right) \]

subject to \(\sum_j x_{tj}\leq\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_t x_{tj}\leq\kappa_j\) for each placement. The solution is the allocation masses \(x_{tj}\). This is a transport LP, hence tractable when the types and placements are explicitly listed. The expected consumer-value term is linear, a special case of the convex welfare functions in Theorem 4.

The regime is many placements and many similar merchants, with \(n\) large relative to the number \(\tau\) of complete merchant types. Mass records the share of the merchant population in each type, and allocations are fractional only at the population level. This fits the paper’s discussion of assignments to multiple slots and is more plausible for repeated locality-day markets than for its single-coupon auction.

The soft spot is substantial: the proposed LP computes the welfare-maximizing allocation from reported types. It does not itself solve the paper’s central strategic problem of eliciting merchants’ private valuations and beliefs in this population model. A truthful mechanism for the mass allocation needs its own proof. The paper offers conceptual support for that extension, especially Theorem 4, but no computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror of this paper is that it has no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1 and 4 establish truthful implementation; Theorems 3 and 5 characterize which welfare functions can be implemented; Theorem 2 proves an impossibility for a quality-threshold objective. None studies the running time or complexity of computing an outcome. In the one-winner model, the rule is to choose the highest reported \(v_i+g(p_i)\); in the general model, the outcome set and its representation are left arbitrary. The paper therefore does not define a computational problem whose population-continuous complexity it charts.

The proposed cohort-allocation LP does not fill that gap as a mirror. It changes the choice from selecting a merchant under the paper’s mechanism to allocating positive masses of merchants across placements. It adds scaled capacities and an additive allocation objective, and its input consists of reported types. Those are reasonable modeling choices, but the LP does not analyze how the mechanism elicits private valuations and beliefs—the paper’s central contribution. Theorem 4 supplies a payment construction for an argmax over outcomes with finitely many named bidders; it does not prove truthful implementation for a flow of merchant mass.

A stronger version would retain strategic behavior: represent merchants by a distribution over complete types, scale placement capacity with population size, optimize assignments of type mass, and define payments and incentive constraints for the resulting continuum. But that forces a substantive choice about who can deviate. If merchants are atomless, an individual report has no effect on the allocation, so individual dominant-strategy truthfulness risks becoming vacuous. If a whole type cohort can deviate together, the strategic player is the cohort, not the individual merchant. And if positive-mass merchants remain individually influential, the model retains atoms rather than taking the population continuum in the usual sense. These choices may be resolvable, but they define a new population market-design problem; they are not supplied by the paper’s theorems.

So I find no surviving *paper-anchored* computational mirror. The limitation of this negative case is real: a carefully developed continuum market with aggregate allocations and meaningful incentive constraints could still be worthwhile in its own right. The paper gives reasons to explore that extension, but it does not establish it as a computational question arising from its results.
