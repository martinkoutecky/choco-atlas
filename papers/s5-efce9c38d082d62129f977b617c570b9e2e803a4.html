The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its discussion of hard and tractable approval-based ranking methods points to other literature; it is not a named result of this paper. The numbered questions in the conclusion are research questions, not computational anchors. So there is no eligible anchor for the case you requested.

The strongest plausible *candidate* mirror is the paper’s maximum-coverage approach in Section 4.3. Fix a snapshot of a deliberation platform, a finite set \(P\) of proposals, and a support \(\mathcal A\) of approval types. A type \(A\in\mathcal A\) is the complete set of proposals a participant approves at that snapshot; its mass \(\mu_A\) is that type’s fraction of participants, with \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\mu_A=1\). Given an attention horizon \(K\le |P|\), Continuous Approval-Coverage Ranking asks for a permutation \(\pi\) of \(P\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\ \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A:\\A\cap\{\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_k\}\ne\varnothing}}\mu_A. \]
A solution is an optimal proposal ordering. This makes “how many users have a proposal they approve high up in the list” precise across the first \(K\) positions. The proposals remain discrete; the population is the continuous object.

A plausible regime is a large municipal or regional deliberation with hundreds or thousands of proposals and many participants. The paper reports a median of 628 proposals per case and a maximum of 18,859. If recurring civic groups or shared issue priorities produce repeated approval sets, the number of distinct types \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\) could be much smaller than the number of participants. The mirror fits the paper’s problem: it models the common list shown during online deliberation and formalizes the paper’s own maximum-coverage proposal. Its scope is only that approval-based ranking question, with other proposal attributes held equal as in the paper’s comparison; it does not cover the paper’s broader normative framework or its questions about proposal quality and legitimacy.

I would expect this candidate problem to retain hardness if its finite-population version is hard: a finite approval profile embeds by setting \(\mu_A=n_A/N\). That is a prediction about this proposed formalization, not a result established by the paper, so it cannot serve as the requested anchor. Further questions include how complexity varies with \(K\) and \(\tau\), and how to incorporate inspection counts or proposal metadata.

The weak point is that full approval sets over thousands of proposals may produce nearly as many distinct types as participants, undermining the high-multiplicity advantage. More decisively for the requested case, the paper never proves or cites a numbered computational result about this problem. The mirror is plausible, but it is unanchored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper offers no computational result to continuize. Its main contributions are a conceptual framework, a survey of platform practice, and normative arguments about how proposals should be shown. The computational work it mentions on approval rankings belongs to other papers. Its own open questions concern empirical effects, user preferences, and how to measure inspection. None is a theorem about an algorithmic problem in this paper.

Maximum coverage is the closest candidate, but the proposed formula adds a key choice the paper leaves open: the horizon \(K\) and the assumption that seeing any approved proposal in the first \(K\) positions counts as coverage. Other choices produce different problems. The paper supplies a normative direction—help participants encounter proposals they support—but no defined objective whose computational complexity the continuous model would answer. A richer exposure model could make a sensible new research problem, but its computational content would come from the formalization, not from a result established here.

There is also a practical weakness in the high-multiplicity story. A participant’s complete approval set across hundreds or thousands of proposals can be a highly individual signature; sharing broad issue priorities does not mean sharing that whole set. Grouping people more coarsely would discard distinctions the coverage objective uses. That makes the hoped-for small support size uncertain, though it does not rule out structured cases.

So the case against a *paper-anchored* continuous mirror is strong: there is no eligible result to mirror, and the maximum-coverage candidate is a newly specified algorithmic question. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise from this paper is weaker. A carefully chosen exposure objective over a large, structured participant population could be worthwhile on its own; I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario.
