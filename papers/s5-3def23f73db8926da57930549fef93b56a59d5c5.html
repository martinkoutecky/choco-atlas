What the mirror covers

The cohort restatement covers the Section 2 example and the Section 3 ordinal generalization; it does not supply a computational mirror for either.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive seed is the paper’s Section 2 argument associated with Necessity-Restricted All-Affected Stochastic Pareto. The authors prove that ex post average utilitarianism can rank prospect \(A\) above \(B\), even though the only affected necessary person, Bob, weakly prefers \(B\) by stochastic dominance, and the contingent person, Ann, is unchanged. In their example, the expected average is \(4\) for \(A\) and \(3\) for \(B\). Section 3 extends the argument to ordinally ordered utility using a generalized average. Both are proved in this paper.

There is a natural continuous-population restatement. Take a large policy population with two cohorts, each of mass \(1/2\), whose members share the same complete welfare profile across the two states and the two prospects. One cohort has Ann’s profile: well-being \(1\) in \(S_1\) and nonexistence \(\Omega\) in \(S_2\), unchanged between prospects. The other has Bob’s profile: \((1,7)\) under \(A\) and \((9,1)\) under \(B\). With equal state probabilities, the state averages are \(1\) and \(7\) under \(A\), and \(5\) and \(1\) under \(B\), so the expected averages remain \(4\) and \(3\). Bob’s welfare distribution under \(B\) weakly first-order stochastically dominates his distribution under \(A\). A society with many people in these two cohorts is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors would likely recognize the resulting comparison as their population-ethics issue.

But this is not a qualifying ChoCo anchor. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result—no decision or optimization problem, algorithm, or complexity classification. The named principle and its counterexample are normative results, not computational ones. I cannot honestly give a continuous computational problem “per anchor” without inventing a new task; deciding whether a supplied pair of prospects exhibits this disagreement would be a computational wrapper around the authors’ argument, not a problem they study.

So the strongest honest positive case is limited: the population itself can be continuized coherently here, with small support and a recognizable large-population scenario. Its weakest point is decisive for this programme: the paper’s contribution is an axiological comparison under risk, not computational complexity. Its lotteries are part of the ethical question, but their presence does not turn the society into a continuous population or supply a computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponents’ strongest anchor is the paper’s counterexample to Necessity-Restricted All-Affected Stochastic Pareto, together with the ordinal generalization in Section 3. A continuum version is easy to write down: replace Ann and Bob with cohorts of positive mass, preserving their state-contingent welfare profiles. That is a coherent large-population model, and it preserves the authors’ ethical comparison.

But it remains a model of an axiological claim, not a continuous computational mirror. The paper asks whether one social ordering can conflict with a stated dominance principle. It does not study how to find or optimize over feasible prospects, how people can change them, or the complexity of any such task. Evaluating the ordering on an encoded pair of prospects is possible, but making that the mirror would add a computational question the paper does not pose. Adding a policy set and asking for its best prospect would add still more structure.

That also defeats the stronger version of the mirror: the cohorts make population mass meaningful, but they do not supply the missing algorithmic problem. The ordinal extension does not supply one either; it generalizes the ethical argument over an abstract ordered utility set, without specifying a computational representation for that set or its generalized average. These results can be valuable for continuous population ethics, but the programme’s scope is computational questions about a continuous society.

So the negative case is strong on the ChoCo criterion, though not an impossibility claim about all future work: someone could build a computational project around ex post average welfare, but that would be a new project, not a mirror of this paper’s contribution.
