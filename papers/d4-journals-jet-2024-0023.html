The best unanchored follow-on I can see is priority-group mass matching. In a large exchange pool, let each complete type encode a recipient’s and donor’s clinical phenotypes, priority group, and participation status. Give each type \(t\) rational mass \(\mu_t\). For each permitted cycle or chain configuration \(k\), choose a nonnegative mass \(x_k\), subject to the capacity constraint that configurations use at most \(\mu_t\) of each type. Optimize lexicographically: first maximize matched mass in each priority group, then maximize the mass receiving fully compatible kidneys. One could ask for the complexity of finding an optimal allocation from the type masses and compatibility data.

A plausible regime would be a large regional or international pool with many pairs drawn from relatively few complete clinical types. But that is a substantial qualification: kidney compatibility is individualized, and merging pairs into bins that omit clinically relevant differences would no longer preserve the paper’s problem. This is a possible research direction, not a mirror anchored by a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror anchored in this paper is that it contains no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes conflict-proofness using pure simplicial complexes; Propositions 2–5 characterize exchange structures; and the later propositions concern Pareto efficiency and classes of matchings. They are structural results, not complexity classifications or algorithmic theorems. The paper mentions sequential optimization, but does not prove a computational result of its own. So the proponent raised no qualifying anchor.

Their proposed priority-group mass-matching problem is the strongest follow-on, and it is coherent: give each complete clinical type a rational mass, allow that mass to be split among exchange configurations, and lexicographically maximize matched mass across priority groups. But this formulates a new optimization problem rather than continuizing a computational result in the paper. Preserving the paper’s arbitrary compatibility graphs could require each pair’s type to encode its individual compatibility pattern, leaving little repeated mass. Using clinical types to obtain a compact support is more plausible, but changes the model by requiring compatibility to follow from type-level data. The mass version also turns priorities over named recipients into priorities over groups.

Those are genuine costs, but they do not defeat the best version. Large pools with repeated clinical types are conceivable, and the paper’s priority-group objectives offer a natural basis for a continuous allocation question. I can make a strong case that this paper is a poor *anchored* target for the programme.
