The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest positive case is that large school assignment is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many incoming students can share a complete type comprising their school ranking and outcome-score vector, with \(\mu_t\) representing each type’s share and school capacities scaled to population size. But the paper supplies no qualifying anchor for that mirror. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result.

Section 2.2 does say that the mechanism’s feasibility checks are standard linear assignment problems solvable in polynomial time. That is an unnumbered implementation claim, not a named result of the kind you require. The paper’s strategy-proofness and constrained-efficiency claims are also unnumbered, and neither is a computational result. I therefore cannot give the required named anchor or a continuous problem *per anchor* without misrepresenting the paper. A mass-matching LP that merely enforces capacities and the average-score threshold would be a plausible allocation problem, but it would not capture the paper’s sequential priority mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent does not identify a qualifying anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its computational claim is that the mechanism repeatedly solves standard linear assignment problems, for which polynomial algorithms are known. Its strategy-proofness and constrained-efficiency results are axiomatic, not computational. That leaves no named computational result for the proposed mirror to continue.

School assignment is still a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large district could have many students sharing a complete type of preferences and outcome scores, with school capacities scaled to population mass. That is evidence the regime makes sense, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The suggested mass-allocation LP is the strongest rescue, but it solves a different problem. With \(x_{t\ell}\) denoting mass of type \(t\) assigned to location \(\ell\), capacities and the score threshold give a finite LP:
\[ \sum_\ell x_{t\ell}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t\ell}\le q_\ell,\qquad \sum_{t,\ell}g_t(\ell)x_{t\ell}\ge\bar g,\qquad x_{t\ell}\ge0. \]
It can find a feasible assignment or the highest attainable average score. But it does not reproduce the paper’s defining rule: process named agents in priority order, giving each their best location that still permits a feasible completion. The LP’s allocation need not be the mechanism’s allocation, and it does not establish the mechanism’s strategy-proofness. Its finite formulation is also an ordinary transportation-style optimization problem; the paper already says its discrete feasibility checks are polynomial-time assignment problems.

A more faithful continuous model would have to preserve priority position alongside type. Type masses alone omit the sequence in which different types meet scarce seats and the score constraint. Add each agent’s priority rank to the type, and the population no longer compresses into a moderate finite support. Replace the permutation with a continuous priority coordinate, and one has a fluid version of the mechanism whose input and feasibility checks need a new formulation. That could be worth studying, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result established in this paper.

The paper’s experiments on reordering do not supply a better anchor either. They report variation across sampled orders and test a variance-based ordering heuristic; they do not state a complexity result. Optimizing an order for the top-three metric could be formulated as a new problem, but using reported preferences to select the order conflicts with strategy-proofness, while selecting it from predicted preferences changes the problem being studied.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper is a poor ChoCo target: its stated computation is already polynomial, and its most natural aggregate LP drops the priority mechanism that gives the paper its identity. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be devised in any scenario. Large-scale school assignment is a credible population regime, and a carefully defined fluid priority model might be interesting. That possibility is the weak point in the universal negative case; it does not, however, rescue the anchors actually offered here.
