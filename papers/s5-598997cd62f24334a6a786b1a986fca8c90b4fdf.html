Under the maximum cost objective, any deterministic strategyproof mechanism has an approximation ratio of at least \(3\).

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite multiset \(A\) of at least two rational site slots and a finite-support rational location distribution \(\mu\) for agents interested in both facilities, determine \(\inf_F\sup_{A,\mu}\mathrm{MC}_{\mu}(F(A,\mu))/\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{MC}}(A,\mu)\), where \(F\) deterministically selects two distinct slots and is group-strategyproof against every positive-mass coalition's location-report changes; is this optimum exactly \(3\)?

The model it lives in

Types are locations \(x\in\mathbb{Q}\), with rational masses \(\mu_x\); every agent is interested in both facilities. The feasible outcomes are pairs of distinct slots from the finite multiset \(A\). For an outcome \(y=(y_1,y_2)\), \(\mathrm{MC}_{\mu}(y)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\max_{j\in\{1,2\}}|x-y_j|\). The decision is a deterministic facility-location rule, evaluated by its worst-case ratio to the minimum feasible max cost and constrained by group strategyproofness against positive-mass coalitions.

The objection that survived

The max-cost objective gives every supported location equal influence regardless of its mass, so the mirror retains a support-extremal incentive question rather than making welfare sensitive to population proportions; this bounds the mirror's reach but does not invalidate it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the compulsory setting's max-cost threshold: Mechanism 2's ratio-\(3\) group-strategyproof guarantee and the lower bound excluding deterministic strategyproof ratios below \(3\). It leaves the sum-cost results, both optional-setting theorems, the optimal-solution lemmas, and the one-facility subroutines outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact ratio-\(3\) characterization extend from finite-support rational distributions to arbitrary probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\) under a well-defined positive-mass group-deviation notion?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the compulsory, maximum-cost part of the paper. One caveat up front: the supplied text prints no theorem numbers, and it contains no NP-hardness, polynomial-time classification, or parameterized-complexity result. The anchors below are the unnumbered theorem blocks identified by their content; both are proved in the paper.

Take a society to be a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}w_t\delta_{x_t}\), where each type is a location \(x_t\) and every agent is interested in both facilities. Mass \(w_t\) is the fraction of residents at that location. The candidate set \(A\) is the paper’s multiset of site slots, so a feasible outcome selects two distinct slots, even if they share a coordinate. For outcome \(y=(y_1,y_2)\), define
\[ \mathrm{MC}_{\mu}(y)=\max_{t:w_t>0}\max\{|x_t-y_1|,\ |x_t-y_2|\}, \qquad \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\mu)=\min_{y\text{ feasible}}\mathrm{MC}_{\mu}(y). \]
The maximum over the support is exactly the continuous version of the worst-served agent’s cost.

This is plausible for large cohorts of residents at shared addresses along a corridor who all need access to two complementary services, with facilities restricted to designated sites. There may be millions of residents but only \(\tau\) distinct location types. The objective and facility decision remain the paper’s; the population is represented by masses.

The first anchor is the unnumbered theorem in Section 3.2 stating that under the maximum-cost objective, every deterministic strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio at least \(3\). It is proved here, in Appendix A.6. Its continuous question is whether any deterministic mechanism that is immune to profitable positive-mass coalitions can guarantee a ratio below \(3\), across all candidate multisets and finite-support societies. A solution would be an impossibility proof. The paper’s two-agent witness becomes two atoms of mass \(1/2\): the same site multiset and the same location reports reproduce the lower-bound argument. This is a transfer of a strategic lower bound from a discrete witness, not a claim of computational hardness.

The second anchor is the unnumbered theorem following Mechanism 2: Mechanism 2 is group strategyproof and has approximation ratio \(3\) under the maximum-cost objective. This is also proved here, in Appendix A.7. Its continuous problem is to construct a deterministic rule \(F(A,\mu)\) that is group strategyproof against reassignments of positive mass and always satisfies
\[ \mathrm{MC}_{\mu}(F(A,\mu))\le 3\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\mu). \]
The paper’s rule gives a concrete solution: choose the adjacent pair in \(AP\) that is the peak of the leftmost supported location. The proof extends naturally to finite-support masses: the rule depends on the leftmost type, the max-cost guarantee depends on the support’s extremes, and a coalition that changes the pivotal leftmost report contains mass that cannot all benefit from the change. Computing the rule takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. This is the stronger constructive anchor and points toward tractability, Class A.

Together, these questions ask whether the best max-cost approximation achievable by a mass-group-strategyproof rule is exactly \(3\). The lower-bound proof supplies the “no better than \(3\)” side; the explicit rule supplies the “\(3\) is attainable” side. The paper covers only this compulsory max-cost result in my mirror, not its optional-setting bounds or its sum-cost results. Further questions include whether the optional setting admits analogous mass-based guarantees and whether the conclusions extend from finite supports to general measures.

The weakest point is the incentive notion. In a nonatomic population, one person cannot change \(\mu\), so ordinary individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. The mirror therefore uses positive-mass coalition deviations. That is a real model co-modification, though group strategyproofness is already central to the paper and the two-atom lower-bound witness survives it. There is also a narrower fit with ChoCo: this paper supplies an approximation-mechanism result and a tractable rule, but no computational-complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about fit with ChoCo’s question, not whether the proposed mirrors are mathematically coherent. The paper’s theorem statements concern strategyproofness and approximation ratios. They do not classify the complexity of computing a solution to a computational problem. In particular, Mechanism 2’s output can be found from the leftmost report and the finite set \(AP\); the optimum for the maximum-cost objective can be found from the center and \(AP\). With support size \(\tau\), these are direct computations. A continuous version of either theorem would ask whether a rule has a certain incentive and welfare guarantee, not whether an algorithmic problem becomes tractable or hard.

The two max-cost anchors are coherent, but their mass interpretation adds little to the objective. For a distribution \(\mu\),
\[ \mathrm{MC}_{\mu}(y)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\max_j |y_j-x|. \]
Thus the weights do not affect welfare: an arbitrarily small positive mass at an extreme location matters as much as a large mass there. The lower-bound witness with two locations of mass \(1/2\) preserves the strategic argument, but that makes it a transfer of a two-agent incentive impossibility, not a new computational result about a continuous society. The upper-bound rule likewise selects from the leftmost support location; its guarantee uses the support’s extremes. These are natural statements about mass-based strategyproofness, but they remain distortion results.

A stronger alternative would use the sum-cost results, where weights matter: replace sums by weighted integrals, use weighted medians, and ask for mass-group-strategyproof mechanisms with bounded approximation. The optional setting could similarly use types \((x,p)\). Those are plausible high-multiplicity models, and I would not dismiss them on the ground that large resident cohorts are unrealistic. But the questions they yield are still about incentive compatibility and approximation guarantees. The paper’s finite outcome set also makes evaluating candidate facility pairs straightforward from the support; continuization does not expose a hard pricing problem or another computational bottleneck of the kind ChoCo is organized to study.

So I would reject these anchors as worthwhile ChoCo computational mirrors, while conceding that they could be worthwhile continuations of mechanism-design research. The negative case is weaker on modeling: positive-mass coalitional deviations are a defensible co-modification, and the two-location witness transfers cleanly. Its force comes from the programme’s computational scope, not from showing that a continuous population model is nonsensical.
