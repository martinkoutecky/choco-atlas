Theorem 7

The continuous mirror question

Given \(C\), rational \(\epsilon,\delta\), and one-pass access to an ordered sequence \(((t_i,a_i))_{i=1}^q\) of positive rational mass increments summing to \(1\), where each \(t_i\in\{(+,c),(-,c):c\in C\}\), let \(\mu_t=\sum_{i:t_i=t}a_i\). For \(m=|C|\ge 1/\epsilon\) and \(1-\delta>3\epsilon/2\), what is the minimum worst-case memory for a randomized algorithm that outputs, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), a candidate \(c\) for which some transfer \(x_{t,t'}\ge0\), with \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\le\epsilon\) and \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\), makes \(c\) maximize the resulting score \(\mu'_{(+,c)}-\mu'_{(-,c)}\), where \(\mu'_t=\mu_t-\sum_{u\ne t}x_{t,u}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{u,t}\)?

The model it lives in

A society has at most \(2m\) types: approving or disapproving one candidate. Its mass arrives in an arbitrary ordered stream of rational increments; the algorithm makes one pass and must return a candidate that can win after moving at most \(\epsilon\) mass. The order and memory restriction retain the paper's streaming question, while replacing individual votes with population mass.

The objection that survived

The result's difficulty depends on ordered one-pass access: from a final support list, summing the \(2m\) masses gives an exact winner immediately. The proponent flags, but does not settle, whether online mass streams belong to the programme's input model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers Theorem 7's generalized-plurality space lower bound only; it leaves the paper's other voting-rule bounds, upper bounds, and sliding-window results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For arbitrary rational mass increments, what is the tight one-pass space complexity in \(m,\tau,L,\epsilon,\delta\)?
  • Does the equal-mass embedding characterize the lower bound, or do arbitrary rational increments yield stronger bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow, online mirror of the paper’s generalized-plurality result. In a large participatory poll, each person can be treated as one of at most \(2m\) types: approve candidate \(c\), or disapprove candidate \(c\). Millions of submissions may arrive in arbitrary order while the number of types stays small. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s notion of an \(\epsilon\)-winner already asks how much of the population’s voting mass would need to change.

My anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper by a reduction from Augmented-indexing, using a communication lower bound cited there. Its full result says: when \(m\ge 1/\epsilon\), every one-pass \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-Winner Determination algorithm for approval, \(k\)-approval for \(k=O(m^\lambda)\) with \(\lambda\in[0,1)\), generalized plurality, Borda, maximin, Copeland, and plurality with run off uses \(\Omega((1-\delta)(1/\epsilon)\log(1/\epsilon))\) bits, even when the number of votes is known exactly, provided \(1-\delta>3\epsilon/2\). I rely on its generalized-plurality clause.

The corresponding problem is One-pass Generalized-plurality Mass-\(\epsilon\)-Winner. An instance has candidates \(C\), rational \(\epsilon\), and an ordered stream \(S=((t_i,a_i))_{i=1}^q\) of positive rational mass increments, with total mass \(1\). Each type \(t_i\) is one of the \(2m\) approve/disapprove types. The stream defines a society \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the total mass of type \(t\). A one-pass randomized algorithm must output a candidate \(c\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\), for which there is a mass transfer \(x_{t,t'}\ge 0\) changing total mass at most \(\epsilon\), such that \(c\) has maximum generalized-plurality score afterward. Here the score is \(\mu_{(+,c)}-\mu_{(-,c)}\); the solution is the candidate, as in the paper, and the objective is to minimize the algorithm’s worst-case memory. Ordinary elections are the special case of \(n\) arrivals, each of mass \(1/n\).

In that ordered-stream model, I expect a hardness-transfer result in the programme’s sense: the discrete equal-mass streams are instances of the continuous mass-stream problem, so Theorem 7’s space lower bound carries over. This is a memory lower bound, not an NP-hardness claim. It also invites a concrete next question: how do the upper and lower bounds change when arrivals carry arbitrary rational masses, with complexity stated in \(m,\tau,L,\epsilon,\delta\)?

The soft spot is substantial: the lower bound is driven by arbitrary arrival order and the one-pass memory restriction. If the input is simply the final support list of masses \(\mu_t\), generalized-plurality scores can be summed directly and an exact winner is automatically an \(\epsilon\)-winner. So this is a credible continuous mirror only if the authors and referee accept an online stream of population mass as part of their problem. It supports a case for a focused mirror of the paper’s online result, not for continuizing its entire streaming complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious objection is about the input model. The paper’s Theorem 7 is a named computational result, and the proposed generalized-plurality population is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: it has at most \(2m\) types, with score \(\mu_{(+,c)}-\mu_{(-,c)}\). Neither the fact that the lower bound may carry over nor the possibility that the answer is unchanged counts against asking the question.

The proposed mirror gets its difficulty from feeding the society to the algorithm as an ordered stream and limiting it to one pass of memory. In ChoCo’s central model, the input is the final support list of masses. For generalized plurality, summing those masses gives every candidate’s exact score, and an exact winner is already an \(\epsilon\)-winner. The stream formulation therefore asks about memory under a particular arrival protocol; it does not expose a difficulty created by representing the population as mass. This is the best case against treating it as a mirror of the programme’s distribution-input problems.

A better version would let rational mass increments arrive online, perhaps asking for a winner at each prefix or over a sliding window. But that preserves the same dependence on sequence, access restriction, and memory. The population is continuous in that model, yet the computational question is chiefly about processing a stream of weighted updates. If the programme accepts such online mass streams as in scope, Theorem 7’s equal-mass instances make this a credible mirror. I do not see an honest way to defeat that version on the stated grounds.

So the negative case is weak. The anchor is not already answered continuously, and the stream-versus-support distinction is a scope argument, not a fundamental obstruction. If the authors count online mass streams as societies given by distributions, this anchor survives.
