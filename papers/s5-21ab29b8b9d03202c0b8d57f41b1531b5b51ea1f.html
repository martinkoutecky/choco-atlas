The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives and a finite support of complete acceptability types \(t\), each with utility vector \(u_t\), threshold \(\theta_t\), and rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to one, decide whether there is a lottery \(x\in\Delta(S)\) such that \(\sum_{t:\langle u_t,x\rangle\ge\theta_t}\mu_t\ge\rho\), for a given rational quota \(\rho\); equivalently, compute the maximum accepted mass. Is this problem NP-hard, including when utilities are binary and the masses come from the paper’s clique construction?

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete utility-and-threshold profiles. A lottery \(x\in\Delta(S)\) is accepted by type \(t\) when \(\langle u_t,x\rangle\ge\theta_t\); its accepted population mass is \(W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t:\langle u_t,x\rangle\ge\theta_t}\mu_t\). The decision objective is to find \(x\) with \(W_\mu(x)\ge\rho\), or certify that none exists.

The objection that survived

Requiring a type to share an exact utility vector and threshold may describe only narrow stakeholder cohorts; the standardized-rubric scenario makes that regime plausible but does not establish how common it is.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem B.3’s hardness result for maximum acceptance. It leaves the paper’s unanimous-query algorithms and lower bounds, and its learning-augmented results, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does maximum accepted mass admit approximation algorithms under restrictions on \(m\) or support size \(\tau\)?
  • Which structured families of utility-and-threshold profiles make this optimization tractable?
  • How does the complexity change when a small positive-mass type can veto the lottery, returning the objective to unanimity?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Maximum-Acceptance Lottery, a population-mass version of the paper’s \(k\)-ACCEPT-LOTTERY problem.

A type \(t\) is a complete acceptability profile: a utility vector \(u_t\in[0,1]^m\) over the \(m\) alternatives and a threshold \(\theta_t\). The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\), with positive binary-rational masses summing to one. For a lottery \(x\in\Delta(S)\), type \(t\) accepts when \(\langle u_t,x\rangle\ge\theta_t\), so its accepted population mass is
\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t:\,\langle u_t,x\rangle\ge\theta_t}\mu_t. \]
Given a rational quota \(\rho\), the problem asks whether there is a lottery \(x\) with \(W_\mu(x)\ge\rho\); a yes-solution is such an \(x\). Equivalently, optimize \(W_\mu(x)\). The input records the supported types and their masses, so its natural parameters include \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

The regime is a large population facing a fixed menu of deployment modes, with many people sharing exact evaluation profiles and minimum bars—for example, user cohorts evaluated under standardized task and risk rubrics. Here \(n\), the number of people, can greatly exceed \(\tau\), the number of distinct profiles. This is a real restriction: a type must agree on the entire utility vector and threshold, not just on a broad label. The reduction below also gives a concrete high-multiplicity family: it has \(n=Rm+m+M\) people but only \(\tau=2m+M\) types, where \(M\) is the number of graph edges and \(R=m+M+1\); the ratio \(n/\tau\) grows with \(m\).

The anchor is Theorem B.3, proved in the paper: \(k\)-ACCEPT-LOTTERY is NP-hard even when every utility \(u_i(s_j)\) is binary. The paper proves it by reducing from CLIQUE. Its construction groups agents into \(R\) copies of each vertex’s upper-bound profile, one lower-bound profile per vertex, and one profile per edge. For a graph with \(m\) vertices and \(M\) edges, these are \(2m+M\) distinct profiles. Under the mass formulation, give each upper-bound type mass \(R/n\), and each other type mass \(1/n\). Set \(\rho=(Rm+\kappa+\binom{\kappa}{2})/n\), where \(\kappa\) is the requested clique size. The paper’s proof shows that some lottery reaches this quota exactly when the graph has a \(\kappa\)-clique: the vertex profiles constrain which alternatives can receive probability, and the edge profiles count pairs receiving the required mass. Thus the discrete instance embeds as a rational population distribution, and the reduction’s combinatorics remain in the alternatives and their edge relations.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. For any discrete instance, group agents with identical complete profiles into types and set each type’s mass to its count divided by \(n\). The accepted mass is then exactly the accepted-agent count divided by \(n\), so a quota \(k\) becomes \(\rho=k/n\). In the reverse direction, the paper’s clique reduction already has polynomially encoded rational masses. This is not hardness caused only by the number of people: the alternatives encode the graph vertices, and the edge profiles enforce the clique condition.

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous-population analogue. They explicitly introduce maximizing the number of accepting agents as a natural relaxation of unanimity and prove Theorem B.3 for it. Replacing counts by population fractions preserves that objective under normalization. The mirror keeps the paper’s lottery over alternatives and changes the society into a distribution over complete stakeholder types. It covers Theorem B.3’s maximum-acceptance result; it does not claim to mirror the main query-complexity theorems for unanimous feasibility.

The weakest point is exactly that scope: this is the paper’s appendix result for a relaxation, not its central unanimous-query problem, and the mirror assumes the type profiles and masses are given explicitly. Still, it is a named hardness result that the authors themselves identify as a meaningful extension, and its hardness survives a genuine high-multiplicity encoding.

It raises useful follow-up questions: can maximizing accepted mass be approximated under restrictions on \(m\) or \(\tau\)? Which structured profile families permit tractable optimization? And how does the picture change if the goal returns to unanimity, where even a very small positive-mass type can veto a lottery?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this mirror sits at the edge of the paper’s main question. Theorem B.3 switches from learning unknown constraints through queries to a full-information optimization problem. Its mass version asks for a lottery maximizing
\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t:\langle u_t,x\rangle\ge\theta_t}\mu_t, \]
a weighted maximum-feasible-subsystem problem. The paper’s LP feasibility machinery for unanimity does not carry over to this objective, and the stated hardness reduction uses multiplicities to weight gadgets built from alternatives and graph edges. That makes the result less informative about whether continuous optimization helps with the paper’s central elicitation problem.

There is also a modeling concern. A type must share the *entire* utility vector and threshold. In the motivating deployment setting, many affected users may share a rubric or broad risk category while still having different cardinal evaluations or minimum bars. The reduction supplies repeated exact profiles, but they are engineered to enforce the CLIQUE constraints. That establishes a hard instance regime; it gives limited evidence that the same regime is compelling for real stakeholder populations.

Those are reasons to regard this as a weaker mirror, not reasons that defeat it. The mirror is well posed, Theorem B.3 is a named computational result, and the authors explicitly identify maximum acceptance as a natural relaxation. The reduction survives the population model exactly: set each type’s mass to its multiplicity divided by \(n\) and set \(\rho=k/n\). Its support has \(2m+M\) types while the population has \(Rm+m+M\) agents, with \(R=m+M+1\); the population-to-support ratio grows with \(m\), and the masses have polynomial bit length. Every rational finite-support distribution can also be expanded into a discrete electorate after multiplying by a common denominator, though that denominator can be large relative to the encoded input. That compression is precisely the high-multiplicity distinction.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The strongest negative case is that it is a narrower, less central, and potentially less representative mirror than the query-feasibility results. But the programme’s stated criteria admit a mirror of one named result, and the exact mass formulation clears that bar.
