The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets on both sides, rational type masses, compatible pairs, strict rankings over counterpart types with unmatched last, and a critical label for each type, model each type as an atomless population. A matching pairs equal-mass subsets one-to-one along compatible pairs; it is critical if it maximizes the mass of matched critical individuals. For two matchings, define the vote margin by comparing each same individual’s assigned counterpart type, counting the mass who prefer the assignment in \(N\) minus the mass who prefer it in \(M\). Does a popular critical matching \(M\), satisfying \(\Delta(N,M)\leq 0\) for every critical \(N\), always exist, and can one with maximum matched mass be computed in time polynomial in the number of types, compatible pairs, and input bit length?

The model it lives in

Use atomless populations for each type, with rational masses and shared rankings over counterpart types. The decision is a measurable one-to-one mass pairing; criticality maximizes matched critical mass, and popularity compares the same individuals across pairings. Finite type-to-type flows describe feasible pairings, while within-type assignment overlaps preserve voter margins between competing pairings.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 4 and 5: existence and maximum- or minimum-size optimization for popular critical matchings. It does not cover the paper’s background results on other matching objectives.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every such rational typed market have a popular critical matching, and can a maximum-size one be computed in time polynomial in the type support, compatible pairs, and input bit length?
  • Under the same model, can a minimum-size popular critical matching be computed with those complexity bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s popular critical matching problem in a market with many interchangeable agents. Picture a large centralized placement market: residents and hospitals, or workers and employers, arrive in sizable cohorts with the same eligibility, critical status, and preferences over counterpart types. The number of people \(n\) is large while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) is much smaller. This is a scenario, not a claim that every matching market has a useful high-multiplicity regime.

An instance has finite type sets \(\mathcal T_A,\mathcal T_B\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatible pairs of types, a strict ranking of eligible opposite-side types for each type, and a critical/noncritical label. Members of a type share all these properties and are indifferent among individual members of any one counterpart type. That last feature is a deliberate co-modification: the paper ranks named partners strictly, whereas a finite type description treats otherwise-identical partners as interchangeable.

Represent each type’s population as an interval of length \(\mu_t\). A matching is a measurable, one-to-one pairing of equal-mass subsets on the two sides, using only compatible type pairs; each individual is matched to at most one partner, and unmatched is the worst option. A critical matching maximizes the mass of matched critical individuals. For two matchings \(M,N\), define \(\Delta(N,M)\) as the mass of individuals who prefer their assignment under \(N\) to their assignment under \(M\), minus the mass who prefer \(M\). A critical matching \(M\) is popular if \(\Delta(N,M)\leq 0\) for every critical \(N\). Its size \(s(M)\) is the matched mass on side \(A\), equal to that on side \(B\). A solution can be given as finitely many interval pairings with rational endpoints; popularity is still tested against all measurable competing matchings.

My lead is continuous max-size popular critical matching. Its anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for every critical set \(C\), popular critical matchings exist, and a largest one can be computed in \(O(|C|m+m)\) time in the finite graph. The mirror asks: given the finite type data and rational masses, does a popular critical matching always exist, and can one be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of compatible type pairs, and the input bit length \(L\), with \(s(M)\) largest among all popular critical matchings? This preserves the theorem’s two claims—existence and largest size—while asking whether the population can be represented through its support rather than expanded into individual agents.

A second, separate question is continuous min-size popular critical matching. Its anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: from a marriage instance and critical set \(C\), a smallest popular critical matching can be computed in the same \(O(|C|m+m)\) time. The mirror asks for a popular critical matching of minimum \(s(M)\) under the same type-based input and popularity definition, again with a polynomial-in-\(\tau,q,L\) algorithm as the target. I expect both questions to be tractable, Class A: the paper already builds its algorithms around stable-matchings machinery and LP-dual witnesses, and a finite type support suggests a route to mass-based optimization. The continuous questions are whether those witnesses and constructions survive fractional masses and admit a finite, polynomial-size description.

These two theorems are the right anchors because the article gives no numbered hardness result for its main problem. It does mention a cited NP-hardness result for minimizing blocking edges in maximum matchings, but that is background about a different objective, not a numbered result of this paper; I would not use it as an anchor. This mirror covers Theorems 4 and 5 only. The matching stays one-to-one at the individual level; continuity is in the population mass, not the outcomes.

The main risk is that popularity depends on comparing the *same voters* across two matchings. A type-to-type mass matrix alone may lose which members of a cohort receive which counterpart types, and that alignment can affect the vote margin. The mirror therefore retains measurable subsets of individuals rather than declaring a flow matrix to be the whole solution. Whether that identity information can be handled with a finite polynomial-size representation is part of the question, not something the paper’s finite algorithm settles. The tied-within-type preference assumption is the other soft spot; it is a plausible clone-market model, but it is a genuine change from the paper’s strict rankings over named partners.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a model-fidelity problem shared by Theorems 4 and 5. The paper gives every vertex a strict ranking of named neighbors, and popularity compares the votes of those same vertices across matchings. If cohorts retain those named-person rankings, their descriptions grow with the population: there is no fixed finite support of types that records the full preferences. To get a finite type model, the proposed mirror ranks counterpart types and treats members of a type as interchangeable. That is a coherent co-modification, but it changes what a voter compares.

A type-to-type mass matrix alone does not record enough for those comparisons. For example, let one cohort be split into three equal groups with preferences \(x \succ y \succ z\). Matching \(M\) assigns them respectively to \(x,y,z\); matching \(N\) assigns them to \(y,z,x\). Two thirds prefer \(M\). Reverse the reassignment while keeping the same type-to-type masses, and two thirds prefer the alternative to \(M\). Thus the same aggregate flows can have different pairwise voter margins. An algorithm that treats a flow matrix as the whole matching cannot simply inherit the paper’s popularity definition.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror, though. A matching can retain measurable subsets of individuals, as the proponent proposes, and the relevant overlap between assignments may be represented by joint masses over finitely many types and assignment categories. This makes the theorem’s algorithms far from automatic, but the identity issue alone does not establish that a finite representation or efficient method is impossible. Cohorts with shared preferences over counterpart categories also remain a plausible regime for placement markets or sailor-and-billet assignments.

The objection applies equally to both anchors: Theorem 4’s maximum-size popular critical matching and Theorem 5’s minimum-size one optimize over the same popularity condition. Each is a named computational result, and neither is already answered by a continuous-population treatment in the material here. The honest negative case is therefore weak: the mirrors require a careful account of identity and voter coupling, but I cannot show that the best version fails or that no worthwhile scenario exists. On this record, both anchors survive.
