The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) divisible goods and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete trader types \(\theta=(w_\theta,u_\theta)\), with strongly connected economy graph and \(u_\theta(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{\theta,j}(x_j)\) having at most \(2\) segments, \(27\)-bounded slopes, and \(23\)-sparse support, find a normalized rational price vector \(p\) and per-type bundles \(x_\theta\in\mathbb{R}_+^n\) such that each \(x_\theta\) maximizes \(u_\theta\) subject to \(p\cdot x_\theta\le p\cdot w_\theta\), and \(\left|\sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta,j}-\sum_\theta\mu_\theta w_{\theta,j}\right|\le n^{-13}\sum_\theta\mu_\theta w_{\theta,j}\) for every good \(j\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity Arrow–Debreu exchange economy with finite divisible goods, type masses \(\mu_\theta\), per-capita endowments \(w_\theta\), additive PLC utilities \(u_\theta\), price vector \(p\), per-type optimal bundles \(x_\theta\), and aggregate approximate-clearing constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3 and its Sparse Market PPAD-hardness reduction. It does not independently mirror Theorem 2's existence and rationality statement, the price-regulation property, or the paper's auxiliary lemmas and cited algorithms.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does PPAD-hardness persist when \(|\Theta|\) is bounded independently of \(n\)?
  • What is the complexity as a function of \(n\), \(|\Theta|\), and the encoding length of rational masses?
  • Do fixed-\(n\) or fixed-\(|\Theta|\) versions admit polynomial-time algorithms?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity Arrow–Debreu mirror of the paper’s main problem. It is a strong hardness mirror, although not a claim that the continuum makes this market problem easier.

A trader type is the complete profile

\[ \theta=(w_\theta,u_\theta), \qquad u_\theta(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{\theta,j}(x_j), \]

where \(w_\theta\) is the trader’s per-capita endowment and \(u_\theta\) is an additively separable PLC utility function. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many such types. Thus \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of the trader population having exactly that endowment and utility schedule. The aggregate supply of good \(j\) is

\[ S_j(\mu)=\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta w_{\theta,j}. \]

The action variable is an allocation \(x_\theta\in\mathbb R_+^n\) for each type, together with a market price vector \(p\). Each type individually maximizes utility subject to the budget generated by its endowment,

\[ x_\theta\in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\ge 0} \left\{ u_\theta(x):p\cdot x\le p\cdot w_\theta \right\}. \]

The market-level task is to find a normalized rational price vector and such type-level allocations satisfying approximate aggregate clearing:

\[ \left| \sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta,j} - \sum_\theta\mu_\theta w_{\theta,j} \right| \le n^{-13} \sum_\theta\mu_\theta w_{\theta,j} \]

for every good \(j\). The goods remain finite and divisible exactly as in the paper; the new continuous object is the population measure \(\mu\), not the outcome space.

This is naturally a high-multiplicity regime. Think of a large exchange platform containing \(K\) copies of every role or contract profile: many households or institutions have the same portfolio and the same marginal utility schedule. If there are \(\tau\) profiles, the physical population has \(K\tau\) traders while the input records only the \(\tau\) types and their masses. In the paper’s hard family, the types are carefully engineered market roles, but each role can represent a large cohort rather than one named trader. This is a credible scenario for repeated standardized market participants, even though the number of distinct roles grows with the instance.

The anchor is Theorem 3 (Main), proved in this paper. It states:

Sparse Market is PPAD-complete.

Here Sparse Market takes as input a rational, strongly connected Arrow–Debreu market with additively separable PLC utilities satisfying all of the following:

  • every utility component has at most two linear segments;
  • every nonzero slope lies between \(1\) and \(27\);
  • every trader owns at most \(23\) goods initially;
  • every trader values at most \(23\) goods;
  • the output must be an \(n^{-13}\)-approximate market equilibrium, where \(n\) is the number of goods.

An approximate equilibrium consists of a normalized price vector \(p\) and an allocation in which every trader receives a utility-maximizing bundle at prices \(p\), while every good clears to relative error at most \(n^{-13}\). The paper proves PPAD-completeness by reducing from Sparse Bimatrix, whose PPAD-completeness is cited as Theorem 1 from Chen, Deng, and Teng [6]. Thus the anchor is not merely a formulation or an outside citation: the market-equilibrium hardness result itself is proved here.

I would formulate the continuous problem as Cohort Sparse Market Equilibrium\(_\infty\):

Given finitely many trader types \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), \(n\) divisible goods, rational per-capita endowments \(w_\theta\), and two-segment, \(27\)-bounded, \(23\)-sparse additive PLC utilities, find a normalized rational price vector \(p\) and type allocations \(x_\theta\) satisfying individual optimality and the aggregate clearing inequalities above.

This is not a weakened social-welfare surrogate. It asks exactly the underlying question computed in the paper: which prices support individually optimal trading while clearing the market? The individual objective remains utility maximization, and the global output remains an equilibrium price.

The paper’s finite hard problem embeds directly. Given an instance with \(M\) traders, make each trader one type, assign it mass \(1/M\), and retain its original endowment and utility. Then

\[ \left| \frac1M\sum_i x_{i,j} - \frac1M\sum_i w_{i,j} \right| \le n^{-13}\frac1M\sum_iw_{i,j} \]

is equivalent, after multiplying by \(M\), to the paper’s finite-market clearing condition. Individual optimality is unchanged. Moreover, the same continuous society can be realized physically by taking \(K\) identical copies of every type. Therefore any polynomial-time algorithm for Cohort Sparse Market Equilibrium\(_\infty\), running in the number of goods, types, and encoding length rather than the physical population, would solve Sparse Market. PPAD-hardness therefore transfers.

The reduction’s combinatorics live in the goods and in the price coordinates encoding the two-player game, not in the existence of individually named traders. Replicating each trader into a cohort does not remove the price-regulation gadget or the Nash-equilibrium constraints. I would consequently classify this mirror as Class B: hardness transfers. Under the usual weighted-market representation, I would expect the continuous problem also to lie in PPAD, hence to remain PPAD-complete, but the unconditional conclusion needed here is the transferred PPAD-hardness.

The main further questions are whether the hardness survives with a genuinely small type catalogue, whether fixed \(\tau\) or fixed \(n\) gives polynomial algorithms, and how the complexity changes when masses are arbitrary binary rationals rather than equal cohort shares. One could also ask whether the paper’s carefully engineered hardness survives under stronger economic restrictions, such as common endowment scales or fewer than \(23\) active goods per type.

My weakest point is semantic rather than computational: the paper’s hard instances have \(O(n^2)\) highly specialized trader profiles, so the proposed regime is “many copies of many roles,” not a fixed small set of homogeneous household classes. If “continuum” were required to mean fixed \(\tau\) as \(n\) grows, this particular reduction would not establish it. But under the programme’s stated high-multiplicity criterion, that is not fatal. Each type is a complete agent profile, each role has arbitrarily many identical agents, and the paper’s finite market embeds exactly into the population-distribution model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent’s mirror does not genuinely abstract the paper’s population. The reduction uses \(O(n^2)\) highly engineered trader roles, each carrying game-specific endowments and utility segments. Replacing each role by a type and assigning it mass \(1/M\) merely renames the original traders; the population multiplicity \(K\) is an irrelevant replication factor. A more genuinely continuous model with a fixed, small catalogue of household or trader types would not obviously preserve the price-regulation gadget or its pairwise payoff constraints.

That objection is ultimately insufficient under ChoCo’s rules. The paper has an explicit computational anchor: Theorem 3, asserting that Sparse Market is PPAD-complete. Its agents are fully described by endowments and utility functions, and the problem never tracks individual identities or compares allocations between named traders. Consequently, aggregation by complete type is lossless.

Given a finite instance with traders \(i=1,\ldots,M\), define \(\mu_i=1/M\), \(w_{\theta_i}=w_i\), and \(u_{\theta_i}=u_i\). Then the continuous clearing condition

\[ \left| \sum_i \mu_i x_{i,j}-\sum_i\mu_i w_{i,j} \right| \le \varepsilon\sum_i\mu_i w_{i,j} \]

is exactly the paper’s condition after multiplication by \(M\). Individual optimality is unchanged. Conversely, taking \(K\) identical copies of every trader realizes precisely this society with \(KM\) physical agents. There is no collapse of the objective, no vanishing cost, and no loss of the market’s price-regulation mechanism.

The stronger semantic scenario also survives: a market platform can contain large cohorts of agents sharing standardized endowment and utility profiles. The fact that the hard construction has many specialized profiles is not disqualifying; types are allowed to depend on the instance, just as voting type spaces grow with the number of candidates. Requiring a fixed small type catalogue would be an additional restriction, not a defect in this mirror.

Thus the negative case can fairly say that this is a Class B mirror whose main novelty lies in transferring population representation rather than in making the problem easier. But that is explicitly a worthwhile outcome in the programme. Theorem 3 therefore survives every serious objection, and I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror.
