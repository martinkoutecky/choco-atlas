The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper a compliant computational anchor. It has named implementation results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity.

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: for a finite type space, an SCF is implementable by contingent contracts if and only if it is acyclic, and every such SCF can also be implemented by a linear contract. Lemma 1, also proved there, gives the necessary direction: implementability implies acyclicity. Theorem 2, proved there, says every aggregate payoff maximizer with consistent tie-breaking is implementable. Theorem 3, proved there, characterizes aggregate payoff maximizers with consistent tie-breaking as exactly the implementable SCFs satisfying binary independence, in a finite rich type space. These are characterizations of implementability, not computational classifications; the constructive proof of Theorem 1 gives no stated running-time result. There is no hardness anchor either.

There is a plausible setting to explore: many small firms contracting with a common principal, with each firm’s payoff map \(v:A\to\mathbb{R}\) drawn from a small finite set. The mass \(\mu_v\) would be the share of firms with payoff type \(v\). A population-level rule could choose an alternative from the reported type distribution, with each firm receiving a report-contingent contract based on its realized payoff. But that proposal is not yet a faithful mirror of the paper’s problem: its dominant-strategy constraints concern each named agent’s report and the resulting alternative, while in an atomless population one agent’s report does not change the aggregate distribution. The paper does not analyze that change or give a result to anchor its complexity.

So the strongest honest positive case is only motivational: recurring payoff types make a high-multiplicity population plausible. Under the requested anchor standard, however, this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror, and I would not claim a continuous problem as its computational analogue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper supplies no computational anchor. Theorem 1, Lemma 1, and Theorems 2–3 characterize which social choice functions can be implemented; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The proof of Theorem 1 constructs a contract, but gives no running-time result or computational input model. Calling these results anchors would miss the programme’s stated gate.

The proposed population of firms—or a population of taxpayers with recurring payoff types—is plausible. High multiplicity is not the weak point: grouping agents with identical payoff maps is consistent with the paper’s model. The problem is what happens to its dominant-strategy constraints in an atomless society. If the selected alternative depends on the reported type distribution, one agent’s report does not change that distribution. With a contract held constant across reports, every report then gives the same outcome and payoff, so truthful reporting is weakly dominant for every aggregate social choice function. The paper’s implementability characterization becomes vacuous.

A more substantive version could let a positive-mass cohort deviate, but that changes the incentive notion from individual dominant strategies to bloc or coalitional deviations. Alternatively, retain finitely many agents, so each report changes the empirical distribution by \(1/n\); then the problem remains a finite-agent one, not its atomless mirror. Either repair could motivate new work, but neither mirrors a named result in this paper. Studying the complexity of checking acyclicity or constructing contracts would also be a new computational project, not a complexity result the authors established.

So the negative case is strong against a continuous mirror of this paper’s results: there is no computational result to mirror, and the most natural atomless reformulation erases the individual incentive constraints that give those results their content. I cannot honestly prove the universal claim that no scenario could ever make a related computational question worthwhile. A paper that added an algorithmic problem, or adopted a non-atomic coalition model, might create one. But that would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror supplied by this paper.
