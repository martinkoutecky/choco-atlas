The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_u>0\) summing to one, positive rational weights \(w_u\), nonnegative rational coefficients \(\kappa_{tu}\), a rational quota offset \(\theta\), a target type \(t\), and accuracy \(\epsilon>0\), form populations of size \(N\) with \(N\mu_u\) members of each type. Set \(q_N=N\bar w/2+\lfloor\theta\sqrt N\rfloor\), where \(\bar w=\sum_u\mu_u w_u\); for distinct members \(i,j\) of types \(t,u\), set \(a^{(N)}_{ij}=\lfloor\kappa_{tu}\sqrt N\rfloor/N\), with \(a^{(N)}_{ii}=1\). Choose uniformly a coalition containing a tagged type-\(t\) member, and let \(\beta_{t,N}\) be the probability that it meets the paper’s association-adjusted criticality condition. Compute the limit \(\lim_{N\to\infty}\beta_{t,N}\) to additive error \(\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A type-mass model with distribution \(\mu\), type weights \(w_u\), and type-level interaction coefficients \(\kappa_{tu}\). The target is the limiting criticality probability of a type-\(t\) member under the specified central quota and interaction scaling; it equals \(\Phi((\theta+R_t)/\sigma)-\Phi(\theta/\sigma)\), where \(R_t=\sum_u\mu_u\kappa_{tu}w_u\) and \(\sigma^2=\frac14\sum_u\mu_u w_u^2\).

The objection that survived

The original paper and its generic sampling lemma do not motivate the tuned \(N^{-1/2}\) association scale and central quota window; the proponent explains why they yield a nondegenerate limit, but does not establish that authors would find this regime representative.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the approximation of the association-adjusted index in Lemma 18 through its high-multiplicity limit; it leaves the paper’s bounds, comparisons with the ordinary index, and EU calculations outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine how the limit changes under other association scalings or for multiple quotas.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s association-adjusted Banzhaf index. Its anchor is Lemma 18 (Confidence intervals), proved in the paper using the cited Hoeffding inequality. For \(n\) sampled coalitions containing player \(p_i\), let \(X_k\) indicate whether \(p_i\) is critical in sample \(k\), and let \(\hat\beta_{\Phi,i}=\sum_{k=1}^n X_k/n\). Lemma 18 gives the interval \(\left[\hat\beta_{\Phi,i}-\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2n)},\ \hat\beta_{\Phi,i}+\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2n)}\right]\) at confidence at least \(1-\delta\). It also gives the large-sample \(t\)-interval \(\left[\hat\beta_{\Phi,i}-t_{\delta/2}S/\sqrt n,\ \hat\beta_{\Phi,i}+t_{\delta/2}S/\sqrt n\right]\), where \(S\) is the sample standard deviation. The first interval yields additive error \(\epsilon\) with \(n\ge \ln(2/\delta)/(2\epsilon^2)\) samples. This is a named randomized approximation result, though not a complexity-class theorem. The paper’s sentence about exact Banzhaf computation being NP-complete cites an outside result but is not itself a numbered theorem, so I do not use it as an anchor.

Call the mirror High-Multiplicity Association Banzhaf Limit. An instance has a fixed finite type set \(T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, positive rational voting weights \(w_t\), nonnegative rational interaction parameters \(\kappa_{tu}\), a rational quota offset \(\theta\), a target type \(t\), and an additive accuracy \(\epsilon\). A type completely specifies a member’s weight and its interaction with every type: members of type \(t\) have the same coefficient toward every member of type \(u\). The task is to output a number within \(\epsilon\) of the limiting absolute Banzhaf index of a type-\(t\) member.

Here is the finite population sequence that defines the limit. For each sufficiently large \(N\) divisible by the mass denominators, make \(N\mu_u\) members of each type \(u\). Give them weight \(w_u\), and set the quota to \(q_N=N\bar w/2+\lfloor\theta\sqrt N\rfloor\), where \(\bar w=\sum_u\mu_u w_u\). For distinct members \(i,j\) of types \(t,u\), set \(a^{(N)}_{ij}=\lfloor\kappa_{tu}\sqrt N\rfloor/N\), and set \(a^{(N)}_{ii}=1\). These are rational association matrices with entries in the paper’s allowed range for all sufficiently large \(N\). Choose uniformly among coalitions containing a tagged type-\(t\) member, just as in the paper, and call that member critical according to the paper’s association-adjusted definition. Let \(\beta_{t,N}\) be its probability of being critical. The requested value is \(\lim_{N\to\infty}\beta_{t,N}\).

This limit has a direct expression. Define \(\sigma^2=\frac14\sum_u\mu_u w_u^2\) and \(R_t=\sum_u\mu_u\kappa_{tu}w_u\). Then the answer is \(\Phi((\theta+R_t)/\sigma)-\Phi(\theta/\sigma)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. Under uniform coalition sampling, the coalition weight, centered at \(N\bar w/2\) and scaled by \(\sqrt N\), converges to a normal random variable with variance \(\sigma^2\). The association adjustment for a type-\(t\) member, scaled the same way, converges to \(R_t\). Thus the limiting critical event is that the normal value lies in \([\theta,\theta+R_t)\). A solution is a numerical approximation to that CDF difference; there is no optimization variable, and additive error is the objective.

This is a plausible regime for a large federation or electorate with many delegates in a small number of recurring affiliation and influence classes: \(N\) is large while \(\tau\) stays moderate. The coefficients model diffuse, individually weak persuasion links whose aggregate effect is on the scale of coalition-weight fluctuations. The mirror retains the weighted threshold game, the association-adjusted criticality test, and the probability over coalitions containing a representative member. In that sense it asks for the same type’s power, rather than replacing the question with a fractional outcome or an average over named players. For this single-quota mirror the limit is tractable: compute \(\sigma\) and \(R_t\), then evaluate a one-dimensional normal CDF. Alternatively, sample the limiting normal variable and test the critical event; Lemma 18(a)’s Hoeffding guarantee applies to those Bernoulli samples, with the same sample bound. The expected direction is therefore tractability.

The scenario is genuinely high multiplicity: finite populations with the same type proportions approximate the continuous society \(\mu\), and a type’s power is the power shared by its indistinguishable members. It also gives a formal discrete-to-continuous question: prove convergence of the finite indices to the displayed limit, and quantify the convergence rate.

The weakest point is the scaling. The quota is in the central window around the mean weight of a random coalition, and pairwise association coefficients shrink as \(N^{-1/2}\). Those choices produce a nondegenerate limit; fixed association coefficients or quotas far from that window can yield degenerate power instead. The mirror also assumes block-structured relationships: if every member has an idiosyncratic association row, then \(\tau\) may grow with \(N\) and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. I would still expect the authors to recognize this as a continuous asymptotic form of their problem, because it preserves their coalition sampling and criticality rule while making the population itself the limiting object. The scope is Lemma 18’s approximation of association-adjusted power, not the paper’s EU calculation or all of its theoretical bounds. Further questions include proving quantitative finite-\(N\) error bounds, treating multiple quotas via multivariate Gaussian probabilities, and understanding other association scalings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Lemma 18 is a generic sampling guarantee, not a result about the complexity of the association model. It applies whenever independent samples produce a yes/no event: here, whether a sampled coalition makes one player critical. It says how many samples estimate that event’s probability; it does not analyze high multiplicity, or even use the association structure. The paper’s NP-completeness claim is cited from earlier work on ordinary Banzhaf computation, not proved for this association-adjusted index. So the proposed mirror has a named lemma to point to, but not a complexity result whose computational content carries over.

There is also a mismatch between the paper’s central application and a population of repeated types. Its players are countries, and the association matrix records directed relationships between particular pairs of countries. A complete type would have to include a player’s weight and its relationship to every other type. In the EU example, that produces essentially unique types. A large legislature could provide many players, but grouping them requires block-constant relationships: everyone in a type must have the same weight and exert the same influence on every other type. That may describe a different, simplified model of factional influence; it discards the pair-specific soft-power information that motivates this paper’s index.

The proponent’s nonzero limit also relies on a particular population-size scaling. With \(a_{ij}=\Theta(N^{-1/2})\), a player’s total association adjustment grows as \(\Theta(\sqrt N)\), matching the fluctuations of a random coalition’s total weight. Under the more extensive scaling \(a_{ij}=\Theta(N^{-1})\), that adjustment stays bounded, while the coalition’s fluctuations remain \(\Theta(\sqrt N)\); the unscaled probability that a tagged player is critical then typically tends to zero. One could instead study \(\sqrt N\,\beta_{t,N}\), or use the proponent’s scaling, but those are additional asymptotic choices. The limiting society \(\mu\) alone does not select the association normalization, quota window, or coalition-sampling law.

The best alternative mirror is therefore a block-structured high-multiplicity sequence with an explicitly specified fluctuation model. The proposed Gaussian calculation is coherent for that sequence, and a legislature with genuinely repeated delegate types is not impossible. But it answers a specialized asymptotic question about random coalition fluctuations, rather than giving the paper’s general association-adjusted index a continuous-population computational formulation. The valid Hoeffding interval already estimates the finite instance without taking a population limit.

That is a case against prioritizing this paper, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proponent has supplied a mathematically plausible mirror of Lemma 18. The negative case is weakest precisely where the assignment demands universality: a block model and its large-population limit are conceivable, and the supplied material does not rule them out.
