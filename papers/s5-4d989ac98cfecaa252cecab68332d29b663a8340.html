What the mirror covers

No theorem yields a qualifying mirror; the cited results concern implementability and the structure of valuation domains, while the proposed population-level mechanism task is not established by them.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is a large market with many quasi-linear agents and a small number \(\tau\) of shared valuation types. A type would be a valuation vector \(v_t\in\mathbb{R}^A\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of agents with that vector. Given a type-level allocation rule \(q(t)\), one could ask whether there are type-level payments \(p(t)\) making truthful reporting optimal for every type. That is a well-posed high-multiplicity mechanism question.

But it is not anchored by a named computational result in this paper. Theorem 1, attributed to Rochet, characterizes implementability by cyclic monotonicity. Theorem 2, attributed to Saks and Yu, says deterministic allocation rules on convex valuation domains are implementable. Theorem 3, proved here, gives a monotone finite-valued non-implementable sub-probability rule when a domain of dimension at least two has non-convex closure. Theorem 11, also proved here, characterizes monotonicity domains through projection onto the zero-sum hyperplane. These are structural mechanism-design results; none asserts a complexity classification or algorithm for a computational problem.

That gap also weakens the proposed mirror itself: the paper studies properties of a single agent’s valuation domain, and the distribution \(\mu\) over a large population would not affect those properties. Its continuous valuation space and randomized allocation outcomes do not, by themselves, continuize the population. So although the market scenario is plausible, I cannot honestly present it as a qualifying continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own concession is decisive: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes implementability by cyclic monotonicity; Theorem 2 gives an implementability result on convex domains; Theorem 3 proves that certain domains admit a monotone but non-implementable rule; and Theorem 11 characterizes monotonicity domains via projection. These are structural results, not complexity claims or algorithms for a computational problem. Theorem 3’s existence of a counterexample is not a hardness result, and the paper gives no input representation for a computational task over arbitrary valuation domains.

The proposed large market does not repair that gap. The paper’s domain \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^A\) is the set of possible valuations for one agent; its randomized allocation rule maps a valuation to a lottery. Neither is a population distribution. For a type-level mechanism with allocation \(q(t)\) and payment \(p(t)\), dominant-strategy truthfulness compares each type’s utility from every report. The population masses \(\mu_t\) do not enter those inequalities. Repeating each type many times therefore leaves the paper’s implementability question unchanged. The paper’s single-agent reduction makes this especially clear: the results are about an agent’s valuation domain, not how many agents occupy each point in it.

The strongest salvage would be to define a new task: given rational type masses, optimize expected revenue, or test whether a finite type domain admits a monotone but non-implementable rule. The first makes the masses part of an objective, but is a new mechanism-design problem rather than a result in this paper. In the second, the masses still do not affect the property being tested; the task would concern an explicitly listed finite domain, and the paper supplies a characterization rather than a complexity result for that task. Coupling agents through aggregate feasibility could make population mass matter, but would require a new model and new theorems.

I cannot rule out a worthwhile adjacent project built from these ideas. The negative case is strong on the narrower question the paper supports: none of its results anchors a continuous-population computational mirror, and the proponent’s market scenario is a plausible extension rather than such a mirror.
