The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(x\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(B\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(p_{t,d}\) summing to \(\mu_t\) for each \(t\), where \(d\) ranges from \(0\) to the number of candidates ranked above \(x\) in \(t\), such that the average number of upward adjacent swaps is at most \(B\) and \(x\) strictly beats every other candidate pairwise in the resulting society.

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete ranking \(t\); mass \(\mu_t\) may be split among outcomes \(t[d]\), formed by moving \(x\) upward by \(d\) adjacent swaps. The decision variables are \(p_{t,d}\), the mass assigned to each outcome; the objective is to meet the average swap budget \(B\) while making \(x\) a strict Condorcet winner. Pairwise margins and average swap cost are linear in these variables.

What the mirror covers

Covers the budgeted decision problem underlying Theorem 6, but not its kernelization lower bound as such; it leaves Theorem 9's \(n\)-parameterized hardness without a proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the integrality gap between the minimum continuous average swap cost and the discrete Dodgson score?
  • For which discrete elections do the continuous and discrete optimum costs coincide?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Dodgson Threshold, a mass-based version of the decision problem underlying Theorem 6. The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: \(k\)-Dodgson Score—given candidates, a distinguished candidate \(x\), a multiset of votes, and a swap budget \(k\), decide whether \(x\) can be made a Condorcet winner using at most \(k\) adjacent swaps—has no polynomial kernel unless \(\mathrm{PH}=\Sigma_3^p\). The paper proves this by a polynomial parameter transformation from Small Universe Set Cover, using that problem’s cited kernel lower bound.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), target \(x\), a support of complete rankings \(t\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(B\). For each ranking \(t\), let \(d\) range from \(0\) to the number of candidates above \(x\), and let \(t[d]\) be the ranking obtained by moving \(x\) upward by \(d\) adjacent swaps. A solution assigns mass \(p_{t,d}\ge 0\) to these outcomes, with \(\sum_d p_{t,d}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\). It is accepted if its average swap cost is at most \(B\) and \(x\) strictly beats every other candidate pairwise in the resulting society:

\[ \sum_{t,d} d\,p_{t,d}\le B,\qquad \sum_{t,d}p_{t,d} \bigl(\mathbf{1}[x\succ_{t[d]}y]-\mathbf{1}[y\succ_{t[d]}x]\bigr)>0 \quad\text{for each }y\ne x. \]

The output is the mass allocation \(p\). This is directly about Dodgson’s underlying question: how much adjacent-swap effort is needed to make the designated candidate a Condorcet winner. A discrete election with \(n\) voters and \(n_t\) voters of each ranking maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); a budget of \(k\) discrete swaps maps to \(B=k/n\). In the continuous version, mass of a single ranking type may be divided among different upward moves.

The natural regime is a large election or large electorate with many repeated ballots: for example, millions of voters but a comparatively modest support of ranking types. A type is a complete ranking, and its mass is its share of the electorate. The paper uses uniform adjacent-swap costs, so this mirror does too. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for the authors’ problem: it preserves the target, the rankings, the pairwise Condorcet test, and the swap operation.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable. There are at most \(m\) movement choices per supported ranking, so the formulation has \(O(\tau m)\) variables and linear constraints. Strict wins can be handled by maximizing a common margin \(\delta\), subject to each pairwise margin being at least \(\delta\), and accepting exactly when the maximum is positive. Thus the threshold question is solvable by linear programming in the input size, including \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the rational bit lengths. This makes the theorem’s discrete kernelization obstruction an interesting boundary to investigate: it does not persist as an obstacle to solving this continuous threshold problem. The continuous question does not itself assert a kernelization theorem corresponding to Theorem 6.

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s \(k\)-Dodgson Score problem. I would not claim that it mirrors Theorem 9’s W[1]-hardness parameterized by the number of votes \(n\): my continuous input uses support size \(\tau\), and that theorem’s small-\(n\) parameterization does not transfer to it. The main follow-up questions are how the continuous optimum relates to the discrete Dodgson score—especially its integrality gap and when the two coincide—and whether similar LP formulations survive with richer swap costs.

The soft spot is the split: in a real election, a voter either moves \(x\) a given number of places or does not, while this model may divide a type’s mass among several moves. That is a substantive relaxation. It is also exactly what makes the high-multiplicity question meaningful: the type-level average move can be fractional even though every individual ballot receives an integral ranking. The authors should recognize the mirror as their problem’s continuous-population counterpart, while a claim that it preserves every finite-election detail would be too strong.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 6 is specifically a lower bound against polynomial kernels for \(k\)-Dodgson Score. The proposed LP answers a different question: whether a candidate can be made a Condorcet winner under a fractional mass budget. It says nothing about kernelization. Normalizing the electorate also turns the swap budget \(k\) into \(B=k/n\), so the theorem’s parameter has no direct continuous counterpart unless the model retains a total population scale. Theorem 9’s parameter, number of votes \(n\), has a similar mismatch; support size \(\tau\) is a plausible replacement, but it is not the same parameter.

That mismatch limits what the proposed mirror can claim, yet it does not defeat the underlying continuous question. A large electorate with many repeated rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Ranking type captures everything Dodgson’s model uses, and allowing mass to split across upward moves is an appropriate continuization. The pairwise winning conditions and average swap cost are linear, so the threshold problem has a polynomial-size LP. The strict-win condition can be handled by maximizing a common margin and checking whether it is positive.

The programme’s prior continuous Dodgson result means this LP may already be settled; under the stated prior-art rule, that changes the question a prover should receive, not whether the mirror is well-posed. The natural next questions—such as the discrete-to-continuous score gap—remain computational. So the negative case is weak: it shows that this is not a direct mirror of Theorem 6’s kernelization lower bound, but it does not rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of Dodgson Score.
