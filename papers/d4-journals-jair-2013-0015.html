Why no mirror

The proposed mass-transfer problems are coherent high-multiplicity extensions, but neither named theorem asserts an algorithm, hardness result, approximation guarantee, or computational formulation used to compute. Keeping the Gaussian fluctuation preserves the paper's noise model, while dropping it yields a new deterministic manipulation problem. Thus bit (a) fails even though the paper motivates plausible follow-up questions.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers coalition reachability, absolute power, and the \(c\sqrt n\) critical window; it does not supply a computational analogue of the paper's probabilistic phase-transition theorems.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the explicit-hyperplane version of minimum-mass coalition reachability admit an efficient algorithm when \(m\) varies?
  • Can the Gaussian critical-window functions be approximated with guarantees without treating the finite-\(n\) fluctuation law as the primary continuous object?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is real, but qualified: this paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its usable anchor is instead its main asymptotic result, which is computationally relevant because it characterizes when coalition manipulation becomes possible. Both Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 are proved in this paper, although they extend earlier results.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1.2. Under the paper’s Assumptions 1–4—constant \(m\), anonymous social choice function, i.i.d. votes from a full-support distribution \(p\) with \(p(\pi)\ge\delta\), and every candidate winning with probability at least \(\varepsilon\)—and assuming that the rule is a generalized scoring rule, the four limiting probabilities for coalitions of size \(c\sqrt n\) exist. The probability that some coalition can change the winner is at most \(Kc\), where \(K=K(f,\delta)<\infty\), and therefore tends to zero as \(c\to0\). For every \(c>0\), the probabilities that some or a fixed coalition can change the winner, and that some or a fixed coalition can elect any candidate, are strictly between \(0\) and \(1\). All four functions of \(c\) are continuously differentiable with bounded derivative. This is the paper’s smooth phase-transition theorem, proved here.

A natural continuous mirror is the following problem, which I would call Continuous Critical-Mass Coalition Manipulation.

Let \(T=S_m\) be the finite set of ranking types and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) be the continuous society: \(\mu_t\) is the mass of voters of type \(t\). Let \(F:\Delta(T)\to[m]\) be the generalized scoring rule. Given a mass budget \(\alpha\), a free coalition chooses nonnegative transfers \(x_{t,t'}\), subject to \(\sum_{t'}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,t'}x_{t,t'}\le\alpha\). The resulting society is

\[ \mu'_{t} = \mu_t-\sum_{u}x_{t,u}+\sum_{u}x_{u,t}. \]

The decision question is whether there is such a transfer with \(F(\mu')\ne F(\mu)\), or, for a specified candidate \(a\), with \(F(\mu')=a\). The optimization version minimizes \(\alpha\). A solution is a transfer matrix \(x\), or a certificate that no such transfer exists.

This is exactly the underlying problem studied in the paper: changing \(k\) voters becomes moving at most \(k/n\) units of population mass between ranking types. The active variable is population mass, not merely a probability used to describe a finite profile. If the coalition is fixed, its composition is an input submeasure \(\kappa\le\mu\), and the action is to replace \(\kappa\) by another mass distribution \(\lambda\) of the same total mass, yielding \(\mu'=\mu-\kappa+\lambda\).

The paper’s critical-window quantities arise by evaluating this continuous problem at \(\alpha=c/\sqrt n\) under the finite-population fluctuation law. Writing \(\mu=p+\xi/\sqrt n\), the rescaled fluctuation converges to \(\xi\sim N(0,\Sigma)\), where \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^{\mathsf T}\). Thus the continuous computational question is not merely “is the transition smooth?” but: given a generalized scoring rule and \(p\), compute or approximate the four profile functions giving the Gaussian probability that a free or fixed mass coalition of scaled size \(c\) can change the winner or elect every candidate. For a realized \(\mu\), the witness problem is a linear program: each winning region is defined by linear inequalities, and the transfer variables are linear.

I would expect the realized-society decision and minimum-mass problems to be Class A when the type set and hyperplane description are explicit. The full exact profile—Gaussian volume of the regions reachable by mass transfer—may itself be continuum-specifically difficult, while approximation by sampling plus LP witnesses looks plausible. This creates genuine follow-up questions: can the profile be approximated uniformly in \(c\); can it be computed through a separation oracle when \(m\) varies; and how do fixed-coalition and freely chosen-coalition versions differ algorithmically?

The regime is convincing. Consider a large election or platform vote with millions of voters but fixed \(m\), so \(n\gg\tau=m!\). Each ranking type contains many exchangeable voters, and \(\mu_t\) records the honest quantity: the fraction of society holding that ranking. The coalition of \(c\sqrt n\) voters becomes a mass of \(c/\sqrt n\), precisely the scale at which the paper finds the transition. The authors should recognize this mirror: Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3 already identify vote changes with \(L_1\) movement in the profile simplex, and Corollary 3.7 expresses manipulation as membership in a blown-up continuous boundary.

Theorem 1.1 supplies a useful secondary anchor. It proves that, under Assumptions 1–4, for every \(\eta>0\) there is an explicit \(c\) such that a fixed coalition of \(c\sqrt n\) voters can elect every candidate with probability at least \(1-\eta\), for every \(n\); one valid choice is \(c=(4/\delta)\log(2m!/\eta)\bigl(\sqrt{\log(2m/\eta)}+\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)}\bigr)\). Consequently, the probability tends to \(1\) as \(c\to\infty\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Absolute-Power Coalition. The instance contains \((F,\mu,\kappa)\), where \(\kappa\le\mu\) is a designated coalition submass. The coalition may replace \(\kappa\) by any \(\lambda\ge0\) with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=\sum_t\kappa_t\). The question is whether, for every candidate \(a\), there exists a replacement \(\lambda^{a}\) such that \(F(\mu-\kappa+\lambda^{a})=a\). A solution consists of one witnessing replacement for each candidate. Under an explicit hyperplane description this again decomposes into finitely many LP feasibility problems.

This second mirror covers the paper’s fixed-coalition “absolute power” result, while the first covers its manipulation and margin-of-victory profile. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s arbitrary anonymous-SCF theorem when the rule is supplied as a black box; the computational version needs a finite representation of the winning regions.

The weakest point is the scaling. In an ordinary normalized continuum, \(c\sqrt n/n\to0\), so the paper’s critical window disappears. A meaningful mirror must therefore retain either the finite-size parameter \(\alpha=c/\sqrt n\) or the rescaled Gaussian fluctuation. An opponent can fairly say that this imports the paper’s noise model. The answer is that noise is only the evaluation layer: the core object being continuized is still the population, and the core action is still mass transfer between voter types. But this is not as clean a continuum limit as continuous bribery with a fixed positive mass budget.

So the paper supports a credible, author-recognizable continuous mirror for coalitional manipulation and winner robustness, probably with LP-trivial witness problems and a more interesting Gaussian-volume layer. It is a positive case for continuization, but not a source of a hardness-transfer result or a named complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than the proponent allows because the paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2 are asymptotic probability theorems about random profiles. They prove neither a complexity classification nor an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm. The paper’s remarks about worst-case manipulation and margin-of-victory algorithms merely cite other work. Under ChoCo’s anchor rule, that is decisive: there is no named computational result here to continuize.

Even granting a broader notion of “computationally relevant,” the proposed Continuous Critical-Mass Coalition problem does not preserve the paper’s central object. In a normalized society, a coalition of \(c\sqrt n\) voters has mass

\[ \frac{c\sqrt n}{n}=\frac{c}{\sqrt n}\longrightarrow 0. \]

At any fixed continuous society away from a winning boundary, that coalition eventually has no effect. To retain the paper’s nontrivial limit one must simultaneously keep the finite-size parameter and write

\[ \mu=p+\frac{\xi}{\sqrt n},\qquad \alpha=\frac{c}{\sqrt n}, \]

with \(\xi\) converging to the Gaussian fluctuation used in the paper. The resulting object is therefore a central-limit/noise model for finite profiles, not a standalone continuous population problem. The paper already develops precisely this profile-simplex and Gaussian geometry in Sections 3.1–3.3, including the \(L_1\) correspondence between vote changes and movement of population proportions. Calling that mass transfer a new mirror largely renames the paper’s proof apparatus.

There is a legitimate deterministic high-multiplicity problem here, but it is a different and much weaker object. Given \(\mu\), a generalized scoring rule, and a fixed positive mass budget \(\alpha\), one can ask whether a transfer crosses a winning boundary. For fixed \(m\), however, \(T=S_m\) is fixed and the winning regions are finite polyhedral regions. The resulting feasibility or minimum-distance problem is exactly the finite-dimensional geometry already used in Lemmas 3.2–3.3. It does not compute the paper’s quantities \(q(c),\bar q(c),r(c),\bar r(c)\), which are probabilities over random fluctuations. Asking for those probabilities instead turns the problem into Gaussian-volume computation; the interesting continuous object is then the random perturbation, which is outside the programme’s population-continuization target.

Theorem 1.1 is no better. Its conclusion depends essentially on i.i.d. sampling, full support \(\delta\), concentration, and the assumption that every candidate has winning probability at least \(\varepsilon\). A deterministic input \((\mu,\kappa)\) for a continuous coalition has none of these probabilistic quantifiers. It asks only whether a specified mass can reach each winning region. To recover the theorem, one must randomize \(\mu\) and the coalition composition according to the multinomial or Gaussian law. That restores the paper’s noise model rather than producing a new computational population model. Moreover, for the theorem’s arbitrary anonymous social choice functions, no computational representation is supplied: a black-box \(F\) gives no finite certificate of reachability, while an explicit finite representation is constant-dimensional under the paper’s fixed-\(m\) assumptions.

Allowing \(m\) to grow does not rescue the proposed anchor. It could produce a new study of Gaussian volumes or succinctly represented generalized scoring rules, but that changes the paper’s assumptions, representation model, and theorem. It would be a new computational problem motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of either named result. Likewise, using unnormalised population mass \(n\) merely retains the finite parameter \(n\); it does not yield a genuine population limit.

The high-multiplicity scenario itself is perfectly sensible: large elections do contain many voters of each ranking type, and rational mass distributions encode finite profiles exactly. That is not the objection. The objection is that the sensible high-multiplicity reformulation is a deterministic restatement of the paper’s profile geometry, whereas the paper’s actual contribution is the stochastic \(\sqrt n\)-scale transition. Preserving the contribution preserves the noise model; removing the noise model removes the contribution.

A creative follow-up could certainly study variable-\(m\) complexity of continuous manipulation or Gaussian-volume approximation. That is the weak point in the negative case. But it would require introducing a new computational theorem and a new input model. As presented, this paper supplies no computational anchor and no population-continuous problem whose computational content survives independently of its finite-population fluctuation analysis.
