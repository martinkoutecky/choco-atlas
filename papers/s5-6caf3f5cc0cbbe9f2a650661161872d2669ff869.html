The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. Its numbered results are axiomatic impossibility and possibility results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Under your anchor rule, I can’t build a compliant affirmative case around them.

The most plausible continuous mirror is a large deliberation platform. A type is a strict ranking of the arguments involved in cycles; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants with ranking \(t\). A mass-based aggregator maps \(\mu\) to a collective ranking, which determines the defeat relation and then the grounded extension. The corresponding question is whether the collective extension preserves arguments accepted by every supported type and rejects arguments rejected by every supported type. This is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s model, but it is an axiomatic question, not a computational result from the paper.

The closest results are Propositions 1–5, all proved in this paper. Proposition 1 says every Condorcet method violates Acceptance Unanimity and Rejection Unanimity for \(m\ge4\) cycle arguments and \(n\ge3\) agents, except when \(n=m=4\). Proposition 2 says every positional scoring rule violates both axioms for \(m\ge4\), for some number of agents. Proposition 3 says every Condorcet method and positional scoring rule satisfies both axioms when \(m=2\). Proposition 4 says Plurality with agent-based tie-breaking satisfies both when \(m=3\). Proposition 5 says every representative-agent rule satisfies both for every number of agents and arguments. None is a complexity anchor.

There is some positive evidence for a continuous *axiomatic* mirror: the impossibility constructions in Propositions 1 and 2 use only a few distinct rankings, so their profiles can be represented by rational masses on a small support. A representative-agent rule can likewise select a ranking from the support. That makes the population model credible and the transfer worth studying.

The weakest point is decisive for this assignment: the paper asks which aggregation rules satisfy normative properties. It does not analyze the computational complexity of finding an aggregate outcome or grounded extension. Any such algorithmic problem would be a new question attached to the paper’s model, rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is that a continuous society is easy to define here: let each type be a complete ranking of the arguments, and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the deliberation platform. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But the proposed mirror asks whether mass-weighted aggregation preserves Acceptance and Rejection Unanimity. It remains an axiomatic question; the programme is about computational questions.

That distinction disposes of every result the proponent names. Propositions 1 and 2 classify when Condorcet methods and positional scoring rules violate the axioms. Propositions 3 and 4 classify small-\(m\) cases, and Proposition 5 covers representative-agent rules. None asserts an algorithm, complexity bound, hardness result, or approximation guarantee. The fact that the impossibility witnesses use only a few rankings—and can therefore be encoded as rational masses on small support—makes them good continuous *axiomatic* examples. It does not turn them into computational results.

The best stronger version would ask for an algorithmic task around the paper’s pipeline: given \(\mu\), an argumentation framework, and a specified rule such as Kemeny, compute the collective ranking and resulting grounded extension. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem. It would also be a new computational question: the paper studies which rules satisfy normative properties, not the cost of computing their outputs. Likewise, asking how to encode a rule so that one can computationally test its axioms adds a rule-verification problem the paper does not pose. Either could be worth studying on its own, but neither mirrors one of this paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: every proposed anchor is axiomatic, and the programme explicitly puts axiomatic continuization outside scope. I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile computational project could grow from the model. A weighted Kemeny computation, for example, could be a worthwhile adjacent project; it just would not be a continuous mirror of the paper’s named results.
