What the mirror covers

The proposed reading concerns Proposition 5’s SED aggregation identity; it does not extend the paper’s other results into computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but the paper has no qualifying computational anchor under your stated rule. Propositions 1–7 and Theorems 10–14 analyze aggregation and fixing procedures; none classifies a problem’s complexity or studies an algorithm’s running time.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved in the paper. It says that the weighted coherent approximation principle using squared Euclidean distance equals linear pooling followed or preceded by SED fixing, and also equals SED aggregation followed or preceded by SED fixing. For GKL in the first direction, it says the corresponding procedure equals geometric pooling and the matching fix/aggregate combinations. These identities explicitly compute optimizers, but they are analytical results about finite-agent procedures, not computational-complexity results.

A natural extension would take each type \(t\) to be a complete credence vector \(c_t\in[0,1]^m\), with \(\mu_t\) the population fraction of forecasters of that type. Given finitely many types with rational masses, ask for a coherent aggregate
\[ q^*\in\Delta_m \quad\text{minimizing}\quad \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{SED}(q,c_t). \]
The scenario could be a large forecasting organization whose members share a small set of evidence sources and elicitation protocols, so that there are many forecasters but relatively few distinct credence types. Proposition 5 makes this recognizable as their aggregation problem. The mirror would be tractable: \(q^*\) is the SED projection onto the simplex of the population mean credence vector. It also stays within scope: the society becomes continuous while the propositions and aggregate credences remain the objects being studied.

The weak point is substantial: the paper already handles weighted finite populations, so replacing finite weighted sums with population masses adds little by itself. More importantly, the explicit aggregation identities do not meet the required anchor standard. I would therefore present this as a plausible continuous extension, but not as a valid anchored case for the paper’s computational complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent chose the strongest candidate: Proposition 5. But it gives identities between aggregation and fixing procedures; it does not state a computational problem’s complexity or analyze an algorithm’s running time. The other named results have the same character. Under ChoCo’s computational gate, there is no result here to mirror.

The proposed forecasting society is sensible: many people can share a complete credence type, with \(\mu_t\) giving each type’s population mass. For finite support, though, the proposal is the paper’s existing weighted-agent setup with \(\alpha_i=\mu_t\). A continuous population reading makes the interpretation natural, but it does not create a new computational problem in the paper’s results. Asking for runtime or approximation guarantees would be a new analysis of those procedures, not a continuization of a complexity result the paper established.

A measure over continuously many credence types is a stronger version of the proposal. But then a computational question needs an input representation for that measure: a density, a finite approximation, or some other encoding. That choice brings in a separate problem about representing and computing with distributions, rather than extending the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strongest at the programme’s stated boundary: this paper has a plausible continuous-population interpretation, but no anchored continuous *computational* mirror. I would not claim that opinion pooling could never motivate a worthwhile computational study; the paper simply does not provide the computational result such a mirror would extend.
