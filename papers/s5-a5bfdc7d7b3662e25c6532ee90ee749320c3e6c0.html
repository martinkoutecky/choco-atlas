The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), an explicitly listed support \(T\) of complete rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a target \(c^\star\), and a budget \(k\), does there exist \(D \subseteq C \setminus \{c^\star\}\) with \(|D| \le k\) such that \(c^\star\) is the unique Llull winner after deleting \(D\) and restricting each ranking to \(C \setminus D\)? For each remaining pair \(a,b\), \(a\) defeats \(b\) when the mass ranking \(a\) above \(b\) exceeds \(1/2\); a tie awards both candidates a point.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the listed ranking types; the chair chooses a discrete candidate-deletion set \(D\), while \(\mu\) remains fixed. The objective is to minimize \(|D|\), subject to the target being the unique Llull winner under pairwise outcomes computed from the masses.

The objection that survived

Once pairwise outcomes are computed, the mass distribution collapses to the defeat graph, so this mirror adds little continuous-optimization structure; that limits payoff but does not undermine the question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its Llull candidate-control interpretation. It leaves the Minimum Degree Deletion and Bounded Degree Deletion results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is High-Multiplicity Llull Candidate Deletion. It keeps the paper’s candidate-control question intact and continuizes the electorate, which is the part of society that can naturally have high multiplicity. A plausible regime is a large association election with millions of voters but a relatively small set of recurring ballot types. Each type is a complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of voters with that ranking. Thus the instance has support size \(\tau\), with each mass given as a binary rational; the number of voters can be much larger than \(\tau\).

Formally, an instance consists of candidates \(C\), a distribution \(\mu\) over a listed support of rankings of \(C\), a target candidate \(c^\star\), and a deletion budget \(k\). For candidates \(a,b\), let \(\mu_{ab}\) be the mass ranking \(a\) above \(b\). In any remaining candidate set \(C'\), Llull awards \(a\) one point against \(b\) when \(\mu_{ab}\ge \tfrac12\): a strict pairwise win earns a point, and a tie earns both candidates a point. The question is whether there is a set \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{c^\star\}\), \(|D|\le k\), such that \(c^\star\) is the unique Llull winner on \(C\setminus D\). A solution is the candidate set \(D\); equivalently, the optimization version minimizes \(|D|\). The society’s mass distribution remains fixed when candidates are deleted, and each ballot is restricted to the remaining candidates.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this thesis. It states that Minimum Indegree Deletion is W[2]-hard parameterized by either the size \(s_v\) of a feedback vertex set or the size \(s_a\) of a feedback arc set. The underlying decision problem takes a directed graph \(D\), a distinguished vertex \(w_c\), and a budget \(k\), and asks whether at most \(k\) other vertices can be deleted so that \(w_c\) is the unique vertex of minimum indegree. The theorem’s reduction is from Dominating Set: it constructs an instance with feedback vertex set size at most \(k+1\) and feedback arc set size at most \((k+1)^2\), and the constructed deletion instance with budget \(n-k\) is a yes-instance exactly when the Dominating Set instance is. The paper identifies Minimum Indegree Deletion with constructive candidate control in Llull elections, so this is a direct anchor for the proposed mirror.

In the mirror, the defeat digraph has an arc \(a\to b\) when more than half the mass ranks \(a\) above \(b\); a tied pair has no arc. Llull score is \(|C'|-1\) minus indegree in this graph, so a unique Llull winner is exactly a unique minimum-indegree candidate. The mirror’s parameters are the feedback vertex set and feedback arc set sizes of the initial defeat digraph. The continuous question is therefore whether Theorem 1’s hardness persists when the election is supplied as rational masses on ranking types, with running time measured in \(m,\tau,L\), and the feedback-set parameter.

I expect Class B: hardness transfers. The chair still makes integral candidate-deletion choices, and the reduction’s combinatorics concern the candidate graph rather than how many voters share a ballot. A finite election with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/\sum_u n_u\), so the mirror also has a clear high-multiplicity interpretation: ballot counts become compact rational masses. Further questions include whether the hardness reduction can be realized by ranking profiles while preserving both feedback-set bounds, and whether additional restrictions on support size \(\tau\) make the mirror tractable.

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its Llull control interpretation; it does not claim to continuize the thesis’s Minimum Degree Deletion or Bounded Degree Deletion results.

My weakest point is the profile encoding. Theorem 1 is proved for directed graph instances. The thesis says the Minimum Indegree Deletion results transfer to Llull control, which supports this mirror, but a hardness proof for the stated distribution-input problem should still exhibit ranking types and rational masses that realize the reduction while preserving the parameter bounds. That is a concrete transfer obligation, not a reason to reject the question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 1’s computational object is a candidate graph. In the strongest continuous mirror, the society is a rational distribution over ranking types, but its only effect is to determine pairwise majority outcomes. Those can be computed from the masses; after that, the chair deletes candidates from the resulting defeat graph. No voter mass is transferred, and the optimization remains over discrete candidate sets. That makes this a less compelling use of continuous optimization than a mirror whose intervention acts directly on population mass.

But that objection does not defeat the mirror. A high-multiplicity electorate with recurring ballot types is a sensible regime, and the proposed model asks a computational question about that continuous society. The fact that its answer can be summarized by a graph is not a scope violation. Nor is it an objection that candidate deletion stays discrete: the programme continuizes the population, not every object in the problem.

The stronger mirror is the one the proponent gave: represent the electorate by rational ranking masses, compute the Llull defeat relation from those masses, and ask whether deleting at most \(k\) candidates makes the target the unique winner. This is recognizable as Llull candidate control. The paper itself connects that control problem to Minimum Indegree Deletion; if realization of the reduction were questioned, the paper’s stated transfer to Llull control, together with weighted profiles realizing pairwise outcomes, addresses the basic concern. The population need not be individually targeted for its multiplicity to matter as input.

So my case against is weak. The mirror may offer little new continuous-optimization structure because the mass distribution is compressed into pairwise outcomes, but that is a concern about payoff, not a fundamental reason the question is unworthwhile. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives this anchor.
