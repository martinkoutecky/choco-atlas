The continuous mirror question

There are three room categories, \(\tau\) tenant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational supplies \(q_j\) summing to \(1\). Each type-category pair has a membership oracle for a convex acceptable-rent set \(A_{tj}\subseteq\Delta^2\) containing the corresponding facet \(F_j\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\) and a promise that some exact feasible rent vector and assignment exist, determine the worst-case number of binary membership queries needed to find \(p\in\Delta^2\) and masses \(x_{tj}\geq0\) with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tj}=q_j\), and \(x_{tj}>0\) only when \(p\in A^\varepsilon_{tj}\). In particular, is query complexity polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), or is there a lower bound ruling this out?

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete tenant preference types, three standardized room categories with per-capita supplies \(q\), a shared rent-share vector \(p\in\Delta^2\), and a type-to-category mass assignment \(x\). The objective is to minimize worst-case membership queries needed to output an \(\varepsilon\)-fair rent vector and feasible assignment.

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends only Theorem 5.2’s \(d=3\), convex, facet-containing rental-harmony setting with type masses and room capacities. It leaves the cake-cutting results, the linear-preference results in Section 4, and other dimensions and preference classes aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the weighted matching constraints be handled with query complexity polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • What oracle lower bounds hold, and can exact-feasibility be replaced by a weaker weighted existence condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the rental harmony result, recast as a market with many residents and a small number of shared preference types. That keeps the paper’s central objects—room categories, rent shares, acceptable room choices, and approximate fairness—and makes the population itself continuous.

My anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. It says that when \(d=3\) and each preference set \(A_{ij}\) is convex and contains the corresponding facet \(F_j\), a binary-mode algorithm finds an \(\varepsilon\)-fair division point using at most \(6(\lceil\log_2 n\rceil^2+\lceil\log_2 n\rceil)\) queries, where \(n=\lceil1/\varepsilon\rceil\). The paper’s proof builds on its Lemma 5.1 and uses convexity to find a point that works for a room assignment. This is an algorithmic anchor; the paper does not prove a named hardness result of its own.

Here is the continuous question I would put to a prover: Three-Category Rental Harmony with Mass Types. There are three room categories. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) tenant types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type’s complete description includes its preferences over rent shares and its willingness to rent each category. Room supply is a rational vector \(q\in\Delta^2\); if the population has \(N\) residents, category \(j\) has \(Nq_j\) rooms. Rent shares are \(p\in\Delta^2\). For each type \(t\) and category \(j\), the set \(A_{tj}\subseteq\Delta^2\) contains the rent shares at which that type accepts that category. Require each \(A_{tj}\) to be convex and to contain \(F_j\), as in Theorem 5.2.

The input gives membership oracles for the \(A_{tj}\), together with \(\mu\), \(q\), and \(\varepsilon\), and promises an exact feasible allocation. The question is to find a rent vector \(p\) and a mass assignment \(x_{tj}\geq0\) such that \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_tx_{tj}=q_j\), and \(x_{tj}>0\) only if \(p\in A^\varepsilon_{tj}\). Here \(A^\varepsilon_{tj}\) is the paper’s \(\varepsilon\)-neighbourhood of the preference set. A binary query asks whether \(p\in A_{tj}\); the objective is to minimize worst-case query count as a function of \(\tau\) and \(\varepsilon\). The output is both the rent vector and the assignment of tenant mass to room categories.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity instance regime: a large housing programme or co-living market with many residents, three standardized room categories, and a relatively small number of recurring preference and affordability profiles. For example, \(N\) may be in the thousands while \(\tau\) is in the tens. The type is a complete preference description, and room supply scales with the population. For rational masses and supplies, choosing a common denominator gives a finite market with many copies of each type. The mass assignment is then the high-multiplicity version of matching those residents to rooms; transportation-flow integrality also means a feasible rational mass assignment can be represented by an integral assignment in a sufficiently large replicated market.

The connection to Theorem 5.2 is specific: both problems search over the same two-dimensional rent simplex using convex, facet-containing acceptability sets, with membership queries as the information source. The mass version asks whether the paper’s logarithmic dependence on accuracy can survive when the assignment is a weighted matching rather than a permutation. I would expect this problem to be tractable in the oracle sense—perhaps with query complexity polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)—because there are only three room categories and feasibility at a given price is a small transportation problem. Establishing that bound, or a matching oracle lower bound, is the computational question.

The weakest point is that Theorem 5.2’s proof handles three individual tenants, whereas the mirror has \(\tau\) types and weighted matching constraints. Its query bound does not transfer directly: mass feasibility introduces weighted Hall constraints that the paper’s rainbow argument does not address. That is a real new problem, but it remains recognizably rental harmony. The mirror covers the convex, three-category rental-harmony result only; it makes no claim about the cake-cutting results or higher-dimensional convex cases. Further questions include whether an exact-feasibility promise can be replaced by a weighted existence condition, and how query complexity changes when the number of room categories exceeds three.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the one anchor raised, Theorem 5.2. It concerns exactly three tenants and three individually identified rooms. Here \(d=3\) fixes the rent simplex at dimension two; the logarithmic query bound comes from the geometry of those three tenants’ convex preference sets.

Repeating the tenants \(N\) times does not keep that problem intact. In literal rental harmony, there are then \(N\) rooms and an \(N\)-dimensional rent simplex. Keeping only three room categories instead changes the feasibility condition: a point on the rent simplex must now support a capacitated matching between tenant types and room supplies. At each proposed price, the relevant condition is a weighted flow or Hall-type condition. Theorem 5.2’s argument finds a point for three individual preference systems; it gives no method for satisfying those weighted constraints.

That is a real break in the proposed mirror, but it does not settle the case against it. The capacitated version is a plausible high-multiplicity rental market: many residents can share a small number of complete preference types, and rational masses can represent replicated finite populations. Its membership-query complexity is a genuine computational question. Theorem 5.2 would not answer it, but the programme is interested in new complexity questions, so lack of a transferred proof is not a reason to dismiss the mirror.

The honest negative case is therefore limited. The theorem is a poor basis for claiming that its logarithmic guarantee extends to a continuous population: the proposed model introduces a new matching constraint that may become the central problem. But I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The type-based rental market survives that challenge; the paper provides motivation for it, even though it does not provide its algorithm.
