The continuous mirror question

Given \(r\) binary issues and a finite support \(S\subseteq\{0,1\}^r\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_v\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), find \(x\in\{0,1\}^r\) maximizing \(A_\mu(x)=\sum_{v\in S}\mu_v\,\mathbf{1}[d_H(v,x)<r/2]\).

The model it lives in

Voter types are complete issue-preference vectors \(v\in\{0,1\}^r\), each carrying population mass \(\mu_v\). The candidate chooses a reported platform \(x\in\{0,1\}^r\) to maximize the mass approving under the paper’s strict-majority agreement rule.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s single-round Maximum Approval Pandering problem. It does not cover the CMAP formulation, the multi-round MDP, or the reinforcement-learning experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of optimizing \(A_\mu(x)\) as a function of \(r\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\), beyond the NP-hardness inherited from Theorem 1?
  • What approximation guarantees are possible for this distributional optimization problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Distributional Maximum Approval Pandering. A type is a voter’s complete binary preference vector over the \(r\) issues, \(v\in\{0,1\}^r\). In a large constituency, let \(\mu_v\) be the fraction of voters of type \(v\), with positive mass on a finite support \(S\) and \(\sum_{v\in S}\mu_v=1\). A strategic candidate chooses a reported platform \(x\in\{0,1\}^r\). The question is to find a report maximizing the approval share
\[ A_\mu(x)=\sum_{v\in S}\mu_v\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[d_H(v,x)<\frac r2\right]. \]
A solution is an optimal report \(x^\star\), optionally with its approval share. This preserves the paper’s strict-majority agreement threshold and its unrestricted reporting choice; the candidate is maximizing approvals, not a different utility.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper by a Karp reduction from Max 2-SAT, adapting an argument by Neal Young. It states that Maximum Approval Pandering is NP-hard: given \(n\) voters’ binary preference vectors over \(r\) issues, find a binary report that maximizes the number of voters whose Hamming distance from it is strictly less than \(r/2\). This is the paper’s single-round problem, where the candidate knows the voter profile and seeks to maximize approvals.

The proposed regime is a large constituency facing an issue agenda: many people have the same pattern of positions across those issues, so the population has far more members than distinct preference types. For this problem, equal issue-preference vectors make voters indistinguishable in every respect the objective uses. Their mass is simply their share of the constituency. That is a natural high-multiplicity setting for representative politics, and the authors’ own approval rule already singles out exactly these types.

The mirror preserves the candidate’s actual decision problem. The candidate still chooses one platform, and each voter approves according to the same issue-by-issue agreement test. Only the electorate’s representation changes from an individual list to type masses. That seems recognizable to the authors as their problem at population scale: the binary preference vector is already a complete type for MAP, and maximizing the share approving is the per-capita form of maximizing the number approving.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: the hardness transfers. Given any finite MAP profile \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\), set \(\mu_v=|\{i:v_i=v\}|/n\). Then, for every report \(x\),
\[ nA_\mu(x)=|\{i:d_H(v_i,x)<r/2\}|, \]
so the optimal reports are identical. Moreover, repeating every voter \(K\) times leaves \(\mu\) and the optimum unchanged while making the electorate arbitrarily large relative to its support. Thus the hardness does not depend on distinguishing individuals or targeting them one by one; the paper’s reduction gets its combinatorics from the issue dimension \(r\).

The scope here is deliberately limited to Theorem 1’s single-round, unrestricted MAP problem. The paper’s CMAP formulation and its multi-round MDP and reinforcement-learning experiments are not anchors for this case: the supplied paper gives no numbered complexity result for them. They could motivate further mirrors, but extending the present one to those settings would need separate questions and anchors.

This formulation also yields concrete questions for a prover: what is the complexity of exact optimization as a function of \(r\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\)? Can the mass formulation support useful approximation guarantees? Brute-force enumeration of the \(2^r\) reports takes \(O(2^r\tau r)\) evaluations, so fixed \(r\) is tractable; the hardness concerns growing agendas.

The weakest point is that this is a strong mirror but not a tractability gain: on empirical distributions, the continuous objective is exactly the discrete objective divided by \(n\). The theorem’s hardness is agenda-driven, so continuization does not dissolve it. That limits what this anchor says about new continuous-optimization algorithms, but it still gives a precise, plausible continuous-population problem and a clean example of where the boundary lies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a credible case that this anchor fails. Theorem 1 is a named computational result, and its MAP problem has a direct population mirror. Let a voter’s type be their complete issue vector \(v\in\{0,1\}^r\), and let \(\mu_v\) be that type’s population share. The candidate still chooses \(x\in\{0,1\}^r\), and the approval share is
\[ A_\mu(x)=\sum_v \mu_v\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[d_H(v,x)<\frac r2\right]. \]
This keeps the paper’s strict threshold and the candidate’s decision intact.

The large-constituency scenario is plausible: with a fixed issue agenda, a growing population can contain many voters of each preference type. More formally, any finite profile \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\) gives \(\mu_v=|\{i:v_i=v\}|/n\), so for every report \(x\), \(nA_\mu(x)\) equals its number of approvals in the original profile. The mass representation loses no information the MAP objective uses. The paper’s particular random voter samples do not rule out this regime; multiplicity is a property of the instance regime, not of that experiment.

The strongest reservation is that this mirror may be a poor route to new continuous-optimization methods: the candidate still searches over \(2^r\) reports, and the central objective is unchanged up to normalization. But that is a limitation on the likely algorithmic payoff, not a reason the question is ill-posed or outside scope. Agenda growth also appears in the paper’s single-round experiments. Calling the mirror unworthy because its complexity may transfer would rely on a ground the programme explicitly rules out.

So the negative case is weak. I see no sound multiplicity, identity, degeneracy, or prior-continuous-treatment objection to this anchor. At most, it looks more like a useful boundary case than a likely tractability gain.
