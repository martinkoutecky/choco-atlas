The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result: it gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a problem as tractable or hard. Section 3.6 analyzes the algorithms’ running time without a numbered result; Section 3.5 says exact slate optimization may be NP-hard but explicitly offers no proof.

The best formal foothold is Proposition 1, proved here. It says that an option \(o\), dominated under the current preference set \(R\), becomes Pareto-optimal after adding a preference \(r_i\) if and only if \(o\) is strictly better under \(r_i\) than every option that dominates it under \(R\), and is not worse under \(r_i\) than every option equally preferred under \(R\). This precisely supports the paper’s look-ahead suggestion method, but it is not a complexity result and so cannot serve as a valid anchor under your specification.

A plausible continuous extension, if that weaker foothold is acceptable, is Continuous Look-Ahead Slate Selection. Consider a large product-search cohort, such as visitors to a housing site. A type is a user’s complete preference model, including their currently stated preferences and their next hidden preference. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be the fraction of users of type \(\theta\). Assume many users but a moderate number \(\tau\) of recurring preference types, arising from a shared catalog and common attribute choices and thresholds.

For each currently visible preference state \(R\), choose a slate \(G_R\) of at most \(\ell\) suggestions, each dominated under \(R\). The instance gives the finite catalog and its attributes, the types and their binary-rational masses, each type’s visible state and next hidden preference, and \(\ell\). The solution is the collection of slates maximizing

\[ \sum_R \sum_{\theta:\,\bar R_\theta=R} \mu_\theta\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[ \exists o\in G_R: o \text{ is Pareto-optimal under } R\cup\{r_\theta\} \right]. \]

In words, choose suggestions that give the greatest total mass of users at least one option that becomes Pareto-optimal when their next preference is considered. Proposition 1 gives the test for each option and type. This mirrors the paper’s look-ahead principle and its set-of-suggestions objective in Section 3.5, while making the population itself the weighted object.

My provisional expectation is Class B: the difficult combinatorics appear to lie in choosing suggestions from the catalog, and fractional user masses do not obviously remove that difficulty. But that is a research hypothesis, not a result established by this paper. Natural follow-up questions include exact complexity, approximation guarantees, and parameterized algorithms in \(\ell\) and \(\tau\). This mirror covers suggestion selection and the look-ahead criterion; it does not mirror the full interactive process or the reported user-study accuracy gains.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s central problem is helping each user make an individual choice. Optimizing mass-weighted recommendation success across a cohort is a recognizable system-design extension, but the authors might not regard it as the continuous form of their own problem. The absence of a qualifying named complexity anchor makes the positive case incomplete under your rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the best formal anchor, but it does not establish a computational result. It characterizes when one option, dominated under a user’s current preferences, becomes Pareto-optimal after one more preference is added. The paper gives no complexity classification for that property. Its complexity discussion in Section 3.6 analyzes particular suggestion algorithms over a finite catalog; its suggestion that exact slate optimization may be NP-hard is explicitly unproved.

The proposed cohort objective is coherent, but it changes the question. The paper studies an individual’s search: suggestions prompt that user to reveal preferences and help them find their own target. A strong cohort version could give each user type a distribution \(q_\theta(h\mid R)\) over hidden preferences and choose a slate \(G_R\) to maximize expected success across masses \(\mu_\theta\). That avoids assuming the system knows each user’s hidden preference. But it asks how to design a recommendation policy for a population, not for the computational task Proposition 1 characterizes. If the policy is personalized to each full type, the mass-weighted objective just aggregates those type-specific tasks; requiring a shared slate creates the new cohort-design problem. The paper’s aggregate user-study metrics make that extension natural, but they do not make it a named computational result of the paper.

So I would reject this as a surviving mirror of the anchor. I would not claim the broader idea is worthless in every scenario: high-volume product search with recurring user types could support a worthwhile population-level policy problem. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that this would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper establishes.
