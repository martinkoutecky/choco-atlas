What the mirror covers

The candidate degree-class activation-allocation objective is not a result of the paper; its statistical estimates in Eqs. (3)–(5) and empirical validations remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive direction is an optimization problem suggested by the paper’s statistical model, but the paper does not establish it as a computational result. Equations (3)–(5) estimate the fraction of nodes whose neighbors are mostly active from the degree distribution, degree correlations, and activation rates by degree. A continuous version could treat \(k\)-degree active and inactive agents as types, with mass \(\mu_{k,x}\); take the degree-mixing pattern as part of the input; and ask how to allocate a fixed active population across degree classes to maximize the predicted majority-illusion fraction. That is plausible in a large platform where many users share degree and behavior classes. Its weakness is that it turns the paper’s descriptive calculation into a new intervention problem, and its degree-based model does not capture all the higher-order network structure the authors discuss.

There is no valid named anchor in the article: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Equations (1)–(5) and the empirical figures are not such anchors. So there is no paper result to mirror under the stated requirements, and I can’t honestly present that prospective optimization problem as an anchored continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. Equations (1)–(5) define network statistics and estimate the fraction of nodes experiencing the majority illusion; the experiments test those estimates. The paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Under the proposed-anchor requirement, that is decisive: the article supplies no anchor.

The suggested optimization—allocate a fixed active population across degree classes to maximize the predicted illusion—adds a decision the paper never studies. The paper varies degree–attribute correlation to examine the effect; it does not ask how to choose that correlation or allocate activists. A platform or campaign scenario could make that a sensible new intervention problem, but it would be a project prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results. Computing the paper’s prediction from its supplied degree and correlation data is already a finite sum of binomial probabilities; the paper does not expose a computational bottleneck that continuization would resolve.

A more faithful version would preserve the actual graph and optimize which nodes are active, or model a large network with a graphon or an edge-type distribution. But then the majority statistic depends on who is connected to whom. The paper itself notes that networks with the same degree distribution and assortativity can exhibit different illusion strengths. Compressing nodes to degree-and-state types therefore gives the paper’s statistical approximation, not the exact graph phenomenon. Preserving enough neighborhood structure instead risks making types nearly individual-specific, leaving little multiplicity to exploit. A graphon intervention model might be worth studying, but it would introduce a new network-optimization problem rather than continuize a computational result in this paper.

Many large networks do have repeated degree and attribute classes, so lack of multiplicity is not the sound objection. The stronger point is that plausible mass-based models either evaluate the paper’s existing aggregate predictor or add a new intervention objective. I cannot establish that no related continuous problem could ever be worthwhile; I can say that none of the proposed directions is an anchored continuous mirror of this article.
