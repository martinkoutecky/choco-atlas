The case FOR (proponent)

On the strict anchor rule, this paper has no named computational complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as being in P, hard, FPT, or gives an algorithmic runtime. The closest positive foothold is an approximation result, but I would not relabel it as an in-P theorem.

That foothold is Theorem 3, proved in the paper (Appendix §7.2.2). For a multi-type random network \((P,n)\), define \(d_k=\sum_\ell n_\ell P_{k\ell}\) and \(Q_{k\ell}=n_\ell P_{k\ell}/d_k\). For every \(\delta>0\), there is a \(K\) such that if \(\min_k d_k>K\log^2 n\), then with probability at least \(1-\delta\), the second eigenvalue in magnitude of the realized network’s updating matrix \(T(A(P,n))\) differs from that of \(Q(P,n)\) by at most \(\delta\). This is a probabilistic reduction from a large network to a matrix with one row and column per type; it does not itself claim a complexity class.

If quantitative approximation results count as computational anchors, the strongest honest mirror I see is Type-Mass Convergence-Rate Evaluation. An instance is a finite support \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational matrix \(P\) of link probabilities between types, and a rational tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\). Assume every type has positive expected degree and the type-level chain is irreducible. Ask for an interval of width at most \(\varepsilon\) containing \(|\lambda_2(Q^\mu)|\), where
\[ Q^\mu_{tu}=\frac{\mu_uP_{tu}}{\sum_v\mu_vP_{tv}}. \]
A solution is a certified interval containing that spectral rate. There is no intervention variable or optimization objective here: it is a computation of the speed of the averaging or random-walk process. For a finite realization with \(N\) agents and type counts approaching \(N\mu_t\), Theorem 3 is the bridge between this rate and the realized network’s rate when the expected degrees meet its condition.

A plausible regime is a very large online community or school system, with millions of people but relatively few interaction-relevant types. A type must include the attributes and update behavior that determine how its members link and learn; members of the same type share the same row of \(P\). Mass means the fraction of the population in that type. The number of agents is then much larger than the support size \(\tau=|T|\). This is not an invented fit to the paper: the authors explicitly say their representative-agent results reduce the study of \(n\) individuals to the interaction across \(m\) types. Replacing \(n_k/n\) by \(\mu_k\) is a natural continuous-population version of that model.

I would expect this evaluation problem to be tractable: \(Q^\mu\) has dimension \(\tau\), and, under the stated assumptions, it is similar to a symmetric matrix, so its eigenvalue can be approximated with standard numerical methods. That expectation is mine; the paper does not establish the runtime. The resulting programme could ask how rates vary with \(\mu\), how sensitive they are to errors in \(P\), or whether changing type masses under constraints can speed convergence. Those optimization questions are follow-ons, not anchors in this paper.

The mirror covers the representative-agent spectral result and the paper’s linear-updating and random-walk analysis. It does not cover shortest-path communication, the empirical regressions, or a control problem. The weakest point is substantial: the paper is an analytic study of network processes, not computational social choice, and its asymptotic type reduction already does much of the conceptual work the mirror would propose. The positive case is therefore a plausible computational extension of the authors’ central model, but it does not meet the strict requirement for a named complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I do not see a computational result in this paper for ChoCo to mirror. The pro case has one anchor: Theorem 3. It is a probabilistic spectral approximation theorem for random graphs. It gives no computational problem, algorithm, running time, or hardness classification. The paper’s other numbered results are also analytic: they relate network structure, homophily, eigenvalues, and convergence times. That is a strong reason to reject the proposed mirror under the programme’s stated anchor standard.

The proposed Type-Mass Convergence-Rate Evaluation is coherent as a new task. I grant that a very large population with a small number of interaction types is plausible, and that replacing type counts by proportions gives a sensible model. But evaluating \(\lvert\lambda_2(Q^\mu)\rvert\) is an added computational question about a finite type-level matrix. Theorem 3 uses that matrix as the analytic proxy for a finite random network; it does not pose its evaluation as the computational problem the paper studies. Calling the calculation an anchor would stretch “mirror” to cover nearly any computable statistic in a paper.

The strongest repair would be to optimize the masses: for fixed linking probabilities \(P\), find a feasible redistribution of types that makes learning as fast or slow as possible. That is a more substantive population problem, but it adds a planner, constraints, and an intervention absent from the paper. The authors study how a given network structure affects communication. They do not study changing the population to achieve an objective. Nor is this computational social choice: the paper has no preferences, voting rule, winner, or control action to which the optimization could attach without importing a different problem.

I would not reject the mirror because high multiplicity is implausible, because the continuum answer might be easy, or because the paper’s spectral approximation already identifies a useful type-level object. Those points do not settle the question. The negative case is narrower: this paper’s contributions are analytical, and turning one of their summary statistics into a computation—or adding a population-design problem—does not mirror a named computational result. If “mirror” is broadened to include any computational question inspired by the model, the proponent has a plausible case; under the programme’s stated standard, that is the weak point in my case.
