The case FOR (proponent)

There is no honest qualifying anchor in this paper. Its numbered results analyze error probabilities in finite \(M\)-ary relay trees; they do not classify the computational complexity of a problem.

The closest examples are Theorem 3, proved here, which bounds the total error probability under majority dominance, and Corollary 3, which gives \(\log_2 P_N^{-1}=\Theta(N^{\log_M \lambda_M})\), where \(\lambda_M=\lfloor(M+1)/2\rfloor\). These are analytic convergence-rate results, not claims that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The Bayesian likelihood-ratio results have the same limitation.

A plausible adjacent model would describe a large sensor population by masses over sensor types, with each type specifying its observation law. But preserving this paper’s problem also requires the relay tree: an agent’s role and position affect how its message is fused. Turning that model into a continuous optimization over sensor masses or fusion rules would introduce a new computational question, not mirror one of the paper’s named results.

So the strongest positive case is only a research lead: a continuous sensor-population model may be worth formulating. This paper itself supplies no named computational result to anchor the required prover-ready mirror. Its study of behavior as \(N\) grows is an analytic population-size limit, which does not by itself establish a continuous-population computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchors all fail at the same point. Theorem 3 and Corollary 3 bound the error of a prescribed majority-fusion protocol as the integer number of leaves \(N=M^k\) grows. Theorem 4 and Corollary 4 do the same for prescribed Bayesian fusion. These are convergence-rate results, not computational-complexity results: the paper gives no encoded computational input, decision problem, or algorithmic classification. The formula for choosing a sufficient \(N\) to meet an error target is a direct calculation from scalar parameters, and the paper does not establish an exact minimum-sample problem or its complexity. The message-alphabet theorems likewise derive rates and message sizes. None supplies the named computational result a mirror needs.

The strongest repaired mirror would give sensors several possible types, each with its own observation law, and ask how a distribution of types affects fusion in a large relay tree. That is a plausible new research direction, but it does not mirror these theorems. The paper assumes identically distributed leaf measurements, a fixed regular tree, and specified fusion rules; it has no allocation, intervention, or optimization over the population. With the paper’s assumptions, the continuous type distribution is just \(\mu_t=1\) for the single sensor type. Allowing heterogeneous types requires specifying how types occupy tree positions and sibling groups, since those relationships determine the recursive error calculation. Optimizing that assignment or the fusion rules would add a computational problem the paper does not formulate or analyze.

There is also a real limit issue. The error rates arise from finite independent measurements. In an exact atomless population of such sensors, aggregate signal frequencies become exact, so the sampling fluctuations that generate Type I and Type II error disappear. Preserving those fluctuations requires retaining a finite-sample parameter or modeling a random empirical population; either choice returns the analysis to a statistical experiment indexed by \(N\), rather than producing a computational-complexity landscape over society distributions.

This is not proof that no related continuous model could be worthwhile. A heterogeneous sensor-allocation or fusion-optimization problem could be interesting on its own. The negative case is that it would be a new problem attached to this paper’s setting, while every named result the proponent offers remains analytic. On the evidence here, no qualifying anchor survives.
