The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many hidden task-quality types \(q\) with rational masses \(\mu_q\), fresh workers drawn independently from \(F\), and expected response budget \(\gamma\) per unit task mass, what is the minimum limiting average classification error achievable by a policy that assigns further queries using observed response histories? For the Theorem 2.2 counterpart, assume the policy is also supplied the exact average difficulty of the unresolved mass at each round, and characterize the achievable error rate under the paper’s bounded-support-ratio conditions.

The model it lives in

Task-quality types \(q\) carry masses \(\mu_q\), with truth \(\operatorname{sign}(2q-1)\); each task unit receives an integral number of noisy labels from fresh workers with hidden \(p\sim F\). A policy chooses which fractions of tasks in each observed response-history class to query next. Its cost is expected response mass, and its objective is limiting average error mass; the analyzed version supplies the exact average difficulty of unresolved mass.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2.2 assumes exact knowledge of the average difficulty among unresolved tasks, although the platform cannot observe their latent qualities; the proponent notes this assumption but does not reconcile it with the stated history-only policy.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the adaptive budget-error guarantee of Theorem 2.2 and the minimax lower bound of Theorem 2.1. It leaves the non-adaptive cohort formulation of Theorem 3.2 and the paper’s spectral and parameter-estimation analyses aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact-oracle guarantee be replaced by a proved guarantee for a policy using only observable response histories?
  • What is the computational complexity of optimizing the finite-type mass policy as the type support and number of histories grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a continuous population of annotation tasks. Imagine a platform labeling a very large dataset: the number of tasks \(m\) grows, while the number of distinct task-difficulty types and worker-reliability types stays small. A task type is its latent \(q\), which determines its true label; a worker type is its reliability \(p\). Mass is the fraction of tasks of each type. Each task still receives an integral number of noisy labels, and workers still arrive individually with hidden types; mass lets the platform allocate more work to a fraction of tasks based on their response histories. The action is which histories to query next, the cost is the expected number of responses per unit task mass, and the objective is to minimize that cost subject to an average error target—or minimize error under a budget.

That regime is unusually natural for this paper: its objective is already average error over \(m\) tasks, it models task qualities through \(G\), and Algorithm 1 repeatedly carries forward the mass of tasks not yet classified. A continuous version can track fractions of tasks with each difficulty and response history while retaining the paper’s noisy-response model. The authors should recognize that as a high-multiplicity version of their task-assignment problem.

Theorem 2.2 is my lead anchor (proved in this paper, Section 6.4). It assumes the estimator in Algorithm 3 gives the exact average difficulty of the remaining tasks in each sub-round. For quantized task difficulties with \(\delta_{\max}/\delta_{\min}=O(1)\) and \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}=O(1)\), and budget \(\Gamma=\Theta(m\log m)\), Algorithm 1 uses at most \(\Gamma\) responses in expectation asymptotically. Its average error is at most
\[ C_1 e^{-(C_\delta/4)(\Gamma/m)\lambda\sigma^2} \]
when \((\Gamma/m)\lambda\sigma^2=\Theta(1)\), and tends to zero when that product is \(\omega(1)\). Here \(C_\delta=(4+\lceil\log(2\delta_{\max}/\delta_{\min})\rceil)^{-1}\), and \(C_1=\log_2(2\delta_{\max}/\delta_{\min})\log_2(2\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min})\).

Its continuous question is Adaptive Mass Annotation: given rational masses for finitely many task types, the worker-quality statistics \(\mu,\sigma^2\), the quantized difficulty distribution, and a target budget per task mass \(\gamma\), find an adaptive allocation policy and labeling rule minimizing limiting error mass. The policy may query different fractions of tasks with different response histories, but must use only the information available to the paper’s requester. I expect the bounded-support, bounded-ratio version to be tractable at the level of its asymptotic budget–error curve; the theorem gives a constructive policy. It does not establish the running time of an exact optimizer for the full continuous policy problem.

Theorem 2.1 (proved here, Section 6.1) supplies the lower-bound side of that question. For \(\sigma^2<1\), the worst-case average error over task distributions with collective difficulty \(\lambda\) and worker distributions with collective reliability \(\sigma^2\), minimized over all adaptive schemes using expected budget at most \(\Gamma\) and all inference rules, is at least
\[ \frac14 e^{-C'\Gamma\lambda\sigma^2/m} \]
for a positive constant \(C'\). Thus achieving error \(\varepsilon\) requires budget on the order of \(m(\lambda\sigma^2)^{-1}\log(1/\varepsilon)\). Its mirror is Minimax Mass Error: at a fixed budget per task mass, minimize worst-case error mass over adaptive policies and decoders, with the adversary restricted to the same \(\lambda,\sigma^2\) classes. I expect its rate version to be tractable: the paper gives a closed-form lower-bound rate, and Theorem 2.2 matches it up to constants under its assumptions. Exact finite-instance policy optimization remains open.

Theorem 3.2 (proved here, Section 6.8) gives a separate non-adaptive mirror. It proves that there is a worker distribution with reliability statistic \(\sigma^2\) such that, when task \(i\) has \(\lambda_i<1\), assigning it fewer than
\[ \frac{C'}{\sigma^2\lambda_i}\log(1/\varepsilon) \]
workers in any non-adaptive scheme prevents any estimator from attaining conditional error below \(\varepsilon\), for any \(m\) and \(r\). Its proof uses a lower bound from [15]. The corresponding question, Preassigned Mass Annotation, asks for the least response mass assigned in advance to a task-difficulty cohort to meet a conditional error target under the worst worker distribution allowed by \(\sigma^2\). I expect the rate problem to be tractable by typewise budget allocation. Together, these adaptive and non-adaptive questions preserve the paper’s central distinction: the adaptive budget depends on collective difficulty \(\lambda\), while the non-adaptive barrier is governed by the hardest tasks, \(\lambda_{\min}\). None of these anchors is an NP-hardness result, so there is no hardness-transfer claim to make.

The mirror also generates useful follow-up questions: can the exact-oracle assumption in Theorem 2.2 be removed with a proved guarantee; what happens when the type support grows; and can the mass-policy problem be expressed as a tractable optimization problem with a useful separation oracle?

The weak point is substantial. The clean continuum here is a population of tasks, not the society of preference-bearing agents that ChoCo is built around. The worker distribution is already part of the paper’s probabilistic model, and taking an unlimited continuum of workers while keeping tasks fixed could wash away response noise. Also, these theorems establish statistical budget–accuracy bounds, not the computational-complexity classifications that are ChoCo’s main target. So this is a credible high-multiplicity statistical-control mirror, especially for the task population the authors already study; it is only a borderline fit for ChoCo’s stricter computational-complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The three anchors establish statistical budget–accuracy trade-offs, not computational complexity results. Theorem 2.1 lower-bounds error for every adaptive policy and decoder; Theorem 3.2 gives a conditional per-task error lower bound for non-adaptive assignment. Theorem 2.2 analyzes one particular algorithm’s asymptotic error and expected budget, under an exact-oracle assumption for the average difficulty of the remaining tasks. None asks how hard it is to compute an optimal solution, or classifies that computation by input size, support size, or bit length. Calling a question “minimize error over policies” does not by itself make the paper’s theorem a computational result.

The strongest upgraded mirror is a continuous population of tasks: keep finitely many latent task types, represent their proportions as masses, track mass across response histories, and choose how much mass to query next. That is a plausible high-multiplicity crowdsourcing model. I would not object that tasks cannot have multiplicity: they are exchangeable in the model, the objective is average error, and the paper already reasons in terms of distributions over task difficulty. But this makes the workload continuous, not the society of agents in ChoCo’s computational-social-choice programme. Tasks are records to classify; workers supply noisy labels; the method infers each record’s label. There are no agents expressing preferences, no collective choice, and no social-choice rule whose computational problem is being continuized. This is a credible neighboring research direction, not a mirror for this programme.

For Theorem 2.2, that upgraded task-mass model would support a new stochastic-control problem over masses of response histories. The theorem does not solve or characterize the computational complexity of that problem: it analyzes Algorithm 1’s rate, with the oracle assumption, as the number of tasks grows. For Theorem 2.1, replacing an average over tasks by an average over task mass leaves an information-theoretic lower bound, not an algorithmic complexity question. For Theorem 3.2, a “cohort allocation” formulation that assigns work by latent difficulty assumes the requester can identify a task’s difficulty, which the paper explicitly says is hidden. Enforcing that information constraint means modeling adaptive responses and histories again; removing it changes the problem. Either way, the result remains a statistical rate statement, not a computational classification.

Making workers the continuous population does not improve the fit. The paper already models workers as fresh IID draws from \(F\), with no persistent identity to target or revisit. Replacing those draws with their limiting type distribution may simplify a statistical description, but it supplies no new society-level computational question of the kind ChoCo studies.

The negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo, not as a claim that no continuous model of this paper could be worth studying. A mass-based task-control problem could be worthwhile in crowdsourcing or active learning, and the paper’s asymptotic analysis makes that regime quite natural. If “continuous mirror” includes any continuous population in an adjacent field, the universal negative is weak. The anchors fail for ChoCo because their results are statistical and their natural population is a task population, not because high multiplicity is implausible.
