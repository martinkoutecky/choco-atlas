The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The absence of a numbered complexity theorem is real, but it does not establish that the paper has no worthwhile mirror. Its conclusion gives named empirical findings, and the strongest anchor is the Section 6.3 result: applying inferred four-approval and inferred-knapsack methods to the same rankings produces different project-cost outcomes. The paper is studying how ballot and aggregation rules change the funded portfolio—the object a computational mirror can ask questions about.

A natural mirror is minimum-mass project control. Let \(T\) be the complete rankings of the \(M\) projects and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of residents with ranking \(t\). Fix one of the paper’s aggregation rules, including its knapsack skip convention, and a target project \(p^\star\). Given a cost \(\gamma(t,t')\) for changing a voter’s ranking from \(t\) to \(t'\), ask for the least-cost mass transfer that makes \(p^\star\) funded:

\[ \min_{x\ge 0}\ \sum_{t,t'}\gamma(t,t')x_{t,t'} \quad\text{subject to}\quad p^\star\in A_r(\mu'), \]

where \(\mu'\) is the resulting distribution and \(A_r\) is the allocation produced by rule \(r\). Comparing this value for approval and knapsack aggregation asks whether the cost differences the paper reports also change how much of the population must shift to alter the actual allocation. Its complexity in the number of projects, support size, and input bit length is a computational question about the same population-to-allocation process.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a citywide PB election with many residents, a relatively small project list, and repeated complete rankings. Each type groups voters identical for the modeled preferences and ballot costs; the target remains the paper’s discrete project allocation. The question is naturally expressed in population shares, and voter identities are not needed to determine the outcome.

The paper’s empirical result does not already answer this control problem. It motivates it. So the proponent’s “no named computational result” point is a serious limitation on how directly the mirror is anchored, but it does not rule out a recognizable computational question about the paper’s central allocation pipeline. I would not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
