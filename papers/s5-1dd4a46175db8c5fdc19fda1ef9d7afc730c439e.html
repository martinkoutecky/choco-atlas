Theorem 2 says that if an apportionment at house size \(h\) satisfies pure lower quota, then a \(Q\)-apportionment at \(h\) does too. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for a mirror names no qualifying anchor. The strongest candidates are Theorems 1, 2, and 3, but each is a mathematical characterization or existence result. Theorem 1 characterizes quotatone solutions; Theorem 2 guarantees a \(Q\)-apportionment with pure lower quota under its stated condition; Theorem 3 gives the corresponding characterization with minimum requirements. None states the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s descriptions of methods as “eminently computable” and “computationally simplest” do not give an input encoding or a running-time result.

The strongest plausible setting would be a very large federation of interchangeable municipalities. Give each municipality a type \(t=(p,r)\), recording its population and minimum seat requirement, and represent the distribution of municipalities by \(\mu_t\). Scale the house with the number of municipalities and record, for each type, the distribution of integral seat bundles it receives. This could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But asking how to compute such an allocation, or how to extend the paper’s axioms to it, would introduce a new computational or axiomatic question. The theorems provide no complexity claim for that question to mirror.

Treating residents as the continuous society does not supply one either. The paper already takes each state’s population as an aggregate number \(p_i\); it does not make individual residents the objects receiving seats. Assigning fractional representation to resident mass would define a different allocation problem. The more faithful continuous construction uses municipalities as the repeated units, and runs into the absence of a computational result in the paper all the same.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated criterion: no result here anchors a continuous computational mirror. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile research could be built from the paper. A complexity study of a continuous apportionment model might be valuable, but it would be a new project inspired by these characterizations, rather than a mirror of one of the paper’s computational results.
