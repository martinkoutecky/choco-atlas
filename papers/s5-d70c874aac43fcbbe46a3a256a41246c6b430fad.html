Why no mirror

The mass-resistance tree problem is a plausible, author-recognizable high-multiplicity extension of Theorem 1. But Theorems 1, 2(ii), and 3 characterize equilibria without stating an algorithm, complexity result, or computational formulation used to compute them, so no anchor meets the required computational bit.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 1's Condorcet-selection claim under the BRM; it leaves Theorem 2(ii)'s policy-grid limit and Theorem 3's logit welfare and Borda result outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass-resistance tree criterion arise as a high-multiplicity limit of the finite-electorate BRM under a specified order of limits?
  • For explicit \(A\), do the mass-resistance roots preserve the Condorcet-winner conclusion for every \(\alpha\geq 1/2\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the required kind. Its main theorems characterize long-run equilibria; they do not classify an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. For example, Theorem 1 (proved here) says that, if a Condorcet winner exists, it is uniquely stochastically stable under the BRM for every quota \(q\geq \underline q\). Theorem 2(ii) (proved here) says that in the multidimensional Euclidean model, for quotas above the min-max quota, stationary probability concentrates on the min-max set as the policy grid is refined. Theorem 3 (proved here) says that under unanimity and logit choice, the stochastically stable policies are exactly the maximizers of total utility. These are equilibrium characterizations, not complexity claims. Theorem A.1’s minimum-cost-tree characterization is attributed to earlier work by Kandori, Mailath, and Rob and by Young; it is not a complexity result either. And the limit in Theorem 2 is over the policy grid, not the population.

The strongest positive lead is a continuous-population version of the problem behind Theorem 1. Consider a large electorate in which many residents share the same complete ranking over an explicit finite policy set \(A\). Represent it by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types \(T\), where \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population share. A status quo policy is repeatedly challenged, every policy has positive proposal probability, and a challenge needs at least an \(\alpha\) share of the vote, with \(\alpha\geq 1/2\). For policies \(a,b\), let \(s_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t\). Define the mass resistance of the transition \(a\to b\) as
\[ r_\alpha(a,b)=\max\{\alpha-s_\mu(a,b),0\}. \]
A precise follow-on problem is: given \(A,T,\mu,\alpha\), return all policies that minimize the total resistance of a directed spanning tree rooted at that policy. The tree-root criterion is the continuous analogue of the paper’s rare-mistake stability calculation. It keeps the paper’s status quo, pairwise voting, quota, and long-run robustness question, while replacing voter counts with population shares.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a city or region with many residents but relatively few distinct preference types. A type records everything the voting model uses, and \(\mu_t\) is a fraction of the population, not a fractional voter. Authors working with the paper’s repeated-vote process could recognize this as their stability question in a high-multiplicity electorate. Theorem 1 is the closest result supporting that case: its natural population-share counterpart predicts that a policy defeating each rival with more than half the mass is uniquely stable for every \(\alpha\geq 1/2\). But Theorem 1 is not an eligible computational anchor under the stated rule.

For an explicit policy set, the follow-on problem looks tractable: compute the pairwise support shares, form rational edge resistances, and find minimum-cost rooted arborescences. That suggests a Class A result, with running time polynomial in \(|A|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\). This is a derived algorithmic question, not a result asserted by the paper. It also yields no discrete hardness result whose dissolution in the continuum could be studied.

The weakest point is therefore decisive for the requested anchored case: there is no qualifying anchor, and the paper’s most explicitly continuous analysis continuizes policies rather than the population. The electorate mirror is concrete and defensible as a follow-on, but it cannot honestly be presented as an anchor-backed computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes stochastic stability; Theorem 2(ii) characterizes a limit over policy grids; Theorem 3 characterizes stable policies under logit choice. None states the complexity of a specified input-output problem. That matters here because the proposed mass-transfer tree calculation is a new algorithmic question, rather than a computational result from the paper carried into a population model.

The electorate mirror itself is sensible: many residents can share one of finitely many complete rankings, with rational masses over types. For Theorem 1, the proposed resistance \(r_\alpha(a,b)\) gives a coherent way to extend rare-mistake costs. But once the policies are explicitly listed, the input reduces to pairwise mass comparisons and a directed minimum-arborescence problem. The latter has a standard polynomial algorithm; population data only determine its edge weights. That may be a useful baseline exercise, but it supplies no pricing problem, continuous-optimization barrier, or complexity question specific to this paper. Making the policy set implicit could add difficulty, but the paper gives no succinct representation of such a set, so that would introduce a separate source of complexity.

Theorem 3’s best mirror is even more direct. Give each type its cardinal utilities and compute \(\sum_t \mu_t u_t(a)\) for every listed policy; the stable set is the maximizers. In the linear-ranking case, this is weighted Borda aggregation. With an explicit policy list and type support, this takes polynomial time by straightforward aggregation. The paper states a welfare characterization, not a difficult computation over societies.

Theorem 2(ii) is the strongest challenge to this negative case. A distribution over ideal-point types is a plausible population model, and computing its min-max set could lead to a real computational-geometry question. On a finite policy grid, though, finite support again gives a weighted pairwise-voting table. An exact problem over \(\mathbb R^h\) would need a representation of the distribution and a computational question the paper does not provide. It could be worthwhile follow-on work; the paper’s theorem alone does not establish that it is a worthwhile ChoCo mirror.

So the strongest defensible case against is about fit and contribution, not about whether a continuous electorate makes sense. The high-multiplicity regime is credible, and the logit and BRM models can be extended to masses. The weakness is that all three proposed anchors are equilibrium characterizations, while the computational tasks one can attach to them are either routine finite aggregation or newly specified problems. That leaves the universal claim—no scenario could produce a worthwhile mirror—unproved, especially for a richer ideal-point model.
