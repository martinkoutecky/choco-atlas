Why no mirror

A population distribution over response patterns gives a sensible way to restate the paper’s type classification and prediction tables. But the paper contains no named computational result to anchor that mirror, and the proposed calculation is only a weighted tally, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed summary covers Section 3’s rational and emotional type shares and their conditional answer distributions for P5 and P6. It leaves the six problems’ fairness comparisons, demographic analyses, and proposed fairness principles outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case for a continuous mirror is that the paper’s central empirical object is already a distribution over response patterns. Take a respondent’s type to be their six answers, \(t\in T=\{A,B,I\}^6\), where \(I\) denotes indifference. In a large repeated survey, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of respondents with pattern \(t\). The number of respondents could be far larger than the at most \(3^6=729\) patterns; people sharing a pattern are indistinguishable for the paper’s classification and prediction questions.

A concrete candidate is Continuous Fairness-Type Prevalence. Given the support and rational masses of \(\mu\), return the mass classified as “rational” (at least three indifferences in the first four answers) and “emotional” (within one answer of \((A,A,B,B)\) on the first four), along with the conditional answer distributions for questions P5 and P6 in each group. Its solution is those exact rational masses and conditional probabilities. This mirrors Section 3’s type classification and its test of whether the types predict later answers. I would expect it to be tractable: the outputs are weighted sums over the supported patterns. It is a plausible population-level version of the authors’ question, though a small one. It covers that empirical analysis, not the paper’s broader discussion of fairness principles. Further questions—such as bounding type prevalence from incomplete aggregate data—would be extensions.

There are no qualifying anchors in the paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. The reported type shares and response frequencies are empirical findings, not named computational results. The theorem mentioned in the discussion of P4 is Gibbard’s cited result about random dictatorship and axiomatic properties; it is neither a numbered result of this paper nor a complexity result. So I cannot honestly present the candidate above as a continuous problem *per anchor*.

That is the case’s weakest point and its limit: the continuous population model is sensible for the paper’s survey question, but its computation is essentially weighted recounting. The paper supplies no discrete hardness result whose behavior in the continuum could be studied, and no named algorithmic result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The closest continuous mirror is the one proposed: treat each six-answer pattern as a type and its share as a mass. That is a sensible high-multiplicity description of a survey population. But it only turns the paper’s tables into weighted sums. For each supported pattern, one checks whether it meets the paper’s “rational” or “emotional” definition, then adds its mass to the relevant total. The conditional P5 and P6 distributions are the same kind of tally, followed by division. With six questions there are at most \(3^6=729\) patterns, and even if the number of questions grew, the explicit-support calculation would still just scan the input. This is a valid descriptive computation, but it gives no computational question of the kind the programme is meant to chart.

A stronger candidate might ask how much the population’s composition must change to alter the reported type shares or predictions. That would add an intervention or robustness problem the paper does not study. The paper specifies no costs for changing responses, no allowed changes, and no objective for choosing among them. Supplying those ingredients could produce an optimization problem, but it would be a new model built around the survey data, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper. Likewise, optimizing over a larger menu of procedures would require preference information the questionnaire never elicited: its six pairwise comparisons do not give a common ranking over an extensible set of procedures.

The central limitation is that the paper reports experimental findings and proposes explanations; it proves no named computational result. Its type classification is a way of summarizing observed responses, not an algorithmic problem whose complexity or tractability the paper investigates. The Gibbard result mentioned in P4 is a cited axiomatic characterization, not a result of this paper and not a computational anchor.

So the negative case should not rest on denying that survey respondents can share types. They plainly can, and a distribution over those types is a reasonable population model. The problem is that the faithful mirror is only recounting, while a computationally richer version needs substantive assumptions and questions added from outside the paper. I cannot rule out a worthwhile research project on computational models of perceived procedural fairness; I can say this paper does not supply a compelling continuous-complexity mirror to motivate one.
