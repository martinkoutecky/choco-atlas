The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\) rational, piecewise-linear, nondecreasing concave production functions \(p_i:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), with \(p_i(0)=0\), an initial allocation \(x^0\in\Delta_k\), and a resource \(q\) whose marginal output exceeds that of every other resource at every feasible load, determine the least number \(R\) of rounds needed for \(p_q(1)-\sum_i p_i(x_i^R)\le\varepsilon\). In each synchronous round, each infinitesimal unit of mass samples a resource uniformly and switches when its offered marginal output exceeds its current one; offers use the pre-round allocation, and marginal values at kinks use the appropriate one-sided slopes.

The model it lives in

A single type of interchangeable agents has unit mass split among \(k\) resources, with allocation \(x\in\Delta_k\) and total welfare \(\sum_i p_i(x_i)\). Under the dominant-resource condition, the optimum is \(p_q(1)\); the protocol’s mass dynamics and the number of rounds to reach welfare gap at most \(\varepsilon\) are the computational objects.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s dominant-resource convergence bound. It leaves the general convergence and strategic-behavior results, including Theorems 3–5, unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the tight worst-case round bound in terms of \(k\), \(\varepsilon\), and the initial allocation?
  • Under the stated per-capita scaling, how uniformly does the finite stochastic protocol approach the deterministic mass trajectory as \(n\) grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named NP-hardness or standard complexity-class result, so the strongest honest anchors are its algorithmic results. My lead is Theorem 6, proved here: with \(n\) agents and \(k\) resources, the proposed randomized protocol reaches an optimal allocation in expected \(O(k\ln n)\) rounds, with the paper giving \(E(X)<4k\ln n\). Its analysis handles a highly unbalanced optimum in which one resource’s marginal production dominates the others, then argues that case is worst. A second useful anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here: under the suggested strategies, every protocol-stable allocation is optimal.

A natural regime is a large batch of identical grid or cloud jobs that each need exactly one of \(k\) storage resources. They share the same requirements and utility, while each resource’s total output has diminishing returns as more jobs use it. This is already close to the paper’s grid-computing example. There are \(n\) jobs but only one agent type, so \(\tau=1\); mass represents the fraction of the batch assigned to a resource. To model population growth sensibly, take output per capita as the population is scaled: a discrete production curve on \(n\) jobs is represented in the limit by a concave curve on mass in \([0,1]\).

The common continuous instance has rational, piecewise-linear, nondecreasing concave production functions \(p_i:[0,1]\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(p_i(0)=0\), for \(i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\). An allocation is \(x\in\Delta_k\), where \(x_i\) is the mass using resource \(i\), and \(\mathrm{SW}(x)=\sum_i p_i(x_i)\). A unit of mass on resource \(i\) is offered its marginal output; at a kink, use the appropriate one-sided slope.

My lead mirror is Dominant-Resource \(\varepsilon\)-Convergence. Its instance adds an initial allocation \(x^0\), a precision \(\varepsilon>0\), and a resource \(q\) whose marginal output exceeds every other resource’s at every feasible load: \(p_q'(u)>p_j'(v)\) for all \(j\ne q\) and feasible \(u,v\). Each round, every mass unit samples a resource uniformly from the \(k\) resources and switches if its offered marginal output is higher than its current one; offers are evaluated at the start of the round. The question is to produce a round \(R\) and the resulting allocation \(x^R\) with \(\mathrm{OPT}-\mathrm{SW}(x^R)\le\varepsilon\), using as few rounds as possible or certifying a bound. Here \(\mathrm{OPT}=p_q(1)\), and each round gives \(x_q^{r+1}=x_q^r+(1-x_q^r)/k\). If \(M=\max_{i,u}p_i'(u)\), then \(\mathrm{OPT}-\mathrm{SW}(x^R)\le M(1-x_q^R)\), so \(O(k\ln(M/\varepsilon))\) rounds suffice. With \(\varepsilon=M/n\), this recovers the \(O(k\ln n)\) scaling. In the atomless model the random choices yield deterministic mass fractions; this is the continuous counterpart of the finite population’s expected bound.

The complementary Concave Mass Allocation and Stability mirror anchors Theorem 3. Given the \(p_i\), find \(x\in\Delta_k\) maximizing \(\mathrm{SW}(x)\). Call \(x\) stable if no infinitesimal transfer from any used resource \(i\) to any resource \(j\) has positive directional derivative: \(p_j^+(x_j)-p_i^-(x_i)\le0\). The question is whether every such stable allocation is globally optimal, and the solution is an allocation attaining the maximum. Concavity makes this the natural limit of “no agent can improve by switching”; the maximum is computable by a linear program over the piecewise-linear curves, so this is Class A. It directly mirrors the underlying allocation question rather than encoding the paper’s protocol.

Theorem 6 is the computational lead; Theorem 3 supplies the cleanest structural bridge. I would not extend this case to the paper’s incentive results: those would need a separate continuous model of resource-owner strategies and profits.

The weak point is that Theorem 6’s finite-agent proof does not itself prove the proposed continuum rate. An atomless society has no last agent, so exact convergence generally becomes \(\varepsilon\)-convergence, and the normalization of discrete production curves is a modeling choice. That leaves a real question for a prover, rather than a result one can claim for free. The mirror remains plausible because the population is the object being continuized, the type regime is exceptionally strong (\(\tau=1\)), and both the mass-allocation objective and the marginal-return stability condition preserve the paper’s central problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about what these results contribute, not whether this paper admits a sensible high-multiplicity regime. It plainly does: a large batch of interchangeable jobs is a credible one-type population, and mass is a natural way to describe its allocation. So the objection that agents are individuated, or that no continuous society makes sense, does not hold.

For Theorem 6, the paper bounds the rounds of a sequential protocol in which individual agents randomly discover resources. A continuous analogue can be made precise: take production curves \(p_i^{(n)}(j)=n f_i(j/n)\), and let mass switch to a resource whose marginal output is higher. If \(q\) dominates, its mass grows at rate \(\dot x_q=(1-x_q)/k\), giving \(1-x_q(t)=(1-x_q(0))e^{-t/k}\). This yields an \(\varepsilon\)-convergence bound of order \(k\ln(M/\varepsilon)\). It is a valid mirror, but it changes the finite-population coupon-collector question into a simple fluid-rate calculation. Exact convergence takes infinite time; setting \(\varepsilon=M/n\) imports a chosen finite resolution into the continuum. That makes the mirror less informative about the paper’s specific runtime result, though it does not make the approximation question illegitimate.

For Theorem 3, a better mirror is also available. With concave production curves, stability against infinitesimal transfers is the first-order optimality condition for maximizing \(\sum_i p_i(x_i)\) over the simplex. For piecewise-linear curves this is an LP. The paper already observes that the finite allocation objective can be optimized greedily, so this mirror isolates a clean structural fact but does not expose a difficult computational boundary. That is a reason to doubt its research yield, not a reason the question fails to qualify: a standard LP is still a continuous computational problem.

Thus I cannot honestly defeat both anchors. The defensible negative is narrower: these mirrors look like a routine concave allocation problem and its fluid dynamics, while the paper’s distinctive contribution is decentralized interaction under private information and strategic behavior. A continuous model could preserve owners and their incentives, but that would require substantial additional modeling and analysis. I do not see a solid basis for the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; the case against is weak on that point.
