The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type graph \(H=(T,E)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), capacities \(b_t\), and deal revenues \(w_{tu}\), define each coalition's worth by \(\Gamma(z)=\max_{q\ge 0}\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}q_{tu}\), subject to \(\sum_{u\ne t:\{t,u\}\in E}q_{tu}+2q_{tt}\le b_tz_t\) for every \(t\), where \(0\le z\le\mu\) and \(q_{tt}\) counts deals between distinct companies of type \(t\). Compute the largest \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and nonnegative per-company payments \(p_t\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_tp_t\le\Gamma(\mu)\) and \(\sum_tz_tp_t\ge\alpha\Gamma(z)\) for every coalition composition \(z\le\mu\).

The model it lives in

A society consists of rational masses \(\mu_t\) of companies of each type \(t\); companies of a type share capacities and deal terms with every other type. The decisions are deal masses \(q_{tu}\) and per-company payments \(p_t\). Maximize the core approximation factor \(\alpha\) subject to grand-coalition budget and all coalition guarantees. The fractional \(b\)-matching dual yields \(\alpha=1\) with a polynomial-size LP.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 11's approximate-core allocation result and Mechanism 6 in fixed-\(\tau\), replicated markets with block-constant interactions. It leaves the discrete arbitrary-graph \(2/3\) integrality-gap tightness and cited core-membership hardness outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed type graph \(H\), what finite-population error bound relates markets with type counts approaching \(n\mu_t\) to the exact core of the continuous model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper. It says that every multiple partners matching game has a \(2/3\)-approximate core, and that \(2/3\) is the best possible factor *relative to the natural LP relaxation*. Mechanism 6 constructs such an allocation. The paper does not give a numbered theorem classifying a problem as P or NP-hard; its co-NP-hardness statement about exact core membership is cited background, not a result proved here.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large marketplace of companies that can make bilateral deals. Companies share a type when they have the same deal capacity and the same opportunities and revenue with every counterpart type. Let \(H=(T,E)\) be the finite graph of compatible types, with capacity \(b_t\) for type \(t\) and deal revenue \(w_{tu}\) for each compatible pair. A loop at \(t\) represents deals between distinct companies of type \(t\). The population has rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. The regime is many companies but fixed or modest \(|T|=\tau\): each type is repeated many times, and all agents of a type are indistinguishable for the game.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous-Type Multiple Partners Core Allocation. For any subpopulation \(z\), where \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), define its worth per member of the whole population as
\[ \Gamma(z)=\max_{q\ge0}\ \sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}q_{tu} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{u\ne t:\{t,u\}\in E}q_{tu}+2q_{tt}\le b_tz_t \quad\text{for each }t. \]
Here \(q_{tu}\) is the mass of deals between the two types; \(q_{tt}\) counts same-type deals, each of which uses capacity from two distinct agents. This is the limiting value of integral b-matchings in large replicated populations, normalized by population size. Fractional deal counts arise from aggregating many agents’ integral deals.

The input is \(H\), its rational capacities and deal revenues, and the masses \(\mu\). The question is to compute the largest \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and nonnegative per-company payoffs \(p_t\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_tp_t\le\Gamma(\mu)\) and, for every coalition composition \(z\le\mu\), \(\sum_tz_tp_t\ge\alpha\Gamma(z)\). The output is \((\alpha,p)\) satisfying those conditions. This keeps the population as the continuous object; the payoff and deal totals are type-level averages of a society made of many companies.

I expect this problem to be tractable, with \(\alpha=1\). For the grand population, take an optimal dual solution \(y\) to the LP defining \(\Gamma(\mu)\), and set \(p_t=b_ty_t\). The dual constraints are \(y_t+y_u\ge w_{tu}\) and, for same-type deals, \(2y_t\ge w_{tt}\). The same \(y\) bounds every coalition’s worth by \(\sum_t b_tz_ty_t=\sum_tz_tp_t\), while duality gives total payment equal to grand-coalition worth. Thus an exact continuous core allocation follows from a polynomial-size LP. This is the continuous analogue of the paper’s allocation question, though its answer improves on the discrete \(2/3\) guarantee.

This case covers Theorem 11’s approximate-core allocation result and its constructive mechanism, in the replicated type-market regime. It does not claim a mirror for arbitrary graphs, exact-core membership hardness, or the paper’s other cited results. Further questions include how quickly finite replicated markets approach the continuous core, and what happens when the number of types grows with the population or companies have individual-specific deal opportunities.

The weakest point is that replication into a finite type graph imposes substantial structure, and the limit removes the odd-cycle integrality loss responsible for the paper’s sharp \(2/3\) bound. So this mirror does not preserve the paper’s tight discrete phenomenon. The positive case is that it retains the same capacity-constrained partnership game and asks what its allocation problem becomes in a genuine high-multiplicity regime; the fractional b-matching LP is already central to the authors’ mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it assumes a narrow graph regime: every company of one type has the same deal opportunities and revenues with every company of another type. That gives a complete block structure. For arbitrary networks, exact types may be rare, so the type count could grow with the population and the claimed compression disappear. The paper’s sharp \(2/3\) example also relies on odd cycles among individual vertices; with a fixed number of repeated types, the normalized integrality loss can vanish.

That is a real limit on the mirror’s reach, but it does not defeat this instance regime. Companies whose deals depend on sector or product category provide a plausible setting for block-constant interactions. In that setting, type masses are meaningful, and the coalition value \(\Gamma(z)\) captures their aggregate capacity. The dual-price argument then gives a well-posed continuous-population core question. The answer \(\alpha=1\) does not make the question invalid under the programme’s criteria.

There is a priority concern: the paper itself notes the connection to linear production games, and this mirror becomes a small LP with dual prices. That could make it a modest contribution. It still does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists; a known or simple answer is not grounds to reject a well-posed question. Theorem 11 is a named constructive allocation result, so the anchor has a genuine computational object to mirror. I cannot honestly make the case that this mirror is not worthwhile in any plausible scenario.
