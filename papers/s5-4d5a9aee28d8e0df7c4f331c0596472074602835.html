The continuous mirror question

Input: a finite hospital set H; a finite type support T with rational masses μ_t>0 summing to 1; each type t has a strict ranking ≻_t over H and a binary acceptability vector a_ht; and each hospital h has a rational per-capita cap κ_h>0. Does there exist an assignment of mass x_th≥0 with ∑_h x_th≤μ_t, x_th=0 when a_ht=0, and ℓ_h=∑_t x_th≤κ_h, such that ℓ_h>0 for every hospital and the allocation is stable? Define q_th=μ_t-∑_g x_tg+∑_{g:h≻_t g}x_tg; stability requires that, for every acceptable pair (t,h), q_th>0 implies ℓ_h=κ_h. Equivalently, maximize z subject to these constraints and z≤ℓ_h for every h, and ask whether the optimum is positive.

The model it lives in

An atomless market with finite H and finite type support T. Each type records a strict hospital ranking and its acceptability vector; rational mass μ_t is allocated through variables x_th. Hospital h has continuous capped-additive value min{κ_h,∑_t a_ht x_th}; each individual receives a whole hospital, while cohort mass can divide across assignments. Stability rules out any positive mass of acceptable type that prefers an underfilled hospital.

The objection that survived

The discrete threshold normalizes to 1/N, but the mirror’s ℓ_h>0 also accepts allocations whose hospital mass vanishes along a sequence of growing markets; a fixed ε threshold would change the objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.6’s stable-coverage decision under capped binary valuations; it leaves the mechanism and welfare results, including Theorems 3.1, 3.7, 4.3, and 4.6’s proof of hardness transfer, aside. Continuum matching with finite institutions and a continuum of students is established neighboring work, including market-clearing characterizations and a deferred-acceptance analogue [Azevedo and Leshno](https://doi.org/10.1086/687476), [Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881); it does not answer this matroidal hospital-coverage complexity question.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable |H|, can positive-mass stable coverage be decided in polynomial time, or does choosing which hospitals are full make it hard?
  • Can the paper’s SAT gadget force a discrete variable choice despite splittable type mass, or is there a rounding theorem from rational stable allocations to clone markets?
  • How does the answer change when every hospital must receive at least a fixed mass ε>0?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is Stable Coverage\(_\infty\), based on Theorem 4.6, proved in this paper. The theorem says that deciding whether a stable allocation exists in which every hospital receives at least one doctor is NP-complete, even when hospitals have capped binary valuations. The authors prove this by reduction from 2P2N-3SAT, where each variable occurs twice positively and twice negatively. In their construction, a satisfying assignment gives a stable allocation covering every hospital; if the formula is unsatisfiable, every stable allocation leaves some hospital without a doctor.

For the mirror, keep a finite set \(H\) of hospitals and describe the doctor population by its \(\tau\) types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type records a complete strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of the hospitals and its binary acceptability vector \(a_{ht}\in\{0,1\}\): hospital \(h\) values every member of type \(t\) at one if \(a_{ht}=1\), and at zero otherwise. Each hospital has a rational per-capita cap \(\kappa_h>0\). Its value on an assigned mass is the continuous capped-additive extension of the paper’s valuation.

The decision variable is \(x_{th}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to hospital \(h\). Require \(x_{th}\ge 0\), \(\sum_h x_{th}\le\mu_t\), and \(x_{th}=0\) whenever \(a_{ht}=0\). Write \(\ell_h=\sum_t x_{th}\) for hospital \(h\)’s accepted mass, and require \(\ell_h\le\kappa_h\). Unassigned mass is \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_h x_{th}\). For an acceptable pair \((t,h)\), define the mass of type \(t\) currently assigned below \(h\), or unassigned, as \(q_{th}=u_t+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}x_{tg}\). Stability means that if \(q_{th}>0\), then \(h\) is full: \(\ell_h=\kappa_h\). Otherwise a positive, arbitrarily small portion of that type could move to \(h\), strictly improving the doctors’ assignment and increasing the hospital’s value. The question is whether there is a stable \(x\) with \(\ell_h>0\) for every hospital. Equivalently, maximize the minimum hospital mass \(z\), subject to stability and \(z\le\ell_h\) for all \(h\), and ask whether the optimum is positive.

A plausible regime is a large residency or placement market: many applicants, but far fewer distinct combinations of hospital rankings and qualifications, with programs valuing qualified applicants equally up to their intake caps. A type must include both its ranking and its acceptability vector, so these are genuinely interchangeable cohorts under everything the model uses. The mirror is most compelling when the number of applicants \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\). Normalizing a discrete instance gives \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), \(\kappa_h=b_h/N\), and \(x_{th}=n_{th}/N\). The discrete requirement of at least one doctor becomes \(\ell_h\ge 1/N\); its continuous counterpart is \(\ell_h>0\).

This should be recognizable to the authors: it asks their same underlying question—whether stability permits every hospital to be served—while making the doctor population the mass object. A type’s assigned mass represents a fraction of equivalent doctors receiving whole assignments; it does not give an individual a fractional hospital assignment. The mirror covers Theorem 4.6’s stable-coverage question under capped binary valuations. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s mechanism-design or welfare-maximization results.

My working prediction is Class C: hard for continuum-specific reasons, though that is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 4.6. For any fixed set \(F\) of hospitals designated as full, the stability and allocation conditions reduce to rational linear constraints: hospitals in \(F\) have \(\ell_h=\kappa_h\), and every other hospital must have \(q_{th}=0\) for each acceptable type. Enumerating \(F\) gives a \(2^{|H|}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) approach, so the remaining combinatorics may lie in choosing the saturated hospitals. That suggests a tractable fixed-\(|H|\) boundary and a potential hardness question when the number of hospitals grows.

The crucial caveat is that Theorem 4.6’s hardness does not automatically transfer. “At least one doctor” becomes “some positive mass,” which can be arbitrarily small. In the SAT gadget, doctors are indivisible and the capacity accounting forces a variable’s positive and negative literals into a discrete choice. A fractional type mass may split between those literal roles and erase that constraint. The paper’s reduction also does not establish hardness for a regime with \(\tau\ll N\). So the right next questions are whether stable positive coverage admits an integral-clone rounding theorem, whether the saturation choices yield NP-hardness without indivisibility, and how the answer changes if each hospital must receive at least a specified mass \(\varepsilon>0\). The weakness is real: until one of those questions is resolved, this is a credible continuous problem anchored in the paper, but not a hardness-transfer result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is the coverage threshold. Theorem 4.6 asks whether each hospital can receive at least one indivisible doctor. After normalizing a market of size \(N\), that threshold is \(1/N\). Its limit is \(\ell_h>0\), which detects any positive mass, however small. For example, a type of mass \(1/N\) that accepts only hospital \(h\) can cover it in every finite market while its mass vanishes in the limit. Requiring a fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) would avoid that instability, but it changes the question; choosing \(\varepsilon=1/N\) brings the vanishing finite-market scale back into the model. Maximizing \(z=\min_h\ell_h\) is mathematically clean, but \(z>0\) still treats an arbitrarily small allocation as coverage.

That is a real limitation of this anchor, but it does not defeat the better mirror. The proposed type—ranking plus acceptability vector—is complete for the capped-binary case, and moving a positive portion of a type to an underfilled acceptable hospital gives a coherent mass version of blocking. The authors could decide that positive-share coverage, or max-min hospital service, is the computational question they want to study. The discrete hardness proof would not establish hardness for that version, but the failure of hardness to transfer is not itself an objection under the programme’s criteria.

There is also established continuum stable-matching work with a continuum of students and finite institutions, including market-clearing characterizations and deferred-acceptance analogues ([Azevedo and Leshno](https://doi.org/10.1086/687476), [Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)). That makes the cohort setting credible and gives the authors prior art to position against; it does not, by itself, settle the computational complexity of this coverage objective or create a novelty collision.

So the negative case is weak. It exposes a scale choice the mirror must make explicit, but I cannot honestly rule out the positive-mass or max-min version as worthwhile. Theorem 4.6’s anchor survives if the authors recognize that coverage question as theirs.
