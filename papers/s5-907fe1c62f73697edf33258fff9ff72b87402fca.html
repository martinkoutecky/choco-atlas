What the mirror covers

The paper's results concern expert truthfulness, allocation feasibility, amateur best response, false-name proofness, and the impossibility of combining efficiency with expert truthfulness; the proposed LP addresses only welfare allocation after qualities are known.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest foothold is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: even when every good has multiple experts, no protocol can guarantee both Pareto-efficient allocation and strategy-proofness for experts. That is a named impossibility result, but it is not a computational-complexity result: it says nothing about whether a problem is tractable or hard. Theorem 2’s expert truthfulness, Theorem 3’s allocation feasibility, Theorem 4’s result for amateurs, and Theorem 5’s false-name-proofness likewise establish mechanism properties, not complexity classifications. So the paper has no qualifying named complexity anchor for the requested programme.

A plausible population setting would be a large auction market or repeated procurement market with many bidders sharing complete types. A type \(t\) would include whether the bidder is an expert or amateur, the goods they know about, and their valuation for every quality-labelled bundle. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of bidders of that type. If supply scales with the market, let \(b_j\) be the per-capita supply of good \(j\), and let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). The candidate problem is: given \(\mu\), the true quality state \(q\), valuations, and supplies \(b\), find a feasible allocation maximizing
\[ \sum_{t,B} x_{t,B}v_t(B,q), \]
subject to \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:j\in B}x_{t,B}\le b_j\). Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the type-level average assignment is fractional. With valuations listed for all bundles, this is an LP.

That is a precise continuous allocation problem, and its efficiency objective connects to the paper’s discussion around Theorem 6. But it is not a faithful mirror of the paper’s main contribution: it drops the strategic problem of eliciting experts’ private quality information, and its LP tractability does not mirror a result proved in the paper. A more faithful question would ask for a continuous-mass mechanism that is feasible, efficient, and truthful for experts. Yet for atomless individuals, one person’s deviation has zero effect on the reported distribution, making truthfulness vacuous; requiring truthful behavior from positive-mass cohorts changes the strategic model.

So the positive case is limited: there is a sensible continuous welfare-allocation question nearby, but I would not present it as an anchored continuization of this paper’s results. The soft spot is fundamental: preserving the paper’s incentive claims while making the population nonatomic risks removing the very strategic force those claims concern.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 6 proves that Pareto efficiency and expert strategyproofness cannot both be guaranteed in the stated setting. Theorems 2–5 establish properties of the proposed protocol. None studies an algorithm, a complexity class, or a running time. Theorem 6 is the closest anchor, but its continuous counterpart would still be a mechanism-existence claim, not a computational result.

A large market with many bidders sharing complete valuation and expertise profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. That supports trying a continuous model; it does not supply the missing computational question. A careful lift of Theorem 6 could use positive-mass cohorts and ask whether an efficient, expert-truthful mechanism exists. That may be worthwhile mechanism-design research. To make it a computational problem, though, one would need to specify how mechanisms are represented and what finite input the algorithm receives. The paper gives no such framework. I also would not lean on the claim that atomlessness makes truthfulness vacuous: an individual deviation may leave aggregate prices unchanged while still changing that person’s report-dependent allocation. Preserving that strategic question keeps the mirror in mechanism design; it does not make it a complexity result.

The proposed welfare LP is a sound nearby optimization problem. With realized quality and valuations supplied, one can maximize \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}v_t(B,q)\) subject to mass and supply constraints. But that formulation removes the private-quality information and truthfulness at the heart of Theorem 6. The paper itself distinguishes efficient allocation from truthful revelation: it notes that VCG can achieve efficiency while failing to guarantee expert truthfulness. Restoring private information and incentive constraints leads back to the mechanism-existence question. The paper also gives no compact valuation or mechanism encoding from which to derive a distinct algorithmic challenge.

So I would reject the proponent’s anchors as ChoCo computational mirrors: the LP is an adjacent welfare problem, and the strongest faithful lift is a continuous mechanism-design question. The negative case is weak only against the user’s universal wording. I cannot rule out that this lift would be worthwhile for mechanism-design researchers; I can say the paper, as written, offers no named computational result to anchor it in ChoCo’s stated programme.
