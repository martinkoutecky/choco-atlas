The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\), per-capita task-category supplies \(q_j\), rewards \(r_j\), success probabilities \(p_{\theta j}\), and costs \(c_{\theta j}\), choose task shares \(\alpha_j\), masses \(\lambda_{\theta B}\) assigned to integral bundles \(B\), and subsidies \(s_{\theta B}\) for occupied type-bundle states. Subject to \(\sum_B\lambda_{\theta B}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,B:j\in B}\lambda_{\theta B}=q_j\), effort feasibility, and envy-freeness between every pair of occupied states, determine the maximum revenue per unit population, or decide whether it is at least a given rational threshold \(R\). What is the computational complexity as a function of the number of categories \(K\), type-support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A population with finite complete worker types \(\theta\) and rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), supplied with recurring task categories at per-capita rates \(q_j\). Each worker receives an integral bundle \(B\); the decision variables are shared category contract shares \(\alpha_j\), population masses \(\lambda_{\theta B}\), and state subsidies \(s_{\theta B}\). Maximize aggregate revenue per unit population subject to the paper's effort and envy constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror asks about the complexity of optimal EFS revenue maximization in a high-multiplicity setting, motivated by Theorem 5.2. It does not claim to mirror the price-of-fairness bounds, the EF-versus-EFS revenue separation, or the constant-task algorithm.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the problem polynomial-time solvable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), with complexity measured in \(K,\tau,L\)?
  • Does any hardness survive when worker types have positive population mass and task supply scales with the population, or does the fractional mass allocation remove the partition obstruction?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is high-multiplicity EFS revenue maximization for a platform or crowdwork setting with many workers and a continuing supply of task opportunities. The workers have a finite set \(\Theta\) of intrinsic types: two workers share a type exactly when they have the same success probabilities and effort costs for every task category. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be the population fraction of type \(\theta\), with support size \(\tau\). Task supply scales with the workforce: category \(j\) has \(q_j\) opportunities per unit mass of workers. This keeps individual workloads meaningful as the population grows.

Here is the prover’s problem. An instance gives rational \(\mu_\theta\), \(q_j\), rewards \(r_j\), probabilities \(p_{\theta j}\), costs \(c_{\theta j}\), and a revenue threshold \(R\). Each worker still receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq[K]\), with at most one task from each category. The decision variables are task shares \(\alpha_j\in[0,1]\), population masses \(\lambda_{\theta B}\) assigned to each type and bundle, and subsidies \(s_{\theta B}\ge 0\) for occupied type-bundle states. They must satisfy
\[ \sum_B\lambda_{\theta B}=\mu_\theta,\qquad \sum_{\theta,B:j\in B}\lambda_{\theta B}=q_j. \]
For every occupied state \((\theta,B)\), assigned tasks must satisfy \(\alpha_jp_{\theta j}r_j-c_{\theta j}\ge 0\). For every pair of occupied states \((\theta,B)\), \((\theta',B')\), envy-freeness requires
\[ \sum_{j\in B}(\alpha_jp_{\theta j}r_j-c_{\theta j})+s_{\theta B} \;\ge\; \sum_{j\in B'}\max\{\alpha_jp_{\theta j}r_j-c_{\theta j},0\} +s_{\theta'B'}. \]
The revenue per unit population is
\[ \sum_{\theta,B}\lambda_{\theta B} \left( \sum_{j\in B}(1-\alpha_j)p_{\theta j}r_j-s_{\theta B} \right). \]
The question is whether a feasible plan reaches \(R\), or equivalently what the exact optimal revenue is. Each worker has a whole bundle; the \(\lambda_{\theta B}\) are fractional population masses. This preserves the paper’s allocation, incentive, subsidy, envy, and revenue choices while putting the population into the continuous object.

The scenario is plausible for a large platform with recurring task categories and a relatively small number of worker profiles. With \(N\) workers, type \(\theta\) has about \(\mu_\theta N\) workers and category \(j\) has about \(q_jN\) opportunities. The paper’s task categories and agent parameters are explicit in this model; repeated opportunities with identical parameters are grouped. A subsidy can be shared by a type-bundle state: if two agents have the same intrinsic type and bundle, envy-freeness in both directions forces their subsidies to agree.

My anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper: unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm computes an optimal EFS contract in the paper’s finite-agent, finite-task model. The authors prove it by reducing from restricted \(3\)-Partition. Their construction has \(q+1\) agents and \(3q+1\) tasks, and establishes that optimal revenue reaches \(1/2\) exactly when the \(3\)-Partition instance is solvable. Thus even the threshold version is NP-hard. The hardness proof is the paper’s result; \(3\)-Partition is the cited source problem.

I expect the high-multiplicity version to be a Class A candidate, especially for fixed or small \(\tau\). The reduction’s normal agents have identical intrinsic parameters, while its packing step assigns indivisible item tasks among named agents. In the mirror, the mass assigned to a type can be divided among bundle states, so that packing step may admit fractional solutions. The algorithmic question is whether those states can be handled by a configuration LP or an efficient pricing procedure, and whether the envy constraints on occupied states can be represented without making support selection itself hard. I would ask for complexity in \(K,\tau,L\), and in particular whether the problem is FPT in \(\tau\) or polynomial for fixed \(\tau\). This is an expectation, not a claimed tractability result.

The weakest point is that the reduction does not directly become an instance of this mirror. Its single special agent has vanishing population share as \(q\) grows, and its distinct item tasks carry the indivisibility that drives the partition. The mirror scales task supply with population and lets each category’s mass be assigned across workers. That co-modification is allowed for a per-capita supply regime, and recurring task categories make it credible, but it means the continuous problem is a genuine new question rather than a limit already established by the theorem. I would keep the claim narrow: this mirrors Theorem 5.2’s EFS revenue-optimization problem, not the paper’s price-of-fairness bounds or all its results. The key follow-up is whether the \(3\)-Partition gap survives with positive-mass worker types and divisible category supply, or whether it disappears in the continuous model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 5.2 does not itself provide a faithful population limit. Its \(3\)-Partition reduction has \(q\) normal agents with identical parameters, but its \(3q\) one-off item tasks carry the distinct values \(a_k\). The revenue threshold forces each normal agent to receive items summing to exactly \(B\), which recovers the partition. The reduction’s certificate is therefore a packing of individually specified tasks, not a distinction among otherwise interchangeable workers.

Keeping those one-off tasks while making the workers nonatomic creates a problem: each task can go to only one worker, a set of zero population mass, so per-capita revenue degenerates. To get a meaningful per-capita model, task supply must scale with the workforce. That is allowed by the programme, but it replaces the one-copy items with recurring task categories and does not carry over the reduction as stated. This weakens Theorem 5.2 as evidence for a *direct* continuous counterpart; it does not show that the counterpart has no value.

The proponent’s revised model survives that objection. A platform with many workers, a finite set of complete worker types, and recurring task categories is plausible, and the paper itself motivates its setting with platform work and crowdwork. Its mass allocations over integral bundles preserve the individual allocation choice; the subsidies and envy constraints can be defined for occupied type-bundle states. The question is still computational and still continuizes the population. The fact that the reduction’s special agent has vanishing share, or that replicated task supply changes its partition gadget, does not invalidate this model. Nor would a different complexity outcome count against asking the question.

I also see no “already done” objection in the text: the cited subsidy work concerns fair division or fixed contracts, not joint optimization of incentives, task assignments, subsidies, and revenue in this continuous-population model. And the paper’s \(q\) normal agents already show that repeated worker profiles are not foreign to its mathematical setting.

So I can undercut the claimed connection between the \(3\)-Partition proof and the proposed mirror, but I cannot honestly defeat the mirror itself. The negative case is weak exactly where the brief says it must be strongest: a plausible per-capita task-supply regime makes the continuous question coherent, and no fundamental obstacle rules it out.
