The continuous mirror question

Input: \(m\ge2\) groups, each with two nominees, and a rational-mass distribution \(\mu\) over member types. Each type specifies a group affiliation and a complete utility vector over all nominees; define each group’s aggregate utility for nominee \(x\) by summing \(\mu_t v_t(x)\) over its types. Require the resulting instance to satisfy the paper’s egoism and monotonicity conditions and social-utility bound, and include a polynomial-time winning-probability procedure \(\Pi\) as in the paper. Decide whether there is a nomination profile \(s\) such that no group can increase its expected aggregate member utility by unilaterally changing nominees.

The model it lives in

A finite set of strategic groups retains its discrete nomination choices, while a society of members is represented by rational masses over complete utility types. The input includes the winning-probability procedure \(\Pi\); group payoffs are computed from type-mass aggregates. Taking one type per group with mass \(1/m\) and per-member utilities scaled by \(m\) reproduces the paper’s aggregate utility table and preserves Theorem 1’s hardness.

The objection that survived

The reduction’s hardness is encoded in the general-form winning-probability procedure, not in population composition, and the mass data disappear once group utilities are aggregated. The proponent acknowledges this limitation, so it bounds the interpretation rather than defeating the mass-profile question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s NP-completeness result for general-form winning-probability procedures. It leaves the paper’s existence examples, FPT algorithm, price-of-anarchy results, and simulation untouched; Theorem 1 does not establish hardness for a fixed rule such as softmax.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does PSNE existence remain NP-complete when the winning-probability rule is fixed to softmax on these rational-mass member-type instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead is Theorem 1, “NP-completeness in general-form representation.” Proved in this paper, it says that EGOISTIC-PSNE-EXIST-GF—deciding whether an egoistic election game has a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium—is NP-complete. The result holds even when every group has exactly two candidates, \(\beta=200\), and the game is monotone. The instance includes a polynomial-time procedure \(\Pi\) for computing winning probabilities. The hardness reduction is from SAT: groups’ nomination choices encode an assignment, and \(\Pi\) uses that assignment to determine the game’s winning probabilities.

A plausible mirror is Mass-Profile Egoistic PSNE Existence. Take \(m\ge2\) organizational groups, each with two nominees. The organization has a rational-mass population of types \((i,t)\), where \(i\) identifies a group and \(t\) describes members’ complete utility vector over all nominees. Let \(\lambda_{i,t}>0\) be each type’s share of the whole population, with \(\sum_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}=1\), and let \(v_{i,t}(x)\) be that type’s utility for nominee \(x\). The aggregate utility of group \(i\) for \(x\) is

\[ u_i(x)=\sum_{t\in T_i}\lambda_{i,t}v_{i,t}(x). \]

Require the resulting instance to satisfy the paper’s egoism condition and \(u(x)=\sum_i u_i(x)\in[0,200]\). Its input also includes a polynomial-time WP procedure \(\Pi\), as in the paper, that returns probabilities \(p_j(s)\) for a nomination profile \(s\) and satisfies the monotonicity condition. Each group chooses one of its two nominees. Its payoff is the expected utility of its members,

\[ R_i(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_j(s)\,u_i(x_{j,s_j}). \]

The question is whether there is a profile \(s\) such that \(R_i(s)\ge R_i(r,s_{-i})\) for every group \(i\) and either nominee \(r\). A solution is such a profile: no group can improve its expected member utility by changing its nomination alone.

This has a credible high-multiplicity setting: a large organization with many members in each faculty or faction, where members fall into a relatively small number of shared evaluation types. For the hardness embedding, use one type per group, give each group mass \(1/m\), and set its members’ utilities to \(m\) times the paper’s utility values. The resulting group aggregates are exactly the original \(u_i(x)\), so the nominations, payoffs, and equilibrium predicate are unchanged. Thus the mirror can have arbitrarily many members and only \(m\) types while retaining the theorem’s hardness.

I expect hardness to transfer, placing this stated problem in Class B, rather than to become tractable through continuization. The strategic choices remain discrete nominations, and the SAT assignment in the reduction remains encoded in those choices. This is also a question the authors should recognize: their model already defines group payoffs by aggregating member utilities, and their organizational examples explicitly involve large factions. The mirror covers Theorem 1 only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s other equilibrium, FPT, or price-of-anarchy results.

The main follow-up is whether the hardness survives when \(\Pi\) is fixed to a familiar rule such as softmax. The paper explicitly leaves the fixed-softmax complexity question open, and Theorem 1 does not settle it. A corresponding question for other fixed WP rules would separate hardness from the flexibility of putting \(\Pi\) in the input.

The soft spot is that population mass is input data, not a decision variable: after aggregation, the game uses the same utility table as the paper. The reduction’s hardness also relies on a formula-dependent general-form WP procedure, not on a fixed natural rule. So this is a faithful but computationally thin high-multiplicity mirror. I would not claim that it unlocks continuous-optimization methods or shows that population continuity itself creates hardness; its defensible contribution is that the paper’s hardness persists in a plausible large-population interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 1, and the strongest case against it is about what the theorem’s hardness depends on. The proposed member-type model is formally legitimate: a distribution of members’ utility types maps to the paper’s aggregate utilities, and one type per group with mass \(1/m\) and per-member utilities scaled by \(m\) reproduces the original instance. But that mapping also shows how little the population representation does here: once the aggregate utility table is computed, the type masses disappear from the game.

The reduction makes that separation especially clear. Its formula is evaluated inside the input winning-probability procedure \(\Pi\). The variable groups’ candidates have the same social utility, and those groups receive zero winning probability at every profile. Their choices serve as bits for \(\Pi\); the members’ utility distribution does not encode or affect the hard part. So Theorem 1 establishes hardness for a succinct game evaluator, not for a computation that uses population masses. That weakens it as evidence for a population-continuous complexity landscape.

The strongest repair would fix the winning rule to softmax and ask whether PSNE existence remains hard when aggregate utilities come from a rational-mass distribution of member types. The paper itself identifies fixed-softmax complexity as open, so this is a real question. But the mass formulation alone does not sharpen it: the authors could ask the same question directly over their aggregate utility tables. Making the masses themselves central—for example, asking how equilibrium existence changes across a region of population compositions—would create a more population-sensitive problem, but that would be a new parametric question rather than a mirror of Theorem 1. Continuizing the strategic groups instead would change the equilibrium concept: an individual group of measure zero cannot have the paper’s unilateral-deviation effect.

That is a substantial objection to the *strength* of this mirror, but it does not defeat it. Large factions with many members of a few utility types are plausible, the authors would likely recognize the setup, and the user’s standard explicitly allows a continuous problem whose answer is that hardness persists. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile mirror survives: the proposed one is a credible high-multiplicity embedding, though this theorem does little to show that population continuity itself contributes to the computational question.
