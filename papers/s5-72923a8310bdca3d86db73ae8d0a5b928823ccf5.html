The continuous mirror question

Given a connected type graph \(H=(T,F)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and independent aggregate observations \(Z_t\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2/\mu_t)\), compute a type’s exact posterior action at round \(k\) under the paper’s common uniform prior, returning coefficients \(a_{tu}^{(k)}\) such that \(X_t(k)=\sum_{u\in T}a_{tu}^{(k)}Z_u\).

The model it lives in

A population is partitioned into information cells of masses \(\mu_t\); each cell pools its members’ Gaussian measurements and exchanges posterior estimates with adjacent cells in \(H\). The decision variable is the coefficient vector for each cell’s estimate at the requested round, computed using the correlated reports and mass-dependent observation precisions.

The objection that survived

Each mass represents a communicating cohort that has already pooled its members’ signals, so the model is not derived from the paper’s fixed-variance individual-agent process.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a mirror on. The strongest positive case is its algorithmic result, Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper: “There exists an efficient algorithm to calculate the agents’ actions.” Here an action is an agent’s Bayesian estimate \(X_v(t)\). The proof represents each estimate as a linear combination of private signals and computes the coefficients by inverting covariance matrices of size at most \(N\). It gives polynomially many arithmetic operations in \(N\), linear in the round \(t\), under the paper’s real-number computation model. That is a meaningful computational anchor, though not a claim about bit complexity.

My lead mirror is Mass-Weighted Gaussian Social Learning. Imagine a large organization of local information cells: members of each cell pool noisy measurements, then the cells exchange posterior estimates over a connected contact network. The types are the cells’ information and contact roles; members of one type share the same prior, signal technology and reporting role. The mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\) is its fraction of the population. For a fixed number \(\tau\) of types, the number of people can grow while the number of distinct types stays small.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance consists of a connected undirected graph \(H=(T,F)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a known noise scale \(\sigma^2\), and, for each type, an aggregate observation \(Z_t\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2/\mu_t)\), independent across types conditional on \(\theta\). All types have the paper’s common uniform prior. Type \(t\)’s action at round zero is \(X_t(0)=Z_t\); at each later round it reports
\[ X_t(k+1)=\mathbb E\!\left[\theta\mid Z_t,\{X_u(s):(u,t)\in F,\ s\le k\}\right]. \]
The underlying problem is to compute type \(t\)’s exact posterior action at a specified round \(k\): equivalently, return coefficients \(a_{tu}^{(k)}\) such that \(X_t(k)=\sum_{u\in T}a_{tu}^{(k)}Z_u\). This is the estimate minimizing posterior expected squared error. A solver can be asked for the coefficient expression in the same exact-arithmetic model used by the paper.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A): the reports remain linear Gaussian estimators, so the computation reduces to covariance-matrix operations over \(\tau\) types. Mass affects the signal precision, while repeated reports remain correlated and must still be handled as in the paper. The result covers Theorem 2.2’s efficient-action-computation question; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s privacy results or its graph-specific convergence theorems. It also raises questions about coefficient bit complexity, how convergence depends on the masses and the type graph, and what privacy means between cohorts.

The weak point is substantive: the paper gives each individual an independent signal and lets individuals communicate, whereas this mirror pools signals within each cohort before inter-cohort communication. The variance \(\sigma^2/\mu_t\) is a finite-information continuum scaling; without such scaling, pooling an ever-growing number of fixed-quality signals can make the limit degenerate. A referee might therefore accept this as a natural continuous-population extension of the paper’s Bayesian computation problem, but not as the exact limit of its original individual-level model. The positive case rests on the fact that the central computation—Bayesian updating from overlapping reports on a network—survives the change.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The no-anchor objection does not work: Theorem 2.2 is a named computational result. The strongest negative case is about what it computes. Its output is each named vertex’s Bayesian action, given that vertex’s signal history on a specified graph. The graph records who hears whose reports and which information overlaps; type masses alone do not encode that structure. For a generic graph, preserving all those roles requires as many types as vertices. Grouping vertices by a coarse network role can merge agents with different neighborhoods and therefore different information histories.

The proposed cohort model makes the most plausible repair, but it changes the agents and their information. A cohort’s members pool their measurements into one observation, then one type-level agent communicates on \(H\). In the paper, each person has a private signal and updates from reports by individual neighbors. The variance \(\sigma^2/\mu_t\) also imposes a particular finite-information scaling: with \(n\) people and ordinary independent signals of variance \(\sigma^2\), a cohort of mass \(\mu_t\) has sample-mean variance \(\sigma^2/(n\mu_t)\). The proposed variance can define a coherent white-noise aggregate model, but it does not follow from the paper’s fixed-noise population by taking its size to infinity. Its computational task is Bayesian updating for \(\tau\) cohort agents, with masses setting signal precisions.

The strongest version that keeps the original individuals would use a large family of graphs built from repeated network roles. It must still specify which individuals are connected: a type-to-type mixing table will generally lose the overlaps among reports that determine the covariance matrices. Supplying the individual edges preserves the source problem but can make the representation grow with the population. Replacing those edges with a graphon or another aggregate interaction kernel yields a potentially interesting continuum learning model; it also changes what agents observe and what the algorithm must compute. That is a new social-learning problem, not a faithful population mirror of Theorem 2.2. The paper estimates a shared real-valued state; it has no collective choice rule or outcome for ChoCo to analyze.

So I would reject the proposed mirror as a strong ChoCo continuation: its clean formulation pools agents before learning, while preserving the original computation retains vertex-level relational data. The universal negative is less secure. A graph-limit extension could be worthwhile to researchers in Bayesian social learning, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as a follow-up. The case against is strongest if “worthwhile mirror” means a computational social-choice mirror of this paper’s named result; it cannot honestly rule out every valuable continuous extension of the learning model.
