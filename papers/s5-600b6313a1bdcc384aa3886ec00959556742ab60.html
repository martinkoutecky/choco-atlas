The continuous mirror question

For each integer \(N\), let an instance be a distribution \(\mu\) over location types in \([0,1]\), with masses in multiples of \(1/N\) and support size at most \(N\). A randomized rule \(F_N\) selects a facility location; truthfulness requires that an agent at \(x\) cannot improve expected distance by transferring mass \(1/N\) from \(x\) to any reported location \(x'\). Determine the best worst-case approximation ratio \(\alpha_N^*\) for expected minimum utility, where \(M_\mu(y)=\min_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}|x-y|\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}_\mu=\max_{y\in[0,1]}M_\mu(y)\); in particular, can the \(O(\sqrt N)\) guarantee be improved to a constant, or must the ratio grow?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity population of \(N\) agents is encoded by location masses \(\mu_x\), with each occupied location a type. A rule maps \(\mu\) to a facility lottery, and an individual deviation transfers \(1/N\) mass. The objective is the expected distance from the facility to the nearest occupied type; the question is the optimal worst-case strategyproof approximation ratio as a function of \(N\) and possibly the support size \(\tau\).

The objection that survived

Truthfulness remains \(1/N\)-granular, so the mirror does not describe a nonatomic strategic limit; the proponent acknowledges this, and it bounds the interpretation to high-multiplicity populations.

  • Does the best guarantee depend on \(N\), the support size \(\tau\), or both?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is the minimum-utility strand, with Theorem 4 as the lead. The paper has no named P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result; its anchors are approximation guarantees and impossibility results. So this is a mechanism-design mirror, and its negative result is an approximation lower bound, not a complexity classification.

Call the mirror Continuous-Mass Minimum-Utility Facility Design. Its public parameter is the population size \(N\). An instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over rational locations in \([0,1]\), with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), and support size \(\tau\le N\). A type is a location \(x\): agents at the same location have identical utilities. A randomized rule \(F_N\) maps \(\mu\) to a lottery over facility locations. For \(Y\sim F_N(\mu)\), define \(M_\mu(y)=\min_{x:\mu_x>0}|x-y|\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\max_{y\in[0,1]}M_\mu(y)\). Its approximation ratio is measured using \(\mathbb E[M_\mu(Y)]\).

To preserve the paper’s individual incentive constraint, one agent’s misreport moves mass \(1/N\): from type \(x\) to \(x'\), producing \(\mu'=\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_{x'}\). Require \(\mathbb E_{Y\sim F_N(\mu)}|x-Y|\ge\mathbb E_{Y\sim F_N(\mu')}|x-Y|\) whenever that transfer is available. The question is to determine the best worst-case ratio \(\alpha_N^*\) over such rules, and give a rule or an impossibility certificate. In the full definition, \(F_N\) is defined on finite-support distributions so that deviations are allowed even when they temporarily increase support.

Theorem 4, proved in this paper, says that for every \(\rho<2\), some \(n_0\) exists such that for every \(n\ge n_0\), no randomized strategyproof mechanism for \(n\) agents is a \(\rho\)-approximation for minimum utility; consequently, the asymptotic approximation ratio is at least \(2\). This lower bound transfers directly to the mirror: restricting any \(F_N\) to empirical distributions \(\mu=\frac1N\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\) gives a finite-agent mechanism. A unilateral report is exactly a \(1/N\) mass transfer, and both minimum utility and its optimum are unchanged. Thus \(\liminf_{N\to\infty}\alpha_N^*\ge2\). On ChoCo’s trichotomy, this is closest to a Class B-style transfer from embedded discrete instances, though it is an approximation impossibility, not NP-hardness.

The high-multiplicity regime is credible here. Location is a complete type for this problem: all agents at \(x\) have the same utility function. A large campus, town, or facility corridor could have many residents but relatively few relevant occupied sites. More specifically, in Theorem 4’s construction, for fixed \(\rho<2\), the number of grid locations stays fixed as \(n\) grows; the population grows through repeated agents at locations already present. The theorem’s hard instances therefore have \(N\) large relative to \(\tau\).

Theorem 3, also proved here, supplies the constructive side of the same mirror: for every \(n\ge2\), the Endpoint-Augmented Uniform Mechanism with endpoint probability \(q_n=(1-1/\sqrt2)/\sqrt n\) is universally strategyproof and achieves a minimum-utility ratio at most \(\sqrt n+O(1)\). Its continuous-mass counterpart uses the same fixed lottery, so it is incentive compatible under mass transfers. The paper’s gap calculation extends to fractional masses because minimum utility depends on the occupied support, not on the weights; for \(\tau\le N\), it gives an \(O(\sqrt N)\) upper bound. Thus the mirror asks a real open question about the gap between a transferred asymptotic lower bound of \(2\) and a constructive \(O(\sqrt N)\) guarantee. Can the upper bound be made constant, or can the lower bound grow? Does \(\tau\), rather than \(N\), control the right guarantee?

I would scope the case to these two minimum-utility results. I am not treating the paper’s randomized facility outcome as the continuization: the society itself is represented by \(\mu\), with fractional type masses. The proposed rule is also easy to sample, but the paper’s results do not classify its problem by runtime complexity.

The weakest point is that minimum utility depends only on which types have positive mass; a tiny type affects welfare just as much as a large one. That makes this a less mass-sensitive mirror than a social-utility model. The strategic condition also retains a one-person mass unit \(1/N\), rather than taking a fully nonatomic limit. Still, these choices preserve the paper’s objective and individual incentive question, while the input to the mirror is the aggregate distribution that changes as the population grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s theorems bound the quality of strategyproof mechanisms; they do not classify the computational complexity of a social choice problem. Theorem 4 gives an approximation impossibility for truthful rules, and Theorems 1 and 3 give approximation guarantees for particular rules. One can ask for the best ratio \(\alpha_N^*\) in a mass-based model, but that creates a new mechanism-design question rather than mirroring a computational result of the kind ChoCo is set up to chart.

The incentive constraint makes a genuine continuum version difficult. In a nonatomic population, one person has zero mass, so a unilateral report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Strategyproofness then holds vacuously for every rule. The proponent’s repair—retain \(N\) and let a deviation move mass \(1/N\)—preserves the paper’s question, but the resulting object is an \(N\)-person game written as a histogram. That is a sensible high-multiplicity representation, and the lower bound transfers to it; the transfer alone does not turn the mechanism-quality question into a continuous computational one. Replacing individual deviations with deviations by a positive-mass group would make incentives nontrivial, but would ask a different question from the paper.

Theorem 4 also exposes a problem specific to minimum utility. Its objective depends only on which locations have positive mass, not on how much mass each has. In the lower-bound construction, for fixed \(\rho<2\), the grid size stays fixed as \(n\) grows, while some grid locations have only one agent and hence mass \(1/n\). Those locations still matter as much as the dominant type: removing even a tiny positive mass can change the support and the optimum. In a limiting distribution, such types disappear unless the model preserves vanishing masses as separately consequential support points. That is a valid support-sensitive model, but its central object is the occupied set, not aggregate population shares.

Theorem 3 does not escape these issues. Its guarantee is indexed by \(n\), and its endpoint probability \(q_n\) tends to zero as \(n\) grows. For a fixed distribution \(\mu\), \(N\) is not a population parameter of the distribution; adding it preserves a finite-population incentive game. Replacing \(N\) with support size \(\tau\) could be worth investigating, but it would require a new incentive and approximation analysis. Theorem 1’s social-utility kernel is a more promising mass-based extension because social utility genuinely weights types by their masses. Yet its strategyproofness again becomes vacuous for nonatomic individuals, while Theorem 2’s lower bound is built from two agents.

So the strongest negative case is about fit: the paper offers approximation theory for truthful mechanisms, not a computational-complexity landscape, and its main incentive results resist a nontrivial nonatomic interpretation. This is not a proof that the histogram version is uninteresting. If ChoCo expands to high-multiplicity mechanism design or studies support-sensitive objectives, it could be a worthwhile neighboring project; the case against it is weaker under that broader remit.
