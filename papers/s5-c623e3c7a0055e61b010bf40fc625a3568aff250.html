The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s budget-additive equilibrium-preservation result.

The anchor is Theorem 2.3, proved in this paper. Its budget-additive case says: if there is an \(\alpha\)-approximation algorithm for welfare-maximizing combinatorial auctions with budget-additive bidders, run it and then use only polynomially many additional value queries to find a no-overbidding pure Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous second-price auction with the same welfare approximation guarantee. The theorem covers an arbitrary number of budget-additive bidders. That makes it a better anchor for a many-agent mirror than Theorem 3.1’s exponential-communication lower bound: Theorem 3.1 is proved for only two bidders, so it offers little evidence about what changes when the population is large.

A plausible regime is a large procurement market with many purchasing units and standardized item categories. Each unit belongs to one of a small number of profiles: units in a profile share the same values and budget. For instance, there might be millions of units but only dozens of distinct profiles. The units still receive integral bundles; their type-level averages can be fractional.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Budget-Additive Replica Equilibrium Preservation (BA-REP). The input is a set \(M\) of \(m\) item categories; \(\tau\) bidder types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); and budget-additive valuations
\[ v_t(S)=\min\left\{B_t,\sum_{j\in S}a_{tj}\right\}. \]
It also includes a rational per-capita supply \(q_j\) for each item and a feasible initial allocation \(x_{t,S}\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\):
\[ \sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j. \]
Write \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

Each agent bids separately on each item. The market sells \(q_j\) units of item \(j\) per unit of population to the highest bids, splitting ties at the cutoff; winners pay the highest losing bid. Thus in a population of \(N\) agents, supply scales to \(Nq_j\) copies. A strategy profile is a distribution of mass within each type over bid vectors. Each individual receives an integral bundle, with ties resolved independently across items. A solution is a finite description of a no-overbidding Nash equilibrium: every bid vector in use satisfies \(\sum_{j\in S}b_j\le v_t(S)\) for every bundle \(S\), and no infinitesimal agent can improve its expected utility by changing its bids while the aggregate bid distributions stay fixed. The equilibrium’s expected welfare must be at least \(W(x)\).

The computational question is whether this guarantee can be achieved with complexity polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and the support size \(r\) of \(x\), rather than in the number \(N\) of agents. If \(x\) is an \(\alpha\)-approximate welfare allocation, the resulting equilibrium preserves that guarantee. I expect this to be tractable: budget-additive structure and identical types seem to make a mass-aggregated version of the paper’s equilibrium construction plausible. That is a forecast for the mirror, not a consequence established by Theorem 2.3.

This mirror covers the theorem’s budget-additive case; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s subadditive communication lower bound or its XOS-oracle result. It also raises useful follow-up questions: can a mass-based construction preserve the welfare of every feasible starting allocation, and can its running time depend on \(\tau\) rather than \(N\)? How far does the guarantee extend beyond budget-additive valuations, and can exact equilibria always be represented with finite rational support?

My weakest point is the auction limit itself. Scaling item supply and making each bidder infinitesimal changes strategic incentives: an individual no longer moves market prices, and ties divide mass. The finite theorem’s iterative procedure moves whole bidders and has a bound polynomial in the bidder count; neither fact proves the proposed dependence on type support. The mirror is credible because it keeps their underlying task—computing a welfare-preserving no-overbidding equilibrium in simultaneous second-price item auctions—while making repeated bidder types and replicated supply explicit. The authors would still need to accept that replica-market equilibrium as the continuous form of their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2.3’s budget-additive case, and it is a real computational result. Nor can I dismiss its setting as incapable of high multiplicity: identical purchasing units with the same budget and item values are a coherent type. The objection has to be about whether a population limit preserves the problem the theorem studies.

There is a fork. Keep the paper’s \(m\) indivisible items fixed while replicating bidders, and the fraction of bidders who receive anything is at most \(m/n\), which tends to zero. The prices still depend on the highest bids, potentially from bidders occupying zero mass. A distribution over types does not preserve the finite auction’s relevant order statistics.

The proposed repair is to scale each item’s supply with population size. That keeps welfare per capita nonzero, but it also makes each bidder infinitesimal. A unilateral deviation no longer changes the market’s price cutoffs; the equilibrium becomes a price-taking, market-clearing problem. The theorem’s task is to compute a pure Nash equilibrium in a finite-player simultaneous second-price auction. A polynomial algorithm for the repaired model would answer a worthwhile question about a nonatomic auction, but it would not establish a continuous-population version of that finite-player result. Keeping bidders strategically consequential requires retaining atoms, so the population is no longer wholly continuous.

This makes BA-REP a plausible new research direction, but the bridge to Theorem 2.3 is precisely what is missing: a limit that preserves both a nontrivial population and the strategic role of individual bidders. That is the strongest case against this anchor.

It is not airtight. The programme permits per-capita supply as a necessary co-modification, and the authors might recognize the nonatomic auction as their problem. If they do, the anchor survives; the paper and the supplied case do not establish that it would be rejected.
