Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 6.8 — survived: The theorem is a computational approximation result; its continuous analogue is a new mass-market question, not a bound transferred from the finite auction.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite reportable type catalogue \(\mathcal T\) of size \(K\), with each type \(\theta\) specified by a rational budget \(B_\theta\) and a rational piecewise-linear, nondecreasing subadditive value curve \(v_\theta:[0,1]\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) with support size \(\tau\), let per-capita supply be a fixed \(\rho\in(0,1)\). Does there exist a mechanism, computable in time polynomial in \(K,\tau,L\), that maps each reported distribution to an allocation-and-payment menu \((q(\mu,r),p(\mu,r))\) for every report \(r\in\mathcal T\), is feasible with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q(\mu,\theta)\le\rho\), individually rational and budget-feasible for truthful reports, and dominant-strategy truthful at fixed \(\mu\) for an infinitesimal agent? Can it guarantee \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\min\{v_\theta(q(\mu,\theta)),B_\theta\}\ge \bar W^*(\mu,\rho)/O(\log^2(2+\tau))\), where \(\bar W^*(\mu,\rho)=\max_{\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta\le\rho}\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\min\{v_\theta(q_\theta),B_\theta\}\)?

The model it lives in

An atomless auction market with a finite catalogue of complete budget-and-value types, population masses \(\mu\), and scarce per-capita inventory \(\rho\). The mechanism assigns each report a per-agent allocation and payment; truthfulness means each type prefers its truthful menu option at the fixed population distribution, with budget-violating deviations having utility \(-\infty\).

The objection that survived

As written, \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta q_\theta\le 1\) permits \(q_\theta=1\) for every type, making the benchmark non-scarce; the mirror needs a fixed scarce per-capita capacity \(\rho<1\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.8, with Lemma 6.3 as truthfulness support. It leaves the paper's other approximation guarantees, the \(4/3\) lower bound, and the special-case auctions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a type-level truthful menu achieve an \(O(\log^2(2+\tau))\) approximation, or does truthfulness require dependence on the full catalogue size \(K\)?
  • Can the paper's price-ladder idea be adapted when one person's report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged?
  • What approximation guarantees are possible for subadditive value curves compared with concave curves?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 6.8, proved in this paper. It says that in a one-good divisible auction with \(n\) bidders, nondecreasing subadditive value functions, and private budgets, the paper’s Estimate-and-Price auction achieves an \(O(\log^2 n)\) approximation to optimal liquid welfare. The paper’s Lemma 6.3 proves that this auction is incentive compatible. These are approximation and mechanism-design results, rather than a discrete complexity classification; the paper offers no comparable NP-hardness result to anchor instead.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large ad exchange with many small advertisers and standardized campaign templates. A type is the complete pair consisting of an advertiser’s budget and its value curve for impressions. Advertisers using the same template and budget tier have the same type. There might be millions of accounts but only hundreds of such types. Mass is the fraction of accounts of each type. This is a regime claim, not a claim about every auction: named advertisers with distinct budgets and value curves would not be well represented by a small support.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Liquid-Welfare Mechanism Approximation. The input is a finite list of \(\tau\) types \(\theta\), positive rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), rational budgets \(B_\theta\), and nondecreasing subadditive value curves \(v_\theta:[0,1]\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), given by rational piecewise-linear descriptions. Each type is complete: its members share the same curve and budget. Normalize inventory to one unit per unit mass, so a type’s per-agent allocation \(q_\theta\) must satisfy \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta q_\theta\le1\). This is the per-capita supply co-modification needed to keep individual allocations meaningful as the population grows.

The liquid-welfare benchmark is

\[ \bar W^*(\mu)= \max_{\substack{q_\theta\ge0\\\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta\le1}} \sum_\theta\mu_\theta\min\{v_\theta(q_\theta),B_\theta\}. \]

A solution is a polynomial-time anonymous direct mechanism that, for each reported type, specifies a per-agent allocation \(q_\theta\) and payment \(p_\theta\). It must satisfy aggregate feasibility, individual rationality, and budget feasibility for truthful reports. It must also be dominant-strategy truthful: at a fixed population distribution, an individual of type \(\theta\) prefers the contract for \(\theta\) to the contract for any other report. In an atomless population one person’s deviation leaves the distribution unchanged, but the contract they receive can still depend on their report. The question is whether such a mechanism can guarantee

\[ \sum_\theta\mu_\theta\min\{v_\theta(q_\theta),B_\theta\} \ \ge\ \frac{\bar W^*(\mu)}{O(\log^2(2+\tau))} \]

on every input, with running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the total binary encoding length \(L\). The corresponding output for a negative answer would be a lower bound ruling out that guarantee.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, provisionally: the population is summarized by a short type list, and the paper’s price-ladder idea suggests a route to a type-level algorithm. The central follow-up is whether dependence on the number of bidders can really be replaced by dependence on \(\tau\); other questions include the best approximation factor in \(\tau\) and \(L\), and whether subadditive curves require a worse factor than concave ones.

The authors should recognize the underlying problem: budget-constrained buyers, divisible inventory, liquid welfare, and truthful approximation are the paper’s own subject. The model makes the population continuous while retaining those questions; it does not treat the paper’s divisible good as evidence that its population is already continuous. The mirror covers Theorem 6.8 and its truthfulness support in Lemma 6.3, not the paper’s other auction results.

The weak point is real: Estimate-and-Price singles out pivot bidders and processes an ordered list of people. Those steps do not automatically define a truthful, anonymous mechanism for type masses. Moreover, continuum truthfulness leaves an individual with zero influence on aggregate reports, which changes the mechanism-design problem. So Theorem 6.8 motivates this precise new question; it does not establish the proposed \(O(\log^2\tau)\) bound. The case for the mirror rests on the authors recognizing the mass-based mechanism problem as theirs despite that gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sharpest problem is a scale mismatch in the proposed mirror. With \(q_\theta\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta\le1\), assigning \(q_\theta=1\) to every type is feasible. Since values are nondecreasing, that maximizes liquid welfare; assigning it for free is truthful and budget-feasible. So the stated \(O(\log^2\tau)\) guarantee is trivial and does not capture Theorem 6.8’s scarce-good auction.

Making the mirror nontrivial requires scarce inventory per person, say \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta\le\rho\) for \(0<\rho<1\). But with \(n\) bidders and the paper’s fixed supply of one unit, the corresponding constraint is \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta\le1/n\), not \(\rho\). Keeping \(\rho\) fixed scales inventory with the population and asks about a different market-size regime. Keeping inventory fixed requires carrying \(n\), or per-capita supply \(1/n\), alongside the distribution. For fixed positive masses and piecewise-linear value curves, allocations then shrink toward zero as \(n\) grows; eventually fixed positive budgets cease to bind. The limiting question loses much of the private-budget phenomenon that motivates the theorem.

There is also a truthfulness gap. Lemma 6.3 proves truthfulness for a finite auction whose pivot selection and prices depend on individual reports. In an atomless population, one person’s report does not change \(\mu\), so truthfulness becomes a menu condition at fixed \(\mu\). That is a coherent mechanism-design question, but it is not established by the paper’s finite-agent result. And an algorithm that specifies contracts only for types in the observed support still needs to define what happens when an individual reports a type outside that support.

These objections defeat the proposed formulation and the claim that Theorem 6.8 already supports an \(O(\log^2\tau)\) result. They do not defeat the strongest repair: a scarce per-capita inventory, a finite catalogue of complete types, and an anonymous truthful mechanism over their masses. A large market with many advertisers in standardized types could make that a sensible question the authors would recognize. So the negative case is weak at its boundary: I can argue that this is not a clean continuous mirror of the paper’s fixed-supply theorem, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every plausible regime.
