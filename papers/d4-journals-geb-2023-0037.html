The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For a finite set of agents with convex, continuous, nondecreasing-in-own-signal values, it says that an allocation rule is ex-post implementable if and only if, for every agent \(i\) and fixed \(s_{-i}\), \(q_i(s_i,s_{-i})\ge q_i(s'_i,s_{-i})\) whenever \(s_i>s'_i\) and \(s_i>\ell_{v_i}(s_{-i})\). Here \(\ell_{v_i}(s_{-i})\) is the upper end of the interval on which \(i\)’s value is still at its lowest-signal value. The paper proves this characterization; it does not cite it from elsewhere.

There is an important qualification: Theorem 1 is an implementability characterization, not a stated complexity result. The paper has no named theorem that classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, or similar. Its revenue results, including Proposition 3, give optimal allocation rules under stated assumptions, but do not analyze computational complexity. So this is the best near-miss anchor for a positive case, not a strict fit to the requested complexity-anchor criterion.

The mirror I find plausible is Continuum EPIC Density Implementation. Consider a large auction for one indivisible object, with many interchangeable bidders and a fixed, small set of signal levels. A type is a bidder’s signal together with any shared value-relevant attributes; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of bidders of type \(t\). Think of a large pool of suppliers with standardized quality-signal tiers, where a bidder’s value depends on both their own signal and the society’s signal distribution. For instance, \(v(s;\mu)=\max\{s,\sum_t\mu_t t\}\) is convex and nondecreasing in \(s\), with a plateau below the population mean.

An instance gives a rational, finite-support distribution \(\mu\), a piecewise-linear convex value function \(v(\cdot;\mu)\), and a piecewise-linear allocation intensity \(q\). The intensity is scaled so that, in a finite auction with \(n\) bidders, a bidder reporting \(s\) receives allocation probability \(q(s)/n\); feasibility is \(\sum_t\mu_t q(t)=1\). The question is whether there is a transfer function \(p\), likewise scaled by \(n\), such that for every true signal \(s\) and report \(s'\),
\[ q(s)v(s;\mu)-p(s)\ \ge\ q(s')v(s;\mu)-p(s')+[v(s;\mu)-v(s';\mu)]q(s'). \]
A solution is a yes/no answer and, when yes, a transfer function satisfying these constraints.

The proposed continuum characterization is eventual monotonicity: \(q(s)\ge q(s')\) whenever \(s>s'\) and \(s>\ell_{v(\cdot;\mu)}\), where \(\ell_{v(\cdot;\mu)}\) is the end of the value plateau. In the example above, that threshold is the population mean. Under the piecewise-linear input representation, checking the condition and constructing the payment integral are tractable. The expected direction is therefore tractable, though proving the finite-auction limit and its incentive constraints is part of the mirror, not a result already established by the paper.

This is a population mirror: the mass distribution describes how many bidders share each signal type. It does not treat the paper’s continuous signal intervals as though they were a continuous population, or rely on the fact that allocations are probabilities. It covers Theorem 1’s ex-post implementability question; I would not stretch it to claim a mirror of the paper’s revenue-maximization results.

My weakest point is also the main caveat: the paper studies finitely many individually indexed agents, and its allocation probabilities lie in \([0,1]\). The mirror uses a rescaled winner density and a limit in which an individual has vanishing mass. That is a defensible high-multiplicity extension of the authors’ implementability problem, but it is not their stated model—and, strictly applying the requested anchor rule, this paper supplies no named computational-complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theonem 1 is the strongest anchor, and it misses the programme’s computational-result criterion: it characterizes ex-post implementability, but the paper gives no encoded input model or complexity bound. That does not by itself make a mirror uninteresting. The proposed mirror tries to supply the missing computational question, though its displayed incentive constraint is misstated: at fixed opponents’ signals, one correct form is
\[ Q(s)v(s;\nu)-P(s)\ge Q(s')v(s;\nu)-P(s'). \]
The proposed right-hand side adds a value-difference term to this expression, double-counting it. Also, a rule depending only on \(s\) does not represent the paper’s general allocation rules, which may depend on the entire opponents’ profile.

The strongest repair is plausible: take many interchangeable bidders with a fixed finite set of signal levels, let values depend on the opponents’ signal distribution \(\nu\), and represent anonymous allocation and transfers by per-capita densities. That makes the high-multiplicity regime sensible. It also exposes the cost of the repair. A literal continuum agent has zero mass, so one report cannot change \(\nu\), and the agent’s allocation and payment are zero. Scaling both by population size retains first-order incentive constraints, but then the proposed object is a normalized limit of finite auctions. Exact ex-post constraints at finite \(n\) still range over every opponents’ signal profile. A single society distribution \(\mu\) cannot stand in for those profiles; an exact mirror needs rules and constraints over the whole space of opponent distributions.

There is a sharp warning in the paper’s max-value model. With positive mass at the highest signal, every top-signal agent has another top-signal agent among their opponents. In the continuum, their own signal never rises above the plateau threshold, and the eventual-monotonicity constraints can become vacuous. Finite profiles with a unique highest bidder can impose constraints that vanish in the limit. A different interdependence, such as the proposed max-with-mean value function, can avoid that collapse. So this is a strong objection to the natural max-value mirror, not a universal one.

For strictly increasing values, the characterization reduces to ordinary monotonicity in own signal. For plateau values, it remains eventual monotonicity at each opponent distribution. With piecewise-linear inputs, checking those conditions and integrating payments may be easy. Ease is not a valid objection by itself; the concern is that after making the model precise, the population distribution may be only a parameter to a pointwise characterization, while the interesting computational work depends on the added representation of allocation rules over opponent distributions. The paper does not supply that input model, and the proposed mirror does not define it.

I would therefore argue against treating this as a strong anchor: the paper has no named computational result to mirror, and the proposed formulation needs substantial repair before its population-level decision problem is clear. The case is weak against the universal claim, though. A well-specified high-multiplicity model with mean-field interdependent values could still be a worthwhile mirror, and I cannot rule out that version honestly.
