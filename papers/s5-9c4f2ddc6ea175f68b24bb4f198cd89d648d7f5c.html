The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is an adjacent model, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its central named result, Theorem 5.3 (proved here), says that an anonymous binary social choice function on the countably infinite weak-preference domain is coalitionally strategy-proof exactly when it is represented by a unique upper set of the cardinality-signature grid \(G\). That is a structural characterization, not a result about complexity or algorithms. The other main results likewise classify or parameterize rules and grid regions. Under your anchor requirement, there are therefore no anchors—and no honest way to give one continuous problem per anchor.

A plausible continuous extension would model a large referendum population with three complete preference types: strict support for \(a\), strict support for \(b\), and indifference. A society is a mass vector \(\mu=(\mu_a,\mu_b,\mu_i)\) with nonnegative rational coordinates summing to one. A rule maps each such vector to \(a\) or \(b\); a coalition is a positive-mass group of voters who all strictly prefer the alternative they seek, and it may misreport its members’ preferences. One could ask for a characterization of the anonymous rules for which no such coalition can change the outcome to its preferred alternative. Large referenda with many voters concentrated in three preference types make that population regime plausible, and the authors could recognize it as a continuous-population extension of their subject.

That extension does not preserve Theorem 5.3’s central structure automatically. The paper distinguishes finite, infinite non-cofinite, and cofinite support coalitions, and its classification depends on those distinctions and on deviations by infinite coalitions. A mass vector records proportions instead. Moreover, the paper defines no computational input problem, objective, or algorithmic result for this proposed extension. So it is a credible *new characterization question*, but not an anchor-backed computational mirror of the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor. The paper’s named results, especially Theorem 5.3 and Corollary 6.12, characterize rules and describe their geometry; none gives a complexity result, algorithm, or computational input problem to mirror. That is the strongest case against a ChoCo mirror here.

The proposed three-type model is a plausible continuous-population extension, but its question is still an axiomatic characterization of coalition strategy-proof rules. The paper allows arbitrary rules, represented by arbitrary upper sets. To ask an algorithmic question about those rules, one would first need to choose a finite encoding or restrict the class—for example, to rules with a finite list of corners. That may produce a worthwhile new problem, but the encoding and computational task would be additions, not continuizations of results in this paper.

There is also a deeper gap between the proposed mass model and the paper’s population. Full anonymity on \(\mathbb N\) preserves whether a coalition is finite, infinite with infinite complement, or cofinite. Those distinctions drive the grid and the constraints from infinite-coalition deviations. A normalized, atomless mass model cannot preserve them: finite sets have mass zero, and infinite sets can have different densities or no density at all. In fact, there is no countably additive permutation-invariant probability on \(\mathbb N\): equal mass for every singleton would have to be either zero, giving total mass zero, or positive, giving infinite total mass. So the three-type simplex is a different strategic model, not a faithful high-multiplicity encoding of this one.

That does not make the proposed model incoherent. It makes the negative case strong for the specific claim that this paper offers a worthwhile *computational* mirror. The broader claim that no scenario could yield one is harder to defend: someone could add costs, an explicit rule encoding, or another algorithmic task and study it over a continuum. But that would be a new computational project motivated by the paper, rather than a mirror of a computational result it contains.
