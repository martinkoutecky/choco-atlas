The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(L<U\), a rational bias \(b\), a finite target set \(T\subseteq\mathbb{R}\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), form, for each denominator-compatible \(N\), a game with \(N\mu_t\) players of target \(t\). Each chooses \(x_i\in[L,U]\), has weight \(1/N\) in \(\hat{x}_N=b+\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i\), and incurs loss \((\hat{x}_N-t_i)^2\). Characterize all weak limits \((\nu,h)\) of empirical target-report distributions and aggregates along pure Nash equilibria as \(N\to\infty\): \(\nu\) has target marginal \(\mu\), \(h=b+\int x\,d\nu(t,x)\), mass with \(t<h\) reports \(L\), mass with \(t>h\) reports \(U\), and if \(h\in T\), reports of type \(h\) have the mean needed to make the aggregate equal \(h\).

The model it lives in

Types are ideal targets \(t\) with population masses \(\mu_t\); reports \(x\in[L,U]\) are decisions by individual agents in finite games with weight \(1/N\) and squared loss \((\hat{x}_N-t)^2\). The continuous object is the limiting distribution \(\nu\) over target-report pairs and its aggregate \(h=b+\int x\,d\nu(t,x)\); tied-target reports are summarized by their mean.

The objection that survived

The limit object is not an ordinary nonatomic Nash equilibrium, so authors might not accept limits of atomic equilibria as their equilibrium question; this bounds the mirror’s interpretation but does not defeat the explicitly stated limit problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s one-dimensional pure-equilibrium computation. The paper gives no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result. Its strongest computational anchor is Lemma 8, “Nash Equilibria in 1D,” proved here. It states that when \(X=[L,U]\), there is a threshold \(t^\star\) such that every pure Nash equilibrium has \(x_i=L\) for players with \(t_i<t^\star\), and \(x_i=U\) for players with \(t_i>t^\star\). For players with \(t_i=t^\star\), their actions satisfy
\[ \sum_{i:t_i=t^\star} w_i x_i = t^\star-b -\sum_{i:t_i<t^\star} w_iL -\sum_{i:t_i>t^\star}w_iU, \]
where \(b=w_0x_0\). Equivalently, when there are players at the threshold, their actions make the mechanism land at \(t^\star\). In target order, equilibrium reports form a lower-endpoint prefix, a possible tied middle group, and an upper-endpoint suffix. The paper’s unnumbered Remark 9 gives a finite candidate-search procedure based on this characterization.

The mirror: High-Multiplicity Limit Equilibria for 1D Affine Aggregation. An instance consists of rational \(L<U\), a rational bias \(b\), and a finite set of ideal targets \(T\subset\mathbb R\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. Type \(t\) means an agent has ideal outcome \(t\), the same action interval \([L,U]\), and the same influence as every other agent of that type. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that type. For each \(N\) with all \(N\mu_t\) integral, instantiate \(N\) agents, each with weight \(1/N\), mechanism
\[ \hat x_N=b+\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N x_i, \]
and loss \((\hat x_N-t_i)^2\).

The question is to compute all population limits of pure Nash equilibria as \(N\to\infty\). Represent a population profile by a joint distribution \(\nu\) over target-report pairs \((t,x)\), with target marginal \(\mu\). A solution is such a \(\nu\) and its aggregate \(h=b+\int x\,d\nu(t,x)\), satisfying
\[ \nu(\,\cdot\mid t)=\delta_L\quad(t<h),\qquad \nu(\,\cdot\mid t)=\delta_U\quad(t>h). \]
If \(h\in T\), reports at that target can vary, but their mean must make the aggregate equal \(h\):
\[ h=b+L\!\sum_{t<h}\mu_t+U\!\sum_{t>h}\mu_t +\mu_h\int x\,d\nu(x\mid h). \]
If \(h\notin T\), the same equation applies without the last term. The output can be encoded by the candidate thresholds and, for a tied type, its required mean report; any distribution of that type’s reports with that mean is allowed.

This is a high-multiplicity regime with \(N\gg\tau=|T|\): for example, a large public consultation on a continuous policy level, with participants’ declared ideal levels drawn from a small grid. Each person submits a recommendation and cares about the distance between the resulting average and their ideal. Repeated target types are genuinely interchangeable, and the mass is the share of the consultation with each ideal. This covers Lemma 8’s one-dimensional complete-information equilibrium problem. It does not try to mirror the paper’s multidimensional, Bayesian, or finite-action results.

The limit characterization is tractable: test each target as a possible tie threshold, and each gap between targets using the aggregate implied by endpoint reports. There are only \(O(\tau)\) candidates, with rational arithmetic polynomial in the input bit length. The connection to Lemma 8 is direct: replicate each type in proportion to its mass and let each agent’s influence shrink to \(1/N\). The limit retains the paper’s action, loss, affine mechanism, and finite-population unilateral incentives.

The main weakness is that this is a limit of finite-player equilibria, not the ordinary Nash equilibrium of a nonatomic game. In a nonatomic game, a single agent cannot affect the average, so the paper’s exaggeration incentives disappear. Defining the mirror through limits preserves those incentives at every finite \(N\), and makes the link to Lemma 8 precise; whether the authors would accept that asymptotic solution concept is the central point a skeptical reader could contest.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no named complexity result to mirror. Lemma 8 characterizes one-dimensional pure equilibria, while the finite candidate search appears in an unnumbered remark. The paper gives no running-time bound or complexity classification for equilibrium computation. That weakens its fit with a programme whose central deliverable is a complexity landscape.

There is also a real modeling hinge in the proposed mirror. In an ordinary nonatomic society with aggregate report \(h=b+\int x\,d\nu(t,x)\), one person has zero mass. Changing that person’s report cannot change \(h\), so it cannot change their loss \((h-t)^2\). Every report is a best response, and the paper’s exaggeration incentives disappear. Giving a whole type’s mass \(\mu_t\) the power to change its report restores those incentives, but makes each type a strategic player with mass-sized influence. That is a different game, in which a cohort acts together.

The proponent’s finite-population limit is the best way around this problem. With \(N\) people and weight \(1/N\) each, finite equilibria retain individual incentives; their limits satisfy the stated threshold and aggregate conditions. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for, say, a large consultation with many participants sharing ideal targets. But its solution concept is the limit of equilibria in atomic games, not Nash equilibrium in the continuous society itself. Whether that asymptotic object is the problem the authors would want to study is the key uncertainty.

I cannot honestly use that distinction to defeat the anchor. The limit is well-defined, preserves the paper’s central phenomenon, and compresses the computation from the number of participants to the number of target types. Lemma 8 appears to make its characterization a direct extension, rather than a new complexity result, but a simple answer is not by itself a reason the question is unworthy. The Bayesian section also suggests a related population limit; its uniform example already shows the interior reporting interval shrinking as the number of players grows.

So the negative case is weak. The paper has poor direct alignment with the programme’s complexity-classification goal, and its natural nonatomic equilibrium loses the behavior the paper studies. But the proposed limit-of-finite-equilibria mirror survives those objections. I can argue that it is a narrow, asymptotic computational question; I cannot responsibly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
