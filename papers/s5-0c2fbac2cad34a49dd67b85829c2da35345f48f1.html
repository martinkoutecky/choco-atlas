The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no eligible named computational result to anchor a mirror. Its sole displayed THEOREM is unnumbered and characterizes the Invariant method by four axioms; the Appendix lemma is also unnumbered and proves an equivalence, not a complexity claim. Neither asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifiable. So I can’t honestly provide an anchor-based case of the kind requested.

The narrowest positive candidate is an unanchored mirror of computing the ranking itself. Take a finite set \(J\) of \(\tau\) journal types. Articles are the population; type \(j\) represents articles from journal \(j\) with the same normalized reference profile. Let \(\mu_j>0\), with \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), be that type’s share of articles, and let \(P_{ij}\) be the fraction of type \(j\)’s references directed to journal \(i\). Assume \(P\) is an irreducible column-stochastic matrix. The problem is to compute the normalized per-article ranking \(v\in\Delta_J\) satisfying
\[ v_i=\frac{1}{\mu_i}\sum_{j\in J}P_{ij}\mu_jv_j. \]
The unique such vector is a solution; there is no optimization objective. This is the Invariant method’s equation with article counts replaced by article masses.

A plausible regime is a large body of articles spread across a modest number of journals, with stable type-level reference profiles. That fits the paper’s use of journal-level citation aggregates and its interpretation of citations as coming from representative papers. The authors might recognize this as applying their ranking method to a continuum of articles, but the paper’s main question is axiomatic characterization, not computational ranking. The candidate problem should be tractable: it reduces to an exact stationary-distribution calculation and division by the masses, polynomial in \(\tau\) and the rational input length. Further questions include how rankings change under type refinement and how finite article samples approximate the mass model.

The weak point is decisive for the requested case: this computation is not one of the paper’s named results, and the paper gives no complexity claim for it. It is a plausible extension of the application, but it cannot serve as a paper-anchored ChoCo result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its sole theorem characterizes the Invariant method by axioms; the appendix proves that characterization. Neither states a computational problem or a complexity result. The proposed task of computing the ranking is therefore unanchored to the paper’s contribution.

The article-mass model is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime: a large corpus could have stable reference profiles, and splitting articles into finer profile types can accommodate heterogeneity. But its equation is the Invariant method with article counts replaced by masses. It still assigns values to a finite set of journals; it does not pose a mass-transfer, control, or optimization problem. Asking how to compute that ranking would add a computational question to the paper, rather than continuize one of its results. A stronger article-level model, using distributions over individual reference profiles, is also plausible, but the paper presents that as a richer data representation—not as a computational problem.

One could invent a mass-robustness question, such as how much article mass must change before the top-ranked journal changes. That might be worth studying, but it is a new problem with no result in this paper to anchor it. So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror. It is not strong enough to establish that no useful continuous-population question could arise from citation ranking: the article-mass scenario itself is credible, and the robustness extension could be interesting.
