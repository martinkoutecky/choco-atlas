The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition proves NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or a related complexity statement. Its results are axiomatic. The strongest honest positive case is therefore a continuous mirror of its main mathematical problem, with a new computational question attached—not a claim that the paper already establishes a ChoCo-style complexity result.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, on either the unrestricted domain of strict preferences \(\mathcal R\) or the restricted domain \(\underline{\mathcal R}\) where a null object is ranked last, any random assignment rule satisfying ex-post efficiency and equal-treatment-of-equals has the following equivalent properties: it satisfies probabilistic monotonicity; it satisfies strategy-proofness together with weak object-wise non-bossiness; it satisfies pairwise responsiveness; and it is the Random Priority rule \(RP\). Thus the theorem characterizes \(RP\) uniquely from fairness, efficiency, and responsiveness axioms. I do not use Proposition 1 or Corollary 1 as separate anchors: they extend or repackage the same characterization rather than supplying an independent result.

A credible high-multiplicity regime is a large school-assignment or public-housing system. There is a fixed finite set \(O\) of schools or apartments, with capacities proportional to population. A large fraction of applicants may share exactly the same ranking because they face the same coarse location, transport, or school-quality comparison. The relevant type set is \(T\subseteq\mathcal R(O)\), where a type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking. The society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of applicants of type \(t\). Here \(|T|=\tau\) is fixed or moderate while the number of applicants tends to infinity. This is precisely the high-multiplicity regime: agents of one type are interchangeable for the problem, while individual applicants still receive one indivisible object.

The per-capita capacity of object \(x\) is \(q_x\), with \(\sum_xq_x\ge1\). If some applicants may remain unassigned, include a null object \(\emptyset\) with sufficient capacity and rank it last. A feasible continuum allocation is a measurable assignment of unit-mass agents to individual objects such that object \(x\) receives mass at most \(q_x\). Its aggregate representation is a matrix \(P=(p_{t,x})\), where \(p_{t,x}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(x\):

\[ \sum_x p_{t,x}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_t p_{t,x}\le q_x. \]

The entries of \(P\) are fractional only because they aggregate many agents; each individual still receives one object. This is not continuization of the outcome space.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

Continuum Random-Priority Characterization and Computation. Given \(O\), per-capita capacities \(q\), a finite ranking-type set \(T\), and a rational type distribution \(\mu\), characterize all allocation rules on the nonatomic society satisfying continuum versions of ex-post efficiency, equal-treatment-of-equals, and probabilistic monotonicity. Determine whether the unique rule is the high-multiplicity limit of finite Random Priority, and compute its type-object allocation matrix \(P^\infty\).

The intended \(RP_\infty\) is defined canonically from the paper’s rule. For every admissible scale \(n\), create \(n\mu_t\) named agents of type \(t\) and \(nq_x\) copies of object \(x\). Run the paper’s finite \(RP\): choose a uniformly random priority order of all agents, and let agents choose their favourite remaining object. Let

\[ p^{(n)}_{t,x} \]

be the expected fraction of type \(t\) agents receiving \(x\). The continuous Random Priority allocation is

\[ p^\infty_{t,x} = \lim_{n\to\infty}p^{(n)}_{t,x}, \]

whenever the limit is taken along scales making all quantities integral. A solution consists of either:

  • a proof that this limit exists and is the unique rule satisfying the continuum axioms; or
  • a counterexample showing that the axioms permit another continuum rule.

For computation, the solution must output \(P^\infty\), or answer threshold questions such as whether \(p^\infty_{t,x}\ge r\).

There is a natural reason to expect this computational version to be tractable. In the limit, the random permutation can be represented by a priority quantile \(s\in[0,1]\). At each \(s\), type \(t\) arrives at rate \(\mu_t\) and takes its highest-ranked object whose remaining capacity is positive. The aggregate process is a piecewise-linear fluid process: object capacities are exhausted at finitely many events, after which affected types move to their next available objects. One expects an event-driven algorithm whose state is the remaining capacity vector and whose number of relevant changes is controlled by \(m\) and \(\tau\), rather than by the number of named agents or the \(n!\) possible priority orders. This would make the mirror a plausible Class A-style computational question, although that tractability result is not proved in the paper.

The mirror is faithful to Theorem 1. It preserves the same objects, capacities, complete preferences, random-priority mechanism, ex-post efficiency, and equal treatment. The only change is that \(n\) named agents with identical types are represented by their mass. It also has a genuine high-multiplicity bridge: the finite instances with \(n\mu_t\) copies are exactly discrete realizations of the continuous society, and the continuous answer is defined as their limit.

The main weakness is substantial. Theorem 1 compares probabilities of complete deterministic assignments. In a nonatomic population, any particular complete allocation typically has probability zero, so probabilistic monotonicity cannot simply be copied verbatim. It must be formulated through allocation marginals, finite-cylinder events, or the finite high-multiplicity approximants above. That co-modification may affect the characterization, and the paper does not establish that the theorem survives it. Moreover, because the paper has no computational anchor, the expected fluid algorithm and its complexity would be new work rather than a continuization of an existing complexity theorem.

Still, this is a real continuous mirror rather than a restatement. The paper studies finite populations and random lotteries over complete assignments; the proposed problem studies a distribution over preference types, per-capita capacities, and the limiting computation of Random Priority at population scale. The mirror covers the paper’s central result—Theorem 1—while honestly conceding that its strongest contribution would be a new continuous computational theory of that characterization, not a result already present in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s first concession is fatal under the programme’s standard: this paper contains no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization of a finite-agent rule. It proves neither a complexity bound nor an algorithmic decision problem. Computing a newly defined \(P^\infty\) would therefore be a new mean-field analysis of Random Priority, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

More fundamentally, the proposed mirror loses the object that Theorem 1 characterizes. The theorem concerns a probability distribution over complete assignments \(\mu:N\to O\). Its equal-treatment axiom compares two complete assignments that differ only by swapping two named agents, and probabilistic monotonicity compares the probability of one particular complete assignment across preference profiles. Those are identity-level statements.

In the nonatomic limit, a particular complete allocation has probability zero under the proposed random-priority construction. The matrix

\[ P=(p_{t,x}) \]

retains only type-object marginals. Equal-treatment-of-equals is then largely built into the representation: agents of one type are no longer individually represented. The correlations between their assignments, which are precisely what the paper’s axiom compares, have disappeared. The paper’s Appendix A.2 does not repair this loss. It shows that a marginal matrix may admit an equal-treatment decomposition; it does not show that Theorem 1 characterizes a unique marginal rule.

There are only two plausible ways to proceed, and neither gives the proposed mirror.

One can define the continuum rule by finite approximations:

\[ p^\infty_{t,x} = \lim_{n\to\infty}p^{(n)}_{t,x}. \]

But then the axiomatic content is inherited by construction. At every finite \(n\), Theorem 1 already forces the rule to be \(RP\); taking its limit does not produce a new continuum characterization. The remaining task is to analyse a particular hydrodynamic limit of a known mechanism. That may be technically worthwhile, but it is not a continuization of the paper’s result in the ChoCo sense.

Alternatively, one can formulate native continuum axioms using marginals or probabilities of finite-cylinder events. Marginal monotonicity is a new axiom, not probabilistic monotonicity from the paper. Cylinder events concern finitely many sampled, measure-zero agents and do not constrain the aggregate allocation in the way the finite theorem does. Different random couplings can share the same \(P\), so the central identity-level characterization is no longer present.

The school-assignment example is a credible high-multiplicity regime; that is not the objection. The obstruction survives every such scenario. If one preserves named-agent identities and complete-assignment lotteries, one has not really passed to a population continuum. If one quotients agents into masses, one removes the very structure on which Theorem 1 operates. Adding identity-specific information to the type space merely makes the number of types grow with the population and defeats the intended high-multiplicity regime.

Thus the proponent has identified an interesting possible computation of a fluid limit, but not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper. Every named result is axiomatic, and the only proposed computational object either is stipulated as the limit of finite Random Priority or forgets the paper’s decisive notions of complete assignments and identity-level monotonicity. The negative case is weakest only in the modest sense that the resulting fluid-limit problem could deserve an independent paper; it cannot, however, supply the ChoCo mirror claimed here.
