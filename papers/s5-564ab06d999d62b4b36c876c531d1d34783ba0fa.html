The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), ATFG utility tables over types, and a listed assignment of valid finite team profiles \(q\) with rational frequencies \(x_q>0\) satisfying \(\sum_q b_t(q)x_q=\mu_t\), is the assignment Nash stable? Check whether any represented member of type \(t\) in profile \(q\) can strictly improve by leaving that team and joining a team with profile \(r\), with the resulting source and destination teams satisfying the ATFG size and anchor rules; include singleton deviations where permitted.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete participant types, assigned to finite anchored team profiles with frequencies \(x_q\). Utilities remain non-transferable and depend on member, anchor, and pair types; stability tests whether any represented member has a profitable unilateral move between teams.

The objection that survived

The exact masses do not affect which profile-level deviations are profitable, and named-agent pair preferences may make the number of complete types grow with the population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s Nash-stability verification result. It does not cover the existence-hardness discussion or the heuristic experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • What finite high-multiplicity ATFG instances correspond to rational team-profile frequencies, and when does the correspondence run in both directions?
  • Does the existence problem become tractable or remain hard when the type support is small?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest affirmative case I see is narrow: a continuous version of Nash-stability verification for anchored teams, not a mirror of the paper’s main existence-hardness result.

My anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that given an ATFG and a coalition structure, deciding whether the structure is Nash stable takes polynomial time. The proof checks, for every player and every coalition, whether joining it would strictly improve that player’s utility; with the paper’s utility evaluation, this takes \(O(n^4)\) time. This is the paper’s numbered result that directly asserts a complexity classification. The paper also says in prose that Nash-stable coalition-structure existence is NP-complete, deriving this from cited ASHG hardness and the generalization result. Since that claim is not stated as a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition here, I do not use it as an anchor.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. A society has finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and type-level utility tables \(v_t(s)\), \(p_t(\{s,s'\})\), and \(d_t(a)\), plus anchor eligibility. A type is complete: two people share a type only when their utilities toward every other type, every pair of other types, and every anchor type agree. A team profile \(q\) consists of an anchor type and a multiset of other member types, with size between the game’s bounds. Its utility for a member of type \(t\) is the ATFG utility formula applied to those types.

A proposed continuous coalition structure is a finite list of profiles \(q\) and rational frequencies \(x_q\), where \(x_q\) is the number of teams of profile \(q\) per unit population. It must satisfy
\[ \sum_q b_t(q)x_q=\mu_t \]
for every type \(t\), where \(b_t(q)\) counts that type’s slots in the profile. The decision question is: is this assignment Nash stable? Check every type represented in every positive-frequency source profile against every positive-frequency target profile. A move is admissible when removing the member leaves a valid source team and adding them makes a valid target team, under the ATFG size and anchor rules; include singleton deviations wherever those rules allow them. If the move strictly improves the member’s utility, return that type, source profile, and target profile as a blocking witness. Otherwise answer yes.

This keeps the paper’s central choices: an anchored coalition, its size bounds, non-transferable utilities with pair effects, and a unilateral Nash deviation. The population becomes continuous, while each unit of population still occupies a whole slot in a finite-size team. The aggregate frequencies can be fractional; no individual receives a fractional team. The decision problem appears tractable: with the proposed profiles listed explicitly, check the type/profile and source/target pairs using rational arithmetic, in time polynomial in the input length, the number of listed profiles, and the support size \(\tau\).

A plausible regime is a large recurring role-playing event or matchmaking platform that assigns thousands of participants to small tables, each with a game manager. Participants with the same standardized play-style and compatibility profile form a type; anchor willingness is part of that profile, and pair synergies are specified between types. Then \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\), while the modeled preferences still match the paper’s team utility. The authors could reasonably recognize this as their problem at population scale: only the representation of the participant pool changes.

The weakness is that this type structure is a real restriction. In the paper’s motivation, people may know one another and have pair-specific preferences; in a large population, those details could make \(\tau\) large. There is also a gap between the scale of the society and the scale of a team: Nash deviations still concern one person joining a finite team. So this is a continuous population of repeated finite teams, not a mean-field interaction within each team.

That leaves a useful next question, but not one I would pass off as Proposition 1: does the paper’s Nash-stable-existence problem remain hard when the society is represented by a small number of complete types and rational masses? A formal correspondence with finite high-multiplicity instances would help answer it. This mirror covers the paper’s verification result in that regime; it does not cover its existence-hardness discussion or its heuristic experiments.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is a real computational result, so I would not reject the proposed mirror on the ground that there is nothing to mirror. The difficulty is that verification is already a local scan: for each player, compare their utility in their current team with their utility after joining each other team. With complete types and a list of team profiles, the continuous version makes essentially the same comparisons over types and profiles.

The population frequencies add little to that verification question. Once the assignment is feasible, stability depends on which profiles have positive frequency, not on their exact masses. A unilateral move changes one person’s source and destination teams, but changes the aggregate profile frequencies by zero. If the mirror tests representative type-level deviations, it is a support test over finite team configurations. If it instead requires a deviation to change a positive amount of mass, it has changed Nash stability into a coordinated mass-deviation notion. Neither version makes the population masses do computational work in Proposition 1’s question.

There is also a cost to getting a small type support. The paper allows each person to value named individuals and named pairs. A complete type must preserve those interaction signatures; in a general ATFG, that can force many people into distinct types. A role-based, repeated-profile setting could avoid that, and might be plausible for standardized team formation. But then the continuous verifier is a short type-and-profile check, while the paper’s distinctive pair-specific preferences have been substantially restricted. The mirror is well-defined; its claim on ChoCo’s time is weak.

A more ambitious question would ask whether a given type-mass distribution can be partitioned into Nash-stable team profiles. That uses the masses through feasibility constraints and is a better mirror of the paper’s existence discussion than Proposition 1 is. It would be a different computational problem, however, and I cannot rule out that it is worthwhile: with bounded team sizes, one can express feasible assignments as mixtures of finite team profiles, while stability constrains which profiles may coexist.

So my negative case defeats the verifier anchor as a compelling research target, but it does not establish the requested universal claim. The honest weak point is the existence question: a large population with genuinely repeated types and team-level synergies is conceivable, and its mass-feasibility problem may merit study. I would not claim that no continuous mirror survives; I would claim that the particular mirror offered is mostly a repackaging of Proposition 1’s finite local check.
