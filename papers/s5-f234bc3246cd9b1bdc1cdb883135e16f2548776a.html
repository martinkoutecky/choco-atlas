What the mirror covers

The proposed task covers evaluation of a fixed grading rule over type masses. It leaves the paper’s strategy-proofness and axiom characterizations, proxy-policy design, and ranking results without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow, prospective mirror of the paper’s grading component. The paper’s large participatory budgeting example suggests a plausible regime: a fixed slate of projects, a coarse grade scale, and a very large electorate with recurring eligibility and ballot patterns. Think \(n\) around \(10^5\), but only hundreds or thousands of distinct complete ballot types. That support-size assumption is plausible in some deployments, though the paper does not establish it for its examples.

One precise continuous problem would be Continuous Phantom-Proxy Grade Evaluation. An instance has \(m\) candidates and a finite support \(T\) of complete voter types. Each type records its eligibility and ballot entries \(b_\theta(J)\in A\cup\{\emptyset,\otimes,\circ\}\), together with the information used by a specified proxy policy. Each type has rational mass \(\mu_\theta\geq 0\), with \(\sum_{\theta\in T}\mu_\theta=1\). For each candidate \(J\), a type contributes its submitted grade if it graded \(J\); otherwise it contributes the proxy grade assigned by the fixed policy, if any. These contributions define a finite weighted grade distribution \(\nu_J\). Given a quantile \(q_J\in(0,1]\), output the lower \(q_J\)-quantile of \(\nu_J\) as \(J\)’s grade, or \(\emptyset\) if the pool is empty. The smallest-median phantom-proxy rule is the case \(q_J=1/2\).

The object being computed is the candidate grade vector for the specified aggregation rule. With proxy values supplied or computable in polynomial time, sorting at most \(\tau=|T|\) weighted grade values per candidate gives an exact algorithm in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. This keeps the population distribution as the continuous object; the grade scale need not be continuous. It covers fixed-rule grade evaluation, not the paper’s characterizations of which rules satisfy its axioms or its ranking construction. Other questions include how to choose the quantiles and proxy policy, and whether any useful ranking extension survives for mass distributions.

The scenario is recognizable as their problem: it computes the grades their mechanisms produce, in the large-electorate setting that motivates the paper. The paper’s Proposition 3.6 says that every phantom-proxy mechanism in its defined class is strategy-proof. That is a relevant mechanism result, but it says nothing about computational complexity.

Under your anchor requirement, there is no eligible anchor here. Proposition 3.6 establishes strategy-proofness, while other results characterize axioms such as consistency; none is a complexity result. So the evaluation problem above is a plausible computational extension of the paper, but it cannot honestly be presented as a continuous mirror of one of its named computational results.

That is also the weakest point of the positive case: computing a weighted order statistic is straightforward, and the paper’s contribution is axiomatic rather than computational. Moreover, high multiplicity helps only when \(\tau\ll n\), and the proposed evaluation problem does not preserve the paper’s individual strategy-proofness claims as a complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is at the anchor requirement: this paper states no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 3.3 and Proposition 3.6 characterize strategy-proof mechanisms; the later results characterize axioms and properties of the ranking construction. None classifies a problem’s running time or gives an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm. The computational complexity results mentioned in the introduction belong to other papers.

The proposed Continuous Phantom-Proxy Grade Evaluation is the closest population mirror. Give each complete ballot type a rational mass, apply the specified proxy policy, and compute each candidate’s weighted quantile. That is a coherent continuous model, and sorting the \(\tau\) contributions for each of \(m\) candidates takes \(O(m\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons, assuming the proxy values are efficiently computable. It still has no computational theorem in this paper to mirror. Proposition 3.6 supplies no complexity content: in an atomless society, one agent’s report has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. A positive-mass deviation would instead ask about coalition manipulation, a different incentive property.

The best expanded candidate is to optimize the assignment of grading rights using expertise and conflicts of interest. The paper explicitly leaves that process for future work, so it may support an adjacent research project. But the paper gives no objective for assignment quality or model of grading accuracy; those would need to be supplied to define the optimization problem. Section 6’s voter duplication and normalization is also a nearby continuous idea, yet its results concern the ranking rule’s properties, not computational complexity.

The negative case is strongest as a verdict on this paper as a source of computational anchors. It cannot establish that every future continuous project inspired by the paper would be unworthy; repeated ballot and eligibility types are plausible in some large deployments. The proponent’s proposed evaluation task is recognizable and well-posed, though, and the paper offers no named computational result for it to continue. The broader claim that no continuous follow-on could ever be worthwhile remains the weak part of the case.
