What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-on concerns exact and approximate \(\mathrm{IR}\) classifications; it does not provide computational mirrors for the paper's \(H\)-social aggregator or strategy-proofness results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a research lead around robust independence of rankings, but it does not meet your anchor requirement: the paper has no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its main results are structural impossibility theorems. Section 10 even leaves their computational implications as future work.

The closest result is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper: for \(m\ge3\), every social aggregator \(f:S_m^n\to S_m\) that is \(\epsilon\)-IR is \(O(m^8\epsilon)\)-close to either a constant function or a dictatorship of the form \(f(x)=y\circ x_i\), for some voter \(i\) and fixed permutation \(y\). The companion Theorem 3.3, also proved here, says that exact IR forces precisely those two forms. These are useful anchors for an axiomatic mirror, but neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. I would not present them as qualifying anchors.

A plausible continuous-population research question, rather than a valid computational mirror, would use a finite set \(A\) of institutional roles and types \((a,\sigma)\), where \(a\in A\) and \(\sigma\in S_m\) is a ranking. A society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types; the intended regime has many interchangeable people per role and relatively few types. An aggregator \(F\) maps \(\mu\) to a social ranking. A mass version of IR would require that if two societies have the same mass, for each role \(a\), alternative \(j\), and rank \(k\), on rankings placing \(j\) at \(k\), then \(F(\mu)\) and \(F(\nu)\) place \(j\) identically. One could ask for a classification of such aggregators, and then for a quantitative relaxation.

That question has a recognizable connection to the paper’s IR condition, but the obstacle is substantial. The paper’s condition and its dictatorships depend on individual identities: a dictator is one voter, and IR checks changes to one voter while holding the others fixed. Replacing voters by masses within roles makes the rule anonymous within each role and removes the ability to distinguish a dictator from the rest of that role. Keeping identities as part of each type instead makes the support grow with the population, defeating the high-multiplicity premise. The robust version has another gap: the paper measures violations by sampling finite profiles and voter changes, while a continuous version needs a specified measure over society perturbations.

So my positive case is modest: a role-based mass-IR classification could be a sensible follow-on theorem, and the authors might recognize its connection to their work. But I cannot give it a qualifying named computational anchor, a paper-grounded complexity prediction, or a prover-ready computational problem without adding choices that change the paper’s object. The identity-sensitive dictator result is the weakest point, and it is central rather than cosmetic.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.3 and Theorem 3.5 are the paper’s strongest anchors, but both classify social aggregators: exact IR forces a constant or a dictator, and approximate IR gives closeness to one of those forms. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem or supplies an algorithmic result. The paper itself leaves computational implications for future work in §10. That leaves the proposed mirror without a paper-grounded computational anchor.

The role-based mass-IR proposal is a coherent axiomatic follow-on, but its best natural form exposes a deeper mismatch. If IR means that changing one person while keeping their rank of \(j\) fixed cannot change \(j\)’s social rank, then it is vacuous for a zero-mass individual. If it means the proposed invariance under each role’s distribution of ranks, take any rational society with fixed role shares and replicate it into \(N\) voters. The induced finite aggregator is IR and invariant under permuting voters within each role. For \(m\ge3\), Theorem 3.3 says it must be constant or a dictator; the role symmetry rules out a named-voter dictator once each positive-mass role has at least two voters. So at fixed role shares, this exact mass condition forces the outcome to ignore the rankings. Allowing the outcome to depend on role shares gives a quota-based rule, a new axiomatic object rather than the paper’s dictatorship classification.

The robust theorem does not repair the bridge. Its error rate samples finite profiles and individual voter changes. A continuum version needs a specified distribution over societies and a scale for mass perturbations; individual-sized changes vanish as \(N\) grows, while positive-mass changes define a different error measure. One could study computation for a rule representation under a chosen mass-IR criterion, but that would be a newly formulated problem, not a computational mirror of either theorem. I cannot rule out every worthwhile project one might build from the paper; I do find the case against spending ChoCo effort on a continuous mirror persuasive.
