The continuous mirror question

Given candidates, a target ranking \(X\), and a finite support \(T\) of ranking-and-price types \((r_t,p_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), let \(d_t=\kt(X,r_t)\). Given a per-capita budget \(B\) and normalized distance threshold \(K\), does there exist a mass allocation \(x_{t,q}\geq 0\), for integer \(q\in\{0,\ldots,d_t\}\), such that \(\sum_q x_{t,q}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,q}p_t q x_{t,q}\leq B\), and \(\sum_{t,q}(d_t-q)x_{t,q}\leq K\), where every unit of mass assigned \(q\) receives a sequence of \(q\) admissible swaps?

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution over complete ranking-and-price types. Choose the mass \(x_{t,q}\) of each type receiving each feasible integral swap count; minimize \(\sum_{t,q}p_t q x_{t,q}\) subject to preserving each type’s mass and keeping total residual Kendall distance at most \(K\). Compare the optimum with budget \(B\).

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 12 on swap bribery and Theorem 8 on dollar bribery; it leaves the ranking-deletion, candidate-deletion, and possible-Kemeny-score results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a discrete profile with denominator \(n\), what exact or additive rounding guarantees convert a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,q}\) into integer action counts while controlling budget and distance?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Swap-Bribery-KemenyScore\(_\infty\). A type is a complete ranking \(r\) together with its per-swap price \(p_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of that type. Agents retain the paper’s individual action: each one receives an integer number of admissible adjacent swaps. The continuous decision is how to divide each type’s mass among those possible action levels.

Precisely, an instance has \(m\) candidates, a finite support \(T\) of ranking-and-price types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a target ranking \(X\), a per-capita budget \(B\), and a normalized score threshold \(K\). Let \(d_t=\kt(X,r_t)\). Choose \(x_{t,q}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(q\) swaps, for each integer \(q\in\{0,\ldots,d_t\}\). Require
\[ \sum_q x_{t,q}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,q} p_tq\,x_{t,q}\le B,\qquad \sum_{t,q}(d_t-q)x_{t,q}\le K. \]
For every \(q\), the solution includes a sequence of \(q\) admissible swaps for the agents assigned that action level. The paper’s swap argument establishes that such a sequence exists and reduces distance to \(X\) by exactly \(q\). The question is whether such an allocation of mass and swap sequences exists. This is the continuous version of the paper’s problem: it preserves the target score, budget, ranking types, and integral per-agent actions while allowing a type’s mass to split across action levels.

The anchor is Theorem 12, proved in this paper: Swap Bribery-Kemeny Score can be solved in polynomial time. Its discrete problem asks, given candidates, a profile of rankings, a target ranking \(X\), a per-swap cost for each ranking, a budget, and a threshold \(k\), whether some admissible swaps can be performed within budget so that the total Kendall tau distance to \(X\) is at most \(k\). The theorem gives a dynamic program over rankings, numbers of swaps, and residual distance. The continuous formulation above has only \(O(\tau m^2)\) action variables, since \(d_t\leq {m\choose 2}\), and linear constraints with rational data. It is therefore a Class A candidate: an LP gives an efficient algorithm in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length.

A plausible regime is a large ranking-aggregation service or consultation with many respondents ranking the same modest set of options. A campaign may contact respondents in segments defined by their current ranking and a common price per adjacent change, seeking to bring a designated benchmark ranking’s aggregate Kendall distance below a stated level. There may be millions of respondents but far fewer distinct ranking-and-price types. Each contacted person still makes discrete adjacent changes; mass simply records what fraction of a segment receives each number of swaps. This scenario is not the paper’s required motivating story, but its underlying problem is unchanged, so I think the authors would recognize it as their high-multiplicity version.

A second, simpler anchor is Theorem 8, also proved here: \(\$\)-Bribery-Kemeny Score can be solved in polynomial time. In the paper’s discrete problem, each ranking has a price for replacement by \(X\); the question is whether some rankings can be changed to \(X\) within budget so that the total distance to \(X\) is at most \(k\). The paper reduces this to Knapsack with polynomially bounded values. For its continuous mirror, use the same society, target \(X\), and per-type replacement prices \(c_t\). Choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) replaced by \(X\), and ask whether
\[ \sum_t c_tx_t\le B \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)\kt(X,r_t)\le K. \]
This is fractional Knapsack, hence Class A as well. It is a particularly direct mirror: the paper already identifies which action to take—replace selected rankings by \(X\)—and the continuous variable says what fraction of each type to select.

Both mirrors cover only the corresponding Kemeny Score manipulation results, not Kemeny Consensus or Kemeny Winner. That distinction matters here: the question is whether a fixed \(X\)’s total distance to the manipulated profile falls below a threshold, not whether \(X\) becomes a consensus or wins. The paper supplies no numbered hardness result for these manipulation problems, so these algorithmic anchors cannot show that discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum. The natural follow-up is to compare each continuous solution with the discrete high-multiplicity instance \(\mu_t=n_t/n\): how large can the rounding gap be, and when can a feasible mass allocation be converted into integer numbers of agents while preserving budget and score bounds? More general swap prices or constraints on which agents can be contacted would also test where the LP structure stops helping.

My weakest point is that allowing a cohort to split among different numbers of swaps is a genuine relaxation: the mass allocation need not correspond to a discrete profile with the same exact budget and threshold. That is a legitimate continuous population model, but it makes the integral-versus-fractional bridge a substantive question rather than an automatic equivalence. The mirror remains credible because every individual action stays discrete and the only continuous object is the population allocation—the change the programme is meant to study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorems 8 and 12 may be poor priorities for ChoCo: both manipulation problems are driven by additive bookkeeping around a fixed target ranking \(X\). Once the population is a distribution, the continuous versions reduce directly to small linear programs. The swap version has variables \(x_{t,q}\) for the mass of each ranking-and-price type receiving \(q\) swaps, with \(0\le q\le \kt(X,r_t)\). Its budget and residual-distance constraints are linear, and there are \(O(\tau m^2)\) variables. The dollar-bribery version is fractional selection of which mass to replace by \(X\). Neither seems to need the more distinctive machinery of pricing over an exponential set of rankings.

That makes the two anchors closely related, and perhaps less valuable as flagship results than a mirror that reveals a nontrivial boundary of continuous optimization. But it does not defeat either mirror. In the swap case, every individual still makes an integral number of swaps; only an atomless cohort is divided among action levels. Since the paper establishes that any feasible number of swaps can reduce distance by exactly that amount, the mass allocation is well-defined. In the dollar case, replacing a fraction of a type by \(X\) is equally direct. A large consultation or survey with repeated rankings and common contact prices is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for both.

One could object that the relevant types must include prices, so idiosyncratic prices could make the support as large as the population. But that is an instance-regime limitation, not a general obstruction: repeated ranking-and-price cohorts are plausible, and the paper’s problem does not depend on individual identity beyond those attributes. Nor is there evidence in the supplied text that these continuous computational questions have already been answered. The discrete polynomial-time results are not a continuous collision.

So the honest negative case is weak. The models may be elementary and overlap substantially, which is a reason to rank them below richer mirrors. It is not a sound reason to claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: both anchors survive as sensible continuous-population questions, and either one would be enough.
