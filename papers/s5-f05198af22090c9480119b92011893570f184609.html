The continuous mirror question

Given a finite task set \(M\), conflict graph \(G=(M,E)\), common additive valuation \(v:M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\), and rational population scale \(N\geq 1\), choose nonnegative masses \(x_B\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq M\). Require \(\sum_B x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni g}x_B=1/N\) for each task \(g\), EF1 between every pair of bundles with positive mass, and \(\bigl||B|-|D|\bigr|\leq 1\) for all bundles \(B,D\) with positive mass. Is there such a mass allocation with \(N\sum_B x_B\,|E\cap\binom{B}{2}|\leq |E|/N\)?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass continuum of recipients with one common valuation and a finite set of indivisible tasks. The decision variables \(x_B\) are the population masses receiving each whole-task bundle; each task is assigned to mass \(1/N\), and the scaled total number of within-bundle conflict edges is minimized or tested against \(|E|/N\).

The objection that survived

The paper gives no stable task-template or conflict-graph limit for a large-\(N\) regime; the proposed co-scaling defines a valid family, but leaves its canonical or empirical basis less developed.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's identical-valuation EF1 guarantee, balanced bundles, and \(|E|/N\) conflict threshold. It leaves the discrete running-time guarantee and Theorems 2–4 for general valuations or their implementation outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational \(N\geq 1\) admit a balanced EF1 mass allocation meeting the \(|E|/N\) conflict threshold?
  • Can the feasibility problem be solved efficiently in \(m\), \(|E|\), and the bit length of \(N\), or does compatibility among EF1 bundle supports make it hard?
  • How does the mass optimum compare with finite allocations at integer \(N\) as the population scale grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see a narrow but genuine mirror in the identical-valuation result. The plausible regime is a large cohort of interchangeable workers or service units receiving indivisible tasks: every unit has the same additive priority score for each task, and conflict edges mark task pairs that should not go to the same unit. Take the number of units \(N\) and tasks \(m\) to grow together, with only one agent type. That is a concrete high-multiplicity regime, even though it is not the paper’s school-class example.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Section 4.2. For identical additive valuations, it says that CyclicShiftRR computes a balanced EF1 allocation with at most \(|E|/n\) conflict violations in \(O(m\log m+|E|)\) time. The theorem applies for arbitrary \(n\); its algorithm uses the shared valuation order and cyclic shifts to keep conflicts within the stated bound. This is the strongest anchor here for a large-population mirror. The paper offers no comparable named hardness result: its Min UnCut hardness discussion is an external result about minimizing conflicts *without* EF1.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Let \(M\) be the finite set of tasks, \(G=(M,E)\) their conflict graph, and \(v(g)\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\) the common value of task \(g\). Give the homogeneous society total mass \(1\), and specify a population scale \(N\in\mathbb{Q}\), \(N\geq 1\). Each task has supply \(1/N\) in population-mass units: for integer \(N=n\), this is exactly one task among \(n\) agent-equivalents. An allocation is a measure \(x\) over bundles \(B\subseteq M\), where every bundle contains whole tasks and \(x_B\) is the population mass receiving \(B\). Require
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq M}x_B=1,\qquad \sum_{B\ni g}x_B=\frac1N\quad\text{for every }g\in M. \]
For every two positive-mass bundles \(B,D\), require EF1: if \(D\neq\varnothing\), some \(g\in D\) satisfies \(v(B)\geq v(D\setminus\{g\})\). Also require balance, \(\bigl||B|-|D|\bigr|\leq 1\) for every pair of bundles in the support. The objective is to minimize
\[ W(x)=N\sum_{B\subseteq M}x_B\,|E\cap \tbinom{B}{2}|, \]
the total conflict count in \(N\)-agent-equivalent units. The threshold-search form of the problem asks for such an allocation with \(W(x)\leq |E|/N\), or a certificate that none exists. A solution can be given as an explicit list of bundle–mass pairs; once a mutually EF1 support is fixed, the masses can be sparsified while preserving item supplies.

This preserves the paper’s underlying question: each infinitesimal recipient still gets an integral bundle, conflicts are counted within bundles, and fairness is still EF1 under the same additive valuation. Only the recipients are aggregated, and per-capita task supply scales with population. For integer \(N\), any allocation from Theorem 1 yields a feasible measure by setting \(x_B\) to the fraction of agents receiving \(B\). The result therefore gives the continuous problem a discrete boundary case, though it does not prove the bound for noninteger \(N\).

I would tentatively expect this mirror to be Class A. Theorem 1’s cyclic-shift structure suggests a mass-based construction, and a configuration formulation would keep integral bundles explicit while making their population masses the variables. The unresolved algorithmic issue is whether the construction and its EF1 guarantees extend to arbitrary rational \(N\), or whether the needed pricing and support-compatibility problem resists efficient solution. Natural follow-ups are whether the \(|E|/N\) bound always holds for rational \(N\), how the continuous optimum relates to finite allocations as \(N\) grows, and whether the approach extends beyond one valuation type.

The weakest point is that this is a deliberately homogeneous case, and the mass allocation may look like fractional assignment if one focuses on tasks rather than recipients. The model addresses that by keeping each recipient’s bundle integral and interpreting \(1/N\) as the mass of recipients receiving one task. Still, the paper’s algorithm proves its guarantee only for integer numbers of agents; accepting the real-mass version as the authors’ analogue is the main judgment call. I would not use Theorems 2–4 to broaden this case: their two-agent or constant-agent regimes do not make as strong a population-continuum anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational result, so “there is nothing to mirror” is not a sound objection. The difficulty is that its unbounded-agent case is exactly the case where the input society has no nontrivial type distribution: every agent has the same valuation. The combinatorial input is the finite conflict graph on named goods.

The proposed \(x_B\) formulation puts mass on bundles, which are outcomes of the allocation, not exogenous agent types. For integer \(N\), it records the histogram of a finite allocation: each good goes to one of \(N\) agents, and its receiving mass is \(1/N\). For noninteger \(N\), the same constraints give each named good positive mass among recipients. That is a fractional or replicated supply of the good; it no longer represents one indivisible task assigned once. Keeping the bundles integral for each recipient does not remove that change. So the proposal’s extra continuous structure is chiefly in the allocation, where mass is used to describe who receives what.

Scaling up the population does not fix this while keeping the paper’s finite goods model. With a fixed set of \(m\) goods, each good’s mass tends to zero as \(N\) grows, and at most \(m/N\) of the population can receive any good. Avoiding that degeneration requires the task inventory to grow too. For the paper’s arbitrary conflict graphs, that means the task universe and its graph grow with the population; a stable finite type space would require additional structure, such as repeated task templates or a limiting conflict model. That may be a worthwhile new problem, but it is no longer just the population mirror of Theorem 1.

This is the strongest case against the anchor, but it is not airtight. The programme explicitly allows supply to scale with population, and a repeated-template setting could make that scaling natural. If the authors accept bundles as the mass types and task supply as a co-modification, Theorem 1 remains a plausible narrow mirror. I would call the negative case weak against that judgment, rather than claim it rules out every worthwhile scenario.
