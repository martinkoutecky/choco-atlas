The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow: SCRUF-D has a credible continuous-population version of its allocation problem, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to anchor it. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. The closest support is empirical: Section 8.3 reports that, on a synthetic sequence of user cohorts, dynamic allocation mechanisms substantially reduce cumulative fairness regret, with Lottery and Weighted showing at least a tenfold improvement over the baseline. That is an experimental finding, not a complexity result, so I would not present it as an anchor.

My unanchored lead candidate is Dynamic Cohort Allocation for Fair Recommendation. Consider a recommender serving many requests over \(H\) discrete periods. A type \(a\) is a complete recommendation context: users of that type share the same profile, baseline recommendation, compatibility with each fairness agent, agent-generated recommendations, and relevance values used to measure accuracy. Let \(\mu_{t,a}\) be the fraction of requests of type \(a\) in period \(t\). A plausible regime is a large platform whose requests fall into relatively few repeated, standardized contexts, so the number of requests is much greater than the number \(\tau\) of types.

For each type \(a\), let \(\pi_{a,r}\) be the length-\(k\) ranking produced by the paper’s fixed choice mechanism when the system allocates fairness agent \(r\), with \(r=\bot\) denoting no fairness agent. Each individual still receives an ordinary, integral recommendation list; only the mass of requests assigned to each option is fractional. Let \(y_{t,a,r}\) be that mass. Given an accuracy loss \(\ell(\pi)\), protected-item exposure \(e_j(\pi)\), exposure targets \(\rho_j\), and specified time windows \(W\), ask whether there is a rational allocation \(y\) such that

\[ \sum_r y_{t,a,r}=\mu_{t,a}, \qquad \sum_{t\in W,a,r} y_{t,a,r}e_j(\pi_{a,r}) \ge \rho_j\sum_{t\in W,a}\mu_{t,a} \]

for every concern \(j\) and window \(W\), while total accuracy loss is at most \(B\):

\[ \sum_{t,a,r}y_{t,a,r}\ell(\pi_{a,r})\le B. \]

A solution is the allocation table \(y\); it can be implemented by assigning requests within each cohort according to those proportions. This captures the paper’s dynamic allocation of fairness concerns across recommendation opportunities, and its exposure-versus-accuracy tradeoff. It covers the single-agent lottery-style allocation variants and group exposure concerns, not the full range of SCRUF-D’s possible agents and mechanisms.

For this explicit finite-action version, I expect tractability: it is a linear feasibility problem in \(O(H\tau |F|)\) variables. A more ambitious version would let the system choose among rankings not already specified by the agents and choice rule. That creates a pricing problem over rankings and raises the more interesting question of whether continuous-optimization methods can handle the resulting action space.

The mirror is plausible because the paper already formalizes user profiles, fairness-agent compatibility, allocation weights, recommendation lists, and history-dependent fairness measures. Its strongest regime is one where many requests genuinely share all the information those mechanisms use. The weakest point is also substantial: the paper studies an architecture and experiments, not an optimization problem over allocation schedules. This formulation adds a policy optimizer in place of the paper’s chosen allocation heuristics. And if user profiles and recommendation outputs are highly individual, \(\tau\) may approach the number of users, erasing the high-multiplicity advantage. So this is a credible follow-on continuous question about SCRUF-D’s central allocation idea, but not a mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the missing anchor: the paper states no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Its results are experimental comparisons. The synthetic study reports lower cumulative fairness regret for some allocation mechanisms, but that is not a complexity result to continuize. The proposed cohort-allocation LP is therefore a new follow-on problem, not a mirror of a named result in this paper.

That LP also simplifies away what makes SCRUF-D dynamic. In the paper, allocation depends on fairness evaluations of the choice and allocation histories, \(m_i(\mathbf L,\mathbf H)\), and on the current user’s compatibility, \(c_i(\omega)\); the choice mechanism then uses the allocation and recommendation lists, and the resulting choices update the histories. A table \(y_{t,a,r}\) with fixed lists \(\pi_{a,r}\) and aggregate exposure constraints does not encode that feedback. A more faithful model would optimize a policy over contexts and evolving history states. It could be well posed, but it would be a substantially different control problem; the simple LP follows only after fixing the action lists and replacing the feedback with aggregate constraints.

The paper does not establish the small support that the cohort model needs. Its real data contain thousands of distinct users with rating histories, and its synthetic users are varied latent profiles; the three arrival segments are not three complete types in the programme’s sense. A type would need to capture the profile, compatibility, recommender output, fairness-agent outputs, and relevant accuracy values. Grouping users by broad propensity or by approximate similarity could produce useful approximations, but it changes the question and needs its own error analysis.

There is a credible counterexample to any universal claim that no scenario exists: a high-volume service with many repeated, standardized recommendation contexts could have a small number of complete types, making cohort allocation meaningful. That is where this negative case is weakest. Still, the paper gives no evidence that its own setting has that structure, and the faithful dynamic version does not inherit the candidate’s straightforward LP formulation. I would reject this as an anchored mirror of the paper, while conceding that a carefully specified repeated-context policy-optimization study could be worthwhile in its own right.
