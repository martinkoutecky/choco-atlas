The continuous mirror question

Given slot categories \(E\), valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and type valuations \(v_t:2^E\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) that are \(\{a,b\}\)-bivalued submodular with \(a\mid b\), choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) assigned to whole bundles \(S\subseteq E\), subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_g\). First maximize the mass receiving positive utility, \(\sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}\); among those solutions, maximize \(\sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over integral bundles for each valuation type, with each bundle consuming the corresponding per-capita slot supplies. The objectives are lexicographic: maximize positive-utility mass, then maximize mass-weighted log utility.

The objection that survived

The per-agent category restriction and scaled copies are not formalized in the paper, so Theorem 6.1 does not itself provide an algorithm for the mirror.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the allocation-computation results in Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 for maximum Nash welfare and leximin. It leaves the maximin-share guarantees and envy-freeness results outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration pricing problems for these objectives be solved in time polynomial in the number of slot categories, valuation types, and input bit length?
  • How do finite cohorts with scaled supplies map to rational mass assignments, and what rounding or approximation guarantees follow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is high-volume course allocation. Imagine \(N\) students choosing among recurring course or shift slots. Students fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of types, where a type fixes the person’s entire valuation function over bundles. Each type’s valuation is monotone submodular, with marginal values \(a\) or \(b\), and \(a\mid b\). Course or shift capacities scale with the cohort: if slot \(g\) has supply \(q_g\) per student, the finite expansion has \(Nq_g\) indivisible copies. Here \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\).

Let \(T\) be the types and \(E\) the finite catalogue of slot types. The continuous instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and valuations \(v_t:2^E\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). A feasible solution is a mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\ge0\): the mass of type \(t\) receiving the whole bundle \(S\subseteq E\). It obeys
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq E}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}\le q_g. \]
For example, \(x_{t,S}=0.2\) means 20% of type \(t\) receives the integral bundle \(S\). In a finite expansion, assignments correspond to masses in increments of \(1/N\). This retains the paper’s indivisible bundles and utility functions while making the population aggregate continuous. Scaling per-capita supplies with the population is a natural co-modification for this regime.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. For \(\{1,c\}\)-bivalued submodular valuations, where \(c\) is an integer at least 2, it states that Bivalued Yankee Swap computes a max Nash welfare allocation using
\[ \phi_{\mathrm{MNW}}(X,i,d)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{v_i(X_i)+d}{v_i(X_i)},&v_i(X_i)>0,\\ Md,&v_i(X_i)=0, \end{cases} \]
for sufficiently large \(M\). “Max Nash welfare” here first maximizes the number of agents with positive utility, then the product of those agents’ utilities.

The corresponding continuous problem is: given \(T,E,\mu,q\) and the valuations above, find a feasible \(x\) that first maximizes
\[ P(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}, \]
then maximizes
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S). \]
For a finite expansion, the second objective is the log of the product of utilities, divided by \(N\). A solution is an optimal mass assignment, reportable as a list of type, whole bundle, and assigned mass. The expected classification is tractable, Class A: each objective stage is linear optimization over bundle configurations, and the model has a sparse mass-assignment description. The key unresolved algorithmic question is pricing over bundles—whether the bivalued submodular structure supports efficient optimization when bundle configurations are generated implicitly.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 6.2, also proved in this paper. For the same integer-\(c\) valuation class, it states that Bivalued Yankee Swap computes a leximin allocation using \(\phi_{\mathrm{leximin}}(X,i,d)=-(c+1)v_i(X_i)+d\). Leximin maximizes the worst utility, then the second-worst, and so on.

Its continuous mirror uses the same feasible \(x\). Let \(u_0<\cdots<u_r\) be the attainable utility levels and define
\[ F_x(u_j)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S:v_t(S)\le u_j}x_{t,S}. \]
The problem is to find a feasible \(x\) that lexicographically minimizes \((F_x(u_0),\ldots,F_x(u_{r-1}))\). With masses on the \(1/N\) grid, this is the sorted utility-vector comparison from the finite leximin problem. The expected classification is again Class A: each threshold objective is linear in \(x\), so the problem can be expressed as a sequence of lexicographic LP objectives over bundle configurations. As with MNW, the open issue is efficient configuration pricing.

Theorem 6.1 is the stronger anchor for my case: MNW gives a particularly direct finite-to-continuous identity through the log-product objective. Theorem 6.2 adds an independent, recognizable fairness objective. These mirrors cover the paper’s allocation-computation results for MNW and leximin; they do not claim to continuize the MMS guarantees or envy-freeness results.

The paper’s Section 6.5 reports an NP-hardness result for MNW with bivalued additive valuations when the value ratio is nonintegral, citing Akrami et al. That is a useful follow-up boundary question: does the continuous mass model remove that hardness, or does it persist? I do not use it as an anchor, because in the supplied paper it is an unnumbered report of an external result, rather than a numbered result proved or stated here.

The weakest point is that this mirror scales the supply of recurring goods alongside the population. If the goods remain a fixed set of individually distinct objects while the number of agents grows, the proposed regime no longer fits. The course-slot scenario is meant to make the co-scaling credible: students still receive integral bundles, and the continuum represents the fractions of each valuation type receiving each bundle. The key further questions are whether bundle pricing is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether optimal continuous assignments admit useful rounding or approximation guarantees for finite cohorts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the mismatch between the paper’s finite supply and the proposed population limit. Keep the paper’s fixed set of \(m\) goods and let the number of agents grow: because every nonempty bundle has positive value, at most \(m\) agents can receive positive utility. The positive-utility fraction in the MNW mirror is therefore at most \(m/N\), tending to zero; in the limit, leximin also puts all population mass at utility zero. Those limits erase the fairness comparisons Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 optimize.

The proposed repair—scaling supplies with the cohort—keeps the question alive, but changes the finite model. If each course or shift category has many copies while an agent may receive at most one copy of a category, that per-agent restriction is absent from the paper’s allocation model. If agents may receive multiple copies, their valuations must instead be defined over growing multisets. Either version could be a sensible extension, and the authors might recognize it, but the paper’s theorems do not directly establish a mirror for it.

The proposed MNW and leximin programs also have the familiar configuration-LP form: masses are assigned to integral bundles, and the objectives are linear once represented as log utility or utility-threshold counts. Configuration LPs are already used in work on Nash welfare with submodular valuations, though that work does not settle this exact model or its pricing problem ([example](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09669)). So the proponent has not established the claimed Class A result; the hard computational question is whether bundle pricing is tractable for this valuation class. That is a valid question, but it is also the mirror’s main unresolved content.

These objections undercut the claim that the two mirrors are already tractable. They do not defeat the anchors: with per-capita supplies and a recognized repeated-resource model, Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 still lead to coherent continuous questions over distributions of integral bundles. I can’t honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The negative case is weak precisely at that point: the best version survives the modeling objection.
