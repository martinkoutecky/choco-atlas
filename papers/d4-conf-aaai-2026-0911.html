Propositions 6–12 establish which axioms the proposed targeting functions satisfy; Proposition 4 represents a targeting method with a preorder; and Corollary 2 gives an aggregation representation. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the strongest objection: this paper states no complexity theorem. Its numbered results characterize targeting methods and their axiomatic properties. That makes it a less direct ChoCo mirror than a paper about bribery or control, but it does not establish that no worthwhile computational question arises from the results.

Proposition 6 gives a concrete choice rule, and Corollary 2 explains the broader family it belongs to. In the paper’s expert-finder setting, imagine a large audience whose members each choose a target profile and receive recommendations under one of these rules. Let each type \(t\) fully specify a target’s evaluation vector and any other information the recommendation process uses. For a fixed candidate set \(O\), let \(e_t(p)\) denote the share of type \(t\) that endorses \(p\), splitting ties by a specified convention. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types, and the endorsement mass of \(p\) is \(E_\mu(p)=\sum_t\mu_t e_t(p)\).

That gives a specific continuous robustness question: what is the minimum cost of transferring mass between target types so that a chosen alternative \(p^\star\) becomes a most-endorsed recommendation, meaning \(E_{\mu'}(p^\star)\ge E_{\mu'}(q)\) for every \(q\in O\)? The targeting methods in the paper determine the type-level endorsements; the new question studies how the recommendation outcome responds to changes in their population mix. With a finite type space, it has the familiar mass-transfer form. With compactly represented or combinatorial alternatives, computing the endorsements may itself require studying the targeting rule’s optimization problem.

This does add an aggregate recommendation objective that the paper does not study, so it is not a clean mirror of one of its theorems. But it uses the paper’s actual targeting rules, in an audience setting supported by its expert-finder example, and asks a computational question about a distribution over complete types. The lack of a complexity result is a real weakness in the case against a mirror; it is not enough to rule out this more ambitious one.
