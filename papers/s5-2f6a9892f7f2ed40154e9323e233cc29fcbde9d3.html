What the mirror covers

The named results concern manipulability and implementation under different preference assumptions and voting procedures; none supplies a computational result to mirror. The weighted ranking task covers the paper's recommended all-mayors procedure only.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its numbered results concern manipulability, strategy-proofness, and implementation in dominant strategies or Nash equilibria. Those are social-choice properties, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest numbered results are Corollary 1, proved here: every rule in the family of majority rules is manipulable and not implementable in dominant strategies under unrestricted preferences; and Corollary 10, proved here: with unimodal preferences, replacing the prior election of representative mayors with a vote by all mayors on projects makes the resulting two-stage mechanism implementable in dominant strategies, hence also in Nash equilibria. Neither is an eligible computational anchor.

There is a plausible population-level extension behind the paper’s recommendation that all mayors vote on projects. Let each type \(t\) be a single-peaked ranking of projects along a geographic ordering, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of municipal governments with that ranking. Given the ordered projects and the finite-support distribution \(\mu\), the task could be to compute the ranking produced by repeatedly selecting the median peak and removing the selected project. That is a concrete, plausibly tractable problem; a large collection of municipalities sharing relatively few geographic preference types is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The authors might recognize it as an extension of their proposed all-mayors mechanism.

But this would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper models three government-level agents and studies implementation; it does not analyze the complexity of computing outcomes from a distribution of municipal types. So the positive case is limited to a plausible setting for follow-up work. Under the requested anchor standard, there is no paper result for which I can honestly state a continuous problem as its mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the decisive limit: there is no computational result in this paper to mirror. Corollary 1 concerns manipulability and dominant-strategy implementation; Corollary 10 concerns implementation under unimodal preferences and a changed first stage. They are claims about strategic properties, not the complexity of computing an outcome. The proposed task—compute the ranking produced by repeated median selection—is a plausible follow-up, but it is not a continuous version of a computational problem the paper analyzes.

The closest attempt to make it a mirror runs into a mismatch in what “population” means here. At the OCAD, the strategic players are three government tiers, each with one vote. If the mayors in a tier are instead treated as an atomless continuum, an individual mayor has measure zero and cannot change the median or the resulting ranking. Strategy-proofness then becomes vacuous at the individual level. To give a deviation positive effect, one must let a positive-mass bloc coordinate or treat each preference type as a strategic player. Either move changes the paper’s unit of strategy and asks a new coalition-manipulation question. Keeping the paper’s three strategic players, meanwhile, leaves no population to continuize.

The proposed outcome-computation task does have a coherent weighted version: given masses on single-peaked preference types, compute the median peak, remove that project, and repeat. But that is an algorithm for evaluating the authors’ suggested reform. It neither mirrors an established complexity result nor preserves the individual-deviation question their implementation results answer. Adding a mass-minimization objective for manipulation could produce a computational problem, but the paper supplies neither that objective nor a result to transfer; it would be a new research direction.

I would not claim that no useful continuous project could ever grow out of this paper. The weighted-median extension is plausible, and the authors might recognize it as relevant to their recommendation. The negative case is narrower: on the paper and the proposed extension, there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result. The best candidate is a new, well-posed computation about a proposed mechanism, not the missing complexity landscape this programme is meant to chart.
