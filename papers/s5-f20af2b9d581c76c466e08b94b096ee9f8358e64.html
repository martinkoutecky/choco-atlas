The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(i\in I\) with rational masses \(\alpha_i\), weak rankings, and per-unit assignment costs \(w_{ij}\), and position types \(j\in J\) with rational capacities \(\beta_j\), find a minimum-cost popular assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), or report that none exists. Require \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\), with each type’s last-resort option having capacity \(\alpha_i\). An assignment is popular if, for every feasible challenger flow \(z_{ijk}\ge0\) with \(\sum_k z_{ijk}=x_{ij}\) and \(\sum_{i,j}z_{ijk}\le\beta_k\), \(\sum_{i,j,k}z_{ijk}(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_i j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_i k])\le0\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population of applicant types carries mass \(\alpha_i\); each type has identical complete preferences and costs. Position categories have capacities \(\beta_j\). The decision variables \(x_{ij}\) assign mass at linear cost, while challenger flows \(z_{ijk}\) encode pairwise popularity comparisons.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the minimum-cost popular-matching problem with ties in §4; it leaves the enumeration discussion and the cited strict-preference variants aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can popularity for a given \(x\) be characterized or separated efficiently using weighted covers or duality?
  • What is the exact complexity of minimum-cost popular mass assignment as a function of the number of types, position categories, and rational input bit length?
  • How does the continuous optimum relate to finite markets with repeated applicant and position types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a mirror of the paper’s minimum-cost popular matching with ties, in a large placement market where many applicants share the same complete preference list and assignment costs, and many available positions are interchangeable within a category. For example, a placement round might have hundreds of thousands of applicants but only a few dozen applicant types and job categories. Each applicant type records its full weak ranking and its per-unit assignment costs; each job type records a category of equivalent positions and its capacity. The type counts must stay small relative to the population for this to be a useful high-multiplicity regime.

Call the problem Minimum-Cost Popular Mass Assignment with Ties, or \(\mathrm{MCPM}_\infty\). An instance gives applicant types \(i\in I\) with rational masses \(\alpha_i\), normalized so that \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\), and position types \(j\in J\) with rational capacities \(\beta_j\). Each applicant type has a weak ranking over its acceptable position types and a last-resort option \(r_i\) of capacity \(\alpha_i\). It also has a cost \(w_{ij}\) for assignment to each acceptable destination, including its last resort. The rankings and costs are part of the type, so applicants grouped together are identical in every respect the problem uses.

A candidate solution is an assignment \(x_{ij}\geq 0\): type \(i\) sends mass \(x_{ij}\) to destination \(j\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\) and each real position type receiving at most \(\beta_j\). Its cost is \(\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}x_{ij}\). It is popular if no feasible reassignment of the same population gives strictly more mass an outcome they prefer than mass an outcome they prefer less. More precisely, for an incumbent assignment \(x\), let \(z_{ijk}\) be mass of type \(i\) currently assigned to \(j\) that a challenger assigns to \(k\). Require \(\sum_k z_{ijk}=x_{ij}\), and require the challenger’s total assignment to each position type to respect its capacity. For every such feasible \(z\), the net vote
\[ \sum_{i,j,k} z_{ijk} \bigl(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_i j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_i k]\bigr) \]
must be at most zero. Tied outcomes contribute zero. The question is to find a minimum-cost popular \(x\), or report that none exists. Because the population is atomless and agents within each type are interchangeable, this transition flow captures every way a challenger can reassign the mass.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It assumes a popular matching exists, takes a minimum vertex cover \(X\) of the first-choice graph \(G_1\), and sets \(\tilde P=P\cap X\). It defines \(\tilde E\) to consist of the \(E_1\) edges with exactly one endpoint in \(X\), together with the \(E_2\) edges whose endpoints are both outside \(X\). The theorem says an applicant-complete matching \(M\) is popular if and only if every edge of \(M\) belongs to \(\tilde E\) and every post in \(\tilde P\) is matched. This is a characterization, not a numbered “in P” theorem: in §4 the paper uses it to reduce minimum-cost popular matching to a minimum-cost assignment problem, which it says can be solved in \(O(n(n\log n+m))\) time. Theorem 1 is cited from Abraham et al.; Theorem 2 is the paper’s own result.

I expect \(\mathrm{MCPM}_\infty\) to be Class A, tractable through a capacity-aware version of the paper’s flow, duality, and assignment approach. The source’s first-choice graph and cover provide a concrete starting point for a type-level characterization, and costs aggregate linearly by type. But Theorem 2 does not itself establish that its minimum-cover characterization carries over: capacities and masses may require weighted dual objects, and the continuous question must retain the paper’s majority-comparison definition. Proving such a characterization is the core computational question, not something to assume from the finite result. The result also prompts questions about an exact weighted-cover or dual characterization, the dependence of running time on type support and rational bit length, and how the continuous optimum relates to finite markets with repeated types. This mirror covers the paper’s minimum-cost problem with ties; it does not try to continuize its enumeration discussion or the cited strict-preference variants.

The mirror should be recognizable to the authors: it preserves applicants’ weak rankings, one-sided preferences, one-to-one assignment at the individual level, majority comparison between matchings, and the minimum edge-cost objective. Position categories and capacities are a co-modification needed to express many equivalent posts as mass; last-resort capacity scales with applicant mass, just as the paper’s last resorts guarantee a complete assignment. A finite market with \(n_i\) applicants of type \(i\) and \(q_j\) posts of type \(j\) maps to \(\alpha_i=n_i/N\) and \(\beta_j=q_j/N\), where \(N\) is the applicant count.

The weakest point is the need to make the comparison between an incumbent and a challenger precise when type-level assignments split mass. The transition variable \(z\) does that by allowing every feasible pairing of current and new assignments among otherwise identical applicants. That is faithful to the paper’s named-applicant vote count, but the paper’s cover theorem does not show that this mass-level popularity notion has the same clean characterization. If the characterization fails, the proposed tractability expectation may fail too; the mirror remains a direct computational question about the same assignment and majority rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent’s anchor is not, strictly speaking, a numbered complexity theorem. Theorem 2 characterizes popular matchings; the paper’s minimum-cost algorithm appears later as a reduction to a standard assignment problem. So the proposed mirror is less a new complexity result than a capacitated version of the paper’s structural characterization.

That is not enough to defeat it. The paper does give a computational problem and an algorithm, and the high-multiplicity version is a precise question: with rational masses for applicant types and capacities for position types, find a minimum-cost popular assignment. Applicants can remain individually assigned; an atomless type’s mass can be partitioned among posts. The transition variables \(z_{ijk}\) capture feasible challenger reassignments, so popularity still means that no challenger wins more than half the population’s comparison. Replacing private last-resort posts with type-level outside options is a natural co-modification, and grouping applicants by their full preferences and costs follows the programme’s definition of type.

The usual objections do not hold here: identities need not matter once applicants are identical in every relevant respect; majority comparisons remain meaningful because they measure mass; and an easy continuous algorithm would be an answer to the question, not a reason the question was unworthy. The paper gives no continuous computational treatment that already settles it.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case rests mainly on limited novelty: the mirror may amount to extending a known matching formulation to capacities. But the population model is natural, and the type-based problem is well posed. The case that no worthwhile mirror exists is weak.
