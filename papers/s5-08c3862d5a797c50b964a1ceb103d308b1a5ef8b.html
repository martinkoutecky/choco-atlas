The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly speaking, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor: no named theorem states membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, W[1]-hardness, or similar. Theorems 1–4 are structural, existence, and asymptotic results. So the case below is for a computational mirror anchored in the paper’s strongest named result, not a claim that the paper already contains a complexity result.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4, proved in the paper. It says that if a hospital quality class \(F_n^k\) has a non-vanishing fraction of hospitals, \(\lim_{n\to\infty}|F_n^k|/n>0\), then there exist matching processes \(\{\mu_n^*\}\), a patience threshold \(\bar\delta<1\), a market-size threshold \(N\), and \(p^*>0\), such that for every \(\delta\in(\bar\delta,1)\) and \(n>N\): first, \(\mu_n^*\) is self-enforcing; and second, at every on-path ex post history \(h\), every hospital \(f\in F_n^k\) has probability at least \(p^*\) of receiving fewer than its full quota, \(\Pr(|\mu_n^*(f\mid h)|<q)\ge p^*\). The theorem is proved here, although its proof uses a cited result of Che and Tercieux through Lemma 4.

The natural high-multiplicity regime is a repeated resident market with a very large number of hospitals and new residents, but only finitely many hospital and resident types. A hospital type \(a\) includes its capacity \(q_a\), utility vector over resident types, quality class, and any other payoff-relevant parameter. A resident type \(b\) includes its ranking \(\succ_b\) over hospital types and unemployment. Let \(\lambda_a\) be the fraction of hospitals of type \(a\), and \(\rho_b\) the mass of residents of type \(b\) in each cohort. Thus the regime has \(n\) agents but fixed or moderate \(|A|+|B|\), with \(n\gg |A|+|B|\).

A per-period aggregate matching is a mass flow \(z_{ab}\ge0\), with \(\sum_b z_{ab}\le\lambda_aq_a\) and \(\sum_a z_{ab}\le\rho_b\). This is only a type-level average: each individual hospital still receives an integral bundle of residents, while \(z_{ab}\) records the mass of such assignments. A hospital of type \(a\) has per-hospital utility \(u_a(y_a)=\sum_b v_{ab}y_{ab}\), where \(y_{ab}=z_{ab}/\lambda_a\). The public randomization and fresh-cohort uncertainty from the paper remain, but they are not what is continuized; only the populations are.

I would name the resulting problem Mass Capacity-Reduction\(_\infty\). Its input is the finite type data \((A,B,\lambda,\rho,q,v,\succ)\), a discount factor \(\delta\), a target hospital type \(a^\star\), and a probability threshold \(\pi>0\). The question is whether there exists a history-dependent matching process \(P\) such that:

\(P\) is self-enforcing at every ex post history;

every resident is individually rational;

no hospital has a profitable feasible deviation plan, where a deviation consists of recruiting up to its capacity from resident mass that strictly prefers the deviating hospital to its recommended assignment; and

for every hospital of type \(a^\star\), at every on-path history, \(\Pr_P(\text{that hospital receives fewer than }q_{a^\star}\text{ residents})\ge\pi\).

A solution is an explicit type-level matching process, including its public lottery, reward states, punishment states, transition rules, and a certificate of all incentive inequalities. An optimization version maximizes \(\pi\), or instead maximizes expected unfilled capacity and hence the mass of residents released to rural hospitals.

This is a faithful mirror of the paper’s question. It retains long-lived hospitals, short-lived cohorts, capacity, matching stability, history dependence, coalition deviations, and the policy objective of making residents available to rural hospitals. It does not replace the population by fractional outcomes: fractional flows are merely the correct high-multiplicity description of many integral assignments.

I would initially expect the finite-type version to be Class A. The aggregate matching set is a transportation polytope; hospital utilities are linear; and, with finitely many resident types, a hospital’s best feasible deviation can be found by selecting the most valuable types that prefer it. Lemma 1, proved in the paper, further supports this direction: self-enforcement can be checked through one-shot deviations rather than arbitrary deviation plans. The reward-and-punishment construction behind Theorem 4 suggests a finite certificate consisting of an on-path lottery, one punishment template per hospital type, and continuation-value inequalities. Whether unrestricted history dependence always admits such polynomial-size certificates would itself be a worthwhile complexity question.

Theorem 4 gives strong evidence for the expected answer: if \(\lambda_{a^\star}>0\) and the type is not blocked by a mass-level top coalition, then sufficiently patient hospitals should admit a positive-probability capacity reduction. The paper’s Theorem 1 supplies the complementary obstruction: a top-coalition hospital is matched to its top-coalition students in every static stable matching and, for every \(0<\delta<1\), in every self-enforcing matching process at every history. In the continuum mirror, this predicts that the maximum sustainable diversion of top-coalition mass is exactly zero.

Further questions include the minimum patience threshold for a prescribed reduction, the maximum sustainable freed-resident mass, the complexity of computing the reduced-game minmax values, and the precise finite-to-continuum approximation guarantee.

The weakest point is serious: Theorem 4 relies on strict, identity-level rankings and idiosyncratic match-quality shocks \(\zeta_{f,w}\). Collapsing these into finitely many complete types may remove the horizontal competition that makes the punishment algorithm work. A defensible mirror must therefore retain finite priority or quality subtypes, or explicitly ask whether the result survives after replacing pair-specific shocks by a finite type distribution. The zero-mass “elite hospital” case is also invisible in a pure continuum and should be treated as a separate boundary problem.

So the positive case is real but qualified: this paper does not provide a computational result, yet Theorem 4 gives an unusually direct semantic anchor for a continuous-population optimization problem whose natural variables are mass matchings and whose likely tractability comes from finite-type linear optimization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1–4 are structural, existence, incentive, and asymptotic statements; none asserts membership in \(P\), hardness, an exact algorithm, or an approximation guarantee. Under the programme’s own gate, this is already a red flag: a computational problem invented around one of these theorems is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 4 is the strongest proposed anchor, but it does not survive the proposed finite-type reduction. The theorem quantifies over an arbitrary history-dependent process \(\mu:H\to M\), and self-enforcement is a pointwise incentive condition for every named hospital at every history. Lemma 1 reduces the deviations that must be checked once a process is supplied; it does not provide a finite representation of the process, nor an algorithm for finding one. Without a bound on memory, states, or description length, “does there exist a self-enforcing process?” is not a finite computational problem. With such a bound, one has added a new protocol-synthesis problem that the paper never studies.

More importantly, the proposed mass flow \(z_{ab}\) omits the ingredients that make Theorem 4 work. The proof uses pair-specific shocks \(\zeta_{f,w}\), random rankings among named hospitals, hospital-specific punishment matchings \(m_n^f\), and exact identification of the hospital that deviated. None of this is represented by \((\lambda,\rho,q,v,\succ)\). A type-level flow records average assignments, but not whether a particular hospital can find a particular better resident, whether that resident would accept the deviation, or which hospital must be punished next.

There are only two ways to repair this. If complete types include the full preference and shock vectors over counterpart identities, then almost every hospital or resident has a distinct type; the number of types grows with \(n\), and the high-multiplicity regime disappears. If instead one replaces those vectors by finite distributions over quality classes, the horizontal competition and order-statistic argument underlying the punishment algorithm disappear. One may then obtain a sensible stochastic mean-field matching model, but it is a new model whose central problem is dynamic mechanism synthesis, not a transportation LP unlocked by continuization.

Theorem 1 fares no better. A top coalition in the paper is a finite named hospital together with a finite named set of residents. In a continuum, that coalition has mass zero. Its impossibility can therefore survive only as an invisible individual-level statement, with no effect on aggregate capacity or released resident mass. To preserve a nonzero effect, one must replace it by a positive-mass mutually favourite block. That requires anonymous preferences, tie-breaking, and a new mass-level stability concept; it is not the theorem’s top-coalition result. Keeping a distinguished top hospital instead produces a finite-atom/continuum hybrid rather than a continuous society.

The same problem appears with the elite hospitals of Theorem 3. Their defining property is precisely that their mass vanishes. In a continuous population they disappear from the aggregate instance, while the theorem’s conclusion concerns each surviving individual elite hospital. Retaining those hospitals as atoms preserves the economic statement but abandons the proposed mass-only mirror.

Thus the proponent’s suggested problem can certainly be made interesting, but only by adding substantial new structure: a finite-state restriction on matching processes, a monitoring model, a representation of identity-specific shocks, and a choice between individual and aggregate incentive constraints. That could support a new research programme in computational dynamic matching. It cannot honestly be presented as a computational continuization of this paper’s results.

The universal claim that no conceivable reformulation could ever be worthwhile is too strong to prove. But the strongest defensible verdict is negative: every faithful finite-type mirror loses the identity-level mechanism driving the paper, while every version that retains it ceases to be a finite-type continuous-population problem.
