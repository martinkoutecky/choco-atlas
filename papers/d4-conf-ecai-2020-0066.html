The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard. Its numbered theorems classify whether deterministic Pareto-efficient, false-name-proof social choice functions exist—for example, Theorem 3 proves existence on single-peaked cycles \(C_k\) for \(3\le k\le5\), while Theorem 4 proves nonexistence for \(k\ge6\). Those are axiomatic existence results, not complexity or algorithmic results about a named computational problem.

A possible population model would put mass \(\mu_v\) on each graph vertex and ask a mechanism to choose a facility location. But the paper’s Theorem 1 shows that eligible mechanisms can be replaced by outcome-equivalent ones that ignore duplicate ballots. That makes the support, rather than the population shares, decisive; in a nonatomic version, an individual’s fake identities also have no effect unless the model changes what counts as a manipulation. This is a weak basis for a continuous-population mirror of the paper’s results, and it does not supply the required anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts before choosing a continuous model: none of the paper’s named results is a computational result in the programme’s sense. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 classify when a Pareto-efficient, false-name-proof social choice function exists on a cycle. The hypergrid and single-dipped theorems make similar existence claims. They do not give the complexity of a named problem or an algorithm for it. One could recast “does such a function exist for this graph?” as a decision problem, but that would be a new computational target, not a result this paper establishes.

The most charitable population model would put mass \(\mu_v\) at each vertex and choose a facility location. That is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. The problem is specific to these results, not to high multiplicity generally: Theorem 1 shows that whenever an eligible rule exists, there is an outcome-equivalent eligible rule that ignores duplicate ballots. For that witness, the decisive population information is which vertices have positive mass, not the amounts \(\mu_v\). The proposed mirror therefore remains an axiomatic existence question over supports; the continuous range of population shares supplies no computational problem for the paper’s theorems to answer.

False-name-proofness makes a mass interpretation harder still. If a fake identity has zero mass, its report cannot affect an ordinary mass-based rule. If any positive mass at a new vertex counts, manipulation turns on a support change, which is discontinuous at zero and recovers the support-based formulation. One could instead let an agent fabricate a positive mass of fake voters, but with the paper’s unrestricted, cost-free fake identities that gives a different strategic model; adding a cost or asking for the minimum mass needed to change the outcome would create a new robustness problem.

A study of those new problems could be worthwhile. But it would add an algorithmic objective or a quantitative manipulation model absent from the paper, rather than continuize one of its named computational results.
