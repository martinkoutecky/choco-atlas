The continuous mirror question

Given item types \(J\), per-capita supplies \(\bar c_j\), and a finite support \(T\) of bidder types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type has a complete XOR menu of bids \((a_{tk},b_{tk})\), compute an efficient mass allocation \(y_{tk}\) and nonnegative prices \(p_j\). Each type’s assigned mass must not exceed \(\mu_t\), total item use must not exceed per-capita supply, every used bid must maximize \(b_{tk}-p\cdot a_{tk}\) over that type’s menu and the outside option, and positively priced supply must clear.

The model it lives in

A finite-support, high-multiplicity combinatorial auction: each type represents indistinguishable bidders with the same full bid menu, \(y_{tk}\) is the mass assigned bid \(k\), and per-capita supplies scale with population. The welfare-maximizing allocation is a linear program, with dual item prices supporting the used bids.

What the mirror covers

Covers efficient allocation and supporting-price computation for a high-multiplicity market; leaves the finite CA’s binary cut generation and the PME/MAP selection results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For \(n\) replicated bidders with \(n\mu_t\) integral and supplies \(n\bar c_j\), what welfare-gap bounds hold between the discrete winner-determination problem and the type-mass LP, and under what conditions do supporting prices converge?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a narrow high-multiplicity mirror of Proposition 8 in Appendix C. It is proved in this paper, but it is a convergence result, not a polynomial-time classification: Algorithm 1 repeatedly finds a valid cut that separates a fractional optimum of WDP-LP from the integer winner-determination optimum, scales the cut to integer coefficients, and adds it to the auction. Proposition 8 says this process converges to AWE prices for any CA instance. The paper discusses NP-hardness of WDP in prose, citing earlier work, but gives no numbered hardness or tractability classification to anchor a stronger complexity claim.

Call the mirror Type-Mass Equilibrium Pricing. An instance has item types \(J\), per-capita supplies \(\bar c_j\), and a finite support \(T\) of bidder types with masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type includes the full XOR bid menu: for each \(k\in K_t\), a bidder requests bundle \(a_{tk}\) for value \(b_{tk}\). Every individual still receives an integral bundle; \(y_{tk}\) denotes the mass of type \(t\) assigned bid \(k\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_{k\in K_t}y_{tk}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,k}a_{tkj}y_{tk}\leq\bar c_j\). The problem is to compute an efficient allocation \(y\) and supporting prices. A solution is \(y\) and nonnegative item prices \(p\) such that \(y\) maximizes \(\sum_{t,k}b_{tk}y_{tk}\), each used bid maximizes \(b_{tk}-p\cdot a_{tk}\) among that type’s bids and the outside option, and positively priced supply clears. One may also return priced artificial items: each is a nonnegative integer cut on the type-bid masses, valid for every feasible mass allocation, and any positively priced cut must be fully used.

The plausible regime is a large allocation market with many small, standardized requests—for example, workload classes buying bundles of shared compute capacity. A type captures the complete bid menu and value pattern for one class; the number of requests \(n\) is much larger than the number of types \(\tau\), and capacity scales with the population. Then masses are the honest unit of change, and per-capita prices make sense. This is recognizable as the paper’s combinatorial allocation and price-support problem, though the scenario is not the paper’s small set of strategic auction bidders.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The type-mass allocation is an explicit linear program in the listed bids and supplies. Its dual supplies item prices and type surplus variables; complementary slackness gives efficient allocation, envy-freeness, and market clearing. Thus a continuous society has a natural Walrasian equilibrium, so the continuous analogue of Proposition 8 can be answered without generating artificial cuts. For rational masses and supplies, the allocation also has a high-multiplicity bridge: rounding each \(y_{tk}\) down to multiples of \(1/n\) preserves feasibility and loses at most \(O(K/n)\) welfare, where \(K=\sum_t|K_t|\) and bid values are bounded. That gives an additive approximation for large replicated discrete markets, though it does not by itself preserve equilibrium prices.

The scope is deliberately just Proposition 8’s efficient allocation and price-support question. I would not claim this mirrors the paper’s distinctive PME or MAP selection results. The weakest point is also central: the paper’s artificial cuts address the integrality gap of a finite CA, while allowing agents to form a continuum makes type-level allocations fractional and can remove that gap. The authors would likely recognize the proposed market as a high-multiplicity version of their WDP and WE problem; a referee could reasonably say that the AWE cut-generation question itself has disappeared. The useful follow-up is to quantify that disappearance: how quickly do finite replicated auctions approach the continuous allocation and its prices, and does any nontrivial price-match refinement remain when “removing a bidder” means removing positive mass rather than one infinitesimal agent?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed compute-capacity cohort is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I can’t honestly rule out worthwhile continuous computational social choice for such markets. The negative case is narrower: Proposition 8, the case’s sole anchor, does not state a complexity result. It says Algorithm 1 converges, without a bound on its rounds or runtime; the paper says the required winner-determination solves are NP-hard. A complexity study of the proposed mass market could be worthwhile, but it would be a new result rather than a mirror of a complexity finding in this paper.

There is also a mismatch in the algorithm’s central object. Its cuts are valid for binary winner determinations \(x\), and separate those from fractional points of the WDP-LP. In a nonatomic type-mass society, a feasible value \(y_{tk}\) can be realized by assigning that fraction of type \(t\) to bid \(k\); each person still receives an integral bundle. Those fractional points are actual allocations. A cut that excludes one may therefore be invalid for the continuous society. The proposed model has changed what counts as a feasible allocation, so Algorithm 1’s separation task does not carry over.

A stronger alternative would center the mirror on PME or MAP. But removing one agent from a nonatomic population changes no mass, so the paper’s single-bidder price-match condition loses its force. Removing a positive mass instead would define a different robustness criterion, with a chosen mass threshold and a coalition-like deletion. That could motivate new research, but this paper gives no complexity result for it to mirror.

So I would reject this anchor as a ChoCo mirror while conceding the limit of the negative case: the proposed continuous allocation market itself is sensible. The case against it rests on the gap between that market and the paper’s computational contribution, not on the claim that no useful continuous question could be asked in the surrounding area.
