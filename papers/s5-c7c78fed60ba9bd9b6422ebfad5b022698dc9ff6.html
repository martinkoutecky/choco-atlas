The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a plausible mirror of the paper’s winner-determination problem, with a major qualification: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its NP-hardness statement in the introduction is attributed to reference [1], not proved here. The closest results are empirical comparisons in Tables 1–5 and the unnumbered runtime discussion in Appendix A.1. Those do not meet the requested anchor standard, so there is no qualifying anchor to restate and no per-anchor problem I can supply.

The best candidate mirror is the underlying allocation problem that the paper’s algorithm evaluates. Call it Continuous Multi-unit OR-bid Allocation. There are \(k\) item classes, a finite set \(T\) of bidder types, and a distribution \(\mu\) over those types. A type is a complete OR-bid profile: its members have the same package bids and values. For each type \(t\), let \(v_t(S)\) be its reported value for receiving bundle \(S\), and let \(q_j\) be the per-capita supply of item class \(j\). The input gives the type masses, values, and supplies as binary rationals, along with an explicit list of feasible bundles and their values for each type.

The question is to maximize total declared value by choosing \(x_{t,S}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned the integral bundle \(S\):

\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S} x_{t,S}v_t(S) \]

subject to

\[ \sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad (t\in T), \qquad \sum_{t,S} x_{t,S}\ell_j(S)\le q_j \quad (j=1,\ldots,k), \qquad x_{t,S}\ge 0. \]

Here \(\ell_j(S)\) is the amount of item class \(j\) in bundle \(S\). A solution is the assignment masses \(x_{t,S}\), not fractional bundles for individual bidders. Every bidder still receives an integral bundle; only a large cohort of identical bidders can be split across bundles.

A plausible regime is a large market with many small energy consumers or facilities whose package bids follow a relatively small set of standardized profiles: for example, \(N\) in the tens or hundreds of thousands and \(\tau=|T|\) in the hundreds or low thousands. Supplies scale with population, as they would in a per-capita formulation. This is a reasonable high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s multi-unit electricity-allocation motivation, though it is not the regime established by its experiments.

For the stated explicit-configuration input, this is a linear program and should be polynomial in the number of listed configurations, \(k\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length: a plausible Class A mirror. If the valuations instead have a compact representation with exponentially many bundles, efficient separation over bundles becomes a further question and may preserve combinatorial hardness. The paper itself discusses LPs as a comparison point, so I think its authors would recognize the allocation objective as theirs. The mirror covers winner determination, not the paper’s approximate pricing rule.

The weakest point is that this is a proposed high-multiplicity relaxation, not a result the paper states or analyzes. In particular, the paper’s LocalSearch and transformToSWPM concern finite bid lists, and its budget-balance and individual-rationality claims do not establish a continuous pricing result. Extending pricing raises its own question: in a nonatomic population, removing one infinitesimal bidder has no effect, so a VCG-style counterfactual may yield zero. One would need to define a continuous payment notion that remains meaningful for the authors’ mechanism. The paper explicitly leaves incentive compatibility incompletely analyzed, which makes the pricing mirror weaker still.

So there is a sensible positive research question here: the population can be continuous while each assigned bundle stays discrete. But because the paper has no qualifying named computational result, this remains a candidate mirror rather than an anchor-backed case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The allocation mirror is coherent, but it does not give this paper a strong ChoCo anchor. The paper’s most plausible computational result is the unnumbered Appendix A.1 analysis of transformToSWPM: a finite-bid-list runtime bound of \(O(k^3N)\), refined to \(O(w^2kN)\) when the final number of winners \(w\) is known. So I would not rest the negative case on “there is no computational result at all.” The appendix gives one, even though the paper has no numbered complexity theorem.

The strongest allocation mirror would use large cohorts of consumers with identical full OR-bid profiles, scale supplies per capita, and assign each cohort mass across feasible allocation patterns. That is a sensible scenario; the one-bundle formulation can be repaired to represent bidders who may win several compatible OR bids. But this mirror asks about aggregate welfare allocation. It does not mirror the paper’s local-search approximation result: the paper reports experimental performance for MHC-3, without a theorem about its approximation quality or complexity. Fractional allocation LPs were already used in work on large multi-unit combinatorial auctions, which reinforces that this is a distinct extension of the allocation model rather than a result established by this paper ([Gonen and Lehmann on LPs for large multi-unit auctions](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0202016); [their work on multi-unit auction relaxations](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0202032)).

The appendix bound is a closer anchor, but its payment calculation reruns the finite allocation on removal of a particular winning bid. In an atomless cohort, removing one bidder changes no mass. Removing positive mass prices a cohort as a whole; dividing the change by the removed mass gives a natural marginal price, but it changes the counterfactual and the algorithm whose runtime the appendix analyzes. A continuous pricing question might be worthwhile, but the paper does not establish that marginal-price mechanism or any incentive guarantee for it.

So the best negative case is about fit: the proposed welfare LP is a plausible, author-recognizable research direction, and a continuous pricing extension can be formulated, but neither is a direct mirror of the paper’s main computational contribution. The universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile work is too strong: the repeated-consumer electricity setting is credible. The negative case is strongest if the decision is whether this paper supports a result-anchored ChoCo mirror; it is weak if the decision is whether the adjacent research direction has value.
