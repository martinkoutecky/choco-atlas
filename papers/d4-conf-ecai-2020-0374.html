The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a focused, algorithmic mirror of the paper’s setwise maximum-regret computation. The paper does not give a numbered NP-hardness or complexity-class result, so there is no hardness anchor here. Its useful named computational anchor is Proposition 18, proved in this paper and used in the later algorithms and experiments. It says: for a compact \(W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^p\), linear utilities \(f_w(a)=w^\top\hat a\), a finite set \(A\), and a single alternative \(b\), the setwise max regret \(\mathrm{SMR}_W(A,\{b\})\) is the maximum \(x\) for which there is a \(w\in W\) satisfying \(w^\top(\hat b-\hat a)\ge x\) for every \(a\in A\). With \(W\) given by linear constraints, this is an LP.

A continuous-population version is Population Setwise Maximum Regret. An instance has a finite menu \(A\) and a rival alternative \(b\), each represented by a rational \(p\)-attribute utility vector; a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) complete user types, each with a rational weight vector \(w_t\); and a nonempty rational polytope \(P\) of permitted population compositions, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\). Assume the weights use a common cardinal scale, so utilities can be aggregated across users. For a type \(t\), the best utility available in a menu \(S\) is \(U_t(S)=\max_{a\in S}w_t^\top\hat a\). The task is to return the exact value and a maximizing population \(\mu\) for
\[ \max_{\mu\in P}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(w_t^\top\hat b-U_t(A)\bigr). \]
A nonpositive value means \(A\) has at least as much average utility as \(b\) in every permitted society.

The connection to Proposition 18 is direct at the type level: a society concentrated on type \(t\) has regret \(w_t^\top\hat b-\max_{a\in A}w_t^\top\hat a\), the same quantity Proposition 18 optimizes over scenarios. The population version lets society composition vary continuously within \(P\), for example to represent uncertainty about market shares while requiring each large cohort to keep a minimum share. Here \(\mu\) is a population composition, not a lottery over outcomes. A finite society of \(N\) users with \(n_t\) users of each type has \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); the continuous problem allows fractional shares directly.

This regime is plausible for a large recommendation service comparing two hotel or product offers: millions of users, a much smaller number of stable preference segments, and segment shares constrained by market data. It makes the population continuous while keeping each user’s preferences and the alternatives’ outcomes discrete in the relevant sense. With explicit \(P\), the per-type regrets can be calculated from the menus and the remaining optimization is a linear program. The specified mirror is therefore Class A, with running time polynomial in the input size, including \(\tau\), the number of constraints defining \(P\), the menu size, \(p\), and the bit length of the rational data.

The mirror covers the paper’s regret comparison for a menu against a single alternative. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s possibly strictly optimal alternatives or its minimal-equivalent-subset results. Further questions include whether an efficient analogue exists when alternatives are described implicitly, and how continuous compositions relate to finite-\(N\) populations with integer group counts.

The weakest point is that the paper studies one user’s uncertain preference model, whereas this mirror aggregates regret across many users. That requires both interpersonal comparability of utilities and a shift from uncertainty about one person’s weights to variation in population composition. The connection is credible because the per-type quantity being aggregated is exactly the paper’s menu-regret quantity, and the paper explicitly motivates its methods with recommendation and choice-set applications. Still, an author might regard the population aggregate as a natural extension of their problem rather than its continuous form.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not argue that the paper has no computational result to mirror: Proposition 18 gives an LP characterization of setwise max regret, and Theorem 4 and the algorithms build on it. The problem is that Proposition 18 optimizes over \(w\), one user’s possible preference model. It does not optimize over a population.

The proposed population regret,
\[ \max_{\mu\in P}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \left(w_t^\top\hat b-\max_{a\in A}w_t^\top\hat a\right), \]
changes what the uncertainty means. In the paper, \(W\) describes what the system does not know about the same user’s preferences. In the proposal, \(P\) describes which mixes of different users are possible. The former is preference uncertainty; the latter is uncertainty about population composition. A mix of preference vectors cannot simply stand in for one preference vector either: generally,
\[ \max_{a\in A}\left(\sum_t\mu_t w_t\right)^\top\hat a \leq \sum_t\mu_t\max_{a\in A}w_t^\top\hat a, \]
and the inequality can be strict. Interpreting the weights as a society therefore changes the menu utility being compared.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is a multi-user recommender: many users fall into complete preference types, the system compares menus by average per-user regret, and market data restricts the possible type shares. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem; grouping identical users loses no relevant information. But it adds utilitarian aggregation across users and a common cardinal scale for their utilities—choices the paper’s single-user model does not make. Its finite-user parent would be a new group recommendation problem, not the finite-population version of Proposition 18.

If the aim is to preserve the paper’s original robust comparison for one user, introducing population shares does no work. If the aim is to compare menus for a population, the resulting question may be worthwhile, but it is an extension of the paper’s subject rather than a mirror of its named result. The composition polytope and the cross-user welfare rule supply the new model; Proposition 18 supplies only the per-type regret values.

So the negative case is a scope argument, not a proof that no useful continuous population model exists. It is weakest because the paper’s recommendation applications could plausibly lead its authors to endorse a multi-user extension. The text alone does not establish that they would treat collective recommendation as their problem.
