The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result about computational complexity. Its results concern mechanism properties.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here: the serial dictatorship with project closures (SDPC) is strategy-proof and always produces a constrained Pareto efficient matching, provided a feasible matching exists. Proposition 1, also proved here, shows that ordinary serial dictatorship can fail both strategy-proofness and constrained Pareto efficiency when some project has quorum above \(1\). Neither result says that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The other numbered propositions concern when serial dictatorship has those mechanism properties and whether SDPC spans the Pareto frontier; they do not supply a complexity anchor either.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting nearby: students in a large university system, grouped by complete preference ranking and priority cohort, assigned to courses with enrollment quorums and capacities. A continuous instance could give each cohort mass \(\mu_t\), use \(x_{tp}\) for the mass of cohort \(t\) assigned to course \(p\), and use \(y_p\) to indicate whether \(p\) opens, with per-capita quorum and capacity constraints on total assignments. But turning Theorem 1 into a continuous strategy-proofness question is not a clean computational mirror: individual reports have zero effect in an atomless population, while retaining distinct serial priorities can make almost every student a distinct type. Grouping priorities into cohorts avoids that, but changes the mechanism the theorem studies.

So this setting suggests a possible continuous mechanism-design project, but it does not yield an honest prover question anchored in this paper’s computational results. The missing anchor is the central weakness, and it cannot be repaired by treating Theorem 1’s mechanism guarantees as a complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has identified the strongest negative ground: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves strategy-proofness and constrained Pareto efficiency for SDPC; Proposition 1 gives a counterexample for ordinary serial dictatorship. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The fact that SDPC is described as an algorithm does not supply that missing result: the paper makes no complexity claim about computing its outcome.

The course-assignment example is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not reject it on population grounds. But it does not rescue the theorem as a computational mirror. SDPC depends on a strict order of individual turns. Include each worker’s position in their type and the support grows with the population; merge workers into cohorts and the strict order, and thus the stated mechanism, disappears. A continuum of priority positions can define a new model, but an individual then has zero mass, making unilateral strategy-proofness vacuous. Cohort-level manipulation or a fractional allocation rule could be interesting, but would be a newly specified mechanism and question.

One could also study finding a constrained Pareto-efficient allocation for a distribution of worker types, with project quorums and capacities. That is the strongest alternative: Pareto efficiency can remain meaningful for masses, and the matching setting admits plausible large cohorts. But it is a computational problem inspired by the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper establishes. The paper gives no complexity anchor or canonical selection among its many efficient allocations.

So the negative case is strong against a *qualifying mirror of this paper’s results*. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous computational project could arise from the model: the mass-allocation question remains plausible. The honest objection is that making that project worthwhile would require formulating and justifying a new computational target, rather than continuizing the paper’s contribution.
