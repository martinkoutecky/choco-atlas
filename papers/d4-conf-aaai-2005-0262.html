The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot honestly give an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it has no numbered computational-complexity result. Propositions 1–5 concern bidding and mechanism properties; Proposition 6 gives an expected-closing-price formula; Proposition 7 proves that the closing price tends to \(v\) in probability. They are proved here, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifiable by complexity. The learning simulations report seller profit and auction attendance, but those findings are unnumbered empirical results. Proposition 7 is the closest to a population limit, but it is not a computational anchor.

The strongest positive lead is a new computational follow-up to the learning experiments, rather than a mirror anchored in a named result. Consider a large online marketplace that repeatedly sells equivalent single-unit items through FPSB, Vickrey, and PSA auctions. Many bidders share the same valuation law \(F\), risk preferences, learning parameters, and initial Q-table. Represent each cohort of bidders with the same learned state as a type; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population in each type at period \(t\). Each period, bidders draw valuations as in the paper, choose an auction using its \(\varepsilon\)-greedy rule, receive rewards under that auction’s rules, and update their Q-values. The decision is the PSA parameter pair \((M,P)\). Given rational initial type masses, \(F\), the learning and auction parameters, and a horizon \(H\), ask for an additive-\(\delta\)-optimal \((M,P)\) maximizing the PSA seller’s expected cumulative profit over \(H\) periods. A solution gives the parameters and the resulting expected profit and attendance trajectory.

This scenario has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: perhaps \(10^4\)–\(10^6\) bidders but only a moderate number of learner-state cohorts, with bidder identity irrelevant once the full Q-table and other relevant parameters are fixed. It targets the paper’s central experimental question—whether a seller has an incentive to offer PSA—and the paper already studies repeated, competing auctions with learning bidders. For a fixed horizon and a finite, controlled set of learner states, I would expect approximate computation to be the first tractability target. Long-run behavior and parameter optimization could prove harder, but the paper supplies no result that settles either question.

The weakest point is that this is an extension, not a result the authors already state as a computational problem. Their Q-values are learned individually and can proliferate into many distinct states. More seriously, each auction sells one indivisible item: its outcome depends on the top bids, and a continuum-agent limit can change both winning probabilities and per-bidder rewards. The mass model would need to preserve those finite-auction effects well enough for the Q-updates to remain meaningful. Also, the paper’s distribution \(F\) describes uncertainty over valuations; it does not itself make the bidder population continuous. So the large-cohort scenario is credible, but it does not repair the missing anchor: on the paper’s named results, the honest verdict is that there is no result to mirror under the required standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case has no anchor in the paper’s named results. Propositions 1–5 concern auction properties, Proposition 6 gives a price formula, and Proposition 7 is an asymptotic price result. None is a computational complexity claim. The most promising proposal is therefore a follow-up: optimize \(M\) and \(P\) for expected seller profit in the repeated learning market. That question is recognizable—the authors themselves call for guidelines for choosing PSA parameters—but it is not a continuous mirror of a named result.

The strongest version of that follow-up would represent bidders by their full learner state, including their Q tables, and track the distribution of those states over time. Its central difficulty is that the auction sells one indivisible item. Revenue depends on the top bids; a bidder’s learning reward depends on whether that particular bidder wins. In an atomless population, the top valuation may be a supremum no individual attains, and any winner has zero mass. For a finite population of \(N\) bidders, a given bidder’s chance of winning is typically \(O(1/N)\); in PSA, the rebate per qualified bidder is also \(O(1/N)\). In the exact continuum limit, ordinary bidders receive zero reward, so the Q-learning feedback that is supposed to shift attendance disappears.

The aggregate prices do survive, but in a way that removes much of the paper’s central comparison. With positive mass attending each auction, the highest or second-highest bid approaches the upper valuation bound as \(N\) grows; Proposition 7 establishes this for PSA. The other auctions’ prices approach that bound too. So the price variability vanishes, and the attendance-driven case that PSA can attract enough bidders to recoup the shared profit is lost at leading order. With no modeled auxiliary revenue, sharing then simply reduces the seller’s payoff relative to keeping the full price.

One could retain finite-\(N\) order-statistic corrections, rescale rewards or time, or give bidders a positive share of multiple items. Those choices may support worthwhile research, but they preserve the finite-population effects, alter the learning model, or change the single-item auction. They do not rescue a clean population-continuum mirror of the paper’s result.

This is the strongest negative case, but it is not airtight. The authors’ explicit call for parameter-selection guidelines makes a computational follow-up credible, and one could build a carefully scaled model that keeps some learning signal. I would argue against treating this paper as a strong continuous-mirror candidate; I would not claim to have shown that no worthwhile continuization can be devised.
