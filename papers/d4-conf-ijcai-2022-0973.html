The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item types, a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita item supplies \(q_j\), and a rational-parameter MVNN \(v_t\) for each type, compute a welfare-maximizing mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\): maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j\), and \(x_{t,S}\ge0\). Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle; ask for the exact computational complexity and algorithms as functions of \(m\), \(\tau\), network size, and input bit length.

The model it lives in

A large course-allocation system with repeated complete valuation types and seat supplies scaled with enrollment. The decision variables \(x_{t,S}\) split each type’s mass across integral course bundles; the objective is welfare per capita under per-capita seat constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s MVNN-based winner-determination problem; it leaves Theorem 1’s universality result, the prediction experiments, and MLCA’s elicitation, incentive, and payment results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP be optimized efficiently from compact MVNN inputs, in particular by solving the pricing problem \(\max_{S\subseteq[m]}(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_j)\)?
  • Does hardness of finite winner determination transfer to this mass-assignment problem, or does fractional splitting remove the relevant integrality?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s winner determination problem, not its learning pipeline. The paper gives no numbered NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. My single computational anchor is Theorem 2 (MILP), proved here: for finitely many bidders with fixed MVNN valuations \(N_i\), the problem \(\max_{a\in\mathcal F}\sum_i N_i(a_i)\) is exactly equivalent to a mixed-integer linear program. It uses allocation variables, hidden-layer outputs and auxiliary continuous variables, plus two binary indicators for each bReLU neuron; linear constraints encode the bReLU cutoffs, and the objective maximizes the sum of network outputs. This theorem establishes an exact formulation, not a polynomial-time algorithm. The paper actually solves these formulations inside its MVNN-based MLCA experiments and reports their runtimes, so it qualifies as an anchor under the stated rule.

Here is the lead mirror: Continuous-Mass MVNN Winner Determination. A plausible setting is a large course-allocation system. Students in the same curricular cohort use the same value function over course bundles; the course-seat supply scales with the student population. A regime might have tens of thousands of students but only dozens or hundreds of distinct valuation types. This is a scenario to investigate, not a claim about every university: types must be identical in the complete sense relevant to the problem. The paper itself identifies course allocation as a combinatorial-assignment application, and its monotonicity assumption fits settings where students can drop unwanted courses.

Formally, the input has \(m\) course types, a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita seat supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\). Each type’s value function \(v_t:\{0,1\}^m\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\) is supplied as an MVNN with rational parameters. The question is to find an optimal mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\):

\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq [m]} x_{t,S}v_t(S) \]

subject to

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq [m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\leq q_j \quad(j\in[m]),\qquad x_{t,S}\geq 0. \]

Here \(x_{t,S}\) is the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(S\). Every student still receives an integral bundle; fractional values of \(x\) mean that a type’s mass is split across bundles. For a finite population of size \(n\), the corresponding capacities are \(nq_j\) seats. The objective is welfare per capita. The mirror changes the population representation and scales supply with it, while keeping the underlying task—choosing bundles to maximize total value under item capacities—recognizable as the paper’s WDP.

The key computational question is whether this LP has an efficient algorithm when the valuations are given compactly as MVNNs. It has up to \(\tau 2^m\) bundle variables. Column generation would need a pricing step of the form \(\max_{S\subseteq[m]}(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_j)\) for each type and item-price vector \(\lambda\). Theorem 2 makes the connection concrete: it shows that the paper’s MVNN structure supports exact mixed-integer optimization of the underlying bundle-allocation problem. In the continuous mirror, that structure may also support practical pricing, but the theorem does not give a polynomial-time bound for it.

I would expect hardness to persist, probably in Class B, because the combinatorics remain in the bundle dimension \(m\), rather than in population size. That is a conjectural direction, not a result of this paper: mass splitting can remove integrality that a discrete-winner-determination reduction may rely on. A prover should first settle whether pricing for compact MVNNs is polynomial, and whether any discrete hardness reduction survives this relaxation. If pricing is hard, useful next questions are whether it is fixed-parameter tractable in network width or \(m\), and what additive rounding guarantee relates the continuous optimum to a finite population.

My mirror covers Theorem 2’s MVNN-based WDP. It does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s prediction experiments, MLCA incentives or payments. Its weakest point is the regime: many auction bidders are heterogeneous and relatively few, and even students in a cohort may differ in their values over bundles. The scenario is convincing only where repeated, complete valuation templates are a reasonable model. The paper’s course-allocation motivation gives that scenario a foothold; exact type repetition is still the central assumption a skeptical author or referee is likely to test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 2 does not establish a complexity classification or a polynomial-time algorithm. It gives an exact MILP for a finite-bidder winner determination problem. In the continuous-mass version, students of a given type can be split across bundles, producing a configuration LP with columns \((t,S)\). Its pricing problem is \(\max_{S\subseteq[m]}(v_t(S)-\lambda\cdot\mathbf 1_S)\), where \(\lambda\) contains item prices. That is a different optimization task from the paper’s allocation MILP, and the paper does not analyze its complexity.

But this objection does not defeat the better mirror. Theorem 2’s encoding can be adapted to that pricing objective by adding the item-price terms to the objective. A large course-allocation system with a modest number of repeated valuation types and seat supplies that scale with enrollment is recognizable as the paper’s welfare-maximization problem. Fractional mass means students of one type may receive different integral bundles; it does not make an individual student’s bundle fractional. The paper itself names course allocation, and the stated model makes this a plausible regime. The resulting pricing question is open in the text, and a dull answer would still count as a useful result under the programme’s standard.

The paper’s experiments do not establish that this regime occurs: its spectrum-auction bidders are few and heterogeneous, and the paper trains bidder-specific value models. That weakens the empirical motivation for this mirror in its main application. It does not rule out the course-allocation case, or a population of agents sharing a small set of complete valuation types. I cannot honestly argue that no such scenario is sensible.

So the negative case is weak. Theorem 2 is not itself a complexity result, and the mirror changes the optimization problem from finite-bidder allocation to mass assignment with a pricing oracle. Those points narrow the connection, but they do not eliminate it. The proponent’s anchor survives.
