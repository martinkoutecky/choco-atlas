What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; the main theorems concern axiom implications and equivalences, while the applications restate existence and representation results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is the Aumann exchange economy in §4.2. A plausible high-multiplicity regime would group consumers with identical preferences and endowments into types \(t\), with \(\mu_t\) representing each type’s share of the population. The associated computational question might be: given the type data, find a price vector and type-level allocations that clear the market and give each type a most-preferred affordable bundle.

But that is only a possible new question inspired by the paper. The paper’s unnumbered “Aumann’s Existence Theorem” says that a continuum-consumer exchange economy has a competitive equilibrium if aggregate endowment is positive and consumers have continuous, strongly monotonic preferences satisfying the stated measurability condition. It establishes existence, not an algorithm or a complexity result; the paper’s contribution there is to show how its continuity–solvability results let one replace the continuity assumption.

Under your anchor rule, there are no qualifying anchors. Theorem 1 gives implications among continuity and solvability properties; Theorems 2 and 3 give equivalences under additional assumptions; Theorem 4 gives partial equivalences in infinite-dimensional spaces. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper’s other named results are representation or existence claims, not computational results. So there is no per-anchor continuous problem I can responsibly present as a mirror of a result in this paper.

The Aumann setting makes the population-continuum interpretation plausible, but it does not bridge that gap. The paper studies continuity of preferences over choice spaces; it does not compute with a distribution of consumer types. Turning the proposed equilibrium question into a computational one would also require finite encodings of preferences and endowments that the paper does not specify. That is the weakest point, and it is decisive for the requested case: the foothold is a research direction, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives us no computational result to mirror. The numbered theorems classify implications and equivalences among axioms for a single binary preference relation. They specify no computational problem, encoded input, algorithm, or complexity bound. The applications restate representation and existence results. That leaves no qualifying anchor under the programme’s standard.

The mismatch runs deeper than the absence of an algorithm. The paper’s object is one agent’s preference over a choice space; its coordinates describe choice dimensions, not agents or voter types. Replacing them with type shares would change what the theorems ask. Putting a distribution over preference relations could produce a question about the share of agents whose preferences satisfy an axiom, but that would be a new population statistic, not a continuized version of the paper’s equivalences.

The Aumann exchange economy is the best nearby idea, but the proposed task—compute an equilibrium from finitely many preference and endowment types—is a possible follow-on to Aumann’s existence theorem, not a mirror of this paper’s contribution. The paper uses that theorem to illustrate how continuity assumptions can be replaced; it contributes no result about computing equilibria. And Aumann’s model already has a continuum of consumers, so making the population continuous is not the new step in that proposed task. It might be worthwhile market-computation research, but the paper supplies neither the computational question nor a result for it to continue.

I cannot rule out useful research inspired by the paper. The negative case is narrower: none of its named results provides a computational anchor, and turning either its preference axioms or its Aumann application into a population-computation problem requires supplying a different central question.
