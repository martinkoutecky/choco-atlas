The continuous mirror question

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s classical sample-access lower bound for binary majority, and its application to rules that reduce to majority with two candidates. It leaves aside the quantum algorithm and experiments, and does not claim query bounds for larger supports.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 3, proved in this paper from the majority lower bound in Lemma 3. For fixed \(m\ge2\) and \(\epsilon\in(0,1/2]\), it says that if a voting rule reduces to majority when there are two candidates, then any algorithm whose only information about the profile comes from sampling votes with replacement needs worst-case expected runtime \(\Omega\!\left(n^2(1/2-\epsilon)^2/\mathrm{MoV}^2\right)\) and expected space \(\Omega\!\left(\log(n^2(1/2-\epsilon)^2/\mathrm{MoV}^2)\right)\) to return the correct winner with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\). The theorem covers, among others, positional scoring rules, STV, Copeland, and maximin. This is a sampling access lower bound, rather than an NP-hardness result.

A continuous question that mirrors it is Continuous Sample-Access Winner Identification. Fix two candidates \(a,b\), with voter types \(\rho_a=(a\succ b)\) and \(\rho_b=(b\succ a)\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with rational masses \(\mu(\rho_a)=1/2+\Delta\) and \(\mu(\rho_b)=1/2-\Delta\), or the reverse, for known \(\Delta>0\). Its winner is the majority type. The algorithm gets independent samples from \(\mu\), but not its masses, and must output the winner with error at most \(\epsilon\). The question is to determine the least worst-case expected number of samples and the required workspace as functions of \(\Delta\) and \(\epsilon\). Here \(\Delta\) is the fraction of the population that must switch types to reach a tie, so it is the continuous counterpart of \(\mathrm{MoV}/n\).

This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large election or crowd-ranking population has \(N\) people but only two distinct ballots, so \(N\) can grow while \(\tau=2\). For even \(N\) with \(N\Delta\) integral, replicating the distribution gives \(n=N\) and \(\mathrm{MoV}=N\Delta\). Theorem 3 therefore yields a continuous-query lower bound of \(\Omega((1/2-\epsilon)^2/\Delta^2)\) samples and \(\Omega(\log((1/2-\epsilon)^2/\Delta^2))\) space. At fixed confidence, this is \(\Omega(\Delta^{-2})\) samples; ordinary sampling gives a matching order for this binary problem. The expected direction is therefore hardness that persists under replication—a Class B-like result in query complexity, not a claim of NP-hardness. The paper’s two-candidate reduction also explains why the lower bound applies to the other named rules covered by Theorem 3.

The mirror covers only the classical sample-access lower bound for winner identification. Further questions include the dependence on support size \(\tau\) for general rules and the corresponding query complexity when the population is available through coherent quantum access.

The weak point is the access model. ChoCo’s default representation lists the support and its rational masses; given that list, this two-type winner is immediate to compute. The mirror instead preserves the paper’s explicit sample-only interface, which is central to its sublinear-time question. Because the paper already studies algorithms with exactly that restriction, I think its authors would recognize the problem; a referee who requires the default explicit-mass input could reasonably regard the proposed mirror as too narrow.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the access model. Theorem 3 is a real computational result, and its two-candidate majority case has a natural high-multiplicity interpretation: two ballot types can carry masses \(1/2+\Delta\) and \(1/2-\Delta\). I can’t object that there is no named result or no plausible population regime.

But ChoCo’s stated input is the support and its masses. Given those exact masses, the winner is obtained by comparing them; the \(\Omega(\Delta^{-2})\) sampling cost disappears. Theorem 3 gets its force from restricting the algorithm to sampling, so the proposed mirror must hide the continuous society behind a sample oracle. That makes the problem one of learning the sign of an unknown Bernoulli bias, rather than computing on a society supplied as a distribution. This is the clearest reason to exclude it from the programme’s core mirrors.

That objection is not decisive. Sample access is central to this paper, and its authors could reasonably recognize the oracle version as their problem. The two-type population is also a convincing high-multiplicity scenario. So although I can argue that the mirror falls outside ChoCo’s explicit-mass input model, I can’t honestly defeat it in every reasonable interpretation. The negative case against this anchor is weak; under a broader scope that includes query complexity, it likely survives.
