The continuous mirror question

Given rational finite-state data \(p^0\), \(P\), and \(\delta\), role masses \(\mu_q\), coalition masses \(0\leq\lambda_q\leq\mu_q\), per-member reward vectors \(K_q\), and risk coefficients \(b_q\), where \(P\) and \(K_q\) are induced by a supplied stationary policy, compute the exact mean and variance of \(Z_\lambda=\sum_q\lambda_q\xi_q\) and the value \(\max_{q:\lambda_q>0}\bigl(E[Z_\lambda]+b_q\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_\lambda)}\bigr)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-state Markov environment with a continuum of members in \(\tau\) standardized roles, population masses \(\mu_q\), and coalition masses \(\lambda_q\). Under a supplied stationary policy, the induced transition matrix \(P\) and role reward vectors \(K_q\) determine the population-normalized coalition payoff; the task is to compute its moments and maximum type-specific mean-variance utility.

The objection that survived

The supplied \(P\) and \(K_q\) freeze the strategic response, so changing \(\lambda\) does not model how population-level actions change the game; this confines the mirror to fixed-policy moment evaluation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2’s coalition moment computation for role masses under a fixed stationary policy, with an immediate polynomial-time answer from the paper’s formulas and rational matrix inversion. It leaves the maxmin search over strategies, characteristic-function computation, and core outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can coalition maxmin values and core membership be computed when stationary policies are optimized over role-level actions and transition and reward data depend on aggregate actions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: mass-based coalition moment evaluation for a fixed stationary policy. The paper has no named NP-hardness, tractability, or parameterized-complexity result. Its best anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper in Appendix A.2: for any fixed stationary strategy profile, the expected payoff of a coalition is \(p^0\hat P\sum_{i\in C}K_i\), where \(\hat P=(I-\delta P)^{-1}\); its variance is the sum of the members’ payoff variances plus twice the sum of their pairwise covariances; and each covariance is given by the paper’s formula using \(\tilde P=(I-\delta^2P)^{-1}\), \(\hat P\), and the two players’ payoff vectors. This is a named computation result, though not a complexity classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large cooperative risk pool in a shared Markov environment. Members fall into \(\tau\) standardized roles—same action sets, reward schedule, risk coefficient, and other parameters within each role—with \(\tau\) small compared with the number of members. Let \(\mu_q\) be role \(q\)’s population fraction. For a fixed stationary policy, let \(\lambda_q\leq\mu_q\) be the fraction in a coalition, and let \(K_q\) be role \(q\)’s per-member stage-reward vector. The coalition’s population-normalized random payoff is \(Z_\lambda=\sum_q\lambda_q\xi_q\). This normalization keeps the payoff finite as the pool grows; it is the high-multiplicity counterpart of dividing a finite coalition’s total payoff by population size.

Lead question — Continuous Coalition Moment Evaluation. Given rational \(p^0\), \(P\), \(\delta\), role masses \(\mu_q\), coalition masses \(\lambda_q\), reward vectors \(K_q\), and risk coefficients \(b_q\), compute the exact mean and variance of \(Z_\lambda\), and the coalition’s maximum type-specific mean-variance utility. A solution is the exact pair \((E_\lambda,V_\lambda)\) and the value \(\max_{q:\lambda_q>0}(E_\lambda+b_q\sqrt{V_\lambda})\). With \(K_\lambda=\sum_q\lambda_qK_q\), the moments follow directly from the paper’s formulas:
\[ E_\lambda=p^0\hat P K_\lambda,\qquad V_\lambda=\bigl((p^0\tilde P)\odot K_\lambda^\top\bigr)(2\hat P-I)K_\lambda-E_\lambda^2. \]
For rational inputs these moments can be computed in polynomial time in the number of states, \(\tau\), and the input bit length, using rational matrix inversion. I expect this fixed-policy evaluation problem to be tractable.

The mirror preserves the paper’s discounted stochastic payoff, coalition aggregation, risk sensitivity, and stationary-policy setting, so the authors could plausibly recognize it as their coalition-payoff calculation extended to a population with repeated role types. It covers Proposition 2’s moment computation only. It does not mirror the paper’s maxmin search over coalition and opposing-coalition strategies, or its core.

The weak point is that distinction: Proposition 2 evaluates a specified strategy profile; it does not establish that optimizing over strategies or computing a continuous core is tractable. Those are natural further questions, but I cannot anchor them in a named computational result here. So this supports a real, modest continuous mirror—not a complexity-landscape claim, and not evidence that any discrete hardness dissolves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the proponent’s only named anchor, but it is a formula for the moments of a coalition payoff under a fixed stationary strategy profile. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper gives no complexity result for finding a strategy profile, evaluating a core, or deciding core membership. Those are the computations a more substantial mirror would need to address.

The proposed “Continuous Coalition Moment Evaluation” also supplies the induced transition matrix \(P\) and the role payoff vectors \(K_q\) as inputs. Its population masses do not determine how actions change either one; they only form the weighted reward vector \(K_\lambda=\sum_q\lambda_qK_q\). So it has already removed the strategic interaction that makes this paper a stochastic game. The resulting moment calculation is a useful extension of Proposition 2 to compressed, normalized populations, but it is not a complexity result about the game the authors study.

The stronger candidate would continuize the coalition value \(v(C)\) or the core. There the population would affect the coalition being valued. But in this paper the transition probabilities and payoffs may depend arbitrarily on the full action profile. A support of \(\tau\) player types and their masses does not determine the game’s response to role-level actions unless one adds an anonymous or aggregate-action structure. That structure would make the problem a risk-sensitive mean-field stochastic game. The paper cites mean-field work, but it does not study the computational problems for that model. Moreover, preserving its coalition utility means taking a maximum over members’ utilities: an arbitrarily small positive mass of a type can affect coalition worth, while zero mass removes it. Replacing that maximum with an average might smooth the mass dependence, but would change the characteristic function being mirrored.

There is also a scope problem for ChoCo: the paper has no voters, preferences over candidates, collective outcome, or social choice rule. Its object is a stochastic game among strategic players, followed by a cooperative allocation of stochastic payoffs. A continuum of such players could be worthwhile research, but that alone does not make it a continuous computational-social-choice mirror.

I would not claim that no plausible continuous question exists. A large risk pool with repeated roles and common macroeconomic shocks is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the fixed-policy moment calculation is coherent there. The negative case is strongest as a rejection of this paper as a ChoCo complexity anchor; the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weak.
