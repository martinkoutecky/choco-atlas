What the mirror covers

The paper presents a protocol for secure winner computation under score-based rules and reports protocol costs; neither is stated as a qualifying numbered computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

A continuous population is plausible here: in a national election, many voters may share a relatively small set of ballot types. A type could be a complete score vector, with its mass giving the fraction of voters who cast it; the task would be to identify the top \(K\) candidates while keeping the masses and aggregate scores private.

But the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result for that task. It presents a secure protocol and reports its costs; the comparison circuit’s result is cited as machinery from Nishide and Ohta, without a numbered result in this paper. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I can’t give the required per-anchor continuous problem or predicted complexity class without inventing one. The mass tally is a plausible research direction, but treating it as an anchor would also risk reducing the paper’s central privacy contribution to ordinary score aggregation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “case for” is actually a case against a mirror: it names no result or continuous question. So there is no anchor to defeat in the terms requested. The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that the paper’s contribution is a secure protocol and an empirical cost analysis, not a numbered complexity result about a computational social choice problem. Its winner computation uses score aggregation and an MPC comparison circuit; the comparison machinery is cited from prior work.

The best candidate mirror would be secure top-\(K\) selection from a secret-shared distribution \(\mu\) over ballot types, perhaps with private validation of the ballots. But that changes the protocol’s input model. If the masses are supplied directly, the task no longer includes the paper’s voter-level concerns: authenticating eligible voters, enforcing one ballot per voter, and validating each submitted ballot while hiding it. If voters still submit individual shares so those guarantees remain, the protocol still processes \(N\) submissions; describing their aggregate as \(\mu\) does not itself turn the paper’s result into a high-multiplicity complexity result. A type-level version could be a sensible cryptographic extension, but its central question would be secure computation over weighted inputs, rather than the complexity landscape of a social-choice problem that this programme targets.

That is a scope argument, not proof that no useful continuous formulation exists. A national election with many repeated ballots is an entirely plausible setting for private weighted score tallying. If that application alone counts as a worthwhile mirror, the negative case is weak: the paper gives enough protocol machinery to motivate the question, even though it supplies no qualifying named computational result to anchor it.
