The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible named computational anchor. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition, and it proves no complexity classification. Its closest hook is the unnumbered analysis of the two retailers’ contribution game: Section 3 derives the symmetric first-order conditions for uniform demands and examines the resulting candidates. That is an analytic equilibrium calculation, not a result about computational complexity. The paper’s continuous demand distributions also do not make its population continuous: it still models two strategic retailers.

The strongest positive direction I can offer is therefore a proposed extension, not a mirror of a qualifying named result. Call it *Continuum Pooled-Inventory Equilibrium*. Let \(T\) be a finite set of retailer types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), unit revenue \(r_t\), unit cost \(c_t\), and a finite-support demand distribution \(F_t\), all specified with rational data. Each retailer of type \(t\) contributes \(q_t\) units. A plausible regime is a large pool of independent franchise stores, where many stores share the same format, demand pattern, and economics: the number of stores \(n\) is large relative to the number of types \(\tau=|T|\).

Here is a precise version of the game. In the continuum limit, type \(t\)’s realized demand is distributed according to \(F_t\) across its mass. For a contribution vector \(q\), let
\[ S(q)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbb E_{F_t}[(q_t-X)_+], \qquad D(q)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbb E_{F_t}[(X-q_t)_+]. \]
Define \(\lambda(q)=\min\{1,S(q)/D(q)\}\) when \(D(q)>0\), and \(\lambda(q)=1\) when \(D(q)=0\). A retailer facing demand \(x\) and contribution \(z\) receives
\[ a(x,z,\lambda)=\min\{x,z\}+\lambda(x-z)_+. \]
Thus retailers whose contributions exceed their demand keep what they need, and their unused stock covers the same fraction of each shortfall. With two retailers this reproduces the paper’s allocation rule; with many retailers it supplies a natural extension. Each type’s per-retailer objective is expected profit,
\[ u_t(z;\lambda)=r_t\,\mathbb E_{F_t}[a(X,z,\lambda)]-c_tz. \]
The question is: given the rational type data, does a pure type-symmetric nonatomic Nash equilibrium exist, and if so, return an exact real-algebraic vector \((q,\lambda)\) satisfying the aggregate definition of \(\lambda(q)\) and
\[ q_t\in\arg\max_{0\le z\le M_t}u_t(z;\lambda(q)), \]
where \(M_t\) is the largest demand in \(F_t\). In a unilateral deviation, one retailer has zero mass and therefore takes the aggregate coverage rate \(\lambda\) as fixed.

I would provisionally expect this candidate to be tractable: for a fixed \(\lambda\), each type has a one-dimensional newsvendor-style best response, and finite-support demands give finitely many breakpoints. Whether the resulting consistency conditions can be solved in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the total demand-support size is the computational question; the paper gives no answer to it. Natural follow-ups are the convergence rate from finite retailer games to this limit and uniqueness of the limiting equilibrium.

The soft spot is substantial. In the paper, each of two retailers can affect the pool and hence the other retailer’s allocation. In this continuum version, an individual outlet cannot change aggregate scarcity, and sharing surplus across many shortfall retailers requires an extension the paper does not specify. The authors might recognize it as a natural large-market limit of their contract, especially since it recovers their two-retailer rule, but it is not their proved problem. So this is the strongest plausible positive direction I see, not a per-anchor case: under the requested anchor rule, there are zero anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s object: it proves no named computational result. Its uniform-demand section derives a symmetric equilibrium by solving first-order conditions; there is no complexity classification or stated algorithmic problem to continuize. The paper already uses demand distributions \(F_i\), but that is uncertainty over demand, not a continuous population. Its strategic players are two retailers who each choose a contribution and internalize how pooling affects the other. So the proposed “Continuum Pooled-Inventory Equilibrium” is an extension, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The franchise-store scenario is plausible, but the proposed model changes the game’s defining feature. Each individual store becomes nonatomic and takes the aggregate coverage rate \(\lambda\) as fixed. The allocation rule also changes: \(\lambda\) is a ratio of expected surpluses and shortfalls, whereas the paper allocates inventory according to each realized pair of demands. For example, let \(q_1=q_2=1\), with independent demands \(X_1\in\{0,1\}\) equally likely and \(X_2\in\{1,2\}\) equally likely. The proposed formula gives \(\lambda=1\). When \(X_1=1\) and \(X_2=2\), it allocates \(2\) to retailer 2; the paper’s rule allocates \(1\), since both demands exceed their contributions. The formula can make sense in a large market where aggregate demand converges to its expectation, but then it defines a new, many-retailer rationing contract. It does not reproduce the paper’s two-retailer game.

The more faithful alternatives do not create a population-computation question. Keep the two retailers as the strategic parties, even if each represents many outlets, and the population is bundled into their demand distributions; there are still two players choosing contributions. Make the outlets themselves the nonatomic players, and the individual strategic effect that drives the paper’s analysis disappears. In the proposed model, fixing \(\lambda\) leaves each type with a one-dimensional newsvendor choice, coupled to other types through a single aggregate rate. That may be a worthwhile supply-chain game to study, but it is not the paper’s computational result continued to a population limit.

The proposed convergence and uniqueness follow-ups underscore the mismatch: they are questions about a large-market game’s analytic behavior, while this paper supplies neither a computational-complexity question nor a social-choice problem for the programme to classify. I would not count this as a worthwhile continuous mirror of the paper.

The negative case is strongest as a claim about *this paper*, not as the universal claim that no related large-market problem could be worth studying. A many-store pooling model may be a sensible independent project in algorithmic supply-chain economics. Its plausibility does not supply the missing anchor, and it does not make the proposed model a mirror of the paper’s two-party game.
