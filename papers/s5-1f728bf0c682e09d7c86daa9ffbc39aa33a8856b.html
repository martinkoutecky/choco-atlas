The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) and a valid ranking pattern \(\sigma\) on all \(A\subseteq[m]\) with \(|A|\ge2\), find a finite-support distribution of binary-rational masses \(\mu_\pi\ge0\) over complete rankings \(\pi\in\Pi_m\), summing to \(1\), such that for every \(A\subseteq[m]\) and distinct \(i,j\in A\), \(\sigma(A,i)<\sigma(A,j)\) if and only if \(\alpha_i^\mu(A)>\alpha_j^\mu(A)\), and \(\sigma(A,i)=\sigma(A,j)\) if and only if \(\alpha_i^\mu(A)=\alpha_j^\mu(A)\), where \(\alpha_i^\mu(A)=\sum_{\pi:\,i\text{ is ranked first in }A\text{ by }\pi}\mu_\pi\).

The model it lives in

A society of total mass \(1\) over complete rankings of the candidates; the decision variables are type masses \(\mu_\pi\), and the task is to construct a distribution whose induced choice-probability comparisons realize the input ranking pattern.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no bound on the time, support size, or bit length needed to find a witness, so it does not establish an efficient continuous algorithm.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers realization for strict and weak ranking patterns through Theorem 2, with Theorem 5 giving an explicit witness for strict patterns. The electorate-size results in Theorems 3 and 4 are not themselves captured as continuous objectives.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a witness be found in time polynomial in the input and output encoding sizes?
  • How small can the support and mass bit lengths be while preserving all strict comparisons?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result in the required sense: none of its theorems establishes membership in P, hardness, FPT, or a related classification. I would not present its existence theorems as complexity results. The strongest constructive anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: for every \(m\) and every ranking pattern \(\sigma\), strict or weak, there is a finite voting situation \(N^{(m)}\) that is \(q\)-concordant with \(\sigma\). In full, this means that for every candidate set \(A\) and distinct \(i,j\in A\), \(\sigma(A,i)<\sigma(A,j)\) exactly when \(i\) gets more votes than \(j\) in the election on \(A\), and \(\sigma(A,i)=\sigma(A,j)\) exactly when their vote counts are equal.

The direct continuous mirror is Pattern Realization\(_\infty\). Its input is \(m\) and a valid ranking function \(\sigma(A,\cdot)\) for each \(A\subseteq[m]\) with \(|A|\ge2\). A type is a complete ranking \(\pi\in\Pi_m\); its mass \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of society with that ranking. Find a finite support list of binary-rational masses satisfying \(\mu_\pi\ge0\) and \(\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_m}\mu_\pi=1\), such that, writing
\[ \alpha_i^\mu(A)=\sum_{\pi:\,i\text{ is ranked first in }A\text{ by }\pi}\mu_\pi, \]
we have \(\sigma(A,i)<\sigma(A,j)\) exactly when \(\alpha_i^\mu(A)>\alpha_j^\mu(A)\), and ties in \(\sigma\) exactly when the corresponding masses are equal. This is a feasibility and witness-construction problem, so it has no cost objective. Equivalently, strict comparisons can be required to have a common positive margin \(\delta\), while tied comparisons are equalities.

Theorem 2 gives this problem a strong answer: every valid input has a witness. For strict patterns, Theorem 5, also proved here, makes the construction quantitative. Set \(z_m=17mm!\) and
\[ \bar\theta_m=\prod_{h=2}^{m}\left(hz_m^{h-1}-\frac{h(h-1)}2\right). \]
For each \(\pi=(j_1,\ldots,j_m)\), the theorem defines
\[ N^{(m)}(\pi)=\prod_{r=1}^{m-1} \left[ z_m^{m-r} -\sigma\bigl([m]\setminus\{j_1,\ldots,j_{r-1}\},j_r\bigr)+1 \right]. \]
These integer counts sum to \(\bar\theta_m\) and form a voting situation \(q\)-concordant with \(\sigma\). Thus \(\mu_\pi=N^{(m)}(\pi)/\bar\theta_m\) is a continuous-population witness. Theorem 5’s proof is here; it uses the quantitative load-sharing result recalled as Theorem 7 from [5].

The regime is plausible for a large electorate whose members repeatedly share complete rankings over the same candidate pool—for example, an organization examining many possible slates. The type is the whole ranking, so agents of one type are indistinguishable for the elections at issue. In a large electorate, the number of voters can greatly exceed the number of distinct rankings \(\tau\), even though the universal construction may use all \(m!\) rankings. Fractions of a population receiving each ranking are also a natural way to describe such a profile. The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves the same candidate subsets, individual preferences, and slate-by-slate vote comparisons.

For the trichotomy, I would call the bare realization question solved constructively: every instance is feasible. Theorem 5 gives an explicit witness for strict patterns; Theorem 2 gives an inductive construction for weak ones. That supports a Class A expectation at the level of finding a witness, but the paper proves no running-time bound in \(m,\tau,L\). In particular, the strict construction can have \(m!\) positive types, and the paper does not show that a compact witness can be found efficiently. Useful follow-on questions are whether one can minimize support, control the bit length of the masses, or maximize the minimum margin \(\delta\). Those would make the computational challenge sharper, but they go beyond the paper’s stated existence result.

The weakest point is also the clearest limitation: the continuous feasibility answer is already implied by the paper’s discrete construction. Divide its integer counts by their total. Conversely, every finite binary-rational mass distribution can be scaled to integer counts using a common denominator. The paper also introduces the normalized distribution \(P_N=N/n\) to connect voting situations to probability models. That probability model does not itself make the population continuous—the target remains integer voter counts—but the mirror sits very close to a change of representation. So this is a credible population mirror, supported by explicit high-multiplicity constructions, but it does not uncover a new complexity landscape on its own.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with the missing computational target. Theorem 2 proves that every ranking pattern has a realizing voting situation; Theorem 5 gives an explicit construction for strict patterns. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 study possible electorate sizes. None states a complexity result for an algorithmic problem over a society given as input. So there is no named complexity result here to continuize.

Pattern Realization\(_\infty\) is the best repair: given \(\sigma\), find masses \(\mu_\pi\) that realize it. That is a legitimate search problem, but it makes the society the output. The paper’s population-size results also lose their quantity of interest under normalization: total mass is fixed at \(1\), while minimizing the denominator of rational masses is an arithmetic constraint, not a continuous population objective. Minimizing support or maximizing the margin between choice probabilities could supply a useful objective, but either would add a new problem the paper does not study.

Theorem 5 is the clearest test. Its formula supplies integer counts, which can be normalized to masses. That makes a continuous witness immediate, while leaving the real algorithmic question—whether one can find a compact witness efficiently—unanswered. Theorem 2’s inductive construction raises the same issue for weak patterns. A computational study of witness size or construction time could be worthwhile, but its contribution would be a new analysis of these constructions, rather than a continuous mirror of a complexity result in the paper.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. Large electorates with repeated complete rankings are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s voter types are exactly those rankings. Its probability model over permutations also makes the normalized formulation especially natural. Those facts defeat the strongest modeling objections. My negative case rests on the narrower point that the proposed mirror needs a new computational objective to become a substantive complexity question. If search for any witness alone counts as enough, this paper does have a plausible mirror and the case against it is weak.
