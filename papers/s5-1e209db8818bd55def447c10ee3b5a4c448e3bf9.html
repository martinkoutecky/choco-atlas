Theorem 3.6

The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) items, workforce scale \(N\), a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over per-comparison disutilities, \(\pi\), \(\delta\), the principal's utility and verification-cost parameters, and CrowdSort's fixed task allocation, choose a payment cutoff, verification count \(v\), and replication \(r\) to maximize expected principal utility subject to incentive compatibility and probability at least \(1-\delta\) of recovering the true ranking; output an optimal contract and its running time in \(n,\tau,L,\log N\).

The model it lives in

Workers have per-comparison disutility types \(\theta\) with masses \(\mu_\theta\). At workforce scale \(N\), use CrowdSort's fixed task allocation and choose the effort cutoff, payment, and audit and replication parameters to maximize expected principal utility subject to the paper's incentive and ranking-recovery guarantees.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.6 optimizes \(g\) for CrowdSort's prescribed task allocation; freely optimizing over bundle distributions \(Q\) would need a separate compressed representation preserving workload and audit independence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the heterogeneous-cost contract optimization and CrowdSort's workload, verification, and ranking-recovery guarantees, with its fixed task allocation. It leaves the experiments, model deviations, and future questions about collusion and unknown reliability outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-support cost-mass optimization preserve the finite-\(N\) audit probabilities and compute an optimal contract in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L,\log N\)?
  • Can a mass representation of CrowdSort's task allocation preserve its workload bounds and the independence used by its verification guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s contract design for a large, standardized crowd. Think of a search or preference-labeling platform with \(N\) workers but only \(\tau\ll N\) relevant worker types: a type specifies the worker’s per-comparison disutility \(\psi\), while the effort and error model is the one in the paper. Workers of a type are interchangeable for this task. Represent their shares by a rational distribution \(\mu\), with \(N\mu_\theta\) worker-equivalents of type \(\theta\). The items remain discrete; what becomes a mass distribution is the worker population.

The paper contains no named hardness result. Its anchors are algorithmic guarantees, so the question is whether those guarantees have a useful type-mass version—not whether a known NP-hardness result dissolves.

Lead — Type-Mass Optimal CrowdSort Contract. The anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper in Appendix C. In the paper’s heterogeneous-disutility model, the \(\psi_i\) are i.i.d. from an unknown distribution \(D\), and the principal has an estimate \(\widehat F\) within \(\epsilon\) in CDF distance. For a target of \(g\) effortful agents, the paper sets a quantile-based payment \(p_g\) and chooses the verification and replication parameters \(v_g,r_g\) as in Lemma 3.5. Theorem 3.6 proves that, assuming Cramer’s conjecture, each agent performs \(\tilde O(n^{3/2}/s)\) comparisons; the principal verifies \(O(\log(s/\delta))\) comparisons; CrowdSort returns the correct ranking with probability at least \(1-\delta\); and the utility-maximizing \(g^*\in\{0,\ldots,s\}\) can be computed in \(O(s)\) time.

The continuous question is: given \(n\) items, workforce scale \(N\), a rational finite-support cost distribution \(\mu\), the paper’s common reliability parameter \(\pi\), an estimated cost distribution \(\widehat F\) with error bound \(\epsilon\), and \(\delta\), choose a payment \(p\), a set \(V\) of disjoint verification pairs, and a distribution over worker task bundles \(Q\). Each worker assigned bundle \(Q\) receives the common verified set \(V\) and the unverified comparisons in \(Q\); payment is conditional on passing verification. The task is to maximize the paper’s expected principal utility, subject to the incentive constraints and the \(1-\delta\) correct-ranking guarantee. A solution specifies the payment, verification set, mass assigned to each bundle, and a reporting rule that produces the ranking. Cost types stay private: the contract is common, and the supplied distribution estimate is used to choose its payment.

For this finite-support mirror, the target effort mass is a cumulative mass at a cost cutoff, rather than an integer \(g\). I expect the cutoff optimization to take \(O(\tau)\) candidates. I also expect the task-allocation part to admit a polynomial-size mass formulation, making this a likely Class A problem. That classification is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of Theorem 3.6: it needs a compressed bundle representation that preserves workload limits and the independence properties used for verification.

Supporting anchor — Uniform-Cost Mass CrowdSort. Theorem 2.9, proved in Appendix B, gives the paper’s known-and-equal-disutility guarantee. With \(p^*=d\psi/(\widehat\pi\pi)\) and the paper’s \(v^*,r^*\) from equation (2), CrowdSort uses \(\tilde O(n^{3/2}/s)\) comparisons per agent, verifies \(O(\log(s/\delta))\) comparisons, and retrieves the correct ranking with probability at least \(1-\delta\), assuming Cramer’s conjecture. It also proves that if \(\delta<1/s^2\), \(\pi\) is bounded away from \(1\), and \(\bar\psi/\psi=\Omega(\sqrt n\log n)\), then the principal’s expected utility is at least what she would get by sorting the items herself.

Its continuous problem is the same contract-and-bundle design task in the regime \(\tau=1\): all workers have common disutility \(\psi\), and the input additionally includes \(\bar\psi,\lambda,\delta\). Find a contract and mass allocation that recovers the ranking with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and achieves the stated utility comparison when its hypotheses hold. This is a credible regime for a large pool of workers on a standardized task with a common qualification and pay scale. It is a narrower case than the lead, but it anchors the core ranking-and-incentivization problem directly.

For both questions, the natural expected direction is tractability through a compressed task allocation and optimization over type masses. The source theorems do not establish that continuous algorithm or its bit complexity; the question for a prover is whether the bundle allocation can be represented and solved in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), the mass encoding length, and the other parameters, without expanding the \(N\) worker-equivalents. There is no basis here for claiming hardness transfer or continuum-specific hardness.

The main weakness is the finite-population dependence of the guarantee. CrowdSort’s proof aims to identify every bad agent; with \(v\) disjoint verifications, each bad agent passes with probability \(2^{-v}\). In a literal atomless limit, a positive mass of bad workers remains undetected for every fixed \(v\), while the theorem’s \(O(\log(N/\delta))\) verification count grows with population scale. So the direct mirror should retain \(N\) as the finite worker-equivalent scale while compressing workers by type. A fixed-\(v\), exact-ranking model for a truly atomless crowd would need a different correctness argument. That limitation narrows the mirror, but the high-multiplicity contract-design question remains recognizable: the workers, their task allocation, their incentives, and the principal’s objective are still the paper’s.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is a real computational result, so the negative case cannot rest on “there is nothing to mirror.” The issue is that its guarantee is built around a finite set of named workers and their individual random outcomes. A worker’s reports, audit result, and assigned task bundle are tracked separately. With \(v\) verified comparisons, a bad worker passes with probability \(2^{-v}\); the logarithmic dependence on \(s\) comes from trying to identify every bad worker. A distribution over disutility costs alone does not preserve that mechanism.

In a truly atomless version, any finite audit leaves positive mass of bad workers undetected. One could replace individual identification with mass aggregation: if every item pair receives reports from positive mass of effortful workers, their reports have a strict majority for the true order, since good workers are correct and bad workers’ random rankings are correct on each pair half the time. But then the theorem’s central challenge—screening bad workers to recover the ranking—has vanished. Correctness follows from any positive effort mass; the remaining problem is a mean-field contract-design problem trading off effort, payments, and auditing. That could interest contract theory, but it is a substantially different computational question from the one Theorem 3.6 proves.

The alternative is to retain a finite worker-equivalent scale \(N\), as the proponent proposes. That preserves the individual audit failures and the \(1-\delta\) guarantee, but it also preserves the finite-worker random experiment and the task assignments. The continuous variable then mainly compresses the distribution of costs: the paper already optimizes its target \(g\) in \(O(s)\) time, and a finite-support cost distribution may reduce that scan to its cost cutoffs. That is a plausible high-multiplicity optimization question, but the source of the paper’s algorithmic structure remains the finite task allocation and worker-level verification, rather than a continuous society of the sort ChoCo studies.

Theorem 2.9 does not provide a separate escape from this problem. It is the equal-cost special case of the same audit-and-screen mechanism, so it inherits the finite-worker dependence. The stated utility comparison also concerns the cost of that particular contract; in an atomless model, the route to recovering the ranking can change before that comparison is meaningful.

So the best case against is that both anchors either lose their defining correctness mechanism in the atomless limit or retain the finite-worker machinery in a high-multiplicity contract problem with a thinner connection to ChoCo’s social-choice models. But this is not airtight: a researcher could reasonably find the finite-scale, finite-support workforce question worthwhile. I cannot honestly claim that the proposed \(O(\tau)\) contract optimization is not a sensible mirror; the negative case is weakest against that version of Theorem 3.6.
