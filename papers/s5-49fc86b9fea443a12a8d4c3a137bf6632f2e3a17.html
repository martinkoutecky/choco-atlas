The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is at the model level: a large population of households evaluating public projects could be grouped by their complete economic type—preferences, endowments, cost shares, and any other characteristics used by the model. A type’s mass would be its fraction of the population. The decision could be to find, or test a proposed allocation as, a cost share equilibrium: private bundles by type, a common public project, and a price system supporting agents’ choices. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s authors would recognize its continuum language: Section 3 explicitly constructs a continuum economy by assigning each finite agent a measurable block of identical agents.

But I cannot give this a qualifying anchor under your rules. The paper contains no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The closest is Theorem 4.7, proved here. Under the paper’s assumptions, it says that a Pareto-optimal allocation is a cost share equilibrium if and only if it is in the \(\sigma\)-Aubin core, is Aubin non-dominated, is a \(\sigma\)-Edgeworth equilibrium, and is not \(z\)-dominated in each of the specified perturbed-endowment economies. That is an equilibrium characterization, not a computational complexity result. Theorem 3.15, also proved here, says that if an allocation in the associated equal-treatment continuum economy is outside the \(\hat{\sigma}\)-core, then a blocking coalition exists with any prescribed measure between zero and one.

Those results make a continuum population model especially plausible as a subject for later computational work. They do not supply the computational anchor this task requires. Moreover, the paper permits an abstract public-project set and infinite-dimensional commodity spaces, so a computational problem would need an input encoding and likely additional restrictions that the paper does not give.

So my positive case stops short of a mirror question per anchor: there are no eligible anchors to mirror. The soft spot is decisive for this assignment—the paper’s continuum construction supports the population interpretation, but its results are analytic rather than computational.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.7 characterizes cost share equilibria through core membership, non-domination, and related equilibrium notions; Theorem 3.15 characterizes the sizes of blocking coalitions. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity bound. Under the programme’s stated standard, these are not qualifying anchors.

A better computational question suggested by Theorem 4.7 would be to decide whether a proposed allocation is a cost share equilibrium, or to find supporting prices, in a finitely encoded economy. But the theorem does not specify such an input: its projects, utilities, and commodity space may be abstract or infinite-dimensional. Choosing finite-dimensional commodities, finitely represented projects, and an encoding for preferences and costs would define a new algorithmic problem. It might be worthwhile, but its computational content would come from those added choices, not from a result in this paper.

Theorem 3.15 suggests searching for a blocking coalition or certifying that none exists. Its more literal computational mirror—finding the smallest blocking coalition—degenerates: once an allocation is blocked, the theorem gives a blocker of every positive measure, so the infimum is zero. Searching for a blocker could still be substantive under a suitable encoding, but again that is a new computational task; the paper’s result establishes a size-transfer property, not its complexity.

This is a strong negative case against treating this paper as an anchored continuization target. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The paper itself constructs an equal-treatment continuum economy, so a continuous population model is plainly sensible here. I would therefore reject the proposed anchors as outside the required computational scope, while admitting that a well-chosen, finitely encoded equilibrium or blocking problem could still make a worthwhile research project.
