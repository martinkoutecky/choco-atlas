The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete exchange types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatible type pairs with rational country-utility contributions \(a^p_{ts}\), and a rational target allocation \(x\), choose exchange masses \(y_{ts}\ge 0\). Require \(\sum_{s\ne t} y_{ts}+2y_{tt}\le\mu_t\) for each type \(t\). Maximize total exchange welfare \(W(y)=\sum_{t\le s}(\sum_{p\in P}a^p_{ts})y_{ts}\), then among maximizers minimize \(\max_{p\in P}|x_p-\sum_{t\le s}a^p_{ts}y_{ts}|\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic matching model over complete patient-donor exchange types: each type carries mass \(\mu_t\), and \(y_{ts}\) assigns mass to compatible exchanges, consuming two units for a same-type exchange. The objective first maximizes total welfare, then minimizes the largest country deviation from its target.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s weakly close maximum-weight selection problem. It leaves the core results, the other selection hardness results, and the polynomial-time selection results in Theorems 4 and 5 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What finite-pool rounding or approximation guarantees relate the optimal mass matching to realizable matchings as type multiplicities grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is nonatomic fair selection in a large, repeated-profile kidney exchange pool. The strongest anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper in Section 4: given a directed partitioned matching game with two countries and a target allocation \(x\), it is NP-hard to find a maximum-weight matching that is *weakly close* to \(x\)—one minimizing \(\max_p |x_p-u_p(M)|\) among maximum-weight matchings.

The theorem’s reduction, also proved here, starts from Partition with integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\). Country 1 owns \(v_i,v'_i\), and country 2 owns \(v''_i\). For each \(i\), the graph has the two exchange options \(v_i v'_i\) and \(v_i v''_i\), with both directed arcs of each option weighted \(a_i\). Every maximum matching chooses one option per \(i\): the first gives country utilities \((2a_i,0)\), and the second gives \((a_i,a_i)\). With \(A=\sum_i a_i\), the target is \(x=(3A/2,A/2)\). A maximum matching reaches that target exactly when the chosen integers split evenly, so finding a weakly close maximum matching is NP-hard.

For the mirror, let \(P\) be the finite set of countries and \(T\) the support of the population’s pair types. A type includes its country and its complete clinical and exchange profile: its compatibility with every type and the value of an exchange with each type. Two patient-donor pairs have the same type only when they are indistinguishable in every feature this problem uses. The input gives each type’s rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), the compatible unordered type pairs, and the utility vector \(a_{ts}\) contributed by an exchange between types \(t,s\). Let \(q_{ts}=\sum_{p\in P}a^p_{ts}\) be that exchange’s total weight.

The decision variable \(y_{ts}\) is the mass of exchanges between types \(t\) and \(s\); \(y_{tt}\) pairs two distinct members of type \(t\). Feasibility requires \(y_{ts}\ge 0\) and, for every \(t\), \(\sum_{s\ne t}y_{ts}+2y_{tt}\le\mu_t\). Country \(p\)’s utility is \(u_p(y)=\sum_{t\le s}a^p_{ts}y_{ts}\), and total welfare is \(W(y)=\sum_{t\le s}q_{ts}y_{ts}\).

I would name the resulting problem Weakly Close Maximum-Weight Mass Matching: given this type distribution and a rational target allocation \(x\), find a feasible \(y\) that maximizes \(W(y)\) and, among welfare-maximizing solutions, minimizes \(\delta=\max_{p\in P}|x_p-u_p(y)|\). The output is the exchange-mass vector \(y\) attaining that minimum. This is the paper’s selection question stated for a continuous population: maximize exchange welfare, then choose the closest fair distribution. Individual transplants remain exchanges between two patient-donor pairs; the masses permit partial use of a large cohort of indistinguishable pairs.

The regime I have in mind is a large rolling or regional exchange pool with many pairs—say, thousands or more—and far fewer recurring complete profiles, perhaps tens or hundreds. Those profiles could encode country, compatibility signature, and desensitization-related weight. This is a scenario to examine, not a claim about every current programme. The authors should recognize the mirror because it keeps their countries, weighted exchanges, maximum-welfare requirement, target allocation, and weak-closeness objective. It changes the population representation so that a type’s mass can be divided among exchange options.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). First compute \(W^*=\max_y W(y)\). Then minimize \(\delta\) subject to the mass constraints, \(W(y)=W^*\), and \(-\delta\le u_p(y)-x_p\le\delta\) for every country. Both steps are linear programs with \(O(\tau^2)\) exchange variables, so they are polynomial in the number of types, countries, and input bit length. The source reduction also shows where its discrete hardness goes: in the continuous version, each \(v_i\)-type cohort can split its mass equally between the two options. That reaches the target for every Partition instance, removing the all-or-nothing choice that encodes the reduction.

This case covers Theorem 6’s weakly close maximum-weight selection problem in a high-multiplicity regime. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s core-nonemptiness results or all of its hardness results. It also suggests follow-up questions: how closely does the mass optimum predict the finite high-multiplicity optimum, and what approximation or rounding bounds depend on the support size and type multiplicities?

The weakest point is whether complete exchange profiles actually repeat often enough. Compatibility can depend on patient-specific medical details; if those details make nearly every pair’s interaction pattern unique, then \(\tau\) approaches the population size and the intended high-multiplicity regime disappears. The mirror is strongest for pools where compatibility and weights do recur across substantial cohorts. That limits its reach, but leaves a precise and recognizable analogue of the paper’s fairness-selection problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it is unusually easy to write down, but unusually hard to justify as a useful *research result*. For Theorem 6, once patient-donor pairs are grouped into complete exchange types, there is one variable per compatible type pair. Maximum welfare is a linear program, and weak closeness is another linear objective with the constraints \(-\delta \le u_p(y)-x_p \le \delta\). That is a clean formulation, but it leaves no pricing problem or apparent structural obstacle to study. The Partition reduction’s all-or-nothing choices disappear as soon as a cohort can split its mass between exchange options. So the immediate mirror explains the source of the discrete hardness, but seems to deliver a routine LP rather than a new complexity result.

There is also a serious limit on when that LP describes a useful population regime. A type must preserve country, compatibility, and exchange utility with every other type. If patient-specific compatibility makes most pairs’ profiles distinct, then \(\tau\) grows with the pool and aggregation buys little. Coarser medical categories would give more repeated mass, but could misrepresent which exchanges are feasible or how valuable they are. This makes the mirror’s practical reach depend on a strong empirical condition: substantial cohorts must really share the same complete exchange profile.

That case does not defeat the stronger mirror, however. A pool built from repeated complete profiles is mathematically coherent, and a large cohort can allocate different members to different exchanges while each actual transplant remains integral. The mass matching then has a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation. Repeating each profile many times gives a concrete regime in which the type space stays small relative to the population. Theorem 6’s target allocation and country utilities carry over directly. I cannot rule out such a regime, or argue that the resulting question is one the authors would reject.

A more ambitious mirror could ask for a finite-pool rounding or approximation guarantee: when does the fractional optimum predict a realizable matching’s welfare and country deviations, as a function of type multiplicities and support size? That may be worthwhile, but it is an additional result, not something the basic continuous LP provides. I have no basis here for claiming it is already known or that it cannot yield useful theory.

So the negative case is limited: the direct mirror may be too routine to justify much effort by itself, and exact exchange profiles may be too numerous in realistic pools for it to apply broadly. Those points weaken its value; they do not defeat it in every scenario. Theorem 6 remains a defensible continuous mirror in a genuinely repeated-profile regime.
