The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow, and there is a problem with the required anchors: this paper has no named result that asserts a computational complexity classification. Theorem 1, proved here, says the egalitarian allocation is Lorenz dominant within \(PO^*(G,s,d)\). Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes the egalitarian rule by axioms. Lemma 2 gives a max-flow decomposition. These are substantive results, but none says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Section 6 gives a constructive algorithm, but no named running-time result. So there is no anchor that strictly meets your criterion.

The best nearby result to mirror is Theorem 1. Its continuous-population analogue would be a real computational question, though it is not a qualifying complexity anchor from the paper.

Consider a large regional aid network with many supply sites and receiving sites. Sites of the same type have the same single-peaked preference over the amount they send or receive, and the same compatibility with counterpart types. There might be thousands of sites but only a few dozen supply and demand types. A type records all relevant preferences and links; mass is the fraction of sites of that type. The compatibility graph connects types that can trade.

Precisely, let supplier types \(i\in I\) have masses \(a_i\) and per-site peaks \(s_i\), and demander types \(j\in J\) have masses \(b_j\) and peaks \(d_j\), with total population mass \(\sum_i a_i+\sum_j b_j=1\). The input also gives a bipartite type compatibility graph \(H\) and the single-peaked preferences for each type. Choose nonnegative aggregate transfers \(z_{ij}\), only on edges in \(H\). They induce per-site transfers
\[ x_i=\frac{\sum_j z_{ij}}{a_i}, \qquad y_j=\frac{\sum_i z_{ij}}{b_j}. \]
A solution is such a feasible flow whose allocation is Pareto optimal and weakly rationed, and whose distribution of per-site transfers Lorenz dominates that of every other allocation with those properties. Concretely, if \(Q(u)\) is the increasing quantile function of transfers across the whole population, its Lorenz curve is \(L(r)=\int_0^r Q(u)\,du\); the solution must have \(L(r)\) at least as large at every \(r\in[0,1]\) as every competing allocation. The output is the per-type allocation and any compatible flow implementing it.

The analogy is credible because it preserves the paper’s central ingredients: single-peaked preferences, bilateral feasibility, Pareto efficiency, and the egalitarian selection. The population is what becomes continuous; the commodity is already divisible in the paper. Masses and type-level averages are therefore natural parts of the mirror. I would expect this problem to be tractable for rational input, using weighted max-flow and progressive bottleneck equalization: a type of mass \(a_i\) has aggregate capacity \(a_i\min\{\lambda,s_i\}\) at per-site level \(\lambda\). Running time would be measured in the number of types, compatibility edges, and input bit length. This candidate covers Theorem 1 and the construction in Section 6, not the paper’s strategy-proofness and characterization results. Further questions include exact bit-complexity bounds for the weighted construction and how the allocation changes under perturbations of the type masses.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s main contributions are structural and axiomatic, not complexity results, so this mirror cannot honestly be presented as dissolving a hardness result. Also, the type count stays small only when link compatibility is genuinely shared by classes; arbitrary person-specific links can make the number of types as large as the population. In the proposed regional network, compatibility by site class makes low support plausible, but the positive case is still for a sensible high-multiplicity analogue of Theorem 1—not for a computational anchor the paper does not provide.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that none of the paper’s named results states a computational complexity classification. Theorem 1 characterizes an egalitarian allocation; Theorem 2 characterizes a rule by axioms. The paper gives a constructive procedure, but the proponent’s question—how to compute a weighted version—extends that procedure rather than mirroring a complexity result from the paper.

The proposed type model is coherent, though. With masses \(a_i,b_j\), aggregate flows \(z_{ij}\), and per-person transfers \(x_i,y_j\), feasibility is a rational-capacity flow problem on the type graph. The paper already supplies the progressive bottleneck construction for finite agents, and identifies the allocation with a lexicographically optimal flow. So the proposed continuum model looks like a weighted form of the paper’s existing flow machinery, not a new computational problem whose difficulty is exposed by making the population continuous. That is a limited case against its value for ChoCo’s complexity programme; it cannot rule out a useful algorithmic study.

I cannot honestly defeat this anchor on modelling grounds. A population of aid sites grouped by shared peaks and compatibility is plausible, and the weighted Lorenz question is well defined. Since an easy answer or an unchanged result is not a valid objection under the stated rules, the case against Theorem 1 is weak. The paper lacks a qualifying computational result to mirror, but the proponent’s fallback remains a defensible new computation question.
