The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are Propositions 1–6, and they characterize cores, prices, equilibria, differentiability, and incentives; none asserts a complexity result or presents an algorithm that the paper analyzes. The closest result is Proposition 4, proved here: with strict preferences in the assignment model, every stable matching can be supported by a Walrasian equilibrium without transfers, using positive utility weights and prices that make each matched individual’s net transfer zero. That is an existence result, not a computational one, so it cannot serve as an anchor under the rule you set.

There is still a plausible *unanchored* continuous question near Proposition 4. In a labor market, for example, many workers and employers could share a small number of types, each type specifying its preferences over the other side’s types. Given rational masses for the types, ask for a stable mass matching: output nonnegative matched masses \(x_{ab}\), with each type’s total matched mass at most its population. Call it stable if, for every pair of types \(a,b\), the mass of \(a\)-agents who prefer \(b\) to their current assignment and the mass of \(b\)-agents who prefer \(a\) to theirs are not both positive. Each unit of mass still receives one match; \(x_{ab}\) records how much mass is matched across the pair of types.

This seems like a sensible population version of the assignment model: the agents could be a large hiring cohort with relatively few skill and job types, and the type-level mass split does not require any individual to receive a fractional match. An aggregate deferred-acceptance procedure makes tractability a plausible expectation. The proposed question would cover the stable-matching existence result in Proposition 4, not the paper’s general differentiability and incentive results.

The weakness is decisive for the requested case: the paper does not ask how to compute stable matchings, and Proposition 4 does not claim an algorithm or computational bound. Moreover, the paper already studies population constraints and large-population limits. So this is a plausible computational extension of its assignment model, but I cannot honestly present it as a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. Proposition 4 says that, given a stable matching in the finite assignment model with strict preferences, suitable weights and prices support it as a Walrasian equilibrium without transfers. It proves existence of that support; it does not ask for an algorithm to find the matching or bound the cost of finding prices. Propositions 1–6 likewise give existence, characterization, and incentive claims, not complexity results.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is coherent: take finitely many worker and employer types with rational population masses, and compute a stable mass matching, perhaps with supporting weights and prices. Each unit of mass can still receive one integral match, so aggregation need not fractionalize anyone’s bundle. Copies with identical preferences do undermine literal strict preferences between individuals, but weak stability over type classes is a natural repair. That is not the objection.

The problem is that this repaired task is a new computational question, not the question Proposition 4 answers. Proposition 4 starts with a stable matching and guarantees its support; computing a stable mass matching asks for the matching itself. Even an exact computational version of the proposition would need an input representation for utilities: the paper permits general concave Lipschitz utility functions, but specifies no finite encoding. Choosing finite rankings or rational payoff tables makes the problem computable, but also supplies a model the paper never studies. The complexity question then depends on that new choice.

The same gap blocks stronger candidates from the paper. Its differentiability results concern asymptotic limits, and its manipulation result gives a sufficient condition for nonmanipulability. Neither specifies a finite computational input or an optimization task. A continuous analytic treatment of population constraints is present, but, as the programme’s scope says, that is not a computational collision.

So the negative case is strong for no paper-anchored computational mirror: the paper contains no named computational result to mirror, and Proposition 4 cannot be made one just by changing finite populations to masses. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile project could be built around continuous matching. The proposed mass-matching problem is sensible; what I cannot honestly claim is that it is already a worthwhile mirror of a computational result in this paper.
