The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\) and a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute each candidate’s per-capita Dodgson score \(D_\mu(c)\), defined as the infimum mass-weighted adjacent-swap cost needed to make \(c\) strictly beat every rival, and return the candidates minimizing \(D_\mu(c)\).

The model it lives in

Treat each ranking \(t\) as a type of mass \(\mu_t\). For a fixed candidate \(c\), let \(r_t(a)\) be the position of candidate \(a\) in \(t\), with \(1\) best and \(m\) worst; choose \(y_{t,j}\geq 0\) as the mass of type \(t\) shifted upward by \(j\) adjacent swaps, for \(0\leq j<r_t(c)\), with \(\sum_j y_{t,j}=\mu_t\). Minimize \(\sum_{t,j}j y_{t,j}\), subject for each \(d\ne c\) to \(M_{c,d}+2\sum_{t:r_t(d)<r_t(c)}\sum_{j\geq r_t(c)-r_t(d)}y_{t,j}\geq 0\), where \(M_{c,d}\) is \(c\)’s initial per-capita pairwise margin over \(d\). The weak-margin LP value equals the infimum for strict victories: a boundary allocation can be mixed with an allocation putting all mass on rankings with \(c\) first, at arbitrarily small additional cost.

The objection that survived

The LP replaces Theorem 3.2’s finite-instance self-knowing heuristic guarantee with exact optimization over fractional type mass, so it mirrors the underlying score and winner task rather than the paper’s heuristic-success phenomenon.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It mirrors Theorem 3.2’s Dodgson score and winner computation over ballot-type masses. It leaves aside the frequency bounds in Theorems 4.1 and 4.4, which concern uniformly sampled finite elections, and the theorem’s finite-instance self-knowing guarantee.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do finite Dodgson scores divided by electorate size converge to \(D_\mu(c)\) as type proportions approach \(\mu\), and can one bound the convergence error?
  • When does a positive gap between continuous candidate scores guarantee that the finite electorate has the same Dodgson winner set?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest numbered computational anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper. It says that GreedyScore is self-knowingly correct for Dodgson Score, GreedyWinner is self-knowingly correct for the Dodgson Winner problem, and both algorithms run in polynomial time. “Self-knowingly correct” is conditional: whenever either algorithm labels its answer “definitely,” the score or winner answer is right; on “maybe” inputs the theorem makes no correctness promise. This is not a theorem that computes every discrete Dodgson score or winner correctly in polynomial time. The paper discusses hardness results from earlier work, but does not state them as numbered results of this paper, so I would not use them as anchors.

My lead mirror is Continuous Dodgson Score and Winners. An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of complete rankings \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). For each candidate \(c\), the task is to compute its per-capita Dodgson score and return the candidates with minimum score. Each infinitesimal part of the population has an ordinary, integral ranking; the decision variable allocates mass from each ranking to rankings obtained by moving \(c\) upward. The objective is total mass-weighted adjacent swaps.

More precisely, let \(p_t(c)\) be \(c\)’s position in ranking \(t\), numbered from least to most preferred. For each \(t\), choose masses \(y_{t,j}\geq 0\) for upward shifts \(j=0,\ldots,p_t(c)-1\), with \(\sum_j y_{t,j}=\mu_t\). Let \(M_{c,d}\) be \(c\)’s initial pairwise margin over \(d\), and let \(h_t(c,d)=p_t(d)-p_t(c)\) when \(d\) is above \(c\) in \(t\). Define the score as the infimum of \(\sum_{t,j} j y_{t,j}\), subject to, for every \(d\neq c\),

\[ M_{c,d}+2\sum_{t:\,d\text{ is above }c\text{ in }t}\ \sum_{j\geq h_t(c,d)} y_{t,j}>0. \]

The strict majority condition makes an infimum the natural continuous version: the best boundary plan may leave a tie, while plans making \(c\) strictly beat everyone can approach its cost arbitrarily closely. Equivalently, solve the LP with weak inequalities \( \geq 0 \); mixing any boundary plan with a plan that puts all mass’s \(c\) first gives strictly winning plans with costs arbitrarily close to the LP optimum. A solution is this optimal mass allocation and its score; the winner set consists of candidates whose scores are smallest.

This keeps the paper’s central object intact. A unit of mass still receives an integral ranking and undergoes an integral number of adjacent swaps; fractional \(y_{t,j}\) means that a fraction of a voter type receives each such bundle. Swaps not involving \(c\) do not improve its pairwise standing, and moving \(c\) down cannot help, so restricting the optimization to upward moves loses no cheaper way to make \(c\) a Condorcet winner.

The regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballots—for example, a national election with millions of voters but relatively few distinct rankings. A type is a complete ballot; agents sharing it are indistinguishable for Dodgson score. The mass is the fraction of the electorate holding that ballot, and the objective is the minimum number of adjacent swaps per voter in the limiting population. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every election has few types. The paper’s discussion of succinct profiles as counts of vote types supports the relevance of that regime.

The continuous problem is Class A: tractable. For a fixed candidate, the LP has \(O(m\tau)\) variables and polynomially many constraints, so rational linear programming computes its score in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\). Repeating for each candidate gives the winner set. This is an exact result for the stated continuous problem; it does not turn Theorem 3.2 into a full discrete algorithm or establish that the discrete hardness transfers. Indeed, the discrete model with masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) embeds as a profile, but allowing mass to split across shifts relaxes its indivisibility. The expected tractability comes from that relaxation and the structure of moving one candidate, not from a claim that the paper’s finite heuristic is already exact.

I would not make Theorems 4.1 or 4.4 additional anchors. Their guarantees concern uniformly random, finite electorates sampled voter by voter. They are valuable results, but transferring that sampling probability while leaving the society finite would continuize the noise model, not the population. The proposed mirror treats \(\mu\) as the society itself.

The weakest point is the change from an attained discrete minimum to a continuous infimum: at a boundary optimum, the mass allocation may produce a tie rather than a strict Condorcet win. That is a real departure from the literal finite score, though it is the natural per-capita limit of the strict-majority “plus one” correction. The most useful follow-up questions are whether discrete scores divided by \(n\) converge to these LP values as \(n\) grows, and how gaps between continuous candidate scores control stability of the winner set.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to Theorem 3.2 as an anchor is that its distinctive claim is about a *self-knowing heuristic*: on every finite election, a “definitely” answer is correct, though the algorithm may return “maybe.” The proposed continuous LP answers a different question. It computes a real-valued per-capita score exactly, so its confidence is always “definitely”; it does not mirror the theorem’s conditional guarantee or the paper’s analysis of when that guarantee is useful. The winner claim also needs care: strict majority makes the continuous optimum an infimum, and candidates separated in finite elections by a few swaps can tie at zero per-capita cost in the limit.

There is a fixable error in the proposed LP as written. It numbers positions from least to most preferred, but then gives the ranges and shift thresholds appropriate to the opposite convention. Reindexing the positions repairs this; it does not invalidate the underlying idea.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror. A society with a small number of repeated rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and its per-capita Dodgson score is a well-posed computational question. The corrected formulation is an LP with polynomially many variables and constraints, hence a direct Class A result. The loss of finite tie-breaking information may limit what the continuous winner set says about a particular finite election, but it does not erase the score question.

So I cannot honestly sustain the requested universal negative. The continuous LP is a worthwhile mirror of the paper’s score problem, even though it does not reproduce Theorem 3.2’s self-knowing heuristic result.
