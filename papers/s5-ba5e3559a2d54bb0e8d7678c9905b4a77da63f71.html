The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has no eligible computational anchor, so it cannot support the required anchor-based case for a continuous mirror.

Its closest named result is Proposition 5.1, proved here: applying the same reporting strategy \(q\) independently to two responses cannot increase the square-root agreement measure, and the proposition gives an explicit decrease when the generating model is separated. That is an information-theoretic inequality, not a result that a computational problem is in P or hard. Proposition 11.1, cited from Jurca et al. (2011), says that in a truthful homogeneous binary-response mechanism whose payment depends only on one task’s response pair, the agent’s payment cannot depend on her own response. That is a mechanism-design impossibility, not a computational-complexity result.

The main result in Section 4.3 is also proved here: for an \(\alpha\)-separated model, bounded tasks per agent, and at least two agents per task, sufficiently many tasks make truthful reporting a strict Bayes–Nash equilibrium, with a payoff gap over reporting independently of one’s observation. The theorem number is not printed in the supplied text, and its claim is about incentives, not computational complexity.

There is a continuity foothold in the paper’s many-task limit and its distribution \(P_X\) over entity types. But a continuum equilibrium analysis would extend the paper’s analytic incentive results; it would not mirror a named computational result, and it would continuize tasks rather than the society. Under the requested criteria, there is no continuous problem to state per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The claim that the paper contains no computational-complexity theorem is correct. It does not, by itself, show that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror. Theorem 4.3 gives a concrete computational target: find a mechanism that guarantees truthful reporting incentives across a large population.

Consider a crowdsourcing platform with a distribution \(\mu\) over complete evaluator types. A type records everything the model uses about an evaluator, such as their observation channel, effort cost, and task access. Keep tasks randomized as in §5.3, and let evaluators be sampled from this population. Given a budget \(B\), one can ask for a reward rule that maximizes the smallest incentive margin across types while keeping expected payments within budget. With a finite rational support of size \(\tau\), these constraints can be written over type, observation, and deviation; for a fixed task assignment, mechanism synthesis is a linear program whose size depends on \(\tau\), the answer set, and the input bit length. If task allocation is also chosen, the optimization incorporates the mass assigned to each task and the accuracy of the population estimates. That is a computational question about a continuous *population* of evaluators, not a limit over tasks or over the latent entity distribution \(P_X\).

Proposition 5.1 supplies another part of that formulation. Its square-root agreement quantity is an aggregate of pairwise agreement probabilities. With evaluator types of mass \(\mu_t\), those probabilities depend on the mixture of type-specific observation and reporting channels. One can ask how to choose rewards and peer assignments to preserve a specified truthful incentive margin while controlling the resulting aggregate agreement measure. The proposition itself is analytic, but it identifies the quantity that this optimization must manage.

Proposition 11.1 sets a useful boundary condition. Its impossibility applies when a binary mechanism’s payment depends only on the pair of reports from one task. A platform with a large evaluator population can estimate agreement frequencies across other tasks and use them in its payments, as SRA does. The continuous question is then to find when such population-level estimates permit a budget-feasible truthful mechanism, and how much evaluation mass is needed. In the proposition’s one-task special case, the feasibility problem should recover the impossibility.

The proposed regime also has a plausible setting: the paper discusses crowdsourcing, randomized task assignment, and agents using a common strategy across tasks. If worker characteristics fall into a moderate number of types, aggregating workers into masses is a sensible high-multiplicity model. This does not establish a rich complexity landscape; the fixed-assignment mechanism-synthesis problem may be tractable by a routine LP. The stronger version involving endogenous task allocation and statistical accuracy has more room for substantive optimization results.

So the proponent’s narrow point survives: none of the three cited results is itself a computational-complexity theorem. But that is not enough to establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 4.3, Proposition 5.1, and Proposition 11.1 each motivate a computational question once evaluator population mass—not task count or outcome space—is made the continuous object. The case is strongest as a modest mechanism-synthesis and allocation programme, not as a claim that this paper already contains a hidden hardness result.
