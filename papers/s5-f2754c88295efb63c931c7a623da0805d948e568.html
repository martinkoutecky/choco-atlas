Why no mirror

The other named results are geometric or metric statements, not computational results under the stated criterion. Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is continuous two-bloc ballot clustering. The paper has no numbered result that classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, so there is no direct complexity anchor. Its strongest named computational anchor is Theorem 3.1, Classification stability, proved in this paper. It says: if an election is \((R,r)\)-polarized under a ballot distance \(d\)—there are centers \(x,y\) with \(d(x,y)>R\), and every cast ballot lies within distance \(r\) of one of them—then \(R>4r\) guarantees a unique optimal clustering. If \(R>10r\), that clustering is also optimal for every distance \(d'\) that is 2-biLipschitz-equivalent to \(d\).

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Fix \(m\), a set \(\Omega_m\) of complete and partial ballots, a distance \(d\in\{d_H,d_B\}\), and the valid-ballot center variant. An instance is a support \(S\subseteq\Omega_m\) of size \(\tau\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\sigma\) summing to one. Find two centers \(x,y\in\Omega_m\) and an assignment of the mass of each ballot type to the two blocs that minimize \(\sum_{\sigma\in S}\mu_\sigma\min\{d(\sigma,x),d(\sigma,y)\}\). The solution is the centers and the resulting mass assigned to each bloc; mass can split between centers only on a distance tie. A promised version supplies witnesses \(x,y,R,r\) satisfying Theorem 3.1’s polarization conditions and asks for the global optimum. For that version, the theorem predicts a unique clustering when \(R>4r\). Together with Corollary 2.11, which gives \(d_B\le d_H<2d_B\), it predicts the same clustering under Borda and head-to-head distance when \(R>10r\).

The regime is large elections with many voters casting the same ballot. A ballot is a type because this clustering problem uses preferences alone; its mass is the share of voters casting it. The paper’s Scottish data gives a concrete, if only moderately compressed, example: one election has 11,315 voters and 1,238 distinct ballots, including one ballot cast 1,342 times. Larger electorates with modest candidate counts and relatively concentrated ballot support make the population-to-type ratio more compelling. The authors should recognize the mirror: they already cluster voters by summed ballot distances. The continuous formulation makes type frequencies the input and asks for the exact computation as a function of \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\).

For unrestricted exact optimization, I would expect hardness to transfer: the center choices and ranking geometry still carry combinatorial complexity, while representing repeated voters by rational masses does not remove that structure. Theorem 3.1 identifies a promising tractable promised regime, but uniqueness and stability do not themselves give an algorithm for finding the global optimum. The resulting questions include the exact complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), whether the polarized promise admits an efficient recovery algorithm, and whether the ballot-graph representations in Theorem 2.7 help compute these weighted optima.

This case covers the paper’s voter-bloc clustering and its stability across the two ranking metrics. It does not claim a mirror for slate clustering, the paper’s metric constructions in general, or its empirical validation.

The weak point is that the paper proves no named complexity classification, and its clustering objective already counts repeated finite ballots with their frequencies. For rational \(\mu\), one can scale all masses to integer multiplicities and recover a finite profile with the same optimum. So this is a credible high-multiplicity computational formulation of their clustering problem, but the paper’s theorem does not establish a new continuous complexity result; whether the authors and a referee regard the change of input representation as a sufficiently substantial mirror is the central vulnerability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it is a geometric stability guarantee, not a result about computation. It gives sufficient separation conditions for a clustering to be unique or stable across metrics; it says nothing about the cost of finding that clustering, recognizing the promised regime, or certifying an optimum. Theorem 2.7 and Corollary 2.11 have the same limitation: their distance identities and bounds can be averaged over a distribution, but they do not become computational results by doing so.

There is also a gap between Theorem 3.1 and the proposed weighted optimization problem. The proof compares assignments to centers already in the two witness neighborhoods. It does not rule out an unconstrained optimum placing both centers in a dense neighborhood when the other neighborhood has very little mass and the dense one has internal spread. So the theorem’s separation condition alone does not establish the promised result for weighted two-center optimization. That weakens this particular theorem mirror, though it does not dispose of the optimization problem.

The best repair is a weighted \(k\)-median problem over ballot types, with exact runtime measured in \(m,\tau,L\). That is a sensible object: for this task, voters with the same ballot are indistinguishable, and the Scottish example has 11,315 voters but 1,238 ballot types, including one ballot cast 1,342 times. The long tail—660 types cast once—makes that example only moderate evidence for a small-support regime, but larger elections with concentrated ballot support are plausible.

For rational masses, choosing a common denominator \(N\) turns each type of mass \(\mu_t\) into \(N\mu_t\) copies. The discrete objective is then the same objective scaled by \(N\). That shows the mirror is exactly a high-multiplicity version of finite ballot clustering, rather than a new geometric limit. But under ChoCo’s stated standard, that is a legitimate bridge, not a collision: an algorithm running in support size and bit length could matter greatly when the explicit electorate is huge. The same point applies to a stronger mirror of slate clustering, where the population distribution determines candidate distances and the computation chooses a partition of candidates.

So I can reject the claim that Theorem 3.1 itself anchors a continuous complexity result, and I can question whether its guarantee transfers to the proposed weighted optimum as stated. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Weighted exact clustering is a credible computational question, and the main negative argument—that rational masses can be expanded back into finite multiplicities—is also the programme’s intended high-multiplicity setting.
