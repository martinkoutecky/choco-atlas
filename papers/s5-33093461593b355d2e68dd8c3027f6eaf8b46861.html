Why no mirror

The proposed continuum evaluation is a sensible, concrete question about a high-multiplicity population. But the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting an algorithmic or computational result to anchor it; its simulation procedures and welfare analysis do not meet that requirement. The mirror therefore fails bit (a), even though the prospective question may be worth studying.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers symmetric two-candidate MRPV under IAC* in the large-population limit, for expected welfare and election-inversion probability. It leaves aside IC and IAC, NMPV, asymmetric states, and strategic compact formation.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact welfare and inversion probabilities be computed in time polynomial in \(K\) using dynamic programming or exact integration?
  • Which compact size \(L\) maximizes expected welfare in this continuum model?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no anchor-compliant computational result in this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. Algorithms 1–8 describe simulations, and the paper’s numerical “square root” finding is not a numbered result of that kind. The closest substantive support is Section 4.4 and Appendix 9.5, which derive asymptotic expected-utility formulas for MRPV under IAC*. They are not valid anchors under your rule, so I cannot honestly present a per-anchor mirror.

If an unnumbered result can motivate a prospective mirror, the strongest case is the IAC* limit of that MRPV welfare analysis. Define a society with \(K\) equal-population states and two voter types per state, \((k,D)\) and \((k,R)\). Draw independent \(p_k\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]\); state \(k\) then has masses \(p_k/K\) of type \((k,D)\) and \((1-p_k)/K\) of type \((k,R)\). This is the large-electorate limit of the paper’s IAC* model: the number of voters per state grows while the number of types stays at most \(2K\). The type records everything the model uses about a voter: state and candidate preference.

Call the candidate problem Continuum Regional Compact Welfare Evaluation. Given odd \(K\) and compact size \(L\), compute the expected utilitarian welfare and the probability of an election inversion under MRPV. A nonmember state awards its one electoral vote to \(D\) exactly when \(p_k>1/2\). The compact awards its \(L\) votes to \(D\) exactly when \(\sum_{k\in S}p_k>L/2\), where \(S\) is its set of member states. The Electoral College winner is the candidate with more than \(K/2\) electoral votes. For \(\bar p=K^{-1}\sum_k p_k\), welfare is \(\bar p\) if \(D\) wins and \(1-\bar p\) if \(R\) wins; an inversion occurs when that winner disagrees with the national popular winner, determined by whether \(\bar p>1/2\). The solution is the exact pair of expected welfare and inversion probability for the given \(K,L\). State symmetry means the choice of \(S\) does not matter when its size is fixed.

I expect this restricted problem to be tractable: the uniform-share model and equal state weights give piecewise-polynomial distributions that appear amenable to dynamic programming and exact integration. One natural extension is to return the welfare-maximizing \(L\), which would let a prover study the paper’s compact-size conclusions directly. This mirror covers only the symmetric two-candidate MRPV analysis under IAC* as the population per state tends to infinity. It does not cover the paper’s IAC and IC analyses, NMPV, or strategic compact formation.

The regime is plausible: millions of voters can fall into a small number of state-and-preference types, and the paper’s own IAC* model makes state-level vote shares the relevant random quantities. Its Appendix 9.5 already works with continuous random shares to derive limiting utility formulas, so authors could recognize the underlying population question. But that proximity is also the weakest point: much of this particular limit is already analyzed in the paper, and the paper never frames it as a computational problem. As a positive case under the requested anchor rule, it therefore does not clear the bar; it is a plausible continuous-population restatement and a lead for further algorithmic questions, not an anchored result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no anchor-compliant result here. The paper has no named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Its simulations, numerical square-root finding, and expected-welfare comparisons are substantive findings, but they are not such a result. The proposed mirror therefore has no named computational result to continue.

The proposed state-and-preference types are nevertheless a sound high-multiplicity model. A large state population can be represented by masses \(p_k\) and \(1-p_k\), and the IAC* limit makes those shares continuous. There is no convincing objection based on voter identity, type choice, or a vanishing continuum. The authors already work with continuous shares in Appendix 9.5.

The stronger negative point is that the proposed problem largely repackages work the paper has already done. Appendix 9.5 derives limiting welfare formulas for MRPV under IAC*, and Appendix 4 gives limiting expressions and a simulation route for election inversions. The proposed input \(K,L\) also strips away much of the computational structure: in the symmetric model the identity of the member states does not matter, and optimizing over \(L\) means checking \(K+1\) values. What remains is exact evaluation of integrals over uniform shares. That could yield a useful algorithm, but it is a narrow computational follow-up to formulas already in the paper, not a new complexity landscape anchored in one of its computational results.

This does not establish that the mirror is uninteresting. The paper has not classified the complexity of exact evaluation, and a tractable algorithm could be worthwhile. Nor does the existing continuous analysis count as a collision under the programme’s stated standard: it is not itself a complexity classification. A broader model with heterogeneous states or a different prior might create a richer computational problem, but that would be a weaker continuation of the proposed anchor.

So the case against is strongest on anchoring and marginal novelty, not on whether a continuous population is sensible. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the proponent’s mirror is plausible, though its computational contribution may be modest.
