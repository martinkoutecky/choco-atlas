The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that there is no computational result here to continuize. The paper’s numbered results are claims about equality of power values: Lemma 4 gives equal values to players in a rotation group under WVC, and Lemma 7 relates two ways of averaging and normalizing Banzhaf values. The examples calculate indices for small games. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm whose complexity could be classified. Under ChoCo’s stated criterion, that is decisive: a continuous algorithmic question inspired by this paper would be a new question, not a mirror of one of its results.

The best version of a Lemma 4 mirror might ask whether members of a large, homogeneous rotation class have equal type-level power, or how to compute that power from the class masses. But the equality lemma is a symmetry claim, not a computational finding. More fundamentally, Banzhaf power measures a player’s marginal contribution when that player joins a coalition. In an atomless population, one member has zero mass and cannot change the coalition’s outcome. A group’s aggregate influence could remain meaningful, but that changes the object from individual Banzhaf power to a mass-level power measure. A computational study of that new measure might be worthwhile; Lemma 4 does not supply it as an anchored mirror.

The strongest version of a Lemma 7 mirror would replace the finite average over auxiliary rotation games with an average over a continuous distribution of schedules, then ask whether averaging and normalization commute. But the lemma’s proof uses finite auxiliary games of the same size and counts how often each player appears. Keeping those finite games leaves the population finite; scaling them makes individual swing probabilities vanish and requires a new definition of aggregate power and normalization. Either route changes the mathematical question, and the paper establishes no computational claim about it.

A large association with repeated, identical voting roles is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not reject the idea on the ground that rotation can never involve many agents. Even there, the complete type must include the role and place in the rotation schedule. Aggregating identical members then either preserves a finite-player power calculation by symmetry or asks for a new cohort-level index. One could investigate the complexity of computing that index, but that project would be anchored in the general problem of computing voting power, not in a computational result of this paper.

So the paper-specific negative case is strong: none of the proponent’s candidate anchors survives as a computational mirror. The honest weakness is the universal claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile continuous question. A continuous aggregate-power project may be coherent in a suitably chosen large association; what this paper lacks is a named computational result to make that project its mirror.
