What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort analogue attempts to extend Proposition 5’s local-dominance pruning; it does not mirror the RCC elicitation heuristic, its experiments, or candidate-plan generation, and it fails the population-mirror test.

The case FOR (proponent)

My case is narrow, and its lead is Proposition 5, proved in this paper. It is a structural result used to prune candidate plans, not a theorem placing a problem in \(P\) or proving hardness. The paper gives no numbered complexity-class result; if an anchor must explicitly assert such a classification, this paper has none.

Proposition 5 says: if plan \(pl_1\) has at least as high an expected subutility as \(pl_2\) on every attribute, and overall utility is additive, then \(pl_1\) has at least as high an expected overall utility. In the paper’s model, \(u(x)=\sum_i k_i u_i(x_i)\), with \(k_i\geq 0\) and \(\sum_i k_i=1\); local dominance on attribute \(i\) means \(\mathbb{E}_{pl_1}[u_i(X_i)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{pl_2}[u_i(X_i)]\). The proposition is proved here and is the basis for eliminating plans before all tradeoff weights have been elicited.

A credible regime is a large health service choosing among a finite set of treatment pathways for a large cohort of patients. A type is a treatment-relevant clinical profile: patients of that type have the same eligibility, costs, and outcome distributions under each pathway. The service has many patients but a moderate number \(\tau\) of such profiles. Its support is a distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort of type \(t\). This is close to the paper’s deep-vein-thrombosis application, which evaluates plans on mortality, pulmonary embolism, bleeding, and cost. The shared, incomplete utility model belongs to the service choosing a cohort policy; it does not require patients with different clinical profiles to be interchangeable.

Lead mirror: Cohort Efficient-Frontier Pruning. An instance consists of a finite, explicitly listed set \(P\) of candidate treatment pathways or policies; a finite set of \(n\) attributes; rational type masses \(\mu_t\); and, for each type \(t\), pathway \(p\), and attribute \(i\), the expected subutility \(a_{tpi}\) under \(p\). The service knows the attribute subutilities but not their common additive weights \(k\in\Delta_n\). Define the cohort’s expected attribute score by
\[ \bar a_{pi}=\sum_t\mu_t a_{tpi}, \]
and its overall value under weights \(k\) by
\[ V_\mu(p;k)=\sum_i k_i\bar a_{pi}. \]
The problem is to return the exact set of pathways not robustly dominated: \(p\) is dominated if some \(q\) satisfies \(V_\mu(q;k)\geq V_\mu(p;k)\) for every \(k\in\Delta_n\), with strict inequality for at least one \(k\). That set of pathways is the solution. Robust dominance holds exactly when \(\bar a_{qi}\geq\bar a_{pi}\) for every attribute \(i\). Thus this frontier can be computed in polynomial time in the explicit input size, including \(|P|\), \(n\), \(\tau\), and the rational bit lengths.

This is a direct population analogue of the paper’s pruning question: plan outcomes are averaged over a mass distribution, then local attribute dominance certifies overall dominance under every completion of the additive utility model. I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable. Its scope is the local-dominance test and resulting efficient frontier. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s RCC heuristic, its elicitation interaction, or the generation of candidate plans. Further questions include whether an RCC-style query rule remains useful on cohort-averaged scores, and how to handle groups with different unknown tradeoff weights.

The soft spot is that the paper studies a decision maker choosing a plan, while this scenario has a service choosing for a cohort. The mirror preserves the paper’s multiattribute plan comparison and incomplete additive utility model, and its medical example makes cohort policy selection plausible. But the authors and a referee would have to accept that service-level decision as their problem. It is a positive case for a continuous cohort version of their pruning result, not a claim that the paper’s original single-decision-maker problem already has a population limit or that discrete hardness dissolves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 5 is the only result the case for names, and it is not a computational result: it says that coordinatewise expected-subutility dominance implies overall dominance under additive utility. The paper gives no numbered complexity result for a computational problem. Its most algorithmic contribution is the RCC heuristic for choosing elicitation questions, supported by experiments.

The cohort proposal is plausible as a medical application, but its population distribution collapses into the kind of outcome distribution the paper already models. If \(f_{p,t}\) is the outcome distribution for pathway \(p\) and clinical type \(t\), define \(f_p=\sum_t\mu_t f_{p,t}\). Then
\[ \bar a_{pi}=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbb{E}[u_i(X_i)\mid p,t] =\mathbb{E}_{f_p}[u_i(X_i)]. \]
So the mirror’s cohort scores are just the expected subutilities of a mixture prospect. The patients’ mass does not enter as a population whose members make or influence choices; it enters as a distribution over outcomes for one service-level decision maker. That is outcome uncertainty, not a continuous-society version of Proposition 5.

A stronger cohort model could assign different pathways to different clinical types. But type-contingent policies still produce mixture prospects in the paper’s framework. Add capacity constraints or optimize the assignment of treatment mass, and the problem becomes cohort resource allocation—a potentially valuable new problem, but not a mirror of the named result. Alternatively, make the patients themselves the decision makers: identical utility types merely repeat the same elicitation question, while heterogeneous tradeoff weights require an aggregation rule for competing utilities. That changes the problem into collective preference aggregation, which the paper does not study.

The medical example makes the proposed scenario credible, so the negative case is strongest on what counts as a mirror, not on whether cohort planning could be worthwhile in its own right. I do not see a scenario that preserves both a meaningful continuous population and this paper’s computational question.
