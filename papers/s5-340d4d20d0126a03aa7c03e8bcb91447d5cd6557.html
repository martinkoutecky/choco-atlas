The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying numbered complexity anchor. It prints no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized tractable. The Coq lemmas wins_type_prop and wins_prop_type are unnumbered and establish that the proposed certificates correspond to the Schulze winning condition; they are not complexity results. Section 6 reports that the implementation counts millions of ballots, while the fixed-parameter tractability statement in Section 4 points to [13]. Neither supplies the required anchor from this paper.

The strongest positive mirror I can offer is therefore unanchored: *Rational-Mass Schulze Counting with Certificates*. An instance gives candidates \(C\) and a support \(T\) of ballot types, each a complete ranking (including ties, or a distinct invalid-ballot type if needed), with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). The task is to output the Schulze winner set and checkable evidence for each candidate’s status. For \(a,b\in C\), compute the weighted margin
\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr), \]
then the strongest path value
\[ G_\mu(a,b)=\max_{P:a\leadsto b}\ \min_{(x,y)\in P}M_\mu(x,y). \]
A candidate \(c\) wins exactly when \(G_\mu(c,d)\ge G_\mu(d,c)\) for every \(d\).

The certificate can adapt the paper’s path and co-closed-set witnesses to rational margins. For each claimed winner–rival comparison, give a path establishing a lower bound and a co-closed set ruling out a stronger reverse path; for each nonwinner, give a rival that defeats it with corresponding witnesses. A checker first computes margins from the supplied support, then verifies these witnesses. The expected direction is tractable: margin aggregation takes \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations, and strongest-path computation takes \(O(m^3)\); with rational input, the bit complexity should be polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The regime is a large public election with a small candidate slate and many repeated rankings. For a fixed small slate, the number of possible valid ranking types is limited while the number of voters may be millions. The paper’s own eight-million-ballot case makes this a natural setting: replacing ballot counts with rational shares preserves the margins and winner comparisons. The mirror covers the counting and certification task, not the paper’s legal argument or its formal verification of the implementation. This is a plausible continuous version of the authors’ problem, though extending the certificate proof to rational masses would be new work.

My weakest point is the decisive one for your requested format: this mirrors the paper’s demonstrated computation, not a qualifying named complexity result. The paper’s main contribution is verified, certifiable counting at realistic scale; it does not present that contribution as a theorem about the complexity of a computational social choice problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no named computational complexity result to mirror. Its Coq lemmas establish that certificates correspond to the Schulze winning condition; they do not classify an algorithm’s complexity. The timing experiments show that an implementation handles millions of ballots, and the fixed parameter tractability result is cited from other work. Neither is a complexity theorem established by this paper.

The rational mass version is coherent: compute weighted pairwise margins from the support, find strongest paths, and adapt the path and co-closed-set certificates. But that is a new formulation of the counting task, not a continuous version of the paper’s main contribution. The paper aims to verify a count tied to the ballots cast. If the input is only a distribution, a checker can certify the winner of that asserted distribution, but cannot establish that it represents the ballots. Adding a check against the ballot list restores that link, but also restores the finite ballot input and the work of tallying it.

So the proposed mirror may be worthwhile as a high-multiplicity counting project, but it does not supply the missing complexity result from this paper. I would not claim this proves that no useful continuous question can be asked; that universal negative is weak. The paper-specific case against is stronger: its contribution is formal verification and ballot-linked certification, and the offered mirror changes the object being verified.
