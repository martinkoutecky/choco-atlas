The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(T_A,T_B\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and each type's incomplete ranking of acceptable opposite-side types with ties of size at most \(L\), find a stable allocation \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\), subject to \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\). Stability means that for every acceptable pair \((i,j)\), either zero mass of type \(i\) strictly prefers \(j\) to its assigned partner (counting unmatched mass as worse than acceptable partners), or zero mass of type \(j\) strictly prefers \(i\) to its assigned partner. Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in the support size, input bit length, and \(L\) that returns a stable allocation of mass at least \(\frac{2L-1}{3L-2}\OPT_\infty\), where \(\OPT_\infty\) is the maximum mass in any stable allocation?

The model it lives in

Two populations are distributions over finitely many applicant and vacancy types. The decision variables \(x_{ij}\) give matched mass between types; the objective is maximum matched mass, with weak stability defined by the absence of any acceptable type pair for which both sides have positive mass willing to improve.

The objection that survived

Whether the authors would accept \(L\) as a bound on tied partner categories is inferred rather than established by the paper.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's approximation guarantee for maximum stable matching; it leaves the individual-level proposal analysis, tightness example, and cited discrete hardness unestablished in the mass model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time algorithm attain the ratio \(\frac{2L-1}{3L-2}\) for this mass model?
  • Is that ratio tight for type-level ties and weak stability?
  • For fixed \(L\), can maximum stable matched mass be computed exactly?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of the paper’s two-sided stable matching problem in a large, repeated market. Think of a national entry-level hiring exchange: many applicants share a small set of credential and location profiles, and many vacancies share a small set of job profiles. An applicant type and a vacancy type each have a complete description for the model, including an incomplete ranking of acceptable types on the other side, with ties of size at most \(L\). The regime is hundreds of thousands of people and vacancies represented by tens or hundreds of types.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for an instance of maximum-cardinality stable matching with incomplete preferences and ties of size at most \(L\), the paper gives a polynomial-time algorithm that returns a stable matching \(M\) satisfying
\[ |M|\ge \frac{2L-1}{3L-2}|\OPT|, \]
where \(\OPT\) is a maximum-cardinality stable matching. The introduction mentions NP-hardness, but that statement is unnumbered and cited to earlier work, so it is not a numbered hardness result to anchor this case. Theorem 1 is the paper’s clean named computational result.

Call the mirror Mass Maximum Stable Matching with Ties of Size \(L\). An instance gives two finite supported type sets \(T_A,T_B\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and each type’s acceptable opposite-side types and weak ranking of them. The support size is \(\tau=|T_A|+|T_B|\); the input includes the binary encodings of the masses and rankings. A solution is a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\). Unmatched mass is allowed and ranks below every acceptable partner type.

Use weak stability: for each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(r_A(i,j)\) be the mass of type \(i\) strictly preferring \(j\) to its assigned partner, including its unmatched mass; define \(r_B(i,j)\) symmetrically. The matching is stable if \(r_A(i,j)=0\) or \(r_B(i,j)=0\) for every such pair. Thus a positive mass of mutually willing agents could form a blocking pair exactly when both quantities are positive. The optimization objective is to maximize \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). The continuous question mirroring Theorem 1 is: given this instance, return a stable \(x\) with
\[ \sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\ \ge\ \frac{2L-1}{3L-2}\,\OPT_\infty, \]
where \(\OPT_\infty\) is the maximum matched mass among stable solutions. The output is the allocation itself, not an encoding of the problem. An algorithmic target would ask for time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and \(L\).

I expect this approximation question to be Class A, though the theorem does not establish that directly. The number of agents disappears into the type masses, and the paper’s proposal and matching structure gives a concrete starting point for an aggregate algorithm. The complementarity in the stability condition means a straightforward LP is not enough; a prover would need to show how the structural argument or an optimization formulation handles it. Further questions include whether the same ratio holds, whether it is tight in the mass model, and whether fixed \(L\) allows an exact algorithm.

The soft spot is the change from preferences over named people to preferences over partner types. In a literal clone expansion, all members of a partner type may form a tie whose size grows with the population, so the paper’s bound \(L\) on ties among individuals does not carry over unchanged. Here \(L\) bounds ties among partner categories, an explicit model change. I think the authors could recognize this as their problem’s anonymous large-market analogue: it retains incomplete preferences, bounded ties, weak stability, and maximum matched mass, while making the population itself continuous. But whether they would accept that co-modification is the key point a referee should test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s bounded-tie parameter \(L\) resists the natural high-multiplicity limit. With a fixed set of partner types, agents of a given type are indifferent among the many individuals in each acceptable partner type. Those ties grow with the population, so the paper’s fixed-\(L\) guarantee no longer applies: its ratio tends to \(\frac{3}{2}\) as \(L\) grows. The algorithm and its analysis also rely on individual vertices having degree at most \(L\); aggregating them into types removes that invariant.

One can preserve a useful tie bound by counting tied *partner types* instead of tied individuals. That gives a coherent mass-matching problem, but its stability conditions and approximation analysis must be developed at the type level. This is the best negative case: the paper’s distinctive theorem does not pass directly to a continuous population; the natural mirror requires a new structural result.

I can’t make that objection defeat the stronger mirror, though. Maximum stable matched mass under type-level preferences is a plausible computational question, and typed high-multiplicity work supports the regime’s relevance rather than showing the continuous problem is already answered. For example, Meeks and Rastegari study stable matching when agents rank types and give FPT results for several variants, including Max SMTI, parameterized by the number of types ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109)). That is adjacent evidence, not a continuous computational treatment.

The paper does have a named approximation theorem to anchor the mirror. I therefore can’t honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror survives: the case against is weak, and rests on the gap between individual-level and type-level ties, not on a fundamental flaw in the mass model.
