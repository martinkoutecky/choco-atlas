The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_a\) of worker types \(a\), each with a strict employer ranking and common prior employer \(h_a\), responsive firms with quotas \(q_f\) and strict priorities over types, and a rational starting allocation \(x^0_{a,h_a}=\mu_a\) that is individually rational and firm-quasi-stable, compute the worker-worst mass-stable allocation in which no worker mass is worse off than under \(x^0\). Determine whether it is obtained by assigning to each type the mass that the firm-optimal stable allocation \(x^F\) sends to employers preferred to \(h_a\), and leaving the remaining mass at \(h_a\).

The model it lives in

A finite set of complete worker types carries rational mass; each type includes its preferences and prior employer. Firms have responsive preferences induced by priorities over types and capacities scaled to the population. Each unit of mass receives an integral employer assignment, while type-level allocations may be fractional. Stability rules out any move of positive mass to a preferred firm that the firm would accept under its priority and capacity rule. The candidate restabilized allocation is the typewise join of the starting assignment with \(x^F\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 16’s characterization for firm-quasi-stable inputs; it leaves the lattice results, technical lemmas, and comparative results across the original and changed markets aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under the responsive mass-choice and blocking rules, prove existence and give an algorithm for the firm-optimal stable allocation \(x^F\), with running time in the number of firms \(m\), types \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).
  • Prove that the typewise join of \(x^0\) and \(x^F\) is mass-stable and is the worker-worst stable allocation no worse than \(x^0\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is restabilization. My sole anchor is Theorem 16, proved in this paper: for a firm-quasi-stable input matching \(\mu'\), the Set Offering algorithm’s output is \(\mathrm{SO}(\mu')=\mu'\mathbin{\underline{\vee}_W}\mu^F\), where \(\mu^F\) is the firm-optimal stable matching and each worker gets the preferred of their assignments in \(\mu'\) and \(\mu^F\). This gives an exact characterization of the restabilization outcome. The paper has no named hardness result or explicit complexity classification; Theorem 16 is its best algorithmic anchor because it computes the underlying outcome by a simple representation.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large labor market after retirements or the creation of positions: many workers share a small set of complete types, each specifying their employer ranking and their pre-change employer. Employers have responsive preferences over worker types and capacities that scale with the population. The number of workers can be very large while the number of types \(\tau\) and the number of employers \(m\) remain moderate. Including the pre-change employer in a type ensures that a type’s mass has a common starting assignment.

Call the mirror Fractional SO Restabilization. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_a\) for finitely many worker types \(a\), rational employer capacities, worker rankings over employers and unemployment, employer priorities over worker types, and an induced post-change assignment \(x^0\). Require \(x^0\) to be individually rational and firm-quasi-stable: no employer would drop an incumbent when considering the workers who prefer it to their current assignment. A feasible solution is a mass allocation \(x\) among employers and unemployment, respecting worker supply and employer capacities. It is stable if there is no positive mass of workers who prefer an employer to their current assignment while that employer has room or would accept them over some current assignees.

The question is to find the worker-worst stable allocation among those in which no worker mass is worse off than under \(x^0\). Concretely, compute the firm-optimal stable allocation \(x^F\), then give each type the mass assigned to employers it prefers to its old employer under \(x^F\); leave the rest at its old employer. This is the mass version of Theorem 16’s pointwise choice. The requested output is that exact allocation, represented as a rational flow.

I expect this version to be tractable: type-level deferred acceptance should compute \(x^F\) using finitely many employer–type interactions, and the restabilization formula then gives the answer. This is a plausible Class A question, not a result established by the paper. It covers the paper’s restabilization characterization, not its whole analysis of firm-quasi-stable matchings. Further questions are whether the algorithm and lattice characterization extend to broader substitutable preferences, and how the fractional output relates to discrete high-multiplicity instances.

The weak point is real: Theorem 16 is a structural characterization in a finite-agent model, not a runtime theorem, and the paper does not establish its continuum counterpart. The mirror also uses responsive employer preferences to give fractional choices a canonical meaning. Still, the proposed problem preserves the paper’s central object—restabilization after population change—and its worker-worst stable outcome. Scaling capacities and allowing type-level mass to split across employers are co-modifications required by the population continuum, not a change of subject.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 16 is not a computational complexity result. It identifies the Set Offering algorithm’s output as a lattice-theoretic expression; the paper gives no complexity classification for computing that output. The proposed Fractional SO problem therefore adds a new task—computing a firm-optimal stable allocation in a chosen mass-market model—and uses Theorem 16 only to describe the final step. That makes the anchor less direct than the proponent suggests.

There is also a real tension between faithful types and the paper’s preference model. Firms have strict preferences over sets of named workers. If those preferences distinguish workers, a complete worker type must include how each firm ranks that worker, and the number of types may grow with the population. Keeping \(\tau\) small requires firms to treat workers within a type identically, changing the preference structure that underlies the paper’s results. A fractional version must also specify how firms choose among portions of an interchangeable type.

That tension does not defeat the best mirror. A large cohort with shared employer rankings and a common previous employer, firms that treat equivalent workers alike, and capacities scaled with the cohort is a coherent regime. Including the previous employer in the type preserves the information relevant to restabilization, and a rational mass allocation preserves the outcome at type level. The proponent’s construction addresses the main identity objection; it does not rely on an obviously meaningless population limit.

So the negative case is weak. Theorem 16 is a structural result rather than a complexity theorem, and the proposed continuum version is partly a new stable-matching computation problem. But neither point shows that the question is outside the programme’s scope or unworthy of study. I cannot honestly rule out this mirror in every plausible scenario; the sole anchor survives the strongest objections.
