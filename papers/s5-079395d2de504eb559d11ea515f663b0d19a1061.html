The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see an eligible computational anchor in this paper, so I cannot make a strong positive case under the stated rules. Its named results are analytical claims about incentives and mechanism design, not results classifying the complexity or algorithms of a computational problem.

The closest candidate is Proposition 1, proved here in Appendix A.1. Under the condition in Lemma 1 that prevents a bidder from claiming \(S>V\), it says a bidder chooses the business plan \((V^*,C^*)\) with greatest social surplus \(\psi^*=(V^*-P_0)q-C^*\), subject to the market-liquidity constraint, and makes no bid if \(\psi^*\leq 0\). The strongest zero-profit bid uses \(S=V^*\) and \(R=C^*-(V^*-P_0)t_bq\), yielding auction parameter \(A^*=\psi^*\) and leaving all surplus with the other token holders. If profit \(\Pi_b\) is feasible, the bid instead has \(R=\Pi_b+C^*-(V^*-P_0)t_bq\) and \(A=\psi^*-\Pi_b\). The maximum feasible profit is \(t_m(V^*-P_0)q-C^*\), leaving at least \(F=(1-t_m)(V^*-P_0)q\) for the other holders. This is a substantial result, but it characterizes incentives and optimal choices; it does not give an algorithm or a tractability result for computing them. Lemma 1 and Corollary 1 likewise state conditions on truthful bidding and deposit size, not computational results.

There is a conceivable population angle: large DAOs have many token holders, and the paper explicitly discusses a continuum of potential bidders with densely distributed auction values in Section 4.1.3. But the continuum-bidder passage is an unnumbered assumption about bidding intensity, not a computational finding. And the paper’s proposition turns on each bidder’s private business-plan capability, while the holder population largely enters through token shares and pro-rata transfers. A continuous distribution over holders would therefore change little about the problem Proposition 1 analyzes; a distribution over bidder capabilities would be closer, but the paper offers no named computational result about that problem.

So I would not state a continuous problem as though it were anchored by Proposition 1. The strongest honest positive observation is that the paper supplies a potentially relevant DAO setting and a named surplus-maximization result. It does not supply the required computational anchor, and stretching that observation into a complexity mirror would make the case weaker, not stronger.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its numbered Lemma, Corollary, and Proposition characterize incentives and bidding behavior. They do not define a computational problem with an encoded input, an algorithm, or a complexity claim. The paper is doing mechanism design, not charting the complexity of a social-choice problem.

Proposition 1 is the closest candidate, but its best possible mirror still misses the programme’s population axis. The proposition says that a bidder selects a business plan \((V^*,C^*)\) maximizing \(\psi=(V-P_0)q-C\), then bids using \(S=V^*\) and an appropriate \(R\). The choice depends on the bidder’s private value function \(V(C)\); token holders enter through aggregate quantities such as \(q\), \(t_d\), and pro-rata transfers. Replacing holders with a distribution over types does not change what Proposition 1 characterizes. Replacing bidders with a distribution over business-plan capabilities is a more serious extension, but it makes bidders—not the society of voters—the continuous population and creates a new problem about representing and optimizing over private technologies. The paper gives no computational result about that problem.

Lemma 1 and Corollary 1 do not provide separate anchors. They give conditions on the deposit-forfeit function \(\phi\) that deter overstated value claims and imply a lower bound on \(D_v\). A stronger proposed mirror might ask how to design or verify such a deposit schedule across a distribution of bidder types. But that would require specifying a new input model for schedules, bidder capabilities, and outcomes. The population of token holders still contributes only aggregate quantities to these conditions; making it continuous adds no type-level computational structure to the stated results.

The paper’s continuum of potential bidders in Section 4.1.3 does not rescue the mirror. It is an assumption about bidding intensity and collusion, not a computational finding. Likewise, its discussion of a continuum of bidder types in connection with full surplus extraction is economic mechanism-design context, not an algorithmic result. These are not collisions with continuous-population computational work; they simply do not supply the missing computational anchor.

A different proposal might continueize holders’ governance preferences and study their effect on control auctions. That would add preferences and collective behavior the paper does not model: the auction’s result turns on bidders’ business plans and bids, while holders receive pro-rata benefits. The paper’s references to voting pathologies motivate the mechanism but do not specify a voting rule or prove a computational result about one. Such a project could be worthwhile on its own, but it would not be a mirror of the paper’s named results.

This negative case is strongest under the paper-anchored standard: there is no stated computational question whose continuous-population version the programme could classify. It cannot prove that no useful project could ever be built from the subject matter; a sufficiently rich model of bidder or holder types might inspire one. But the more that model supplies the problem’s computational content, the less it is a mirror of this paper.
