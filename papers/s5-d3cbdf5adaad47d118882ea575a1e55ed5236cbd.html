The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), target \(c^*\), rational \(w>1\), and fixed sincere rankings \(\rho_1,\rho_2\), consider sincere cohort masses \(w/(w+3)\) and \(1/(w+3)\), plus a coordinated manipulator cohort of mass \(2/(w+3)\). Does there exist a rational allocation \(x_\pi\ge0\) over rankings \(\pi\in\Pi(C)\), with \(\sum_\pi x_\pi=2/(w+3)\), such that \(c^*\) has at least as many total Borda points as every candidate after the manipulator mass reports according to that allocation?

The model it lives in

A society with two fixed sincere ranking types of masses \(w/(w+3)\) and \(1/(w+3)\), and a coordinated manipulator type of mass \(2/(w+3)\). The manipulators choose a mass allocation over rankings; the objective is feasibility of the Borda co-winner inequalities for \(c^*\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the NP-hardness ranges of Theorems 4 and 2. It does not cover Theorem 1’s algorithm for \(w\le1\), or the paper’s broader conjecture about other numbers of manipulators and non-manipulators.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(w=1\) hardness boundary persist when the manipulator cohort can divide its reports continuously?
  • How well does continuous feasibility predict finite large-cohort instances, especially when winner margins are small or tied?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s coalitional Borda manipulation problem. It keeps the target, the Borda winner condition, and the manipulators’ freedom to submit any rankings. What changes is the regime: the voters become large cohorts with shared types, and a coalition may divide its mass among different reports.

The two hardness anchors are Theorem 4, proved in this paper, and Theorem 2, also proved here. In the paper’s 2-2BM problem, there are \(z\) candidates, two fixed non-manipulator rankings, and two manipulators who may choose rankings to make the target \(c^*\) a co-winner. One non-manipulator’s Borda scores are multiplied by \(w\); the other non-manipulator and both manipulators have unit weight. Theorem 4 says this problem is NP-hard for rational \(1<w<3\). Theorem 2 says it is NP-hard for rational \(w\ge3\). The reductions are proved here; their starting hardness fact for 2NMTS is established from a cited RN3DM result. Together, these anchors cover the paper’s hardness range \(w>1\).

For each anchor, I would ask the same continuous problem family, restricted to its corresponding range of \(w\). Call it Continuous 2-2 Borda Coalition Manipulation at \(w\). An instance has \(C\), a target \(c^*\), two fixed sincere rankings \(\rho_1,\rho_2\), and rational \(w>1\). The population has total mass one: \(\rho_1\)’s sincere cohort has mass \(w/(w+3)\), \(\rho_2\)’s has mass \(1/(w+3)\), and a coordinated manipulator cohort has mass \(2/(w+3)\). Thus the weighted non-manipulator in the paper is represented by a cohort \(w\) times the size of the unit cohort. For rational \(w=a/b\), scaling gives integer cohort sizes \(a,b,2b\), and these can all grow by a common factor while the number of distinct types stays at most three.

The action is a rational mass allocation \(x_\pi\ge0\) over rankings \(\pi\in\Pi(C)\), with \(\sum_\pi x_\pi=2/(w+3)\). Write \(s_B(\pi,c)=z-\operatorname{rank}_{\pi}(c)\) and \(H(c)=\frac{w}{w+3}s_B(\rho_1,c)+\frac{1}{w+3}s_B(\rho_2,c)\). The question is whether there is such an allocation satisfying \(H(c^*)+\sum_\pi x_\pi s_B(\pi,c^*)\ge H(c)+\sum_\pi x_\pi s_B(\pi,c)\) for every \(c\in C\). A solution is the allocation itself: it describes how much coalition mass submits each ranking. The objective is feasibility, just as in the paper, where success means that \(c^*\) is among the highest-scoring candidates.

This is a plausible setting for large ranked-choice elections or governance votes: many participants share a ranking and a coordinated bloc seeks to elect a particular candidate. The type records the ranking and role; the coalition members are indistinguishable for this problem because their common objective is \(c^*\). Here the two named hardness anchors give two parameter ranges of the continuous question, not a claim that their discrete hardness transfers.

I expect both continuous questions to be tractable. Their LPs have exponentially many ranking variables, but pricing is easy: for any linear objective over rankings, sort candidates by their coefficient and assign the Borda scores \(0,\ldots,z-1\) in that order. That gives a polynomial-time optimization oracle, so standard LP methods can decide feasibility in polynomial time in \(z\) and the input bit length. The resulting solution can have polynomial support. This makes the mirror useful even if its answer differs from the paper’s: it tests whether the NP-hardness survives when a large coalition can distribute its reports as mass.

The weakest point is the change in coalition size and action granularity. The theorems concern exactly two manipulators, each of whom chooses one whole ranking; the continuous model lets a large cohort split across rankings. That fractional freedom may be exactly why the LP is tractable, so this is not an exact continuation of fixed-size 2-2BM. It is, however, a natural high-multiplicity version of coalitional manipulation, with the same decision-makers, target, scoring rule, and winning condition. The follow-up questions are whether finite large-cohort instances approximate this continuous answer when margins are small or tied, and whether the paper’s \(w=1\) boundary persists once coalition reports can be divided continuously.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 4 and Theorem 2 concern an unusually small strategic electorate: exactly two manipulators and two sincere voters. Their hardness reductions encode 2NMTS in how the two manipulators assign complete Borda rank sequences across a growing set of candidates. The paper has no electorate-size parameter to take to a limit. If the two manipulators remain individuals while the sincere electorate grows, their normalized influence vanishes unless their weights grow with the population. If instead they become a positive-mass coalition, its members can divide their reports among rankings. That replaces the two indivisible permutations at the heart of both reductions. Either route changes the strategic object, rather than simply passing the paper’s instance to a continuum.

That objection defeats a *literal* continuous version of these theorems, but it does not defeat the better mirror the proponent describes. For rational \(w=a/b>1\), scale the four roles into cohorts of sizes \(aK,bK,bK,bK\): the weighted sincere cohort, the unit sincere cohort, and two manipulator cohorts. Let the coordinated manipulators divide their mass among rankings. This gives the problem a genuine high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s own conjecture about the relation between total manipulator and non-manipulator weights makes it recognizable as the authors’ problem. Theorem 4 and Theorem 2 then anchor the ranges \(1<w<3\) and \(w\ge3\) of the same continuous question. The fact that fractional reports may remove the discrete hardness is not a reason to reject the mirror; it is exactly the sort of outcome the programme is meant to investigate.

I therefore cannot honestly defeat either anchor. The negative case is that the continuous question is a generalization to large coalitions, not a limit of the paper’s exact four-voter instances. But the programme allows a plausible high-multiplicity scenario beyond the paper’s original framing, and here the paper itself points toward that scenario. The claimed “no worthwhile mirror” is weaker than the case for one.
