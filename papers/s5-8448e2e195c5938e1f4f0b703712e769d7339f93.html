The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of firms \(F\), candidate types \(T\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings of firms by each type and of types by each firm, and binary-rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_f\), can one compute a stable rational assignment \(x_{tf}\) in time polynomial in \(|F|\), \(|T|\), and input bit length \(L\)? Require \(x_{tf}\geq 0\), \(\sum_f x_{tf}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tf}\leq\kappa_f\), and, for each pair \((t,f)\), either no mass of type \(t\) would prefer \(f\) to its assignment or \(f\) has no capacity available after its assignments to types it ranks above \(t\).

The model it lives in

A finite set of firms faces a distribution over candidate types. Members of each type share firm preferences and are indistinguishable in firms' priorities; firms have per-capita capacities and strict priorities over types. The decision variable is a mass assignment \(x_{tf}\), and stability excludes any pair \((t,f)\) with both willing candidate mass and capacity available after higher-priority assignments.

The objection that survived

The paper assumes strict rankings of named candidates, so the proposed mirror is a category-priority extension rather than a high-multiplicity encoding of all instances with individual priorities.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computing a stable assignment for type-homogeneous hiring cohorts with per-capita firm capacities. It leaves the paper's individual-priority implementation comparison and its experimental results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a stable mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(|F|\), \(|T|\), and \(L\)?
  • Do firm-optimality and the Rural Hospitals invariants extend to rational type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is a small, classical result in the paper’s SMP section. Theorem 3.1 says that, for the Stable Marriage Problem with equal numbers of men and women and complete, strict preference lists, the Gale–Shapley algorithm always produces a stable matching. The paper also gives the runtime facts in Lemma 3.3 (at most \(n^2\) proposals) and Lemma 3.4 (each proposal takes \(O(1)\) time), yielding an \(O(n^2)\) algorithm. These are stated in this paper, but their proofs are omitted and referred to earlier sources. Together they anchor one problem: computing a stable assignment by deferred acceptance. The paper has no named hardness result for its Firms/Candidates problem.

My lead mirror is Continuous Type-Mass Firms/Candidates Stable Matching. Consider a finite set \(F\) of firms and a finite support \(T\) of candidate types. The input gives each type \(t\) a binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); each firm \(f\) a binary-rational capacity \(\kappa_f\), measured per capita; each type a strict ranking of the firms; and each firm a strict ranking of the candidate types. A type means that its members have the same firm preferences and are indistinguishable to firms for matching decisions. This describes, for example, a large hiring cohort with few preference archetypes and firms whose vacancies scale with the cohort.

A solution is a rational mass-assignment matrix \(x=(x_{tf})\), where \(x_{tf}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to firm \(f\), satisfying \(x_{tf}\geq 0\), \(\sum_f x_{tf}\leq\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{tf}\leq\kappa_f\). Unassigned candidate mass and unfilled capacity are allowed. Define \(A_{tf}\) as the mass of type \(t\) that is unmatched or assigned to a firm it ranks below \(f\), and \(B_{tf}\) as \(f\)’s unfilled capacity plus the mass it has assigned to types it ranks below \(t\). The assignment is stable when, for every \(t,f\), at least one of \(A_{tf}\) and \(B_{tf}\) is zero. Thus no positive mass of candidates and capacity can be matched at \(f\) while improving both sides. There is no optimization objective: the requested output is any stable assignment.

I expect this problem to be tractable. A type-level deferred-acceptance process can offer mass in preference order and let each side retain its most-preferred mass up to its capacity. With finitely many types and firms, each type–firm pair can be processed only a bounded number of times; the arithmetic uses the rational input masses and capacities. The natural complexity question is whether this yields a polynomial bound in \(|F|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\). Further questions include whether a firm-optimal stable mass assignment is well-defined, when it is unique, and whether it can be rounded to a stable discrete assignment.

The connection to the paper is the stable-assignment problem at the heart of its SMP and FCP sections, including many-to-one capacity. The paper presents the FCP as a hiring problem and stresses that stable assignments matter in large matching markets. The mirror covers that matching question. It does not mirror the paper’s specific comparison-count improvement.

The weak point is the preference model. The paper ranks named individuals strictly; this mirror ranks types, treating members of a type as interchangeable to firms. That change is natural for a high-multiplicity hiring cohort, but it could matter to these authors because their paper explicitly assumes complete lists with no ties. The scenario helps: firms can rank standardized candidate categories strictly, while individuals within a category are indistinguishable for placement. Still, the paper’s named anchor is the classical one-to-one SMP result, and the continuous many-to-one model is a type-level extension. The positive case is therefore strongest as a plausible new computational question about their stable-matching problem, rather than as a direct continuation of their implementation theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that the paper’s exact input has no high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 3.1 is a real anchor: together with Lemmas 3.3 and 3.4, it says deferred acceptance finds a stable matching in \(O(n^2)\) time. Calling it classical or noting that the paper’s contribution is mainly implementation does not defeat it; one surviving mirror is enough.

But in the paper’s FCP, firms rank named candidates in strict order. A candidate’s complete type must include how every firm ranks them, so candidates are distinguishable even when they share the same preferences over firms. Preserving those rankings gives \(\tau=|C|\): replacing counts with masses has not compressed the population or made identity-specific priorities disappear.

The proposed class-based model does create a genuine mass problem, but it does so by changing the firms’ choice rule. Firms rank candidate types rather than applicants, and the stability condition \(A_{tf}=0\) or \(B_{tf}=0\) allows assignments that can split a type’s mass. That is coherent for standardized cohorts, but its small support comes from treating members of a class as interchangeable to firms, whereas the paper’s firms rank applicants individually. Adding individual priority scores to preserve strict rankings restores the distinction between candidates; with finite support, the types then proliferate, and a continuous priority distribution would need a different input representation.

That is the best negative argument against this anchor: the mirror’s tractable population structure depends on replacing the paper’s individual priority data with class priorities. It does not, however, defeat the hiring-cohort version. If these authors would recognize category-based hiring as their stable-matching problem, the mirror remains sensible. I can make the case against this particular bridge, but I cannot honestly establish the requested universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
