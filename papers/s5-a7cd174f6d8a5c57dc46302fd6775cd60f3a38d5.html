The anchor — hardness

Theorem C.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem C.1 — survived: The opponent’s candidate-supply objection is answered by the high-multiplicity regime, where each positive-mass type can supply at least \(k\) representatives.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational metric space \((T,d)\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) with positive mass on each type, suppose each type can supply at least \(k\) distinct candidates. A committee is an integer vector \(y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}y_t=k\), and its normalized cost is \(C_\mu(y)=\sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_t y_u d(t,u)\); let \(C_\mu^\star\) be the minimum over such committees. For \(U\subseteq T\), let \(\mu(U)=\sum_{t\in U}\mu_t\), \(D(U)=\max_{t,t'\in U}d(t,t')\), and \(N_r(U)=\{u\in T:\min_{t\in U}d(t,u)\le r\}\). Given rational \(\rho>1\), decide whether there is a committee \(y\) such that, for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every \(U\subseteq T\) with \(\mu(U)\ge\ell/k\), \(\sum_{u\in N_{D(U)}(U)}y_u\ge\ell\), and \(C_\mu(y)<\rho C_\mu^\star\).

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over metric-location types, with mass divisible within each type; committee seats remain integral, and a type may supply up to \(k\) candidates. Use the paper’s sum cost weighted by \(\mu\), and define PRF using population-share thresholds \(\ell/k\) and representatives within the group’s diameter.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only the PRF branch of Theorem C.1’s cost-threshold hardness; it leaves the mJR branch and the paper’s algorithmic guarantees and other fairness results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a focused case around Theorem C.1, proved by the authors in Appendix C via a reduction from CLIQUE. It says that, for every fixed \(\gamma\in(0,1)\), it is coNP-hard to determine whether there exists a PRF or mJR committee whose cost is better than a \(1+\gamma-O(1/\sqrt n)\) approximation to OPT. In particular, it is coNP-hard to decide whether such a committee exists with ratio \(2-O(1/\sqrt n)\). I would mirror its PRF branch.

Call the mirror Continuous PRF Cost Threshold. An instance consists of a finite rational metric space \(T\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over its points, a committee size \(k\), and a rational threshold \(\rho>1\). Each point \(t\in T\) is a voter type: everyone of that type has the same location and hence the same distances to every other type. A solution is an integer vector \(y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t y_t=k\), where \(y_t\) is the number of committee seats filled by representatives of type \(t\). The population is continuous, but the \(k\) seats remain indivisible.

The cost is
\[ C_\mu(y)=\sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_t y_u d(t,u), \]
and \(C_\mu^\star\) is the minimum of this cost over all such committees. For \(U\subseteq T\), write \(\mu(U)=\sum_{t\in U}\mu_t\), \(D(U)=\max_{t,t'\in U}d(t,t')\), and \(N_r(U)=\{u\in T:\min_{t\in U}d(u,t)\le r\}\). The decision question is whether there is a PRF committee \(y\) with \(C_\mu(y)<\rho C_\mu^\star\), where PRF means that for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every \(U\) with \(\mu(U)\ge\ell/k\),
\[ \sum_{u\in N_{D(U)}(U)}y_u\ge\ell. \]
This is the paper’s population-share version of its “large group gets enough nearby representatives” condition. The theorem supplies a hard family of thresholds \(\rho\) approaching \(1+\gamma\) at the stated \(O(1/\sqrt n)\) rate; \(\rho\) is an explicit input to the continuous decision problem.

A plausible regime is a large electorate choosing a small council: for example, \(n\) in the hundreds of thousands, \(k\) in the tens, and \(\tau=|T|\) recurring ideological or locality profiles. People sharing a profile have the same location in the metric and are interchangeable for the paper’s cost and fairness tests. Their mass is their share of the population. At each positive-mass type there are enough potential representatives to fill up to \(k\) seats, as one expects when the population is large relative to the committee. This keeps the paper’s choice of representatives meaningful while making only the population continuous.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction’s apparent combinatorics lie in the geometry of the support points and in \(k\), rather than in having a large number of copies of each point. A finite rational distribution can be scaled to a high-multiplicity electorate by cloning every type: the masses, distances, normalized costs, and proportionality thresholds stay the same, while each positive-mass type can supply all \(k\) seats. This is my inference from the construction, not a continuous hardness theorem proved in the paper. It also explains why this is a genuine computational question: the unconstrained cost optimum is simple, but asking whether a fair committee can beat a specified cost threshold is the hard decision problem.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the PRF cost-threshold hardness in Theorem C.1, not every fairness notion or result in the paper. It raises useful follow-up questions: does the hardness persist under restricted metric spaces such as trees or fixed-dimensional Euclidean spaces? What changes when \(k\) is fixed, or when algorithms are parameterized by \(k\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length? And do the paper’s approximation guarantees extend directly to rational population masses?

My weakest point is the finite-to-continuous candidate supply. A positive-mass type can supply repeated committee seats in the mirror, whereas a finite instance may have too few agents at that location. High multiplicity resolves that mismatch by cloning, and the construction’s graph-copy structure appears to make repeated selections at one point unable to substitute for representation across distinct copies. Still, that last step is the part of the reduction I would want checked most carefully before claiming the hardness transfer as a theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is candidate supply. The paper’s agents are also the candidates: if type \(t\) occurs \(n_t\) times, a committee must satisfy \(y_t\le n_t\). The distribution \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) forgets those counts, so it does not by itself determine which committees are feasible. The proposed model instead lets any positive-mass type supply all \(k\) seats. That is a real change to the problem, and the reduction would need to survive it.

But the objection does not defeat the better mirror. In a high-multiplicity version, each positive-mass type can supply \(k\) distinct people, all with the same location. Cloning each point in the reduction preserves the mass distribution and normalized cost. A clique of size \(q=n/k\) in a graph copy still has mass \(1/k\) and diameter \(1-\varepsilon\), so PRF still requires a representative near that copy. Repeated representatives at the center cannot meet that requirement, and adding representatives elsewhere cannot lower the cost. The candidate-supply caveat the other analyst raised is therefore likely resolvable; the reduction appears to survive it.

Nor does the possibility that the continuous problem has the same hardness answer make it a collision. Prior work gives algorithms for PRF clustering and studies metric proportional representation, but those results do not answer this paper’s joint sum-cost and fairness-threshold question ([PRF clustering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13917), [metric proportional representation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10369)).

So I can make a case that the proposed formulation needs its candidate-supply assumption stated and justified. I cannot honestly use it to defeat Theorem C.1: under a plausible high-multiplicity regime, the anchor remains well-posed and may transfer directly. The negative case is weak here.
