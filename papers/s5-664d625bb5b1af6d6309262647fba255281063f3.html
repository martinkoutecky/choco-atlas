The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for a continuous mirror: it establishes no problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar.

Theorem 1 is a named impossibility result, but it is axiomatic rather than computational. It says that when the agenda is non-simple and non-separable, every consistent, full-domain aggregation function satisfying acceptance/rejection neutrality is dictatorial. The paper proves this theorem. Corollary 1 further says such a function is dictatorial on every non-simple, non-separable sub-agenda. Neither result studies the computational complexity of a problem.

A continuous population model of judgment aggregation is conceivable, with mass assigned to consistent judgment types and a collective judgment as output. But it would mirror this paper’s axiomatic question, outside the requested computational scope, so it cannot supply a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s strongest possible anchor, and Corollary 1 adds a local version of the same result. Both are axiomatic classifications: under stated conditions, every consistent aggregation function is dictatorial. They give no computational problem, input representation, objective, or complexity claim to mirror. The paper may be about judgment aggregation, but that alone does not make its results computational.

The best continuous-population version would put mass on judgment types and ask whether consistency and acceptance/rejection neutrality force a dictatorial aggregator. That could be a worthwhile axiomatic question, but it remains outside ChoCo’s computational scope. To make it computational, one would have to add a task—such as finding the least mass of voters whose changes alter the collective judgment—and specify an aggregator, costs, and a target. Those additions might motivate a new ChoCo problem, but they are not mirrors of a result in this paper.

Repeated panels or large electorates could make a high-multiplicity population plausible; that does not supply the missing computational question. There is also a mismatch in the theorem’s central conclusion: a finite dictatorship follows a named individual, while a distribution over types forgets which individuals hold each judgment. A continuous analogue could be defined, but deciding what should replace individual dictatorship would be part of a new axiomatic model.

So the case against a worthwhile computational mirror of this paper is strong: neither named result supplies a computational anchor. A continuous axiomatic extension may be interesting, but it would not fill the programme’s stated gap.
