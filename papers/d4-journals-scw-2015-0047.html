What the mirror covers

Lemma 6 and Proposition 9 are the proposed anchors; both concern equilibrium existence, structure, or welfare. The other named results likewise characterize equilibria or compare outcomes, and none supplies a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a regime, not an eligible computational mirror: imagine a very large jury or review committee with two publicly identifiable preference types, hawks and doves. Each member of a type has the same wrongful-conviction aversion and signal accuracy; the numbers of members grow while the two type proportions stay roughly fixed. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting for this model: many jurors, few preference types.

The closest named results are Lemma 6 and Proposition 9, but neither is a computational anchor under the criteria given. Lemma 6 says that a reactive simple subgroup-deliberation equilibrium exists, and that if there are \(K>1\) such equilibria, their threshold pairs form the sequence \((t_H,t_D), (t_H-1,t_D+1),\ldots,(t_H-K+1,t_D+K-1)\). Proposition 9 compares those equilibria with the unique reactive simple plenary equilibrium: in one stated case a subgroup equilibrium is outcome-equivalent and every other one strongly Pareto improves; in the other case every reactive subgroup equilibrium strongly Pareto improves. These are equilibrium and welfare claims, not results about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, so it supplies no eligible anchors and therefore no honest per-anchor computational questions to hand to a prover.

There is a natural analytic extension: let the hawk and dove shares converge to fixed proportions as jury size grows, then study limiting threshold shares and welfare comparisons. But that is outside ChoCo’s computational remit. It also has a real modeling obstacle: the paper’s equilibrium conditions rely on an individual juror being pivotal under unanimity. In an atomless population, one juror has zero mass, so the finite model’s pivotality conditions do not carry over directly. Replacing unanimity with a threshold on the mass of votes would change the voting rule.

So the positive case is that the paper’s two-type jury is a plausible high-multiplicity subject for a future study. The weakness is decisive for this task: that case does not yield a computational mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case establishes a plausible high-multiplicity *regime*: a large jury can contain many hawks and doves. That is real support for the model, and the paper’s equilibrium and welfare results are worth studying. But it does not supply a computational problem to mirror. None of its named results asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a task; they characterize equilibria and compare their welfare. That is the main obstacle for a ChoCo mirror.

The best computational version of Lemma 6 would take type masses and the signal and preference parameters as input, then compute reactive subgroup equilibria and order them by welfare. In the atomless model, however, the equilibrium conditions in Lemma 3 compare a juror’s incentives at adjacent signal counts, \(t_j-1\) and \(t_j\). A single juror changes a group’s signal mass by zero, so those pivotality comparisons disappear. Aggregate signal shares instead converge to \(p\) or \(1-p\), making them deterministic conditional on the state. The limiting equilibrium set therefore needs a new selection or refinement rule; retaining finite-\(n\) pivotality to select it retains the finite model’s corrections. Either way, Lemma 6’s threshold characterization does not become a well-defined algorithmic task over continuous societies.

The strongest version of Proposition 9 would ask whether subgroup deliberation still Pareto-improves on plenary deliberation in that limit. If truthful type-level signal reports are selected, the doves’ aggregate signals already reveal the state in a large jury, so the extra hawk information has no limiting welfare gain. If truth-telling is not selected, atomless individuals have no incentive to make their messages informative, and the comparison depends on the added equilibrium-selection rule. The strict finite-jury comparison thus either collapses in the limit or requires extra modelling choices. And computing this comparison is not a task the paper formulates as an algorithmic problem.

One could extend the paper to many preference types and ask for algorithms to find or select equilibria. The authors themselves identify more types as an extension, so that direction is plausible. But it would add the computational question that the paper lacks; it would not mirror one of its results. The honest negative case is therefore strong for *this paper’s* continuous mirror, though not airtight against a broader ChoCo project that deliberately adds algorithmic equilibrium computation.
