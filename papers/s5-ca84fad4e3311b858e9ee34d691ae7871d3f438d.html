The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting hardness, tractability, or a parameterized complexity classification. The closest results, both proved here, are Theorem 1, which says the \(p\)- and \(u\)-independencies of any \((p,u)\) pair have a perfect map, and Theorem 2, which says \(p\)- and \(u\)-independence jointly imply conditional expected utility independence. Those are structural results, not complexity results. The paper explicitly leaves the computational efficiency of probabilistic and strategic inference for future work.

The best lead for a population scenario would be a large organization with many decision-makers sharing a small set of complete EUN templates: a type would specify the relevant variables, beliefs, utilities, and available decisions; mass would be the fraction of the organization with that template. One might ask how to allocate tasks or compute a mass-weighted optimal policy. But the paper models one decision-maker’s variables and decisions, not a population of agents, and neither theorem establishes a computational claim about that new problem. The auction example is likewise a single-agent analysis, not a population algorithm.

So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover, and no tractability-versus-hardness prediction to attach to an anchor. The weakness is decisive: turning the organization lead into a formal problem would create a new research question, not mirror a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational anchor to defeat: the case for already identifies that cleanly. Theorem 1 gives a perfect map for the \(p\)- and \(u\)-independencies of one \((p,u)\) model; Theorem 2 derives conditional EU independence from them. Neither states an algorithm or a complexity result. Section 6 explicitly leaves the efficiency of probabilistic and strategic inference for future work. Those results may motivate computational questions, but there is no result here for a continuous population to mirror.

The strongest repair would be a society whose types are complete EUN templates, then ask how to compute aggregate expected utility or allocate tasks. With one shared template, mass adds no computational content: absent constraints coupling people, inference reduces to the representative agent’s problem. With heterogeneous templates, aggregation can destroy the independence properties the theorems establish for each type. A worthwhile aggregate problem would need a new objective and new results; it would not be a continuization of Theorem 1 or 2. Turning the variables inside one EUN continuous would change the uncertainty model, not continuize the population.

The auction example does not supply a stronger anchor. It models two bidders from one agent’s perspective and derives a bidding choice; it does not give a computational result. Repeating that auction across a mass of agents gives independent copies, while making the auction itself a large-population game changes the problem.

I cannot rule out a useful future population model of EUNs. The paper’s mention of organizational task allocation leaves room for one. But that would be a new computational question in the paper’s orbit, not a continuous mirror of a result it proves. The negative case is strongest on that boundary: there is no qualifying anchor here, though the broader research direction is not thereby ruled out.
