The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Theorem 1 says that strategy-proofness, unanimity, and multidimensional replacement monotonicity imply strong Pareto-undominated strategy-proofness. Theorem 2 says every multidimensional sequential rule has that property. Corollary 1 establishes it for the multidimensional uniform rule, and Theorem 3 characterizes that rule using Pareto-undominated strategy-proofness, equal treatment, and replacement monotonicity. These are axiomatic claims about allocation rules; none classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard.

The most plausible population setting would be a large rationing economy where many households share each preference type: type \(t\) is a complete preference relation, mass \(\mu_t\) is its population share, and \(x_t\) is the bundle assigned to that type, with \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=\Omega\). But the paper’s individual strategyproofness may become vacuous in an atomless population, since one person’s report does not change the distribution. Making deviations by whole types consequential would change the strategic question. And the paper’s central results are axiomatic, while ChoCo’s scope is computational. So this is a plausible setting to investigate, but there is no honest per-anchor continuous computational question to hand a prover from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The four strongest candidate anchors in the proponent’s case—Theorems 1–3 and Corollary 1—are axiomatic results. Theorem 1 gives a sufficient condition for a rule to be strongly Pareto-undominated; Theorem 2 applies it to sequential rules; Corollary 1 applies it to the uniform rule; Theorem 3 characterizes that rule. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a finite computational problem. A continuum version might yield another axiomatic characterization, but that is outside ChoCo’s computational brief.

The proposed rationing economy is a sensible population regime, and divisible goods are not a reason to dismiss it. Even after normalizing the endowment per capita, a finite-support distribution of preference types gives a meaningful way to compute the uniform allocation: for each good, find a threshold \(\lambda\) satisfying \(\sum_t \mu_t\min\{p_t^\ell,\lambda\}=\omega^\ell\), or the corresponding maximum formula when aggregate peaks are below supply. That is a valid computational task, but it is a new, apparently straightforward evaluation problem—not a mirror of any result the paper proves. The paper’s central theorems concern whole rules over all preference profiles, not the cost of evaluating one rule on a supplied profile.

There is also no direct way to carry the paper’s strategyproofness question over to an atomless population. A single person’s report leaves the type distribution unchanged, making individual incentives vacuous. Treating a positive-mass type as the strategic reporter restores consequential deviations only by changing the unit of strategy from an individual to a coordinated group. That could motivate new economics, but it is a different incentive question, and it still does not turn the paper’s axiomatic results into computational ones.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper offers no worthwhile *ChoCo computational mirror*: its best population extension leads either to a simple rule-evaluation task or to a new axiomatic project. The universal claim remains hard to establish absolutely—a richer finite-cohort model could inspire useful work—but that would be a new problem built from the paper, not a continuous computational question anchored in its results.
