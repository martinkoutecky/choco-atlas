The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_j\) summing to \(1\), values \(v_j>0\), a corruption fraction \(\theta\), and a selection fraction \(\lambda\), find an optimal selection rate \(q_j\in[0,1]\) for each type, with \(\sum_j\mu_jq_j=\lambda\), maximizing \(\min_{0\le b_j\le\mu_j,\ \sum_j b_j\le\theta}\sum_j(\mu_j-b_j)v_jq_j\). Here \(b_j\) is corrupted mass chosen before selection randomness, and \(q_j\) is each member's marginal selection probability; return an optimal \(q\) and its per-capita expected payoff.

The model it lives in

A society has finitely many bidder types \(j\), each with mass \(\mu_j\); all members of a type share value \(v_j\). The organizer randomizes selection so type \(j\) has marginal selection rate \(q_j\), selecting total mass \(\lambda\). An adversary commits to corrupting mass \(b_j\) from each type, with total at most \(\theta\), before the lottery is realized. The objective is to maximize expected per-capita value among honest selected members.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest lead is a high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 3, proved in this paper. Under the paper’s setup, it says that for \(\ell\ge 1\), one can sample in linear time from a distribution \(p\) over size-\(\ell\) subsets that maximizes \(\mathrm{value}(p)\), the minimum expected payoff over every size-\(t\) Byzantine set. If an explicit optimal distribution is needed, it can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) time. The paper proves this result; it is not merely a cited theorem. I find no named hardness result in the supplied text, so this is an algorithmic anchor.

A plausible regime is a large auction for many identical perishable goods, where winning bidders who fail to pay leave goods unsold. Suppose bids come from a small menu of price tiers: there are \(n\) bidders but only \(\tau\) distinct bid values, with \(\tau\ll n\). A type is a bid tier \(j\), with value \(v_j>0\); all bidders in that type have the same payoff if honest and the same consequences in the model. The mass \(\mu_j\) is the fraction of bidders in that tier. The number of bidders grows, while the number of tiers stays fixed or grows slowly. The auction and no-show scenario is one the authors themselves use.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The input is rational masses \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_\tau\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), rational values \(v_j>0\), a corruption fraction \(\theta\in(0,1)\), and an allocation fraction \(\lambda\in(0,1)\). Choose a selection rate \(q_j\in[0,1]\) for each type, with \(\sum_j\mu_jq_j=\lambda\). The rate \(q_j\) is the type’s marginal chance of selection, equivalently its selected fraction in the population limit. The adversary, after seeing the policy and before its random selection is realized, chooses corrupted mass \(b_j\) with \(0\le b_j\le\mu_j\) and \(\sum_j b_j\le\theta\). The task is to return an optimal \(q\) and its value:
\(\displaystyle \max_{\substack{q\in[0,1]^\tau\\ \sum_j\mu_jq_j=\lambda}}\ \min_{\substack{0\le b_j\le\mu_j\\ \sum_jb_j\le\theta}}\ \sum_{j=1}^{\tau}(\mu_j-b_j)v_jq_j.\)
This is normalized expected revenue per bidder. The adversary’s mass can target the types with the largest \(v_jq_j\), respecting each type’s available mass.

This preserves the paper’s underlying game. Along finite populations with \(n_j/n\to\mu_j\), \(t/n\to\theta\), and \(\ell/n\to\lambda\), each bidder in type \(j\) can have inclusion marginal \(q_j\), and a lottery over fixed-size subsets implements those marginals. The adversary’s corrupted set becomes a mass choice, and the team or auction allocation scales with the population. Each individual still receives an integral selection decision; the type-level rate is the aggregate. The paper’s \(p\) is a lottery over subsets of finitely many named boxes. In the mirror, \(\mu\) is the population input itself.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. Its objective is piecewise-linear and concave in \(q\). The hypograph can be written as an LP with one constraint for each feasible attack vector \(b\); a separation oracle finds the most damaging \(b\) by sorting the \(\tau\) scores \(v_jq_j\) and filling the corruption budget in that order. That gives a polynomial-time LP approach in \(\tau\) and the rational input bit length. This complexity claim is for the stated mass problem; Theorem 3’s linear time in \(n\) alone does not give a bound polynomial in the bit length of a rational mass denominator.

The mirror also raises questions about a direct water-filling characterization of optimal \(q\), the per-capita error from rounding \(\mu,\theta,\lambda\) to finite counts, and extensions where types carry additional prices or payoff dimensions.

The soft spot is that Theorem 3 already solves the full finite selection problem, even when every box has a different value. This mirror therefore offers no discrete hardness that disappears in the continuum, and its tractability is already strongly indicated by the paper’s result. Its case rests on a credible population regime and a precise continuous input: the original paper studies finitely many named boxes, while this question represents a large population by type masses and scales both corruption and selection with population size. The paper’s fractional marginals and randomized strategy do not by themselves make its society continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is difficult on modeling grounds. Theorem 3 is a proved algorithmic result, and the paper’s auction example gives a credible high-multiplicity regime: many bidders drawn from a fixed menu of bid tiers. Bidders with the same value are indistinguishable under the paper’s payoff model, so grouping them loses no relevant identity information. Scaling \(t\) and \(\ell\) with the population also preserves the game. I see no sound basis for claiming that this continuous mirror is artificial or degenerate.

The strongest objection is about fit with ChoCo. Theorem 3 concerns robust selection under adversarial corruption: an organizer chooses a lottery over subsets to maximize its own expected payoff. The paper has no voting rule, preference aggregation problem, or computational social-choice theorem. Its connections to Byzantine voting and bribery appear as neighboring applications, not as results the paper proves. A continuous study of its selection theorem would more naturally belong to high-multiplicity robust optimization or Byzantine mechanism design. If ChoCo excludes those areas, this anchor falls outside the programme’s remit.

That objection depends on ChoCo’s scope; it does not defeat the mirror itself. The mass formulation is well-posed, and sorting the scores \(v_jq_j\) supplies a direct separation oracle. The finite algorithm in the paper is not a continuous-population treatment of a compressed input. If ChoCo includes robust selection mechanisms, this is a strong mirror, and I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists.
