What the mirror covers

The proposed calculation would reproduce the concentration indices used in Corollary 2 and the case studies, but the paper states no named computational result for it to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is unanchored: this paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Corollary 2, proved in the paper, is the closest supporting result: when there are \(n\) equally weighted parties and no excluded coalitions, both \(H^{Ho}\) and \(H^{Sh}\) equal \(1/n\), and both tend to zero as \(n\) tends to infinity. That is a useful limit observation, but it is not a computational result—and its limit is over parties, whereas ChoCo’s target is a continuous population.

If a proposed mirror can be put forward without a qualifying anchor, the most faithful one is to compute the paper’s concentration measures from a continuous electorate. An instance would contain a finite set of parties \(N\), a finite set of relevant ballot types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an electoral threshold \(b\), and the parties’ exclusion statements. A type specifies how its voters cast the ballot components used by the seat-allocation rule; its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ballot. The induced vote share for party \(i\) is \(s_i=\sum_{t:\,t\text{ votes for }i}\mu_t\). Applying the paper’s threshold rule gives the party weights \(w_i\); those weights and the exclusion statements determine \(v\) and \(\Gamma\). The task is to output the exact pair \((H^{Sh},H^{Ho})\) from the paper’s definitions. A plausible regime is a national election with millions of voters but relatively few ballot types and parties.

That task stays close to what Hiller computes, but it is not a mirror of a named complexity result, so there is no anchor-specific tractability or hardness verdict to report. My tentative expectation is that exact-evaluation difficulty would persist: the coalition subsets and party orderings remain central, and replacing voter counts with rational masses does not remove that combinatorics. Further questions include exact versus approximate evaluation, and how the complexity changes with the number of parties or restrictions on exclusions.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already works with party vote shares, so representing them as masses may be only a change in input interpretation. It does not, on its own, establish a new continuous-population complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered corollaries give formulas about voting power; the simulations and historical case studies calculate concentration measures, but do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The other analyst’s closest anchor, Corollary 2, says that with \(n\) equally weighted parties and no exclusions, both measures equal \(1/n\), tending to zero as \(n\) grows. That limit is over parties, the players in the game, not over voters in a society. It is neither a complexity result nor a population continuization.

The most faithful voter-population version would give each ballot type \(t\) a rational mass \(\mu_t\), derive party vote shares \(s_i=\sum_{t:\,t\text{ votes for }i}\mu_t\), apply the threshold to obtain party weights, and compute \(H^{Sh}\) and \(H^{Ho}\) from those weights and the exclusion statements. That is a coherent input model, but the measures depend on the electorate only through the resulting party weights. Two different distributions over voter types that induce the same weights and exclusions are identical instances for this task. The paper already takes party vote shares as data and even samples them directly in its simulation. So the proposed computation is evaluation of power indices on a weighted voting game; the voter masses supply one way to produce that game, but are not an object the paper’s question uses.

A better mirror might let the electorate have multiple ballot components, or ask how changing a continuous voter-type distribution affects concentration. The same reduction applies: once the ballot distribution is mapped through the seat-allocation rule, the paper’s measures use only the finite party-weight vector and coalition exclusions. The paper’s suggestion of flexible thresholds based on a target concentration is a more promising computational extension, but its natural inputs are already continuous party vote shares and a threshold. Adding costs for changing voter masses or rules for persuading voter types could create a genuine population-level optimization problem; it would also add a decision model the paper does not specify.

This is not an argument that a continuous version would have the same answer, or that its complexity would be easy or hard. It is that the paper supplies no computational classification to transfer, and the natural task of computing its measures already factors through party shares rather than a voter society. The negative case is weakest around threshold design: pairing that future-research idea with voter-type masses could produce a worthwhile new problem. But as posed, it looks like a separate extension, not a continuous-population mirror of a result in this paper.
