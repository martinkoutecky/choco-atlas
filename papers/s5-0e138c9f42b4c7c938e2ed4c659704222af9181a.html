The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s multi-price school-assignment problem. The paper contains no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time theorem, so I would not pretend that it supplies a complexity anchor. Its strongest usable anchor is instead the algorithmic characterization in Theorem 1.

A natural regime is a large public-school admission market with “seat purchasing,” the paper’s own Section 7.1 application. A type is a complete student description: her ranking over school–tuition pairs, exam-score band, eligibility, and every attribute entering a school’s baseline priority or price-responsiveness policy. Thus two students have the same type exactly when the mechanism treats them identically. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many such types, with \(\tau\ll N\) in the intended regime: many students share score bands, preference templates, and eligibility profiles, while the number of distinct types is relatively small.

Schools \(b\in B\) have per-capita capacities \(\kappa_b\) and flexible-price capacities \(\kappa_b^f\). Tuition terms remain discrete, \(P=\{p^0,\ldots,p^h\}\). Every unit of mass receives either one integral contract \((b,p)\) or remains unmatched; only the aggregate allocation is fractional. The decision variable is

\[ x_{\theta,b,p}\ge 0, \]

the mass of type \(\theta\) assigned to school \(b\) at price \(p\), together with unmatched mass \(u_\theta\). Feasibility requires

\[ \sum_{b,p}x_{\theta,b,p}+u_\theta=\mu_\theta, \]

\[ \sum_{\theta,p}x_{\theta,b,p}\le \kappa_b, \qquad \sum_{\theta,p>p^0}x_{\theta,b,p}\le \kappa_b^f. \]

The continuous object is therefore the population, not the outcome space and not a lottery over students.

The lead question is:

Continuous MPCO Allocation. Given \(\mu\), the school capacities, the discrete price terms, type preferences, baseline priority orders \(\pi_b\), and type-level price-responsiveness orders \(\omega_b\), compute the mass allocation \(x\) produced by the fractional multi-price cumulative-offer procedure. Equivalently, return the unique feasible allocation satisfying the mass versions of individual rationality, non-wastefulness, no priority reversal, enforcement of the price-responsiveness policy, and strategy-proofness.

The mass version of MPCO is direct. Types propose mass to their most-preferred not-yet-rejected school–price contract. Each school first fills base-price capacity according to \(\pi_b\), then fills flexible-price capacity according to \(\omega_b\), using fractional cutoffs when a type is split at a boundary. If several prices of the same type are present, the lower-priority duplicate is discarded exactly as in the paper’s multi-price choice rule. The output is the final mass held by schools.

This is precisely the content of the paper’s Theorem 1, proved there rather than cited from elsewhere:

Fix baseline priority orders \((\pi_b)_{b\in B}\) and price-responsiveness policies \((\omega_b)_{b\in B}\). A direct mechanism satisfies individual rationality, non-wastefulness, no priority reversal, enforcement of the price-responsiveness policy, and strategy-proofness if and only if it is the multi-price cumulative-offer mechanism \(\phi^{MPCO}\).

The continuous question preserves every substantive component of that theorem: multiple schools, multiple prices, flexible-seat caps, heterogeneous preferences, baseline merit, price-responsive priority, and the cumulative-offer mechanism. It is not a welfare problem obtained by making outcomes fractional. It asks whether the paper’s uniquely characterized institution can be computed when the students themselves are represented by a distribution.

I would expect this problem to be Class A. With explicitly listed types, prices, schools, and priority relations, a type-block cumulative-offer implementation has only polynomially many type–school–price proposal events, and each school’s choice step is a sorting and fractional-cutoff operation. Rational \(\mu\) and capacities also give a direct high-multiplicity bridge: choose \(N\) so that \(N\mu_\theta\), \(N\kappa_b\), and \(N\kappa_b^f\) are integral, instantiate that many students of each type, and normalize the finite MPCO outcome. The continuous allocation is then the exact aggregate finite outcome, up to arbitrary tie-breaking among students of the same type.

A second, narrower mirror is supported by the paper’s Proposition 1. That proposition is presented as a result of the paper itself. For a fixed branch \(b\), total capacity \(q_b\), and set of branch contracts \(X\), it states that the number of price-elevated contracts selected by the multi-price choice rule weakly increases when the flexible-price capacity \(q_b^f\) increases, and also weakly increases when the price-responsiveness policy \(\omega_b\) becomes more responsive to a price increase.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Flexible-Seat Comparative Statics. Given an incoming mass of type–price contracts at school \(b\), compute the mass

\[ \rho_b=\sum_{\theta}\sum_{p>p^0}x_{\theta,b,p} \]

selected at elevated prices by the fractional multi-price choice rule. Given two instances with the same incoming mass and total capacity, determine \(\rho_b\) under different \(\kappa_b^f\) and \(\omega_b\), and certify that

\[ \kappa_b^{f\prime}\ge \kappa_b^f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho_b'\ge \rho_b, \]

and that a more responsive \(\omega_b'\) likewise gives \(\rho_b'\ge \rho_b\).

This is also expected to be tractable: it is a finite sorting-and-cutoff computation. More importantly, it captures exactly why the continuous formulation is natural. “\(55\) BRADSOs are charged” becomes “\(5.5\%\) of the relevant population receives elevated-price seats,” while preserving the paper’s discrete contracts and institutional trade-off.

The authors should recognize both questions as their own. The paper explicitly presents school choice with price-responsive seats as an application of the same model, and its four-price Chinese example already has the required structure. The proposed mirror changes only the representation of the student population. It does not remove tuition, school capacities, multiple prices, non-consecutive preferences, merit priorities, or the price-responsiveness policy.

The main weakness is that the paper’s Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity theorem. Thus this case predicts a new Class A result; it does not claim that the paper already proves polynomial-time solvability of the continuous problem. A second technical qualification is that strategy-proofness must be defined carefully for a nonatomic population. I would use atom-robust strategy-proofness: insert an arbitrarily small tagged student of a given type, allow her to change her report, and require that the limiting assignment cannot improve. That is a genuine modelling choice, but it does not undermine the mirror’s core: the multi-price assignment problem, its mass allocation, and its price-responsive priority structure remain intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins before the modelling: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization—“a mechanism satisfies five desiderata iff it is MPCO”—not a theorem about complexity, exact computation, approximation, or parameterized algorithms. Proposition 1 is merely a local comparative-static statement about the number of elevated-price assignments. Neither result defines a computational problem or establishes an algorithmic bound. Thus “Continuous MPCO Allocation” would be a new problem inspired by the paper, not a continuization of a computational result proved in it.

Theorem 1’s proposed mirror is nevertheless mathematically plausible, but it does not preserve the theorem’s central primitives. In the paper, each \(\pi_b\) is a strict order on named cadets, and each \(\omega_b\) is a strict order on named cadet–price contracts. In the Army application, the OML is explicitly an individual ranking. If priority rank is part of a type’s complete description, two cadets at different ranks are different types, so \(\tau\ll N\) disappears. If rank is replaced by a coarser score or a type-level weak order, the model has been changed: the original priority relation, and hence the meaning of no priority reversal, are no longer the same. The Chinese school application is more promising, but the paper assumes distinct merit scores and tie-breaking; repeated score types require a new priority model.

More seriously, strategy-proofness does not survive an atomless population in the form used by Theorem 1. A single agent has measure zero, so changing her report leaves the aggregate allocation unchanged. Individual strategy-proofness therefore becomes vacuous: every mechanism satisfies it. If an entire positive-mass type changes its report, that is a coalition or block deviation, not the paper’s individual axiom. If one inserts a tagged mass \(\varepsilon\) and takes \(\varepsilon\to0\), one has introduced a new perturbation model whose behaviour at capacity cutoffs must be established separately. The uniqueness part of Theorem 1 therefore cannot simply be carried over. A mass MPCO procedure may be defined, but its axiomatic characterization is a new theorem.

The same problem defeats Proposition 1 as an anchor. Proposition 1 fixes one branch and one finite set of contracts \(X\), then proves that an integer count rises with \(q_b^f\) or with responsiveness of \(\omega_b\). Replacing that count by a mass \(\rho_b\) is a relabelling of the local choice rule, not a population-computational problem. With incoming mass fixed, the population distribution does no substantive work. Lifting the statement to a full mass assignment problem merely returns to the unproved continuous MPCO extension.

I do not object to per-capita capacities or fractional aggregate assignments; those are legitimate co-modifications under the programme. Nor do I claim that a tractable answer would make the question uninteresting. The issue is that the paper’s named results are axiomatic and individual-level, while the proposed continuation erases precisely the individual priority and unilateral-deviation structure on which those results are built.

So the negative verdict is strong under ChoCo’s source-anchor rule: this is an unanchored new high-multiplicity matching problem, not a continuous mirror of the paper’s computational contribution. But the universal claim is not airtight. A large school market with repeated exact preference/score types and a deliberately specified type-level priority rule could be a sensible new Class A study. I would therefore reject the submitted case as a qualifying mirror, while conceding that the broader modelling idea is plausible if presented honestly as a new extension rather than as a computational continuation of Theorem 1 or Proposition 1.
