What the mirror covers

Neither Theorem 1’s ordinal nondomination characterization nor Theorem 2’s near-uniform-belief result yields a computational mirror; the examples and remaining discussion are outside any anchored computational question.

The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes TS-local robust ordinal Bayesian incentive compatibility: TS-LOBIC implies ordinal nondomination, and unanimity plus ordinal nondomination is sufficient. Theorem 2, also proved here, shows that a neutral, elementary-monotone, unanimous SCF is TS-LOBIC for beliefs arbitrarily close to the uniform prior. These are incentive-compatibility results, not claims about complexity, algorithms, or computational formulations.

A large electorate with few ranking types is an imaginable setting: types could be complete rankings, and mass could represent each ranking’s share. But that does not give a faithful mirror of these theorems. The paper’s rule maps a finite profile to an outcome, and its incentive constraints depend on each voter’s conditional beliefs over the other voters’ profiles. Under a rule based only on a continuum’s aggregate type distribution, one voter’s report has zero effect on that distribution, so the incentive question changes substantially.

So I can’t honestly state a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor, or forecast its place in the trichotomy. The strongest potential case—a high-multiplicity voting scenario—remains a new modeling direction, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes which finite social choice functions satisfy TS-LOBIC; Theorem 2 establishes that a class of such functions is TS-LOBIC near the uniform prior. Neither studies an algorithm, a complexity class, or a computational problem. One could invent a task about recognizing or constructing functions with these properties, but that would be a new computational question, not a mirror of either theorem.

Theorem 1’s most natural proposed mirror would take a distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types and ask which rules are locally incentive compatible for that society. Large electorates with few ranking types are plausible, so multiplicity itself is not the objection. The problem is that the paper’s incentive constraint concerns one voter changing their report while the other reports are held fixed. In a rule that depends on the population distribution, one voter has mass zero: changing their report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. The resulting truth-telling condition is automatic, and the theorem’s ordinal-nondomination characterization loses its role. Theorem 2 has the same problem: neutrality and elementary monotonicity may still be properties one can state, but the population rule gives an individual no leverage over the outcome, so its incentive claim becomes vacuous.

A richer continuous model could retain a random population measure and condition beliefs about that measure on a voter’s ranking. That would preserve some of the paper’s correlation structure. It would not restore the individual’s effect on the rule’s input. To do that, one must keep a finite-size pivotal correction, give a voter a distinguished role, or let a positive-mass group deviate together. Those choices respectively make the central object an asymptotic finite-electorate problem, an identity-sensitive mechanism, or a coalition-deviation problem. The last two change the paper’s incentive question; the first requires specifying how vanishing pivotal incentives are scaled and how ties behave.

The strongest possible bridge is therefore a sequence of high-multiplicity finite models whose conditional beliefs and pivotal incentives converge in a controlled way. That could be worthwhile research, especially for voting rules with nonvanishing pivotal probability under a particular prior. But its target would be a new asymptotic theory, with the population limit alone discarding the incentive effect that drives both theorems. The negative case is strongest on fit with ChoCo’s computational programme; it cannot rule out value in that adjacent asymptotic project.
