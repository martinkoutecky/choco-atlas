The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 1 — survived: A specified cohort-sampling process makes this a population version of pairwise recovery, with cohort masses determining the available pairwise observations.
  • Theorem 3 — fell: The opponent’s representative-annotator reduction applies directly to the exact-law uniqueness question, whose stated mirror does not use cohort masses.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational cohort masses \(\mu_q\), a dispatch rule that samples worker types according to those masses, and pairwise response observations with counts \(S_{qr}\), recover each cohort confusion matrix \(A_q\), up to column permutation, with the error guarantee of Theorem 1. The promise includes full rank, the stated lower bound \(\eta\) on empirical column mass, and a near-dominant neighboring cohort for each class; the pairwise laws are \(R_{qr}=A_q\operatorname{Diag}(d)A_r^\top\).

The model it lives in

Types are standardized annotator cohorts with shared response channels and dispatch eligibility; \(\mu_q\) is each cohort’s population fraction. Worker-type pairs are sampled under a specified dispatch rule, producing empirical pairwise tables and counts. The decision variable is the estimated channel \(\widehat A_q\); the goal is to bound its maximum recovery error using SPA, with the available counts linked to \(\mu\) by the sampling rule.

The objection that survived

The theorem’s guarantee depends on pairwise sample counts and distinct informative channels, while the case does not specify exactly how cohort masses determine those counts; duplicating workers with the same channel does not create channel diversity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s robust recovery of cohort confusion matrices from pairwise observations. It leaves Theorem 3’s exact identifiability result, estimation of the prior, and downstream label prediction outside the surviving anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • What dispatch and observation budget as a function of \(\mu\) ensures the pairwise sample counts required by Theorem 1 with high probability?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a large annotation workforce organized into a small number of standardized skill or task-eligibility cohorts. A type is a cohort whose workers share the same Dawid–Skene confusion matrix and the same dispatch pattern; within a type, workers are statistically interchangeable. Its mass \(\mu_q\) is the fraction of the workforce in cohort \(q\). The regime is many workers but relatively few distinct cohorts. For a randomly selected common task and workers from cohorts \(q,r\), the pairwise response law is
\[ R_{qr}=A_q\operatorname{Diag}(d)A_r^\top, \]
where \(A_q\) is the cohort’s confusion matrix and \(d\) is the task population’s class prior.

This is a population of annotators, not a claim that the paper’s class prior \(d\) by itself already continuizes the society. The unknowns are cohort response channels and the latent class prior; the decision is to recover them from pairwise co-occurrences. Cohort masses determine how often each type can be sampled, and thus how much data may be available for each pair. They do not, by themselves, create the diversity of channels needed for identification.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It is a robust recovery guarantee for the paper’s successive projection algorithm (SPA). In the cohort version, suppose each observed type pair used by the algorithm has at least \(S\) co-labeled tasks, each column of the constructed empirical matrix has \(\ell_1\)-norm at least \(\eta\), and every \(A_q\) and \(D=\operatorname{Diag}(d)\) has rank \(K\). Also suppose that for every class \(k\), cohort \(q\) has a neighboring cohort \(r\) satisfying the paper’s near-dominance condition
\[ \Pr(X_r=k\mid Y=k)\ge (1-\epsilon)\sum_j\Pr(X_r=k\mid Y=j). \]
Theorem 1 says that, when
\[ \epsilon\le O\!\left(\max\left\{K^{-1}\kappa^{-3}(A_q), \frac{\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)}}{\sigma_{\max}(A_q)\sqrt{S\eta}}\right\}\right), \]
SPA recovers \(A_q\), up to column permutation, with probability greater than \(1-\delta\) and error
\[ O\!\left(\sqrt K\,\kappa^2(A_q) \max\left\{\sigma_{\max}(A_q)\epsilon, \frac{\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{S\eta}}\right\}\right) \]
in the paper’s \(\ell_{2,\infty}\) norm. Here \(\kappa\) and \(\sigma_{\max}\) are the condition number and largest singular value. The paper proves this result using the SPA robustness analysis it cites.

That gives a precise continuous problem: Cohort Pairwise SPA Recovery. An instance consists of \(K\), a finite set of \(\tau\) cohorts with rational masses \(\mu_q>0\) summing to one, a type-pair dispatch graph, and empirical pairwise response tables with their sample counts. The promised instance has full-rank cohort channels and class prior, enough observations and column mass as above, and a near-dominant neighboring cohort for each class. The task is to return cohort confusion matrices whose error meets the Theorem 1 bound, up to class-label permutation. A solution is the set of recovered matrices and the stated error guarantee. The theorem’s assumptions require enough distinct cohort-channel structure; simply having many copies of the same channel does not supply it. Under its promise, I expect this question to be tractable: SPA takes a polynomial number of matrix operations in \(K\) and the number of observed type-pair columns. The theorem gives the statistical guarantee, though it does not itself state a running-time bound.

A second anchor, Theorem 3, also proved here, supports a broader exact-recovery question. Suppose the types can be divided into disjoint sets \(P_1,P_2\). Stack their confusion matrices into \(H^{(1)},H^{(2)}\). The theorem assumes both are sufficiently scattered—in the paper’s Definition 1, their transposed cones contain \(C=\{x:x^\top\mathbf1\ge\sqrt{K-1}\|x\|_2\}\), and the dual-cone boundary meets the boundary of \(C^*\) only on nonnegative coordinate rays. It also assumes full rank of every \(A_q\) and \(D\), availability of every cross-pair table between \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), and at least one observed pair connecting every remaining type to a type in \(P_1\cup P_2\). Under these conditions, solving the paper’s feasibility system
\[ R_{qr}=A_qDA_r^\top,\qquad \mathbf1^\top A_q=\mathbf1^\top,\quad A_q\ge0,\qquad \mathbf1^\top d=1,\quad d\ge0 \]
recovers all cohort matrices and \(D\) up to one common column permutation.

Its mirror is Sufficient-Scatter Cohort Identification. The input is the rational workforce distribution \(\mu\), \(K\), and the exact pairwise response laws \(R_{qr}\) on a type-pair graph. The unknowns are the column-stochastic \(A_q\) and class prior \(d\); a solution is any tuple satisfying the displayed factorization and simplex constraints. The question is whether the promised tuple is uniquely determined up to a shared relabeling of latent classes. Theorem 3 answers yes under its stated conditions. I would tentatively expect the promised computational version to be tractable, but that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of the paper: Theorem 3 establishes identifiability, while the paper’s fitting formulation is nonconvex and its iterative method is not a global polynomial-time guarantee. Efficient global recovery under the sufficient-scatter promise is a natural follow-up.

These are meaningful anchors, but there is an important limit: the paper contains no named theorem establishing a complexity class such as \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hardness. Theorem 1 is a named algorithmic and statistical recovery guarantee; Theorem 3 is a named identifiability guarantee. Neither proves a worst-case complexity classification. The mirror I am arguing for is therefore about the computational recovery questions suggested by those results, not a claim that this paper already classifies them.

The authors would likely recognize both questions: the Dawid–Skene model is built around annotator confusion matrices, and the paper’s pairwise co-occurrence equations and partial co-labeling assumptions survive directly when identical annotators are grouped into types. The scope is model identification from pairwise response laws, including robustness to finite task samples. It does not extend to every experiment or to downstream prediction accuracy.

The weakest point is that the continuous population is the workforce, while the paper’s sample size \(S\) counts shared tasks. Large cohort mass can make those observations easier to obtain, but does not improve identification if all the workers share one channel. The mirror needs a realistic small-support regime with enough distinct, sufficiently informative cohorts. Standardized annotator tiers or repeated machine-classifier deployments make that plausible; arbitrary individual human reliabilities may not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many workers in standardized cohorts. The problem is that, in the proposed mirrors, cohort mass does no work in the recovery problem. Replace each cohort by one representative annotator and set \(M=\tau\). The inputs \(R_{qr}\), the factorization \(R_{qr}=A_qDA_r^\top\), and the identifiability assumptions are exactly the paper’s inputs and assumptions for those representatives. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 already apply.

That makes the weakness in the Theorem 1 mirror quite specific. More workers of a given type may yield more co-labeled tasks, but the theorem’s \(S\) counts those tasks; it is not a measure of workforce mass. If pair counts are supplied, \(\mu\) can be dropped without changing SPA or its guarantee. If counts are generated by sampling workers according to \(\mu\), then mass affects the statistical error through the resulting sample counts. That is a useful sampling question, but the theorem already expresses its recovery guarantee in terms of those counts. And duplicating workers with the same channel does not create the channel diversity behind Theorems 2 and 4: it repeats rows, rather than making the stacked matrices more spread out or sufficiently scattered. The continuous workforce therefore adds a sampling regime, not a new computational recovery problem.

The same reduction defeats the proposed Theorem 3 mirror. Given exact type-pair laws, it is the paper’s finite-index factorization problem with one index per cohort. Its uniqueness result does not depend on the cohort masses. A stronger version could ask for efficient or robust recovery under the sufficient-scatter promise, since the paper’s identifiability theorem does not give a global polynomial-time algorithm and the scatter condition has no quantitative margin. But that question is about computing or stabilizing a finite nonnegative matrix factorization. It remains the same whether each factor represents one annotator or a million interchangeable annotators. It could be valuable matrix-factorization research; the continuous population is not what makes it valuable.

The better workforce-based proposal would put mass to use by optimizing how often each cohort pair is assigned common tasks, subject to cohort capacities, to improve identifiability or reduce estimation error. That is more substantive than simply relabeling annotators. But its central object is an experimental-design or sampling-allocation problem. It is not a complexity question about a collective choice problem, and the paper itself already points toward dispatch design. It belongs to crowdsourcing and statistical learning, not the computational-social-choice landscape ChoCo is trying to chart.

Moving the continuous population to the tasks does not rescue the mirror. The paper already models true labels and responses as random variables and works with population pairwise laws. If a proposed mirror asks for the label of every particular task, replacing tasks by class masses discards the item-level target. If it asks only for aggregate label frequencies or population risk, it becomes a distributional learning question, with the class prior already represented by \(d\).

So the strongest case against is not that annotator cohorts are nonsensical. They are plausible in some machine-classifier deployments, and that is the negative case’s main weakness: a mass-constrained dispatch-design problem could be worth studying there. But neither named theorem yields a new continuous-population computation, and the best improvements either reduce to the paper with \(M=\tau\) or shift to a different problem in sampling design. I would reject these as ChoCo mirrors while acknowledging that a narrower, adjacent research question could survive.
