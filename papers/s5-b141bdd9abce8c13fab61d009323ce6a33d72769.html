The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive reading is a research lead, not a qualifying continuous-computational mirror. The paper keeps a finite committee of \(n\) voters. What is continuous is each voter’s private signal \(t_i\in[0,1]\), drawn from a state-dependent distribution. That is a continuum of signal values, not a continuous population: because the signal distributions have densities, the realized signals are distinct with probability \(1\). In ChoCo’s terms, the continuous object here is not the society’s mass distribution over agent types.

The paper’s relevant named results are proved here, but none is a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes when informative voting at cutoff \(\hat t\) is sincere: below the cutoff, the prior odds must be at most \(f_0(t)/f_1(t)\), and above it they must be at least that ratio. Theorem 2 says a voting rule makes informative voting ex-post efficient exactly when, for every signal profile, the posterior comparison using all signals agrees with the comparison using only the number of signals above \(\hat t\). Theorem 3 says that when such a rule exists, it is the quota rule with threshold \(m^*\). These are characterizations of sincerity and efficient information aggregation; they do not establish a problem’s complexity, give an algorithmic formulation the paper computes with, or report an algorithm.

So the paper contains no named computational result to serve as an anchor under the requested criteria. I can’t honestly supply a continuous problem per anchor.

The nearest positive lead would be a follow-up: group a large population into shared signal types, let mass mean the fraction with each type, and ask whether a cutoff ballot plus a quota on ballot mass preserves Bayes-efficient decisions. Theorem 2 suggests the key question: when does the thresholded mass retain all the decision-relevant information in the full type distribution? But the paper’s theorem does not answer that population problem. Making it precise would require changing its finite committee of continuously drawn signals into a high-multiplicity population of shared types, and I don’t think that change is enough to present it as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper has no named *computational* result to mirror. “Efficient” in Theorem 2 means that the voting rule selects the decision with highest expected utility given the full signal profile; it says nothing about computational efficiency. Theorems 1 and 3 characterize sincere voting and the rule that supports efficient information aggregation. None defines an algorithmic problem, input encoding, running time, or complexity claim.

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate for a better mirror: given a population distribution over signal types, ask whether a cutoff and quota on the mass above it preserve the Bayes-optimal decision. But the paper’s voters have independent, continuously distributed signals, so almost surely every voter has a distinct type. A mass model over those realized signal values has support size \(n\), not a compact high-multiplicity representation. In an infinite-population limit, the observed signal distribution converges to \(F_s\); when the state-conditioned distributions differ, the full distribution identifies the state, making aggregation trivial. A finite cohort model with repeated signal types could avoid that limit, but repeated exact signals have probability zero in this paper’s model. Binning signals or making a type describe a signal category would define a different model.

Theorem 1 does not supply a stronger computational anchor: it gives a condition on priors and likelihood ratios for sincerity at a cutoff. Theorem 3 identifies the quota rule once the condition in Theorem 2 holds. One could formulate a new task around checking that condition or choosing a cutoff to minimize decision error, as in the paper’s example. But the paper allows general piecewise-continuous densities without specifying how they are represented, so there is no computational input on which to state complexity. With a chosen finite representation, that would be a newly specified problem, not a computational question already established by these results.

So the proposed high-multiplicity follow-up may be worth exploring as a separate model, but it does not rescue a continuous-computational mirror of this paper. That conclusion is strongest for the programme’s stated scope; it is not a proof that no useful research question could ever be built from the paper’s ideas.
