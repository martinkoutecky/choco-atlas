The continuous mirror question

Given a continuum consortium with scale \(H>0\), type support \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-member quantities \(q_t>0\), and \(P(z)=a-bz\ge0\) on \([0,H\bar q]\), where \(\bar q=\sum_t\mu_tq_t\), define \(x_\nu=\sum_t\nu_tq_t\) and normalized coalition cost \(C_H(\nu)=x_\nu P(Hx_\nu)\) for \(0\le\nu\le\mu\). Compute exact per-member allocation densities \(r_t\) equal to the Aumann–Shapley path allocation, and determine whether they are efficient and coalitionally stable: \(\sum_t\mu_tr_t=C_H(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t\nu_tr_t\le C_H(\nu)\) for every \(\nu\le\mu\).

The model it lives in

Model the consortium as nonatomic mass \(H\), with type proportions \(\mu\) and per-member demands \(q_t\); \(C_H\) is pooled procurement cost normalized by \(H\). The decision is the per-member allocation density \(r_t=q_t\int_0^1[P(Hs\bar q)+Hs\bar qP'(Hs\bar q)]\,ds\), with efficiency and stability against every coalition mass vector \(\nu\le\mu\). For linear \(P(z)=a-bz\), this gives \(r_t=q_tP(H\bar q)\).

The objection that survived

With fixed \(a,b>0\) and fixed per-member demands, nonnegative prices require \(H\bar q\le a/b\), so an unbounded high-multiplicity scaling sequence needs to be specified.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4’s coincidence of equal price, Shapley value, and nucleolus for linear discounts. It leaves aside the general subadditivity and balance results, the nonlinear BDP and \(\alpha\)-proportional rules, and the paper’s other allocation properties.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under an explicit scaling of \(H\), demands, or the discount schedule, do finite Shapley and nucleolus allocations for nonlinear discounts converge to the Aumann–Shapley allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a narrow case around Proposition 4, proved in this paper. It is not stated as a formal “in P” theorem, and the paper has no named hardness result to compare against. But Proposition 4 gives an exact formula for computing two solution concepts that the authors say are laborious to calculate for large groups: in every cooperative-purchasing game with a linear discount \(P(z)=a-bz\), the equal-price allocation \(\epsilon_i=P(q_N)q_i\) equals both the Shapley value \(\phi_i\) and the nucleolus \(\nu_i\). That identity is a constructive tractability result: compute the group’s total order, evaluate the price, and assign each buyer their order times that price.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large consortium of schools or clinics buying standardized supplies from the same seller. Many buyers may share an order-volume class and the same purchasing terms. The type is a buyer’s per-member quantity \(q_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the consortium in that class. A consortium with thousands of buyers but tens of distinct quantity classes is a credible regime. This preserves the paper’s cost-sharing question: how to allocate the cost reduction from pooling orders. It does not make named buyers disappear by assumption; it groups only buyers who are indistinguishable in the model’s demand and terms.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Linear-Discount Continuum Cost Sharing. An instance consists of a population scale \(H\), a finite support of types \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) summing to one, rational demands \(q_t>0\), and a linear price schedule \(P(z)=a-bz\) that is nonnegative over the relevant range. For any coalition represented by masses \(0\leq\nu_t\leq\mu_t\), let \(x_\nu=\sum_t\nu_tq_t\). Its physical order is \(Hx_\nu\), and its cost normalized per population unit is
\[ C_H(\nu)=P(Hx_\nu)x_\nu. \]
The task is to compute a per-buyer allocation density \(r_t\) for each type that is efficient and stable against every such coalition, and that is the continuous Shapley analogue given by the Aumann–Shapley value along the path from zero population to \(\mu\). A solution is the exact vector \(r\), together with a certificate of efficiency and the coalition inequalities \(\sum_t\nu_t r_t\leq C_H(\nu)\) for all \(\nu\leq\mu\).

The answer is
\[ r_t=q_t\int_0^1\bigl(a-2bHs\bar q\bigr)\,ds =q_t(a-bH\bar q) =q_tP(H\bar q), \qquad \bar q=\sum_t\mu_tq_t. \]
It is efficient because \(\sum_t\mu_t r_t=P(H\bar q)\bar q=C_H(\mu)\). It is coalition-stable because \(x_\nu\leq\bar q\) and the price is decreasing, so \(P(H\bar q)x_\nu\leq P(Hx_\nu)x_\nu\). Computing it takes \(O(\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations, hence polynomial time in the support size and input bit length.

This is an especially close bridge to Proposition 4 for empirical populations: when \(\mu_t=n_t/H\), the finite game has \(n_t\) buyers of type \(t\), and Proposition 4 assigns each of them exactly \(r_t=P(H\bar q)q_t\). The continuous allocation therefore aggregates the finite Shapley and nucleolus allocations by type. The mirror covers the paper’s linear-discount allocation result, not its nonlinear BDP or \(\alpha\)-proportional rules.

The weakest point is that the paper does not formulate a complexity problem, so this is a tractable computation extracted from a structural identity, not a hardness boundary. Also, an atomless population does not have the ordinary finite-player Shapley value; Aumann–Shapley is the continuous counterpart I am choosing. In this one-dimensional aggregate-demand model, that value equals equal price for any differentiable \(P\), so the special role of linearity in Proposition 4 may not survive in the continuum. A useful next question is whether finite Shapley and nucleolus allocations for nonlinear discounts converge to a well-defined continuum solution, and whether that solution retains any distinction between the two.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the paper’s subject: it studies cooperative cost sharing for pooled purchases, not a computational social choice problem in the usual sense. Its central object is an allocation rule for a bill, and the paper’s main results characterize when those allocations are stable and acceptable. It states no decision problem, complexity classification, or algorithmic boundary. Proposition 4 is the closest computational anchor: it proves that, for linear discounts, the Shapley value and nucleolus equal the equal-price allocation. That gives a simple way to compute them, but it is an identity between solution concepts, not a complexity result the paper sets out to establish.

The proposed continuum mirror also has to choose what “continuous Shapley value” means. The finite Shapley value averages marginal contributions over permutations of named buyers; an atomless population has no direct counterpart to that averaging. Aumann–Shapley is a reasonable choice, but it adds a solution concept that the paper does not select. For this model, its path integral gives \(q_tP(H\bar q)\) for any differentiable price schedule, so it does not preserve the paper’s special linear-discount result. The anchor’s most promising continuous question therefore depends on an extra modeling choice and generalizes beyond the result it is meant to mirror.

There is a further scaling wrinkle for that anchor. With fixed positive demands, a linear price \(P(z)=a-bz\), \(b>0\), remains nonnegative only up to \(z=a/b\). An unbounded population with those demands cannot use the same price schedule. A growing-population model must also scale the discount slope, demands, or feasible order range. Such a co-modification may be entirely reasonable, but it makes the proposed limit a new market model rather than a direct continuation of Proposition 4.

These points make the case against weaker than the proponent’s case. Many buyers with repeated order quantities are plausible, and a type distribution preserves the paper’s cost-sharing question. The finite-to-continuous allocation also has a coherent Aumann–Shapley interpretation. I can argue that ChoCo would need to justify why this cooperative purchasing problem belongs in its computational-social-choice programme, and why the chosen continuum value is the one its authors would recognize. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: the consortium example is credible, and Proposition 4 supplies a usable computational anchor.
