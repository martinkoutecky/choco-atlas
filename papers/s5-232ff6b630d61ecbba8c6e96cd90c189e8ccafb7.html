The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many applicant types \(T\), post types \(P\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational capacities \(\kappa_p\), strict type-specific rankings, and a last-resort option for each type, assign each type’s mass to acceptable posts or its last resort. For a feasible assignment \(x\), call it popular if every feasible challenger \(y\) and every type-preserving coupling \(z\) between their assignments has nonpositive majority margin, \(\sum_{t,p,q} z^t_{p,q}(\mathbf{1}[q\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t q])\le 0\). Find a popular assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t,p\in P}x_{t,p}\), or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

An atomless placement market with rational masses of applicant types and per-capita capacities for post types. A mass assignment \(x\) and type-preserving couplings to challenger assignments define the majority comparison; the objective is to maximize mass assigned to ordinary posts.

The objection that survived

Theorem 10’s switching-graph argument is proved for individually represented, unit-capacity posts; whether an analogous structure handles rational capacities and type-level mass splits remains open.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the maximum-cardinality popular matching result. Theorem 10 states: “We can find a maximum-cardinality popular matching, or determine that no such matching exists in NC.” The authors prove it here. Their algorithm starts from a popular matching and uses the switching-graph structure to choose positive-margin cycles and paths, maximizing the number of applicants assigned to ordinary posts rather than their last-resort posts. The structural characterization it relies on, Theorem 9, is cited from McDermid and Irving [23].

Here is a continuous version I think the authors could recognize as their problem. Imagine a large placement market with applicant types \(T\), post or seat types \(P\), and \(\tau\) applicant types of positive mass. A type is a complete description of an applicant’s relevant attributes, including a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) over acceptable post types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of applicants of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each post type \(p\) has capacity \(\kappa_p\), measured as a fraction of the applicant population; each applicant type also has a private last-resort option \(\ell_t\). The instance gives these masses and capacities as binary rationals.

A feasible assignment is a matrix \(x\) with
\[ \sum_{p\in P\cup\{\ell_t\}}x_{t,p}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_t x_{t,p}\le \kappa_p\quad(p\in P), \]
where \(x_{t,p}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(p\). To define popularity, take an atomless cohort of each type. For a baseline assignment \(x\) and a feasible challenger \(y\), let \(z^t_{p,q}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(p\) under \(x\) and \(q\) under \(y\). Its row and column sums are \(x_{t,p}\) and \(y_{t,q}\). Every such coupling is realizable by reassigning agents within the type cohort. Call \(x\) popular if, for every feasible \(y\) and every such coupling,
\[ \sum_{t,p,q} z^t_{p,q} \bigl(\mathbf{1}[q\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t q]\bigr)\le 0. \]
The continuous problem is: find a popular \(x\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,p\in P}x_{t,p}\), or report that none exists. This is maximum-cardinality popular matching measured in mass rather than a count. The solution is the rational assignment matrix, which represents a measurable assignment of the type cohorts.

A plausible regime is a high-volume placement system with standardized preference patterns: for example, a large annual applicant cohort choosing among program or role types, where applicants with the same pathway and priorities share a ranking. There might be millions of applicants but only tens or hundreds of distinct types, with seat supply scaling alongside the cohort. Applicants remain the voting agents; posts remain capacity-constrained destinations; popularity remains a majority comparison over assignments. This is a meaningful high-multiplicity regime, rather than a claim that every matching market is naturally continuous.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, though Theorem 10 does not establish that. For a fixed candidate \(x\), searching for a defeating assignment is a max-cost transportation problem over the old and new post types, so the continuum gives a concrete optimization route for checking popularity. The harder task is finding a popular assignment with maximum matched mass; a type-level counterpart of the paper’s switching structure might make that tractable. The prover’s main questions would be whether such a structure survives capacities and mass splits, whether the problem has an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and how the answer relates to finite high-multiplicity instances. The paper’s NC algorithm runs on an explicitly represented graph; its runtime does not directly yield a polynomial-time algorithm on binary-encoded type masses.

A narrower second anchor supports the model with capacities and ties. Theorem 11, proved in the paper, states that maximum-cardinality bipartite matching is NC-reducible to popular matching. The reduction gives every acceptable edge rank \(1\); the paper proves that in this construction every popular matching is maximum-cardinality, and every maximum-cardinality matching is popular.

Its continuous counterpart is a type graph with rational applicant masses \(\mu_t\), post capacities \(\kappa_p\), and acceptable edges \(E\). Applicants may remain unmatched, and each type is indifferent among all its acceptable posts, ranking each above being unmatched. A feasible mass matching has \(x_{t,p}\ge0\) on \(E\), \(\sum_p x_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,p}\le\kappa_p\). The question is to find a popular mass matching. In this tied case, the vote margin between two assignments is exactly the difference in their total matched mass: changing a matched post to another matched post earns no vote, while matching an otherwise unmatched applicant does. Thus the popular mass matchings are precisely the maximum-mass flows. This is a separate, narrow mirror of the reduction, and I expect it to be Class A, via max flow or linear programming. The theorem is a parallel-reduction result, not an NP-hardness result.

My weakest point is the lead mirror’s change in granularity. The paper’s Theorem 10 has individually represented, unit-capacity posts; the proposed problem aggregates interchangeable seats into post types with fractional capacities. Expanding those capacities into identical slots can introduce ties between copies, and the paper’s NC bound is for the expanded graph, not the compressed mass input. That means Theorem 10’s algorithm does not transfer as stated. The positive case is that the model preserves the one-sided matching question, the applicant majority comparison, and the maximum-cardinality objective while placing them in a plausible high-multiplicity regime. It mirrors this part of the paper, not the stable-matching rotation result or every result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch between Theorem 10’s result and the proposed mass model. The theorem concerns strict preferences over individual posts, each with unit capacity; its algorithm uses that graph structure. If the set of posts stays fixed while the applicant population grows, at most \(|P|\) applicants can receive ordinary posts, so the matched fraction tends to zero. To keep matched mass positive, supply must grow too. Keeping every post distinct makes the instance and output grow with the population; grouping posts into types with capacities changes the problem to a capacitated model and typically introduces ties. That is a natural co-modification, but it means the proposed question is not a continuation of the strict one-to-one setting whose NC algorithm is Theorem 10.

Theorem 11 gives a narrower objection. Its reduction handles arbitrary bipartite graphs, where applicant-specific neighborhoods and named posts carry the input. A regime with few repeated applicant types cannot represent that generality; a regime with as many types as applicants offers little compression. Aggregating repeated neighborhoods gives a sensible mass-matching question, but it studies a restricted type graph rather than the general matching problem used in the reduction.

These points weaken the connection to the paper’s results, but they do not defeat the mirrors. The capacity model is a plausible high-volume placement market, and comparing assignments by a coupling can preserve the applicant-level popularity vote. Theorem 11’s reduction may also motivate a valid high-multiplicity question. Since the resulting answer being easy is not a permitted objection, I can’t honestly claim that either mirror is worthless in every scenario. The negative case is weak precisely where its burden is strongest: both anchors still support worthwhile continuous questions.
