The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types and position types with rational masses, strict rankings over opposite-side types, and a rational distribution over type-and-upward-shift events, find a base-stable matching flow \(x_{bg}\) maximizing the probability that the matching remains stable after the selected event shifts one uniformly sampled member’s list.

The model it lives in

Represent each cohort by a type with mass \(\mu_b\) or \(\nu_g\), and each matching by a nonnegative flow \(x_{bg}\) with the corresponding row and column sums. Base stability requires, for every type pair, that the mass assigned by applicants to positions ranked below that pair or the mass assigned by positions to applicants ranked below it is zero. Maximize the event-weighted probability that the matching remains stable after the sampled member’s list is shifted.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s task of finding a robust stable matching. It leaves the theorem’s succinct representation of all robust matchings, and the later lattice results, for separate formulation over mass flows.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the robust-flow problem be solved in time polynomial in the number of supported types, the event support, and the input bit length?
  • Can the paper’s rotation-boundary structure be replaced by a compressed representation for type-level preferences and mass flows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case I believe is a narrow one: the paper’s robust stable matching problem has a credible type-compressed version in a large cohort market. Imagine a placement clearinghouse matching many applicants to one-to-one positions. Applicants of a type share a ranking of position types; positions of a type share a ranking of applicant types. The matching-relevant input is the type ranking, and people within a type are interchangeable. There might be hundreds of thousands of people on each side but only tens or hundreds of positive-mass types. A type’s mass is its fraction of the side’s population.

A list error can still affect just one person. Draw a type and upward shift according to a finite probability distribution, then choose a uniformly random member of that type whose list receives the shift. This is a plausible regime for a platform that stores shared preference templates and models an error as affecting one member. The matching remains an individual one: mass is split among type pairs, and every person receives one partner. A flow between types records the proportions of those individual matches.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, given a stable matching instance—allowing incomplete preference lists—and a probability distribution over the paper’s domain of upward-shift errors, a polynomial-time algorithm finds a robust stable matching. It also finds a partial order \(\Pi_0\) on \(O(n^2)\) elements whose closed sets correspond to all robust stable matchings; those matchings form a lattice and a sublattice of the lattice of all stable matchings. The paper says the main computational step is one maximum-flow computation on a network with \(O(n^2)\) vertices. This is the right anchor because the paper has no comparable numbered hardness result. The hardness results mentioned in related work concern different uncertainty models.

My lead mirror is Type-Compressed Robust Stable Matching. Its input consists of finite sets \(B\) and \(G\) of applicant and position types; positive rational masses \(\mu_b\) and \(\nu_g\), each summing to \(1\); strict rankings of opposite-side types for every type; and a rational distribution \(p\) over type-and-upward-shift events. Each event shifts the list of one uniformly sampled member of the selected type.

A candidate matching is a nonnegative rational flow \(x_{bg}\), with \(\sum_g x_{bg}=\mu_b\) and \(\sum_b x_{bg}=\nu_g\). For each pair \(b,g\), define \(R^B_{bg}(x)=\sum_{h:\,g\succ_b h}x_{bh}\), the mass of type \(b\) assigned to positions it ranks below \(g\), and \(R^G_{bg}(x)=\sum_{k:\,b\succ_g k}x_{kg}\), the mass of type \(g\) assigned to applicants it ranks below \(b\). The flow is stable in the base instance exactly when, for every \(b,g\), at least one of these two quantities is zero: if both are positive, members on each side can form blocking pairs.

The question is: among base-stable flows \(x\), find one maximizing the probability that the individual matching remains stable after the sampled person’s list is shifted. This probability is determined by the flow. For example, after a shift to a member of applicant type \(b\), if that member is matched to position type \(h\), the matching survives exactly when every type \(g\) that the shifted list ranks above \(h\) has \(R^G_{bg}(x)=0\). The probability of each possible partner type \(h\) for the sampled member is \(x_{bh}/\mu_b\); the position-side case is symmetric. Thus the problem has a finite input and an explicit exact output, with a natural target running time polynomial in the number of supported types, the error support size, and the binary input length.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, as a research conjecture. The paper’s upward-shift structure is promising: it shows that the instability caused by a shift has a tightly controlled boundary in the rotation lattice, then reduces robust selection to maximum flow. The continuous question is whether that structure admits a representation whose size depends on the type support and rational bit lengths. Expanding rational masses into individual clones is not enough: their denominator may be exponential in the input length. Nor does the type-level stability condition itself give an LP; a polynomial algorithm needs to capture the rotation or boundary structure in compressed form.

This mirrors the first output of Theorem 1—the robust stable matching. I would leave its partial-order representation of *all* robust matchings as a follow-up: a continuous model can have infinitely many distinct mass flows, so that output needs a new formulation. Natural further questions are whether the robust flows form an analogous lattice, and whether the compressed method extends to multiple simultaneous errors or downward shifts.

The soft spot is the change in what a type ranks: here it ranks counterpart *types*, with individuals within a counterpart type interchangeable, whereas the paper’s instance ranks named individuals. A single perturbed person has zero mass, so the mirror must preserve the paper’s pointwise blocking test explicitly; treating errors as perturbations of whole cohorts would change the problem. The flow captures the sampled person’s distribution of partner types, which is enough for that test under the stated type preferences. If the authors would not accept category-level preferences and one-person errors as their problem in a cohort market, this would be an extension rather than a faithful mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for a named problem, so the “no computational result to mirror” objection is unavailable. The strongest negative case is that the proposed compression changes what counts as a preference and a blocking pair.

In the paper, each agent ranks named individuals, and an error shifts one named person’s list. The rotation structure is built from those identities. A flow \(x_{bg}\) between categories does not preserve the within-category rankings that determine whether a particular pair blocks. If types retain complete strict rankings over individuals, those rankings grow with the population and the flow no longer captures the relevant information.

The proponent’s improved version instead has agents rank counterpart *types*. That makes everyone within a counterpart type tied. Its condition that the two sides cannot both have positive mass assigned to worse types is a coherent stability condition for those ties, but it is a different semantics from the paper’s strict, identity-level stability. The “one randomly selected member” error then adds a tagged-agent layer to a nonatomic population. That layer can be defined, as the proponent does, but it is an extra modeling choice: the paper’s theorem does not supply its rotation structure, and the tagged error is not simply the paper’s instance with its population axis taken to a limit. Shifting a positive mass of a type would avoid the tagged-agent issue, but would model a cohort error rather than the paper’s single-list error.

This makes a credible case against calling the proposed flow problem a faithful mirror of Theorem 1. It does not establish the universal negative the assignment asks for. A large placement market with category-level preferences and a random tagged-agent perturbation is plausible, and the resulting problem is explicit. If the authors recognize that tied, type-level formulation as their problem, one anchor survives. The negative case is therefore strongest as a challenge to fidelity, and weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
