What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror would compute the rule outcomes and fairness metrics for mass distributions; it would not establish a general guarantee from the paper’s comparisons on 35 instances.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Definitions 1 and 2 define GreedCost and MES; they do not assert a computational result. Sections 3 and 4 report empirical comparisons on 35 instances, but contain no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting complexity. So I cannot honestly claim the paper proves an anchored result whose hardness might dissolve in a continuum.

The strongest positive mirror I can make is a continuous-population version of the paper’s central GreedCost-versus-MES+ comparison. In a large neighborhood budgeting round, take a type to be a submitted approval ballot \(A\subseteq P\), where \(P\) is the fixed project menu. Its mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of residents submitting that ballot, with \(\sum_A\mu_A=1\). Residents with the same ballot are indistinguishable to these rules: project costs and prices are shared, and the rules use the ballot rather than voter identity. A large city or district round with thousands of residents and many repeated ballots is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s rounds had a median of 3,218 voters and 34 projects, but it does not report the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots, so it does not establish that \(\tau\ll n\).

Keep projects indivisible. Let each project \(p\) have cost \(c_p\), let \(B\) be the budget, and provide a fixed tie-breaking order. GreedCost ranks projects by approval mass, \(\sum_{A:p\in A}\mu_A\), then funds them in order whenever they fit the remaining budget. For MES, a type has a virtual balance \(w_A\) per unit of mass, initialized to \(B\), so its aggregate virtual balance is \(\mu_Aw_A\). For each affordable project, equalize supporters’ contributions subject to their remaining balances, weighted by their masses; select the project with the smallest maximum contribution-to-cost ratio, then update balances. MES+ applies GreedCost to the projects and budget left after MES. This is the same population question the authors studied, with ballot counts replaced by ballot masses. For a discrete profile of \(n\) residents, multiplying each resident’s MES balance and payment by \(n\) gives this mass accounting, preserving the project choices.

A concrete candidate problem is: given \(P\), rational costs and budget, a support \(\mathcal A\subseteq2^P\) of \(\tau\) approval types with rational masses, and the tie-breaking order, compute the GreedCost and MES+ project sets and their exact population-level metrics. For \(W\subseteq P\), define type \(A\)’s normalized cost satisfaction as \(s_A(W)=\sum_{p\in A\cap W}c_p/B\). The requested metrics could include average satisfaction \(\sum_A\mu_As_A(W)\), happiness \(\sum_A\mu_A\mathbf{1}[A\cap W\ne\varnothing]\), and the weighted Gini coefficient of the values \(s_A(W)\). The expected classification is tractable: both rules can be simulated over at most \(m\) project selections, using arithmetic over the \(\tau\) supported types, and the metrics can be computed from those types. It is a faithful continuous version of the paper’s rule comparison, though likely a modest Class A result rather than a deep optimization problem.

The weak point is that this candidate is not anchored in a named computational result from the paper, as required. It also may be easy to compute, and the paper’s reported fairness improvements are empirical findings for its sample, not claims about all mass distributions. The missing support-size data further weakens the case for a useful high-multiplicity regime. So the population mirror is plausible and concrete, but this paper does not supply the required complexity anchor for a strong ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Definitions 1 and 2 specify two rules; the numbered example illustrates them; Sections 3 and 4 report empirical comparisons on 35 instances. None states a complexity result for bribery, control, campaigning, robustness, or another computational problem. The reported fairness gains are sample statistics, not a theorem about societies generally.

The proposed population model is coherent. Approval ballots are complete types for these rules, and Amsterdam budgeting provides a plausible large-population setting. But its proposed task—compute GreedCost, MES+, and their metrics from ballot masses—is direct evaluation. GreedCost sorts projects by approval mass. MES performs at most \(m\) project selections, updating balances for the \(\tau\) supported types; MES+ then runs GreedCost on the remainder. The weighted satisfaction, happiness, and Gini metrics can also be computed from those types. With rational inputs and fixed tie-breaking, this is a straightforward polynomial-time simulation in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. It introduces no algorithmic bottleneck of the kind the programme is meant to investigate.

The strongest alternative would ask how robust the paper’s fairness comparison is across all approval distributions—for example, which mass shifts reverse the Gini or happiness comparison. That could be a legitimate follow-up, but it requires adding an outer optimization problem and choosing its domain and objective. The paper makes empirical recommendations from a particular dataset; it does not formulate a universal comparison or a sensitivity question. That makes the alternative a new research direction rather than a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The negative case has a clear limit: I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile population model exists here. The paper’s rules and fairness measures transfer naturally to masses, and its setting has many voters. The case against is narrower: this is a plausible high-multiplicity application without a computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror, and the most direct proposed task is routine rule evaluation. On the programme’s stated standard, that is not enough to make this paper a strong candidate.
