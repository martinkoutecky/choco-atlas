What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population-continuous version of the paper’s fair-division problem, but the paper has no named computational result to serve as a qualifying anchor. Its strongest nearby result is Theorem 3.2, proved here: for finitely many individuals with mutually absolutely continuous, nonatomic finite measures, uniformly continuous and strictly monotone utilities, and a closed partition matrix range, a Pareto optimal envy-free partition exists. That is an existence theorem, not a result that the problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The authors’ closing remark about protocols identifies computation as future work.

A concrete candidate is Type-Mass Pareto-Optimal Envy-Free Allocation. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete preference types, with rational masses \(p_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Let \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\lambda)\) describe the heterogeneous commodity, \(b\) be its per-capita supply profile, and \(\hat\nu_t\) be type \(t\)’s utility on the fractional-bundle space
\[ I=\{f\in L^\infty(\Omega,\mathcal F,\lambda):0\le f\le1\}. \]
Given utilities satisfying suitable versions of the paper’s continuity and strict-monotonicity assumptions, find bundles \(f_t\in I\) such that
\[ \sum_{t\in T}p_t f_t=b \]
almost everywhere, every type weakly prefers its own bundle to every other type’s bundle, and the allocation is Pareto optimal: no feasible replacement makes every type weakly better off and at least one type strictly better off. A solution is the collection of type bundles \( (f_t)_{t\in T} \). There is no scalar cost to minimize; the task is to find an allocation meeting the fairness and efficiency conditions.

A credible regime would be a large cooperative or public allocation of a heterogeneous resource to many households, where households fall into a small number \(\tau=|T|\) of stable, fully specified preference classes. The population might be very large while \(\tau\) remains modest. Supply scales with population, and the fractional bundle for a type is a per-capita allocation. The continuous object here is the population distribution; the commodity’s heterogeneity is part of the original problem and the supply scaling is needed to make a large-population regime meaningful. This seems recognizable to the authors as the same fair-allocation question with repeated agents, though the weighted feasibility constraint is a genuine extension.

For that candidate, I would conjecture that an existence analogue can be proved under appropriate assumptions. I cannot honestly call its computational search Class A, B, or C from this paper: the KKM-based proof is not an efficient algorithm, and the paper gives no finite input representation for arbitrary utility functions or output representation for bundles. A computational version would first need to specify those representations. It would also need to settle whether type-level fractional bundles are the intended allocation, or whether each person receives an integral piece and the model tracks within-type distributions.

So the positive case is a narrow one: the paper’s allocation problem admits a natural, author-recognizable population mirror, and Theorem 3.2 is its closest named anchor. The weakest point is decisive under your anchor rule: that theorem is not computational, and the proposed mirror is a research extension rather than a continuous counterpart to a complexity result in the paper. The paper’s existing continuity is in the commodity, while its population remains finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that Theorem 3.2 is not a computational result. It proves existence under analytic assumptions; it gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or finite representation of utilities and allocations. The authors themselves leave protocols for finding these allocations as future work. So this paper supplies no named computational result for the programme to continuize.

The proposed type-mass problem is coherent, but it is a new model rather than a computational mirror of that theorem. Giving every member of a type one common bundle imposes equal treatment. The theorem does not impose that: even agents with identical utilities may receive different bundles, and the paper expressly avoids convexity assumptions that might make averaging those bundles harmless. The fractional-bundle utility extension does not establish that a common type bundle preserves the original allocation problem.

A more faithful mirror would let members of each type receive different bundles and track their distribution. That preserves the possibility of unequal bundles, but the problem is no longer described by one bundle per type. The paper’s finite-agent proof uses finitely many utility comparisons and a finite partition matrix range; it provides no corresponding representation or computational procedure for a population’s allocation distributions. Alternatively, one could restrict to finitely described commodities and utilities, but that would supply new modeling and encoding choices, not a computational question already present in this paper.

I would therefore reject the anchor, while keeping the negative case’s limit clear: a carefully specified finite-attribute, high-multiplicity fair-division problem could be worthwhile in its own right. What this paper does not establish is that such a problem is its continuous computational mirror.
