The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of complete student types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), project and lecturer sets, type preference orders and lecturer priority orders, and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_p\) and \(\lambda_\ell\), decide whether there are rational assignments \(x_{tp}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_p x_{tp}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tp}\le\kappa_p\), and \(\sum_t\sum_{p\in P_\ell}x_{tp}\le\lambda_\ell\), with no positive mass of any type-project pair forming a super-stability block under the paper’s capacity and priority conditions; return such an assignment or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A finite-support society of complete student types, each carrying mass \(\mu_t\), competes for project and lecturer capacity measured per capita. The variables \(x_{tp}\) give the mass of type \(t\) assigned to project \(p\); each unit of mass receives at most one whole project, and the objective is to find a feasible assignment with no super-stability block.

The objection that survived

The mirror assumes replicated capacity for recurring project templates and coarse priority tiers; the paper’s experiments with mostly strict lecturer preferences do not establish how often such a regime occurs.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s search for a super-stable matching and its student-optimality guarantee as a possible follow-up. It leaves the empirical existence-rate results and the paper’s structural theorems without a continuous analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the proposal-and-deletion algorithm be extended to rational type masses and per-capita capacities, with running time polynomial in the number of types and input bit length?
  • Does a continuous assignment exist that gives each type its best attainable preference tier?
  • Under what conditions can rational mass solutions be scaled to finite SPA-ST instances with the same super-stability outcome?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s central search problem: given SPA-ST preferences and capacities, find a super-stable matching or report that none exists. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that Algorithm SPA-ST-super decides existence in \(O(L)\) time and \(O(n_1n_2)\) space; when a super-stable matching exists, every execution finds one in which each assigned student gets the best project she could obtain in any super-stable matching, and every unassigned student is unassigned in all such matchings. [Theorem 2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10878-020-00632-x) is the right lead: the paper offers no comparable, numbered hardness result of its own. Its introduction mentions NP-hardness for finding a maximum-size weakly stable matching, but attributes that prior result to other work; it is not an anchor for the paper’s super-stability contribution.

A plausible regime is a large university consortium or centralized project-allocation programme: \(N\) students compete for projects offered by a much smaller set of lecturers, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type records a student’s acceptable projects and ranking, plus her acceptability and priority tier in every relevant lecturer’s list. Many students can share a type when they submit the same ranking and lecturers place students with the same profile in the same priority tier. This fits the paper’s own motivation: lecturers may be unable or unwilling to rank every student distinctly, and it discusses allocation in large cohorts. The regime is most credible where preferences recur and lecturer priorities have coarse tie classes; it is not a claim that every student-project instance has few types.

Call the mirror Continuous Super-Stable Project Allocation. Its input is a finite project set \(P\), lecturers \(\mathcal L\) with project sets \(P_\ell\), and a finite support \(T\) of types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a weak preference order over acceptable projects. Each lecturer has a weak priority order over acceptable types. Project and lecturer capacities are rational per-capita rates \(\kappa_p\) and \(\lambda_\ell\), so a cohort of size \(N\) has capacities \(N\kappa_p\) and \(N\lambda_\ell\). The decision variable is \(x_{tp}\ge 0\), the fraction of the whole population consisting of type \(t\) assigned to project \(p\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_p x_{tp}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tp}\le\kappa_p,\qquad \sum_t\sum_{p\in P_\ell}x_{tp}\le\lambda_\ell. \]
The question is whether there is a rational \(x\) satisfying these constraints with no super-stability block, and to return such an \(x\) or report that none exists. There is no welfare-maximization objective, just as the paper’s problem is to find a super-stable matching.

For precision, a type-project pair \((t,p)\) can block only if it is acceptable and there is positive mass of type \(t\) willing to move to \(p\). That mass is
\[ w_{tp}(x)=\mu_t-x_{tp}-\sum_{q:\,q\succ_t p}x_{tq}, \]
so it counts unassigned mass and mass assigned to a worse or tied alternative, but not mass already at \(p\) or strictly better off elsewhere. If \(w_{tp}(x)>0\), the pair blocks under the paper’s same three capacity-and-priority cases: both \(p\) and \(\ell\) have spare capacity; \(p\) has spare capacity while \(\ell\) is full and either already assigns some mass of type \(t\) or ranks \(t\) at least as highly as its worst assigned type; or \(p\) is full and \(\ell\) ranks \(t\) at least as highly as the worst type assigned to \(p\). “Worst assigned type” means the least-preferred type receiving positive mass there. This keeps the paper’s robust no-blocking condition while making the population the continuous object. Individuals still receive at most one whole project; fractional values describe cohort proportions, not fractional projects.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A, but that is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 2. Its proposal-and-deletion structure suggests a mass-based algorithm or a compact optimization formulation, with running time sought in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length rather than in the number of cloned students. The paper’s student-optimality guarantee also suggests a follow-up: does a super-stable mass assignment always exist that gives each type its best attainable preference tier? A useful bridge question is whether rational mass solutions and per-capita capacities can be scaled by a common denominator into finite SPA-ST instances with exactly the same super-stability outcome. Such a dictionary would clarify when the compressed continuous input is genuinely useful.

The weakest point is that type completeness can defeat the hoped-for compression: with a distinct strict priority rank for every student, lecturer priorities alone may make \(\tau\) close to \(N\). The mirror is most convincing for cohorts with recurring preference bundles and tied priority classes. Also, the paper proves an efficient algorithm for explicitly listed students, not for rational masses encoded compactly; partial acceptance and rejection of a type’s mass could change the algorithm substantially. The case remains worthwhile as a precise high-multiplicity extension, but it is a more modest computational payoff than a hardness result whose population-driven combinatorics disappear.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor: it decides super-stable matching existence in \(O(L)\) time and returns a student-optimal solution when one exists. So the case against cannot rest on the absence of a result to mirror. [Theorem 2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10878-020-00632-x)

The strongest mirror is the proposed large-cohort allocation with repeated preference profiles, coarse lecturer priority tiers, and capacities that grow with cohort size. The concern is whether that scaling describes student-project allocation. Projects and supervision places are physical slots. If intake grows while the menu and staffing stay fixed, matched capacity becomes a vanishing fraction of the population. Keeping capacity per capita positive requires expanding the number of places while holding the project and lecturer set fixed. That can describe recurring project templates, but it shifts the setting toward allocation among broad categories of places.

Type compression is also fragile. A complete type must include a student’s ranking and her priority tier at every relevant lecturer. Distinct lecturer ranks can therefore make \(\tau\) grow with the cohort even when student rankings repeat. In the paper’s low-tie experiments, lecturer lists are overwhelmingly strict, so the reported instances do not establish a large-cohort regime with small \(\tau\). Grouping applicants into broad priority bands would help, but it selects a narrower institutional model.

Those are serious limits on how often the mirror applies, but they do not eliminate the best version. A large programme with recurring preference profiles, shared priority bands, and replicated project capacity is coherent; students still receive whole projects, and rational masses can represent the cohort shares. I cannot honestly rule out that scenario or show that the authors would reject it. The negative case is therefore limited to the mirror’s narrow, assumption-heavy domain, and the anchor survives there.
