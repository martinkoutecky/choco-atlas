The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(A\), a support of \(\tau\) complete evaluator rankings \(t\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a strict-majority-margin promise, compute the exact Top Cycle and Uncovered Set of the induced tournament, where \(a\) defeats \(b\) iff \(\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t>1/2\). Return the two membership vectors.

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings of evaluators over the finite candidate set; each type carries mass \(\mu_t\). Aggregate those masses into pairwise-majority edges, then return the Top-Cycle and Uncovered-Set membership vectors. There is no separate optimization objective or decision variable.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5.11 starts from a supplied tournament matrix; deriving that matrix from evaluator-type masses, and the complexity of that step, are not analyzed in the paper.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers computation and recovery of the Top Cycle and Uncovered Set from population-induced pairwise preferences; it leaves neural-model training, calibration, and the specific real-data analyses alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the strict-margin promise be weakened while retaining reliable core recovery?
  • How should ties and missing judgments be represented in the type distribution?
  • How small is the support \(\tau\) for realistic evaluator populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is to treat the evaluators as the continuous population and the systems being evaluated as the finite candidate set. In a large benchmark or arena, many human evaluation instances compare a relatively small catalog of AI systems. A type could be a complete evaluator and task profile that induces the same ranking of those systems; its mass is the fraction of the evaluation population with that ranking. In a structured benchmark, the number \(N\) of evaluation instances could be very large while the number \(\tau\) of recurring rubric and task types is much smaller.

For candidates \(A=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) and ranking types \(t\), the induced pairwise majority matrix is
\[ P_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t. \]
With strict pairwise margins, this defines the same majority tournament that the paper analyzes. This is a population continuization: changing the masses can change the majority edges and hence the core. It is not just a continuous output score.

There is an important anchor limitation. The paper has no numbered result classifying its core-computation problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its complexity table is background. The closest named results are algorithmic and statistical guarantees, so they can support a positive mirror, but they do not establish a complexity classification.

The lead mirror is Continuous Population Tournament-Core Computation. Its instance is a finite candidate set, a support list of \(\tau\) complete ranking types with positive binary-rational masses summing to one, and a strict-margin promise. The task is to return the exact Top Cycle and Uncovered Set of the induced majority tournament; a solution is the pair of membership vectors for those sets.

Its anchor is Theorem 5.11, “Consistency of STE,” proved in this paper. Under a strict-margin assumption, path length \(K\ge n-1\), and all aggregation temperatures tending to zero, the Top-Cycle score tends to \(1\) exactly for agents in the hard tournament’s Top Cycle and to \(0\) for all others; the Uncovered-Set score has the corresponding limit for the hard Uncovered Set. This gives a precise bridge from the paper’s computed scores to the population-weighted core. I expect the mirror problem to be tractable: aggregate the \(\tau\) rankings into pairwise majority edges, then compute the two tournament solutions. That takes polynomial time in \(n,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\). The theorem establishes limiting correctness, though the polynomial-time expectation comes from the straightforward aggregation and graph computation, not from a complexity theorem in the paper.

A second, narrower question is Sampled Population Core Recovery. The latent society is a distribution \(\mu\) over evaluator ranking types, but the input exposes independent pairwise judgments sampled from that population. Given a candidate set, a strict-margin lower bound, and a failure tolerance, return the exact Top Cycle and Uncovered Set with at least the requested success probability.

Its anchor is Proposition 5.14, “Sample Complexity for Core Recovery,” proved in this paper. If each unordered pair receives
\[ m\ge \frac{1}{2\delta^2}\log\!\left(\frac{2{n\choose 2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{total}}}\right) \]
independent comparisons, then with probability at least \(1-\delta_{\mathrm{total}}\) the empirical majority tournament equals the true one; consequently, the zero-temperature limit recovers its true Top Cycle and Uncovered Set. This is a sample-complexity guarantee, not a runtime classification. I expect this recovery task to be statistically tractable under the theorem’s margin and sampling assumptions, followed by polynomial-time computation of the two sets.

This case covers the paper’s Top-Cycle and Uncovered-Set identification and recovery claims. It does not mirror the neural pairwise-model training, calibration, or its particular real-data analyses. Further questions include whether the strict-margin promise can be weakened, how to handle ties and missing judgments, and whether the type support \(\tau\) stays small in realistic evaluator populations.

The weakest point is that the paper’s tournament nodes are the evaluated systems, whereas this mirror makes evaluators the population and systems the alternatives. That role change is plausible given the paper’s preference-data and social-choice framing, but it is an interpretive step. Also, the paper supplies no numbered hardness or polynomial-time result for the underlying core problem, so this is a credible continuous population model with named algorithmic anchors—not a paper-backed complexity separation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper supplies no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem over a population. Theorem 5.11 proves that the soft operators converge to the classical tournament solutions for a given tournament. Proposition 5.14 bounds the samples needed to recover that tournament. Neither studies an input society of types and masses or classifies an algorithm for such an input. The unnumbered runtime discussion concerns the operators on a finite tournament. These results can motivate a population model, but they do not anchor the complexity mirror the programme is looking for.

The best version of the proposed mirror is coherent: keep the evaluated systems as candidates, and let evaluator types be complete pairwise-judgment profiles with rational masses. Their weighted judgments induce a majority tournament, whose Top Cycle and Uncovered Set can be computed. Repeated evaluator types in a large benchmark or arena are plausible, and using full pairwise profiles avoids imposing transitive rankings. This is the proponent’s strongest case; evaluator multiplicity itself is not a sound objection.

But that model changes what the paper is studying. Its tournament probabilities average agent performance over contexts \(x\sim Q\). Evaluator types instead define a population whose preferences are aggregated over the candidate systems. A type based on task performance would make the population the context distribution \(Q\), which is precisely a continuous context model rather than a continuous population of agents. A type based on human judgments gives a sensible voting problem, but it imports that problem into the paper; Theorem 5.11 applies only after the judgments have already been aggregated into \(P\).

The sampled-recovery anchor is weaker still as a population-computation result. Proposition 5.14 assumes repeated observations for each candidate pair and establishes a statistical guarantee under a strict margin. It does not take a supported distribution of evaluator types as input, and its guarantee says nothing about the running time of a population algorithm. Recasting the observations as samples from evaluator types would require a new sampling model and analysis.

So I would reject these as worthwhile computational mirrors of this paper: the named anchors concern soft-operator consistency and finite-sample edge recovery, while the plausible population formulation changes the source of the tournament data. I cannot make the universal claim airtight. A large evaluation panel with recurring evaluator profiles is a credible population regime, and authors might recognize its majority-core question as relevant. That is the strongest surviving possibility, but its connection to the paper’s computational results is thin.
