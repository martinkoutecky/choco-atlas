The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result of the required kind. Its theorems concern strategyproofness and distortion bounds, not whether an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest candidate is Theorem 7, proved in the paper: for the Max-of-Average objective, every strategyproof distributed mechanism has distortion at least \(7/2-\varepsilon\), for every \(\varepsilon>0\). That is a named lower-bound result, but it is a bound on mechanism performance, not a computational hardness result, so it cannot serve as a compliant anchor.

A plausible population model would take each type to be a district and location pair, with \(\mu_d\) a distribution of residents’ locations in district \(d\). The candidate sites remain finite; the mechanism still chooses two representatives per district and then outputs two facilities. Its Max-of-Average objective becomes
\[ \max_d \int \max\{|x-w_1|,|x-w_2|\}\,d\mu_d(x). \]
This fits a high-multiplicity scenario such as many residents across a fixed set of districts, with relatively few distinct location types. But for a nonatomic distribution, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot affect a distribution-based mechanism, making individual strategyproofness vacuous. Preserving meaningful incentives requires retaining a finite agent mass or changing the deviation model.

So there is a plausible continuous-population follow-up about strategyproof distortion, but no anchor-based computational mirror for this paper under the programme’s criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named result offered as an anchor is Theorem 7’s lower bound of \(7/2-\varepsilon\) on strategyproof distortion for the Max-of-Average objective. It is a theorem about the quality of mechanisms, not about the complexity of computing an outcome or finding a mechanism. The other results have the same character: distortion bounds and a strategyproofness proof. Thus there is no computational result here for a continuous mirror to continue.

The proposed district model is plausible as a high-multiplicity population: many residents in each district, represented by a distribution \(\mu_d\) over locations, with objective \(\max_d\int \max\{|x-w_1|,|x-w_2|\}\,d\mu_d(x)\). The obstacle is that the paper’s central constraint is individual strategyproofness. In an atomless limit, one person’s report does not change \(\mu_d\), so that constraint becomes vacuous. Giving a deviation positive mass instead studies coalition or bloc manipulation; retaining a person’s \(1/n\) effect requires keeping a population scale or granularity parameter. Either may be an interesting new model, but neither is simply the paper’s individual-incentive result over a continuous society.

There is a stronger computational formulation one could invent: search for an optimal strategyproof distributed mechanism over a finite report domain. But the paper specifies no representation of mechanisms or finite domain for that search. Its actual quantile mechanisms are straightforward to compute, and for a given finite-support instance one can evaluate candidate facility pairs by enumeration. The hard part in the paper is proving incentive and distortion guarantees, not solving a hidden large-scale optimization problem. Restricting to quantile mechanisms makes the search a small parameter choice; searching over all mechanisms creates a separate mechanism-synthesis problem.

So the best case against is that the population model is sensible, but the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its natural limiting operation erases the individual incentive that drives its results. A continuous distortion theory could still be worthwhile to mechanism-design researchers. Its case for ChoCo is weak unless the programme chooses to include new incentive-model and mechanism-synthesis questions beyond its computational-complexity remit.
