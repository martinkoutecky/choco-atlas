The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result: no theorem or proposition says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results concern equilibrium existence, equilibrium outcomes, and comparative statics. So under the required anchor rule, there are no qualifying anchors.

The strongest positive lead is Proposition 5, proved in this paper, though it is not a qualifying computational anchor. In its binary-payoff large-election setting, a fraction \(q\in[1/2,1)\) of voters gain \(v_w>0\) from the optimal policy and the rest lose \(v_\ell>0\). With perfect informative news arriving independently with probability \(\lambda\), define
\[ \rho=\frac{q-\frac12}{1-q}. \]
If \(v_\ell/v_w>\rho\), then some \(\lambda^*>0\) has the property that, for every \(\lambda<\lambda^*\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\), all sufficiently large odd electorates admit a strict equilibrium in which the inferior policy wins with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\). Otherwise, for every population size and every \(\lambda\), the optimal policy wins with probability \(1\) in every equilibrium.

If a non-complexity result may motivate a new computational question, I would propose Large-Election Fragility Classification as the lead mirror. An instance is rational \(q\in[1/2,1)\) and \(r=v_\ell/v_w\), with \(0<r<q/(1-q)\) so the optimal policy is ex ante optimal. The society is unit mass: in each state, mass \(q\) are winners and mass \(1-q\) are losers; identities within those groups are exchangeable. A voter’s complete type records their state-contingent payoff and signal technology. Ask whether the inferior-policy outcome described in Proposition 5 occurs for all sufficiently scarce information in large finite approximations, or whether every equilibrium selects the optimal policy. A solution is the correct classification. For this restricted input, the expected answer is tractable: compare \(r\) with \(\rho\). This is a derived decision procedure, not a complexity result stated by the paper.

The regime is plausible for large referendums or national elections: millions of voters can share a small number of payoff-and-information types, with \(q\) and \(1-q\) representing population shares. This mirror covers only the paper’s fixed-\(q\), binary-payoff large-election analysis, not its general equilibrium characterization.

The weakness is fundamental: in a standard continuum voting game, an individual voter has zero probability of being pivotal, so the paper’s strategic mechanism disappears. The proposed question preserves it by defining the answer through equilibria of finite approximations. That makes it a credible high-multiplicity limit question, but a weak direct continuum-game mirror—and the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper has no computational-complexity result to mirror. Its named results characterize equilibria and comparative statics; none studies an algorithmic problem or its complexity. Proposition 5, the proponent’s lead, is an asymptotic equilibrium characterization. Turning its condition \(v_\ell/v_w>\rho\) into a yes-or-no question does not by itself create a computational problem: the paper supplies no algorithmic task or complexity landscape for that question.

A stronger proposed mirror might take a rational distribution over payoff and signal types and ask for an algorithm that decides whether an inferior-policy equilibrium exists. But the paper’s mechanism depends on a voter’s chance of being pivotal: exactly \(\tau\) other votes for \(p^*\) make their signals informative about the voter’s payoff. In an atomless continuum, one voter’s vote changes no aggregate vote share. That pivotal event and its posterior disappear; even informed voters need not have a strict incentive to vote according to their signal. Masses can describe the aggregate votes, but they do not preserve the incentives that generate the paper’s equilibrium results.

One could retain those incentives by studying finite elections along a sequence, or by making blocs the strategic players. The first keeps finite pivotality and asks an asymptotic question; the second leaves a finite set of strategic blocs. Either may motivate new work, but neither yields a standalone continuous-population computational problem from this paper. Proposition 5’s fixed-\(q\), binary-payoff case is especially thin as a computational lead: it offers a parameter classification, not a problem involving the optimization technology or complexity questions the ChoCo programme targets.

Large elections are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, so I would not argue that voter populations cannot sensibly be continuized. The negative case is narrower: this paper supplies no computational anchor, and its central strategic mechanism relies on individual pivotality that a direct continuum model removes. A computational study of richer continuum voting games could still be worthwhile, but it would be a new project rather than a mirror of the results here. That leaves the universal claim less than airtight: a sufficiently carefully designed asymptotic-pivotality model might inspire a valuable algorithmic question. The paper itself, however, gives little reason to expect such a question to be a continuous computational-social-choice mirror.
