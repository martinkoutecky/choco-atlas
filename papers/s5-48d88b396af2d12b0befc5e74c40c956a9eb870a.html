The continuous mirror question

Given a finite template set \(X\), a finite support of agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(\kappa_x\), and a fixed fluid service schedule, compute a polynomial-time local policy \(\Pi\) for a target type \(t^\star\) that improves its mean final utility by a factor of at least \(1+\epsilon\) over the all-greedy fluid execution, whenever such a policy exists. Each individual receives an integral feasible bundle; the policy induces the mass flow, and all other types remain greedy.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete agent types, each with a submodular valuation \(f_t\) and feasibility family \(\mathcal I_t\), competes for templates with supplies \(\kappa_x\). The decision rule is a shared local policy for one target type, applied independently to its members during a fixed fluid Round-Robin schedule; its objective is that type’s mean utility in the induced allocation flow.

The objection that survived

A single infinitesimal member cannot affect rival choices in the fluid process, so preserving competition requires treating the shared target-type policy as a positive-mass deviation; the proponent does not fully establish that this change in the unit of action fits the authors’ intended question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers the policy-improvement question in Theorem 4.1. It leaves the optimization guarantees, fairness guarantees, robustness results, and maximin-share results without mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the Theorem 4.1 reduction establish hardness for the specified fluid schedule and positive-mass target-type policy?
  • Which representation of the service schedule and policy gives a well-defined polynomial-time input model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a high multiplicity version of the paper’s *improvement over greedy* problem. The anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n\ge2\), any agent \(j\), and any fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,0.2)\), unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm can consistently find a policy giving \(j\) at least \(1+\epsilon\) times the value of the greedy policy whenever such an improvement is possible—even when every other agent has an additive objective and follows a greedy policy. The authors emphasize that this hardness holds even with no individual feasibility constraints. The proof reduces from monotone submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint, using Theorem 4.2, Feige’s cited result that no polynomial-time algorithm achieves approximation ratio \(1-1/e+\delta\) for any constant \(\delta>0\), unless \(P=NP\).

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Call it Mass-Scaled Improvement over Greedy. An instance has a finite catalogue \(X\) of \(m\) item templates; a finite set \(T\) of agent types with support size \(\tau\); rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one; and, for each type \(t\), a normalized nonnegative submodular valuation \(f_t:2^X\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) and a feasible-family constraint \(\mathcal I_t\). The lead version uses no individual constraints, as in the hardness setting of Theorem 4.1. Item template \(x\) has rational supply \(\kappa_x\), measured in copies per unit population. A member of the population still receives an integral bundle \(S\in\mathcal I_t\); only the mass receiving each bundle can be fractional. Thus an allocation is a measure \(\nu_{t,S}\), with
\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal I_t}\nu_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,S:x\in S}\nu_{t,S}\le\kappa_x. \]

The instance also specifies a fixed per-round service order, analogous to the paper’s fixed agent order. It is a finite sequence of type-mass slots; a type may appear in more than one slot. In each round, agents in those slots take at most one item in sequence. The continuous execution is the corresponding fluid process: as mass takes an item, that mass of its supply is consumed. A greedy agent takes a feasible available template of maximum marginal value \(f_t(x\mid S)\), with fixed tie-breaking. This leaves each person with an integral bundle while tracking the population through mass flows.

Choose a target type \(t^\star\) of positive mass. Let \(V^G\) be its mean final utility when every agent follows the paper’s greedy policy. The problem is to find a local policy \(\Pi\) for members of \(t^\star\), leaving every other type greedy, that produces a measurable allocation flow with mean target-type utility at least \((1+\epsilon)V^G\), whenever such a policy exists. The policy is the solution, together with its induced flow; it is applied independently by each target-type member to their own bundle and the currently available templates. This keeps the question about improving the agents’ repeated choices, rather than replacing it with a centralized allocation problem.

A plausible regime is a large digital-advertising market with many local campaign units but few recurring valuation and constraint profiles. Units of the same type have the same submodular influence objective and feasibility rules. Templates represent standardized placement or audience-segment slots, with inventory scaling in proportion to the population. Then \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of units with a given profile, and \(\kappa_x\) is the available mass of copies of template \(x\). A type’s members compete for the same inventory and still select integral bundles. There can be many agents and few types, \(N\gg\tau\); for any fixed hard instance, the number of copies per type can grow while its type support stays fixed. The paper’s usual story of a few companies may be a poor high multiplicity regime, but repeated local campaigns are a plausible one.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. The hard combinatorics in Theorem 4.1 are in the discrete ground set and the submodular cardinality-maximization task, not in the number of separately named agents. Replicating agents of each role and scaling template supplies is the natural embedding to investigate: the population becomes large, but an individual’s bundle and the search over item subsets remain discrete. The problem’s computational parameters would include \(m,\tau\), the bit length of the masses and supplies, and the size of the service schedule. Establishing the transfer for a particular continuous fluid protocol would be a new proof obligation; I would not present it as already implied by Theorem 4.1.

This covers Theorem 4.1, not the paper’s FEF1, FEFu, or maximin-share guarantees. The allocations remain indivisible per person, so this is population continuization rather than outcome-space continuization. Nor is the paper itself a novelty collision: it studies a finite set of agents competing for indivisible items. Its submodular optimization and fair-division results are useful machinery and context, not a continuous-population complexity analysis.

My weakest point is the passage from one named deviating agent to a positive-mass type cohort. The continuous problem measures the cohort’s average utility, and its members may face different residual inventories as the fluid process runs. That can change which deviations are possible and how the paper’s reduction works. The proposed model remains recognizable as the authors’ policy-improvement question if the type shares a local policy rather than coordinating centrally, but the exact hardness transfer—and likely the authors’ acceptance of this particular service-order convention—needs to be shown.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is a real computational result, but its object is unusually tied to a finite serial allocation: one named agent changes policy, while everyone else keeps their policies, and the comparison is that agent’s realized utility against the greedy run. Theorem 4.2 supplies hardness for submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint; it does not by itself establish a high multiplicity version of that strategic question.

That distinction matters in the proposed mirror. A single deviating member of a nonatomic type has zero mass, so their deviation cannot change the fluid supply flow or other agents’ greedy choices. Their individual best response can still be defined, but it no longer captures the strategic effect at the heart of the paper’s setup. If instead every member of the target type adopts \(\Pi\), the deviation has positive mass and can change others’ choices. The objective is then the type’s average utility under a collective policy change. That may be sensible, but it changes the unit of deviation. If members’ policies depend on their place in the service order, that place must also be represented in the continuum; the finite-support type list alone does not describe it.

There are coherent repairs, and they expose the modeling choice. A fixed fluid background gives a representative infinitesimal agent a best response, but removes their effect on competition. A supply-constrained distribution over integral bundles gives the target cohort a meaningful average utility, but lets the modeler optimize a mass allocation centrally. That is a different problem from finding an individual policy improvement while other agents remain greedy. The proposed service schedule and its rule for splitting a type when supply runs short are therefore substantive parts of the model, not details inherited automatically from Theorem 4.1.

This defeats the proposed anchor as a ready-made mirror: the finite theorem does not transfer to the stated continuous question without deciding whether the object is an individual deviation, a type-wide policy change, or a centrally chosen allocation. It does not establish that any of those repaired questions would be uninteresting. Repeated campaign teams with shared valuation types and supplies scaled with population remain plausible, and the paper’s fairness and maximin-share results invite their own population-level questions. Under the programme’s rules, those are valid possibilities. So the negative case is strongest against this anchor’s claimed fit; it is weak as a case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
