What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that a population-continuum model of this paper’s subject is quite natural, even though the paper gives no eligible computational anchor. The authors’ own application to probabilistic preference aggregation supplies a plausible setting: a large forecasting or expert panel assigns probabilities to claims such as “\(x\) is better than \(y\).” A type could be a complete coherent opinion function \(P\) on the preference agenda, together with any per-person influence weight. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the share of the panel with that same report and weight. Standardized, rounded reports make it plausible that the number of types \(\tau\) is far smaller than the number of people.

One precise, but unanchored, candidate is Finite-Support Continuum Preference Pooling. Its input is a finite alternative set \(K\), \(|K|>2\), and rational data \((P^j,a_j,\mu_j)_{j=1}^{\tau}\), where each \(P^j\) is a coherent probabilistic opinion on the events \(x\succ y\), each \(a_j\ge 0\) is a per-person influence weight, and the masses \(\mu_j>0\) sum to one. The requested solution is the collective opinion \(Q\), with
\[ Q(x\succ y)= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j a_j P^j(x\succ y)} {\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j a_j} \quad\text{for every }x\ne y. \]
This is a coherent opinion because it is a weighted average of coherent opinions. Its computation takes polynomial time in \(|K|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. There is no optimization objective: the paper studies which pooling functions satisfy axioms, rather than minimizing or maximizing a quantity.

The nearest results are Theorem 5 and Corollary 2, both proved in the paper, but neither is a computational result. Theorem 5 says that for a non-simple agenda with more than four nontrivial events, every pooling function satisfying independence and conditional consensus compatibility is linear; for every finite simple nontrivial agenda, a nonlinear pooling function satisfying those same conditions exists. Corollary 2 applies this to the preference agenda: when \(|K|>2\), every such pooling function is linear; when \(|K|=2\), a nonlinear one exists. These characterize admissible rules. They do not establish a complexity class, algorithm, or hardness result.

That is the central limitation: computing a specified weighted pool is easy, but the paper’s theorem does not supply a computational problem about that operation. Asking instead whether continuum versions of the axioms force an integral pooling rule would be a meaningful follow-on, but it is still an axiomatic characterization question. So the population scenario is plausible and close to the authors’ topic; the weakest point is that turning it into a ChoCo computational question requires adding a new task. Strictly applying the requested anchor criterion, this paper has no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 5 and Corollary 2 characterize which pooling functions satisfy axioms; neither gives a complexity result, algorithm, or hardness result. Recasting their conclusions for a continuum of experts would still be an axiomatic characterization, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The proponent’s expert-panel scenario is plausible: repeated coherent reports, with any influence weight included in the type, give a sensible distribution over voter-like types. But the proposed task—compute the specified weighted average—just evaluates a chosen pooling rule, in time polynomial in the support size and agenda size. The theorem does not ask which rule to choose from data; it characterizes all rules that satisfy its axioms. A stronger-looking mirror would ask whether analogous continuum axioms force linear pooling, but that remains axiomatic. Adding report manipulation, weight optimization, or robustness would create a new computational problem rather than mirror a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the question ChoCo actually needs answered: there is no computational anchor here. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population research could grow from the paper. The expert-panel setting survives as a sensible high-multiplicity regime; what does not survive is the claim that this paper supplies a computational result for that mirror.
