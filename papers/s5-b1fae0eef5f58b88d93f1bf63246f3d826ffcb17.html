What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-on applies the Split Cycle rule represented in Proposition 4.16 to a mass distribution; it does not mirror a computational result, and the paper's other results are axiomatic.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is a follow-on about Split Cycle winner determination. Let \(T=\mathcal O(X)\) be the paper’s complete, transitive preference types, and let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s rational share of a large electorate. Replace pairwise voter counts with margins
\[ M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[xP(t)y]-\mathbf 1[yP(t)x]\bigr). \]
Ask whether a specified candidate is a winner: \(x\) wins if no candidate defeats \(x\), where \(x\) defeats \(y\) when \(M_\mu(x,y)>0\) and that margin exceeds the weakest edge margin on every majority path from \(y\) back to \(x\). The answer is the winner set; there is no manipulation variable or optimization objective in this paper. A plausible regime is a large civic or online ranking poll with many respondents but relatively few distinct rankings. The construction preserves the paper’s ballot types and Split Cycle rule, replacing counts with population shares.

The nearest result is Proposition 4.16, proved in this paper: every Split Cycle CCR is AS representable with pairwise margin as its advantage and the maximum splitting number of a majority path from \(y\) to \(x\) as the standard. That gives a particularly direct basis for the continuous formula above, and the authors would likely recognize it as their rule applied to a distribution over voters. But it is a representation result, not a computational-complexity result.

Under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. Theorem 3.13, proved here, characterizes AS representability as equivalent to weak IIA plus orderability. Propositions 4.6 and 4.11, proved here, establish AS representations for the Gillies Covering and Ranked Pairs CCRs. Proposition 5.4, also proved here, shows that an AS-representable CCR with margin advantage and a continuous standard satisfies positive involvement. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. In Proposition 5.4, “continuous” means a one-voter change alters the standard by at most one; it does not mean a continuous population.

So continuous Split Cycle evaluation is a credible *new* high-multiplicity question, and its graph-based calculation appears tractable. It is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. That is the weak point—and it prevents me from presenting it as a qualifying ChoCo anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the paper’s contribution: it states no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.13 characterizes an axiomatic property, Propositions 4.6 and 4.11 give representations of voting rules, and Proposition 5.4 relates a representation to another axiom. In particular, Proposition 4.16 represents Split Cycle; it does not analyze the complexity of computing its winners. So the proposed evaluation problem is a follow-on question about a rule from the paper, not a mirror of a computational result in it.

That distinction survives a better formulation. A distribution over ranking types is sensible, and its pairwise margins \(M_\mu(x,y)\) need not degenerate. But turning those margins into a Split Cycle winner problem—or asking how much mass must change to alter a winner—adds a computational problem the paper never posed. The paper’s axiomatic results give no discrete optimization problem or algorithmic baseline for that question to mirror. Continuizing its axioms would be outside ChoCo’s scope; introducing a new computational task does not make it a mirror of this paper.

This is a narrow negative case. The population model itself is plausible, and a continuous Split Cycle computation could be worth studying on its own. The paper supports the claim that there is no qualifying computational mirror among its results; it does not support the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous follow-on exists in any scenario.
