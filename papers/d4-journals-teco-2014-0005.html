The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, this paper supplies no qualifying named computational result. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize ex-post equilibria for broad valuation domains; Theorems 3 and 4 give equilibrium properties when valuations have maxima; Theorems 5–7 bound welfare loss. None establishes that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and the paper does not compute an encoding as an algorithmic result. So there are no valid anchors here, and I can’t honestly give one continuous prover question per anchor.

The closest positive angle is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It says that for a homogeneous valuation domain of degree \(p\), if each player’s maximum alternative is in the set \(A'\), then a nearly truthful ex-post equilibrium over \(A'\) achieves welfare within a factor \(p\) of the dominant-strategy outcome. A natural population version would have a finite support of complete valuation types \(t\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Let \(W_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t v_t(a)\), and suppose each type has a common maximum \(a_t\). The mass mechanism aggregates type reports and selects \(y\in\arg\max_a\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t(a)\); one could ask whether nearly truthful reports on a set \(A'\) containing every \(a_t\) guarantee \(W_\mu(y)\ge W_\mu(x^\star)/p\), where \(x^\star\) maximizes \(W_\mu\). That is a concrete population version of the welfare bound, but it is a welfare question, not a computational-complexity result.

The scenario is plausible at the level of population modeling: a large market or allocation problem could have many agents but relatively few complete valuation types. The problem is that the paper’s equilibrium condition depends on an individual’s unilateral deviation. In an atomless population, one individual’s report has zero effect on the aggregate outcome, so that strategic question becomes trivial. Treating a positive-mass cohort as the deviator avoids that, but changes the paper’s equilibrium concept. Preserving individual incentives requires retaining the agents’ finite atomic weight.

That is the strongest honest positive case I see: Theorem 6 suggests a clean continuous population welfare analogue. Its weakness is decisive for ChoCo’s remit: it does not anchor a computational question, and the continuum changes the unilateral-deviation structure at the center of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize ex-post equilibria; Theorems 5–7 bound welfare loss. The paper’s “communication complexity” concerns how many values agents must report, not the running time or complexity of an algorithm. A population limit cannot turn those results into algorithmic ones.

Theorem 6 is the proponent’s best candidate. Its welfare bound may have a sensible integral analogue for a distribution over valuation types, and that could be a clean mathematical extension. But it remains a welfare inequality: the mechanism still selects an alternative by maximizing over the paper’s abstract set \(A\), and the paper gives no computational problem or algorithm for doing so. The extension therefore does not supply a ChoCo complexity question.

There is also a problem with carrying over the equilibrium claim. In a genuinely atomless population, one person’s deviation has zero effect on the aggregate report; under the usual VCG externality calculation, removing that person has zero effect too. The unilateral incentive condition then loses its force. Treat a positive-mass type as the deviator and the model becomes one of cohort or coalition incentives, which changes the paper’s solution concept. Keep individually influential agents and the model remains finite.

A repeated-type market can make the population distribution plausible, but it cannot fix both issues: either the equilibrium condition degenerates, or the strategic unit changes, while the paper still contributes no computational result. I would reject a mirror for this programme. The integral welfare bound may be worth studying elsewhere; the negative case is about ChoCo’s computational remit, not about whether that extension has any mathematical interest.
