Theorem 1 (Arrow’s Theorem), cited from Arrow rather than proved here: With three or more alternatives, any aggregation rule satisfying Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and transitivity must be dictatorial.

Theorem 2 (Sen’s Theorem), cited from Sen rather than proved here: There is no choice correspondence satisfying universal domain, minimal liberalism, and Pareto.

The closest plausible population scenario is a large crowd of people ranking possible AI trajectory segments. A type would be a complete weak ranking of those segments, and \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of raters with ranking \(t\). The collective decision would be an ordering or choice set. That gives a concrete continuous-population version of the paper’s *setting*, but its central question—whether an aggregation rule with specified axioms exists—is axiomatic, not computational. Asking how to compute an outcome would require choosing a rule, which the paper does not do.

So there is no qualifying anchor for which I can state the requested continuous computational problem or predict whether it is tractable, transfers hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed crowd model—a distribution \(\mu\) over weak rankings of trajectory segments—is sensible, but it is a continuous version of the paper’s setting, not a computational mirror of either result. The proponent raises no anchor that meets the requested standard: neither numbered theorem states the complexity of a computational problem.

Arrow’s theorem concerns whether a social welfare function exists over the full domain of profiles under specified axioms. Replacing voter counts by masses \(\mu_t\) does not turn that into an instance-by-instance algorithmic question. Every finite profile corresponds to a rational distribution, and every rational distribution can be realized by a finite electorate. The impossibility remains a statement about the axioms on a rule’s entire domain. Fix a rule and computing its output may be easy; ask an algorithm to find a rule and the paper supplies neither a finite representation of candidate rules nor a computational objective. Theorem 1 gives no complexity problem to relax.

Sen’s result is an even poorer fit for a population measure. It concerns rights held by particular individuals, with separate protected domains. A measure over types records how much mass has each preference; it does not record which individual holds a right. One could preserve named rights-holders as atoms, or give rights to positive-mass cohorts, but either choice adds structure or changes the rights claim. Even after that repair, the paper’s result is still an axiomatic impossibility, not a computational bound.

The strongest nearby rescue would be to ask for the minimum mass that must change rankings to alter an outcome, or to optimize how well a specified rule satisfies competing axioms. Those could be worthwhile continuous computational problems. But they require adding a rule or an optimization objective, and the paper does not pose either. They are research questions suggested by its setting, not mirrors of its theorems.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: it offers no named computational result to continuize, and its plausible population model does not supply one. I would not claim that no useful computational question can ever be built from this setting; the paper simply does not provide the anchor for calling one its continuous mirror.
