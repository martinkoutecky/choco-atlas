I cannot make an anchor-based case for a continuous mirror from this paper. It contains no named computational result in the programme’s sense: no theorem or proposition about complexity, algorithms, approximation, or parameterized tractability.

The closest results are Proposition 1, which establishes a unique efficient residual-equivalent envy-free allocation for the paper’s two-agent domain, and Proposition 2, which establishes that this allocation is the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome of the iterated divide-and-choose mechanism. Both are proved in the paper, but they are existence and implementation results, not computational results. Corollary 1’s finite-horizon guarantee is likewise not a complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offers no continuous mirror anchor; that is the strongest part of the negative case. Propositions 1 and 2 establish existence, uniqueness, and implementation of an allocation. Corollary 1 gives a finite-horizon welfare guarantee. None states a complexity bound, algorithm, or hardness result for a computational problem. One could ask how hard it is to compute the REEF allocation or the SPNE outcome from encoded utility densities, but that would add a computational question to an analytical paper, not continuize one of its computational results.

The paper’s continuum is the cake: agents have nonatomic utility measures over an interval. Its population remains exactly two strategic people with persistent identities, acting over sequential stages. Neither the cake’s divisibility nor the mechanism’s potentially infinite sequence of moves supplies population mass; those are outcome and time dimensions, respectively.

The strongest candidate mirror is a mass of repeated two-agent negotiations, with a type recording each pair’s preferences. That is plausible as a separate study, but it aggregates independent instances of the paper’s game. With finite support, one can evaluate the \(\tau^2\) type pairs and weight their outcomes; the hard part, if any, comes from representing the utility functions or integrating over a richer type distribution. It does not make the paper’s two-agent implementation result a population-complexity result.

Putting a continuum of agents around one cake changes the problem more radically: the paper’s two-person single-cut efficiency condition and bilateral mechanism no longer apply, and a finite cake cannot give uncountably many people positive-sized portions. Treating each type’s mass as a collective bargainer instead changes the agents and the meaning of envy; if all members of a bloc have the same normalized preferences, the bloc’s mass may not affect the outcome at all.

So the negative case is strong for a ChoCo mirror, though not a proof that no adjacent population-level project could be worthwhile. Such a project would need a new institutional model and a new computational question. The paper supplies no named computational result for a continuous population to mirror.
