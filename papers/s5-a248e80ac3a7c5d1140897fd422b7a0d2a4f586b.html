The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind ChoCo needs as an anchor. Propositions 1–3 are named mathematical results, but they concern probability and convergence: Proposition 1 gives asymptotic reliability for the first-stage poll; Proposition 2 gives asymptotic trustworthiness for the second-stage actions; Proposition 3 composes them, showing that actions and the poll track the proto-truth in the limit. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

The strongest positive lead is the paper’s large-population limit. One could model many participants as a distribution \(\mu\) over behavioral types \(t\), where each type specifies its competence \(r_t\) and its probabilities of acting for Stag after each poll result. A natural follow-on question would be whether the weighted average competence and hope in this population suffice for the CCJT’s convergence conclusions. But in a continuum limit, those conditions reduce to checking weighted averages such as \(\sum_t \mu_t r_t > 1/2\). That is a plausible heterogeneous-agent extension of the paper’s analysis, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in a result the paper proves.

The weakness is also the decisive obstacle: the paper’s \(n\to\infty\) limit is used to establish probabilistic reliability, not to pose computational questions about a society supplied as rational masses over types. I therefore can’t honestly state a continuous problem per qualifying anchor or assign one the programme’s tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the best objection: Propositions 1–3 are probability and convergence results, not computational results. They specify no computational task, input encoding, or resource measure. Their subject is whether polling and action become reliable as \(n\) grows; the paper does not ask how to compute, optimize, or manipulate a voting outcome.

The proposed type-distribution extension does not supply that missing task. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\), competence \(r_t\), and hope \(h_t\). With a fixed type distribution and independent behavior, an atomless population has an exact poll share \(\sum_t \mu_t r_t\), conditional on the proto-truth, and an exact action share determined by the \(h_t\). The majority outcomes are then determined by threshold inequalities. The finite-population uncertainty that Propositions 1–3 analyze has disappeared: their asymptotic conclusion has become a deterministic property of the model. That makes the extension a possible heterogeneous-agent analysis, but not a computational mirror of those propositions.

A stronger computational proposal might choose a campaign, penalty, or other intervention to change the type masses or agents’ behavior enough to induce cooperation. But the paper treats competence and hope as assumptions, not choices; it supplies no intervention set, costs, or design objective. Adding those would create a new mechanism-design problem. One could also preserve uncertainty by making the population composition random, but that adds a source of uncertainty absent from the fixed-society model.

So I would reject the proposed mirrors as poor fits for ChoCo’s computational programme, not because their continuous answers might be easy. I cannot make the universal claim airtight: a large organization with repeated behavioral types is a plausible population regime, and it could motivate computational work on intervention design. The negative case is strongest on attribution and programme fit: that work would build a new computational problem around the paper, rather than mirror a computational problem the paper actually establishes.
