The continuous mirror question

Given a finite object set \(O\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), complete weak rankings, rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_o\), and rational linear constraints with coefficients \(a^r_{t,o}\) and bounds \(\beta_r\), assume the feasible set is nonempty. Compute an outcome of the type-level Constrained Serial Rule: at each round maximize the minimum per-person share of the current preference prefix over a chosen subset of types, subject to \(\sum_o p_{t,o}=1\), \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{t,o}\leq\sigma_o\), \(\sum_{t,o}\mu_t a^r_{t,o}p_{t,o}\leq\beta_r\), and all prior prefix-share promises; select a minimal bottleneck subset, record its promises, and advance its prefixes until every type receives total share \(1\).

The model it lives in

Use type-level probabilities \(p_{t,o}\), with aggregate assignment masses \(y_{t,o}=\mu_t p_{t,o}\). The rule’s LPs enforce feasibility and prior promises while maximizing a minimum prefix share; bottleneck selection and prefix advancement operate over the \(\tau\) supported types.

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Type-Continuum Constrained Serial Allocation. Consider a large school-placement cohort with \(n\) students but only \(\tau\) distinct complete types: each type specifies a weak ranking of schools and every attribute that affects the linear constraints. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s share of the cohort. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime when many students share preference lists and eligibility or quota attributes; it is not a claim that every school-placement instance has few types.

The relevant named anchor is Proposition 1—the first proposition in the supplied text, referenced by the label prop:terminates (the extraction does not render its numeral). It is proved in this paper. Its statement is that Algorithm 1 terminates and always produces a feasible random assignment; its proof bounds the number of rounds by \(n\rho\). Section 3.3 then gives the polynomial-time consequence: each round uses at most \(n+1\) linear programs, including those used to find a bottleneck set. The qualification is that the paper’s explicit polynomial-time claim is in that section, not in a numbered theorem or proposition. The named Theorems 1 and 2 concern ordinal efficiency and same-type envy-freeness, so I would not present them as complexity anchors. I find no named hardness result here.

Here is the continuous problem corresponding to that algorithm. An instance consists of a finite object set \(O\), a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita object supplies \(\sigma_o\). Each type has a complete weak ranking over \(O\). There are \(R\) linear constraints with rational coefficients \(a^r_{t,o}\) and bounds \(\beta_r\), and the feasible set is promised nonempty. A solution is a matrix \(p_{t,o}\), where \(p_{t,o}\) is the probability that a member of type \(t\) receives object \(o\), such that
\[ \sum_o p_{t,o}=1,\qquad \sum_t\mu_t p_{t,o}\leq \sigma_o,\qquad \sum_{t,o}a^r_{t,o}\mu_t p_{t,o}\leq\beta_r \quad\text{for every }r. \]
The question is to compute an outcome of the type-level Constrained Serial Rule. At each round, for each type’s current preference prefix, maximize the minimum per-person share of that prefix across types, subject to feasibility and the promises from earlier rounds. Choose a minimal bottleneck subset of types, promise each type in it the current minimum share, and advance those types’ prefixes. Return a feasible assignment satisfying the terminal promises. This is a finite, executable solution specification; any valid bottleneck choices permitted by the rule are acceptable.

This keeps the paper’s underlying problem: people have unit demand, retain their rankings and indifferences, and receive lottery shares of indivisible objects subject to arbitrary linear feasibility constraints. The LP variables are aggregate type masses \(y_{t,o}=\mu_t p_{t,o}\). For integer capacities \(q_o\) in a cohort of size \(n\), use \(\sigma_o=q_o/n\); normalize constraint bounds per capita as well. Thus the mass and per-capita supplies co-vary with population size, as they must in this mirror. Each person still receives one object when their lottery is realized; fractional values describe population shares and lottery probabilities, not divisible objects.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). Replacing \(n\) individual rows by \(\tau\) type rows gives LPs of size polynomial in \(\tau\), \(\rho\), \(R\), and the input bit length \(L\). There are at most \(\tau\rho\) rounds, and at most \(\tau\) bottleneck LPs per round. The proposed mirror therefore inherits the paper’s optimization structure without enumerating the full preference domain. Its scope is the computation of constrained serial outcomes; it does not assert that arbitrary constrained random assignments can be implemented as lotteries over deterministic assignments satisfying those constraints.

The soft spot is the move from individual bottlenecks to type-level bottlenecks. The paper’s rule selects subsets of named agents; it does not prove that batching indistinguishable agents into a single type yields exactly the same rule or a limit with the same properties. That extension needs to be proved, including its relation to finite cohorts formed by duplicating types. Still, it is a natural question for these authors: it preserves their rankings, LP, bottleneck logic, and feasibility model, while making the population itself the mass-valued object. The further questions are whether the type-level rule preserves constrained ordinal efficiency and same-type envy-freeness, and when its outcomes correspond to finite-cohort outcomes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the anchor is weaker than its label suggests. The proposition proves that the finite-agent procedure terminates and returns a feasible assignment, with at most \(n\rho\) rounds. The polynomial-time claim appears separately in §3.3. A continuous analogue of the proposition’s round bound would be immediate: with \(\tau\) types and at most \(\rho\) preference thresholds per type, there are at most \(\tau\rho\) advances. That gives little computational substance to the named result itself.

The better mirror is still well-posed. Give each type a complete weak ranking and a common vector of constraint coefficients, assign it rational mass, and scale supplies and constraint bounds per capita. The type-level LP then has variables for each type and object. In applications with symmetric quota constraints, this is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. On such instances, aggregation is an exact compression of repeated agent rows; it preserves the assignment problem and its LP structure.

That leaves a case against the mirror’s *value*, not its validity. This paper already handles its main computation with linear programs, and its runtime is polynomial in the number of agents, objects, and constraints. It has no exponential-variable formulation whose pricing problem a population continuum would newly unlock. When symmetry makes \(\tau\) small, the proposed gain is chiefly a shorter encoding and smaller LP; when arbitrary constraints distinguish agents, a complete type must include those distinctions, and \(\tau\) can grow to \(n\). That makes the continuous contribution look like a useful compressed implementation of an existing algorithm rather than a new computational landscape.

But that is not enough to defeat the anchor under the programme’s stated standard. A compressed high-multiplicity input is still a genuine population-continuous question, and the paper’s existing polynomial algorithm does not make that question illegitimate. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: the negative case is limited, and the type-level mirror survives.
