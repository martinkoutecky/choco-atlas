The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The nearby results are analytic. Theorem 1 (Existence), proved here, establishes a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium under A1–A3 and condition \(C^h\). Theorem 2 (Characterization), proved here, says that with symmetric party motivations the parties choose the same policy, which maximizes a weighted utilitarian objective plus an aggregate of other-regarding preferences. Corollary 1 (Lindbeck–Weibull), proved here as a special case, says that when voters and parties have no other-regarding concerns, the common equilibrium policy maximizes \(W(x)=\sum_i n_i f_i(0)u_i(w_i+x_i)\). Theorem 3 (Uniqueness), proved here, gives uniqueness under strict concavity. None asserts a computational complexity result, so none qualifies as an anchor under your rule.

The strongest prospective computational follow-on is a narrow version of Corollary 1. Call it *Symmetric Redistribution Equilibrium Computation*: given rational group masses \(n_i\), incomes \(w_i\), density values \(f_i(0)\), and strictly concave rational quadratic utilities \(u_i\), find the common equilibrium policy
\[ \hat x\in\arg\max_{x\in X_0}\sum_i n_i f_i(0)u_i(w_i+x_i), \qquad X_0=\{x:\sum_i n_i x_i=0,\ w_i+x_i>0\}. \]
The inputs would be promised to satisfy the paper’s equilibrium assumptions and its condition \(C^h\). In a plausible regime, a large electorate has relatively few socioeconomic groups, with each group’s mass \(n_i\) representing a fraction of voters. For this restricted encoding, computing the policy is a concave quadratic optimization problem, so the expected direction is tractable. The paper’s authors would recognize the objective and equilibrium policy as their own model.

That is a credible *computational extension*, but not a qualifying mirror case: Corollary 1 does not establish tractability, and the paper already models a continuum of voters. Also, its ideological bias is distributed within each group, so a complete ChoCo type would need to include that bias; the paper represents it through a group-specific distribution \(F_i\), not a finite list of identical voter types. My strongest positive claim, then, is limited: the paper offers a natural continuum equilibrium problem for a computational follow-on, but no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has already made the population continuous. It assumes \(n\) socioeconomic groups with masses \(n_i\) summing to one, chooses a transfer for each group, and computes expected vote share by integrating ideological bias through \(F_i\). A faithful type description can include the group’s income and preference parameters, with bias distributed according to \(F_i\). That construction is already in the paper. For a finite electorate with the same group proportions and the paper’s expected-vote objective, the expected share is the same \(\sum_i n_i F_i(\cdot)\); electorate size adds no new population-continuization step. Tracking realized individual biases or optimizing the probability of winning would change the model’s objective.

There is also no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 proves existence, Theorem 2 characterizes the symmetric equilibrium, and Theorem 3 proves uniqueness. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The proponent’s Corollary 1 optimization problem is a plausible computational follow-up, as is computing equilibria under Theorem 1. But both start from the paper’s already-continuous game. Their contribution would be an algorithmic analysis of that model, not a new continuous-population mirror.

This makes the negative case strong against a *mirror* of the paper’s results. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile computational project could come from the paper: Theorem 1 leaves equilibrium computation open, and an explicitly encoded family of utilities and bias distributions could make that a meaningful project. I would not rule out that work. I would say the proponent has identified a possible follow-up, but no qualifying computational anchor and no population continuization still to perform.
