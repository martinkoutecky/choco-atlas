The strongest case against a mirror starts with the missing anchor: I cannot name a theorem, lemma, or corollary in this paper that states a computational result. The best candidate for a related project is to describe people by types containing their ideal points, payoff functions, and prosocial preferences, then compare the uncovered set under weighted majority with the policies that are distributionally fair. A larger polity with recurring types could make a continuous population sensible; that possibility is not the objection. The problem is that this model changes what the paper studies. Its experiment holds a five-person committee’s ideal points fixed and changes the payoff landscape to test whether fairness helps explain observed choices. It also uses named committee positions, a randomly selected agenda setter, sequential proposals, and communication. A mass distribution does not directly represent that experimental process.

One could repair the model by defining a new computational task—for example, finding the least mass that must change type to move a policy into or out of the uncovered set. But the paper gives no population intervention, conversion costs, or robustness objective to motivate that task. Adding them would create a new control or robustness problem; it would not mirror one of the paper’s results. Simply computing a weighted-majority uncovered set alongside a fairness measure would likewise formalize a population-level comparison, not the paper’s experimental finding about why five-person committees choose as they do.

That leaves a real limit to the negative claim: a continuous study of fairness and majority outcomes in a large electorate could be worthwhile in its own right, and I cannot rule out the authors recognizing it as a new question. The case against is narrower but firm: this paper supplies no computational result to anchor such a mirror, and its most plausible continuous-population reformulations require adding the computational problem they would then analyze.
