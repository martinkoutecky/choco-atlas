The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or parameterized tractability. Theorem 3, Theorem 4, and Theorem 5 prove that particular profiles are not \(2\)-Manhattan; Proposition 2 reports a computer-assisted verification that certain smaller profiles are embeddable. Those are structural results, not complexity results.

The closest computational statement is in the prose: recognizing Manhattan preferences is in NP for fixed dimension, citing Peters (2017), while its lower-bound complexity is unknown. It is neither a numbered result proved here nor an anchor of the kind you require.

One could define a continuous recognition problem: given a rational distribution over rankings, decide whether every positive-mass type can be represented by a voter point and common alternative points in the plane so that Manhattan distances induce its ranking. A large consultation with many respondents and relatively few distinct rankings would make the population regime plausible. But for this exact question, the masses do no work: only the support matters, so it reduces to recognition on one voter per supported ranking. That gives no honest named anchor from this paper. Making mass matter—for example, by maximizing the representable share—would change the underlying problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 3, Theorem 4, and Theorem 5 exhibit particular profiles that cannot be embedded; Proposition 2 reports an exhaustive check that certain smaller profiles can be. These are structural results about which rankings admit a geometric representation, not complexity classifications. The paper’s prose notes that recognition complexity remains open, but this paper proves no computational result about it.

The strongest direct mirror is continuous recognition: given a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, decide whether its positive-mass types admit one common Manhattan embedding. But the condition is simply that every type in \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) be represented. Duplicating a ranking adds no constraint: each copy can use the same voter point. Thus the masses have no effect, and the question reduces exactly to discrete recognition on the support. A large survey could make that support scenario plausible, but it does not make the population continuous in a computationally consequential way. The paper’s small-profile results would become statements about support size, unchanged by the distribution’s weights.

A stronger proposal could ask for the maximum mass of rankings that can be represented, or require observed ranking frequencies to arise from voters distributed across a specified city. Those might be worthwhile new problems, but they introduce partial fit or an exogenous spatial density—neither is forced by continuizing this paper’s exact embeddability question. The forbidden substructures and minimal counterexamples do not address those objectives. So the negative case is strongest for mirrors of the paper’s results; it cannot establish that no related population-weighted problem could ever be worthwhile.
