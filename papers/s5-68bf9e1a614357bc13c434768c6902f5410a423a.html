The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow one: this paper supports a population-level SP-voting problem, but it does not establish a complexity-theoretic result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror.

The closest named anchor is Lemma 5, proved in this paper. It says that, in the \(G\)-group Concentric Mixture of Mallows setting of Lemma 2, if the stated identification condition holds and the sample size is \(n \ge O\!\left(m!\sqrt{m\log(m/\delta)}\right)\), then SP-voting recovers the ground-truth ranking with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The proof is given here, drawing on the proof of a result in Hosseini et al. (2024).

For completeness, the “same setting” is: \(\phi_1\le\cdots\le\phi_G\); split the groups into \(G_1=\{1,\ldots,s\}\) and \(G_2=\{s+1,\ldots,G\}\); and let \(\alpha=\sum_{j\in G_1}p_j\). Lemma 2, also proved here, says that if
\[ \frac{\alpha}{Z(\phi_s)}+\frac{1-\alpha}{Z(\phi_G)} \ge 2\left(\frac{\phi_s}{Z(\phi_1)}\alpha+ \frac{\phi_G}{Z(\phi_{s+1})}(1-\alpha)\right), \]
then the population prediction-normalized vote for the ground truth is at least twice that of every other ranking. Lemma 5 turns that population separation into a finite-sample recovery guarantee.

The mirror I would propose is Population-SP Winner. Its instance is a finite ranking set \(S\subseteq\mathcal L(A)\) and a list of \(\tau\) voter types. A type \(t\) consists of a reported vote \(v_t\in S\), a prediction report \(q_t\in\Delta(S)\), and a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A point-mass \(q_t\) represents a predicted ranking; a general \(q_t\) represents a reported prediction distribution. Require every ranking in \(S\) to have positive vote mass and every denominator below to be positive. Define
\[ f_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{t:v_t=\sigma}\mu_t,\qquad h_\mu(\rho\mid\sigma)= \frac{\sum_{t:v_t=\sigma}\mu_t q_t(\rho)}{f_\mu(\sigma)}. \]
The task is to return
\[ \arg\max_{\sigma\in S} f_\mu(\sigma)\sum_{\rho\in S} \frac{h_\mu(\rho\mid\sigma)}{h_\mu(\sigma\mid\rho)}. \]
Equivalently, given a target ranking, decide whether it is the unique maximizer. A solution is the maximizing ranking, or the correct yes/no answer. This is the paper’s prediction-normalized-vote objective evaluated on an explicit population distribution rather than on a finite sample.

A plausible regime is a large platform repeatedly asking users to rank the same small slate—for example, the five-alternative questions in the paper. With a fixed elicitation format, there are at most \(120^2\) vote/prediction pairs; millions of respondents can therefore produce a population with far more agents than distinct report types. Each type’s mass is its share of respondents. The paper’s expert, intermediate, and non-expert mixture gives independent evidence that this kind of population structure is sensible.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: the paper already defines and analyzes the population score \(\overline V\), then asks how many sampled reports are needed for its maximizer to recover the truth. The proposed question asks for the computational status of finding that maximizer when the type masses are the input. On this explicit-support formulation, I expect Class A: only rankings with positive vote mass can win, so there are at most \(\tau\) candidates, and the scores can be computed and compared using rational arithmetic in time polynomial in the input size. That is a complexity question about the underlying winner problem, not about an encoding.

The scope is only full-ranking SP-voting under the CMM-style population objective. It does not cover CMPL sample complexity, partial SP, or the paper’s experimental comparisons. Natural follow-ups are whether a succinct CMM or CMPL parameterization changes the complexity, and how robust the winning ranking is to small changes in type masses.

The soft spot is substantial: Lemma 5 is a sample-complexity result, not a running-time, hardness, or parameterized-complexity result. This mirror replaces inference from finite noisy samples with exact computation from known masses, and the explicit-support winner problem may be too easy to capture the paper’s main statistical contribution. So under a strict requirement for a named result asserting a complexity classification, this paper has no qualifying anchor. The positive case is that its named population-separation and sample-recovery results make the exact population winner a recognizable companion problem, even though they do not themselves answer its computational-complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 5 is the only anchor here, and it is a sample-complexity result: under a population-level separation condition, enough sampled reports make the empirical SP score select the ground truth. It gives no running-time, hardness, or algorithmic classification. Lemmas 2 and 4 likewise establish population-level score separation, not the complexity of a computational problem. That is the strongest objection: the paper’s contribution is statistical recovery, not a computational task whose difficulty might change when voters are represented by masses.

The proposed Population-SP Winner is well-defined, and its large-platform scenario is plausible. But with explicit masses on vote and prediction reports, it replaces the paper’s task. The paper asks whether reports sampled from an unknown population will reveal a latent ground truth; Population-SP Winner assumes the population distribution is already known and computes the rule’s output. With an explicit report support, one can tally the masses, evaluate the score for each reported vote ranking, and compare the results using rational arithmetic. That is a faithful implementation question, but it does not mirror Lemma 5’s sample-complexity question. The distribution that matters in Lemma 5 is the unknown law generating observations; supplying it exactly removes the uncertainty its theorem studies.

A more ambitious mirror would give the population succinctly as CMM or CMPL parameters and ask for the maximizing ranking over all \(m!\) rankings. That avoids the explicit-support computation. Yet it faces a dilemma: if the model includes its central ranking and satisfies the paper’s identification condition, Lemma 2 or 4 already certifies that ranking as the population winner. Outside those conditions, computing the population winner is a new optimization question, but it need not answer whether the latent truth can be recovered from reports. Hiding the central ranking instead turns the problem back into statistical inference, rather than a complexity question on a known continuous society.

One could also ask how much the group masses must change to destroy identification. This is close to the paper’s emphasis on the tension between group proportions and expertise. For the grouped models as stated, though, the paper’s conditions already express that boundary directly in terms of group masses and model parameters. A broader robustness problem over arbitrary report-type masses could be interesting, but it would introduce a new adversarial objective rather than expose an algorithmic gap in the paper.

So I would reject the proposed explicit-support winner problem as a worthwhile mirror of the named result. I cannot honestly rule out a useful computational study of the succinct parametric models or a new robustness formulation. The negative case is strongest against the actual anchor—Lemma 5—and weak exactly where those broader, newly posed questions begin.
