The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a directed type-observation graph, binary signal probabilities \(p_{t,\theta}\), a prior, and type-specific utilities and tie-breaking rules, consider populations at scales \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb{N}\). Agents within each type are identical and receive independent signals conditional on \(\theta\); they observe the fractions of action-\(1\) reports in adjacent cohorts and update by Bayes’ rule. For an uninformative target at time \(2\) and an observed share vector \(h\), let \(\mathcal{X}(h)\subseteq[0,1]^T\) be the signal-share vectors inducing \(h\), and define \(I_\theta(h)=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}(h)}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tD_{\mathrm{KL}}(x_t\Vert p_{t,\theta})\), where \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) is binary relative entropy. Given \(\epsilon>0\), determine the MAP state under the promise \(I_0(h)-I_1(h)\ge\epsilon\) or \(I_0(h)-I_1(h)\le-\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type Bayesian social-learning model with rational cohort masses, conditionally independent private signals, a directed observation relation between cohorts, and Bayesian actions based on observed cohort action shares; the target’s MAP decision is governed asymptotically by the difference between the two states’ minimum relative-entropy costs.

The objection that survived

Pooling reports across cohorts loses the within-network identity and correlation of vertex and edge actions that drives the reduction, so Theorem 1’s hardness transfer is unsupported.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s binary-action posterior-concentration computation. It leaves the revealed-belief hardness result, the general IEIS running-time result, and the transitive and transparent network algorithms unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding the promised MAP answer as a function of \(|T|\), the rational input bit length, and the gap \(\epsilon\)?
  • Do the action-threshold constraints make the feasible signal-share region hard to optimize over, or does fractional pooling yield an efficient algorithm?
  • How does this rate problem relate to finite-population posterior computation when the population scale grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see a defensible mirror for the paper’s strongest hardness result: Theorem 1, “Binary Action Model,” proved here. It says that computing the Bayesian action in the binary-action model is NP-hard at \(t=2\). It also says that, for an \(n\)-agent network, it is NP-hard to distinguish whether the target agent’s posterior assigns probability below \(\exp(-\Omega(n))\) to state \(0\) or below \(\exp(-\Omega(n))\) to state \(1\). The paper proves this by reducing from a gap version of VERTEX-COVER; the gap-hardness result it uses is cited from elsewhere. I would mirror the posterior-concentration claim, which captures the paper’s central inference problem.

Call the mirror Type-Share Bayesian MAP. Its instance is a finite set \(T\) of role types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), binary signal probabilities \(p_{t,\theta}\), and a directed relation specifying which type cohorts observe which other cohorts. A type’s full description includes its signal structure, decision rule, and observation relations. At population scale \(N\), type \(t\) has \(N\mu_t\) agents, all identical in those respects and drawing signals independently conditional on \(\theta\). Agents observe the fraction of each neighboring cohort that reports action \(1\), and update by Bayes’ rule. The target has an uninformative private signal and, at time \(2\), receives a specified vector \(h\) of neighboring action shares.

Let \(x_t\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of type \(t\) with private signal \(1\), and let \(\mathcal X(h)\) be the set of signal-share vectors that produce the target’s observed history under the type-share protocol. For each state, define \(I_\theta(h)=\min_{x\in\mathcal X(h)}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t D(x_t\Vert p_{t,\theta})\), where \(D\) is binary KL divergence. This is the least large-deviation cost of a signal profile consistent with the reports under state \(\theta\). The problem is: given the type data, \(h\), and a promised gap \(\epsilon>0\), return the MAP state when \(\Delta=I_0(h)-I_1(h)\ge\epsilon\) or \(\Delta\le-\epsilon\). The finite-population posterior odds then have exponential rate \(\Delta\): \(\log(\Pr(\theta=1\mid h)/\Pr(\theta=0\mid h))=N\Delta+o(N)\). Thus the requested answer is the state on which the posterior concentrates, matching the scale of Theorem 1’s approximation hardness.

A plausible regime is a large review or deliberation system with many replicated members in each role: each cohort shares a signal quality and the same links to neighboring cohorts, and members exchange cohort-level action shares. For a fixed role graph, \(N\) grows while \(\tau=|T|\) stays fixed; when the graph varies as part of the computational input, \(\tau\) varies too, and running time should be stated in \(\tau\), the rational bit length, and the other input parameters. The input graph’s vertices and edges can supply distinct role types, each occupied by a large cohort. This makes the population large relative to the number of roles without pretending the graph structure is irrelevant.

I think the authors could recognize this as a population-scale version of their problem: the question remains how a rational agent infers the state from other agents’ actions when those actions hide correlated information. The continuum changes what is observed from individually named actions to type-level action shares, a natural co-modification when the population itself becomes the object. It also gives the mirror a clear objective and a finite-dimensional description.

I would expect the full problem to be hard for continuum-specific reasons as \(\tau\) grows, though I would not claim Theorem 1’s reduction transfers. The entropy objective is convex, but the histories consistent with \(h\) can split into regions defined by agents’ threshold actions. Optimizing over that union may retain combinatorial difficulty; fixing an action-consistent region leaves a convex subproblem. A key question is whether fractional signal shares erase the vertex-cover gap or merely change the source of hardness. Further questions include whether the rate problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), which network structures make the feasible region convex or otherwise tractable, and how the rate answer relates to finite-population posterior computation.

The weakest point is the change in observation granularity. The paper’s target sees actions of particular neighboring agents; this mirror’s target sees fractions from whole cohorts. That is a substantive variant, not a theorem about the original model. The case for it is that population-level reports preserve the paper’s Bayesian inference question while making population mass central. I would limit the claim to Theorem 1’s binary-action, \(t=2\) posterior-concentration result, not the paper’s revealed-belief or transitive-network results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, the binary-action result at \(t=2\); there is no honest way to dismiss it as lacking a computational claim. The harder question is whether the proposed Type-Share problem preserves what makes that result a problem about Bayesian social learning on a network.

In the reduction, the graph is the computational input. A vertex agent’s relevant role is its particular incident edges; an edge agent observes the two particular endpoint agents; and the target conditions on every edge agent’s named action. Under the programme’s definition of type, those neighborhood relations are part of a type’s complete description. In a general reduction instance, that makes the roles essentially distinct, so \(\tau\) grows with the graph. Replicating the whole network many times does not give the target more evidence about its own component. If instead the target observes action shares across replicas, it learns a new pooled signal: those shares do not preserve which vertex signals and edge reports co-occurred within each copy.

The proposed cohort protocol faces the same choice. If every member of an edge type observes fractions from endpoint cohorts, its information is not the information of the paper’s edge agent, who observes two specific actions. Marginal signal shares do not encode whether the selected vertices cover every edge in the same graph copy. Preserving that incidence-level coupling requires retaining copy identities or named links; then pooling by type loses information the target needs. Keeping one type for each role retains the graph, but leaves the support size tied to the reduction input.

There is a second gap between the paper’s computation and the proposed rate question. With positive-mass cohorts and independent signals, empirical signal shares converge to their state-conditional means as \(N\) grows. The proposed \(I_\theta(h)\) captures the exponential cost of conditioning on atypical reports from finite populations. That is a coherent asymptotic question, but it is a rare-event rate calculation for pooled reports, not the posterior computation faced by the paper’s target observing individual actions. The large-deviation formulation may be useful; it needs its own case for why these aggregate reports and rare histories are the right social-learning object.

A stronger mirror could make roles into types, retain the graph between roles, and ask a representative member to infer the state from cohort-level action shares. That would be a well-defined Bayesian computation. But its key operation is to give role members pooled cohort reports, which changes the information channel that creates the paper’s hardness. If one instead preserves individual neighbor observations, a representative agent has a specific finite neighborhood and the surrounding population’s mass does not supply that agent with a continuum of observations. Either version could be studied, but neither yet shows that the theorem’s computational question has a continuous mirror.

This is the best negative case I can make, and it has a real limit: it does not rule out a worthwhile finite-type, aggregate-observation MAP problem, and the authors might recognize that as a useful extension. It argues against treating the proponent’s rate problem as a mirror of Theorem 1; it does not establish the universal claim that no scenario or re-modelling could be worth the programme’s time.
