The continuous mirror question

For an integer \(M\geq 3\), a finite set \(T\) of valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational piecewise-constant densities on three layers \([0,M]\) whose total value for each type is \(M\), find a finite piecewise-affine description of maps \(\Phi_t:[0,\mu_t]\to\) bundles of three intervals. Require each bundle to have type-\(t\) value at least \(1\), its intervals to have pairwise disjoint interiors, and, for almost every point \(x\) on each layer \(\ell\), require \(\sum_{t\in T}\lambda\{u\in[0,\mu_t]:x\in I_{t\ell}(u)\}=1/M\). Is such an allocation computable with description size and running time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(\log M\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents represented by finitely many complete valuation types and masses \(\mu_t\), with three layers whose supply scales to \([0,M]\). The decision variables are type-indexed maps from mass coordinates to whole bundles of three intervals; the objective is to find a feasible allocation satisfying per-agent value at least \(1\) and complete aggregate use at rate \(1/M\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s proportional allocation result for three layers and arbitrary agent counts. It leaves Theorem 1’s exact allocation and Theorems 7–8’s results for \(2^a3\) layers unaddressed, as well as envy-free query complexity and efficiency.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every such rational type-mass instance admit a finite piecewise-affine allocation of size polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log M\)?
  • Can the switching-point and marking construction be implemented for type masses with polynomial running time, or does the compact-output problem have inherent hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that for three layers and every number \(n\geq3\) of agents, there exists a complete proportional allocation that is feasible and contiguous. Thus each agent values their own bundle at least \(1/n\) of the whole cake; every layer is allocated; each agent receives an interval on each layer; and that agent’s intervals on different layers do not overlap. The proof gives a cut based construction, relying in part on a majority switching point result cited from Hosseini et al. The paper does not establish that this problem is in \(P\), or give a formal query complexity bound, so this is a constructive algorithmic anchor rather than a complexity class result. It contains no hardness anchor to prefer.

I would mirror it with Mass-Proportional Three-Layer Allocation. An instance has a large rational scale \(M\geq3\), a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types, and type masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) specifies rational piecewise-constant densities \(v_{t\ell}\) on three layers \([0,M]\), normalized so that \(\sum_{\ell=1}^3\int_0^M v_{t\ell}(x)\,dx=M\). The factor \(M\) scales the resource supply with the cohort: after stretching the original unit-length layers by \(M\), the finite proportionality threshold \(1/M\) becomes utility \(1\).

A solution assigns each type’s mass to whole bundles through a map \(\Phi_t\) on \([0,\mu_t]\). At each mass coordinate \(u\), \(\Phi_t(u)\) gives three intervals \(I_{t1}(u),I_{t2}(u),I_{t3}(u)\). Each must be an ordinary bundle: its intervals have disjoint interiors, and its type valuation is at least \(1\). Completeness requires that for every layer \(\ell\), almost every point \(x\in[0,M]\) is used by exactly \(1/M\) of the population:
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\lambda\{u\in[0,\mu_t]:x\in I_{t\ell}(u)\}=\frac{1}{M}. \]
Here \(\lambda\) is interval length. For a computational version, take rational piecewise-constant inputs and ask for a finite piecewise-affine description of the maps, with complexity measured in \(m=3\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), input bit length \(L\), and \(\log M\). The mass coordinate lets a type be divided among different whole bundles; it does not give any person a fractional interval.

The regime is a large cohort of students choosing among three facilities or services, with a small number of shared valuation profiles—for example, recurring schedule archetypes. The relevant condition is that a type captures the full data used by the allocation problem, here its valuations on all three layers. This is most plausible for \(M\gg\tau\). Scaling the service horizon with cohort size is a substantive co-modification: it keeps the proportional share meaningful as the population grows. A fixed, scarce unit-length cake would make the per-agent target vanish in the limit.

The expected direction is Class A, but that is a conjecture for this mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 5. Its switching-point and marking arguments look adaptable to masses: a weighted median can replace an unweighted majority, and type mass can be split across branches. The computational question is whether those cuts and recursive allocations can be compressed into a description polynomial in \(\tau,L,\log M\). A direct simulation of the paper’s agent-by-agent procedure might still take time proportional to \(M\).

The paper’s result covered here is specifically proportional allocation for three layers and arbitrary \(n\). I would not stretch this case to the exact-allocation or envy-free questions. Its weakest point is the population-scaled supply and the aggregate completeness condition: they preserve the paper’s bundle constraints, but the paper itself allocates a fixed cake to named agents. The authors may see this as a natural high-multiplicity extension, yet they would need to accept that changed resource regime. The constructive theorem makes that extension worth asking about; it does not establish that the resulting mass problem has a compact algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would oppose a green grade on the paper-to-program fit of Theorem 5. Its statement is an existence claim, and its proof describes a construction without giving a worst-case bound in a finite input model. The paper’s computation model is exact cut-and-evaluation queries to named agents. The proposed mirror instead asks for polynomial bit complexity in \(\tau,L,\log M\), encodes valuations as rational piecewise-constant functions, and requires a compact description of an allocation over a continuum of type mass. That could be a worthwhile question, but its central challenge—succinct encoding and output—is not a complexity result established by the theorem.

That objection survives a stronger formulation only as a limit on how directly the mirror follows from the paper. It does not defeat the model. A cohort with recurring valuation profiles is plausible; the type can include each agent’s complete valuation; and scaling the resource supply can preserve a meaningful per-person share. The aggregate capacity condition and whole-bundle requirement give the proposed allocation a coherent interpretation. I cannot rule out such a regime by appealing to identity, vanishing shares, or the fact that cake is already divisible.

The paper’s computational setting also gives the proponent some support: the IJCAI predecessor explicitly gives an \(O(1)\)-query protocol for the three-agent, three-layer case and develops recursive proportional-allocation algorithms for other parameter regimes. That work supports treating these constructions as computational objects, though it does not supply the missing bound for this paper’s three-layer, arbitrary-\(n\), contiguous case. [IJCAI paper](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2020/0026.pdf)

So the strongest negative case is that this mirror is a new succinct-computation problem attached to a constructive theorem, rather than a continuous counterpart to a stated complexity result. That is a real reason to rank it below papers with explicit algorithmic or hardness classifications. It is not enough to support the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. The proposed cohort model remains recognizable and the continuous computational question is well-posed; honestly, this negative case is weak against the anchor itself.
