The best *unanchored* candidate is a continuous stable-matching problem for a large hiring market. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite sets of applicant and job types, each type specifying a complete ranking over the other side and the option of remaining unmatched. The input gives rational masses \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_b\), which may differ in total. The decision variable is a nonnegative matching flow \(x_{ab}\), with unmatched masses \(u_a\) and \(v_b\) satisfying the supply and demand totals. A solution is a stable flow: there is no pair of types \(a,b\) for which positive mass of type \(a\) prefers \(b\) to its assigned jobs (or being unmatched), and positive mass of type \(b\) prefers \(a\) to its assigned applicants (or being unmatched). This asks for a matching at the level of type masses, rather than a list of named individuals and pairs.

A plausible regime is a large labor market with many applicants and positions but relatively few distinct preference profiles. That fits the paper’s hiring motivation and is supported as a sensible market setting by its references to unbalanced matching markets [17] and a supply-and-demand framework [22]. I would expect the finite-type version to be tractable, perhaps through a type-compressed deferred-acceptance procedure; establishing the right algorithm and its dependence on the number of types and input bit length would be the actual computational question. This would cover the paper’s concern with stable matching under imbalance, not establish a continuous counterpart of its average-energy formulas.

The weakest point is decisive for this assignment: the article does not formally specify or prove a computational contribution that this proposed problem can mirror.
