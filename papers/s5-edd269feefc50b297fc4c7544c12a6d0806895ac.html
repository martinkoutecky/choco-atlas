The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchors: this paper states no computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered theorems concern convergence, implementation, and mechanism existence or impossibility. Theorem 2 and Theorem 6 are substantial, but neither gives a computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart.

The best version of a Theorem 2 mirror would take a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over preference types and ask for a budget-balanced, contractive mechanism implementing Lindahl allocations. Yet Theorem 2’s construction depends on the number of agents and a common curvature bound, not on the types’ masses. Within the paper’s proposed mechanism family, checking whether the parameters qualify amounts to checking the displayed inequalities. Allowing arbitrary mechanisms instead leaves no finite search space or encoded input for a complexity question until one imposes new restrictions. Computing allocations from \(\mu\) could be a worthwhile separate problem, but it would not mirror Theorem 2’s result about mechanism design.

Theorem 6 fares no better as an anchor. It proves an analytic impossibility for one-dimensional message spaces under a rich type space; it gives no algorithmic decision problem. A continuous version would require choosing a model of strategic behavior for an atomless population. The paper’s mechanisms use messages from named agents, including circular neighbor links. In an atomless anonymous model, one person’s message has zero mass and may not change the aggregate that drives others’ responses. Retaining those links as part of types would preserve the interactions at the cost of losing the natural repeated-type population; redesigning the mechanism anonymously would pose a new existence problem. Either route departs from the result Theorem 6 proves.

Large cohorts of economically similar agents are plausible, so I would not claim that continuous-agent mechanism design could never be worthwhile. The weaker point is the decisive one here: this paper supplies no computational result to anchor such a project, and the most promising computational questions require additional modelling and input-encoding choices that the paper does not motivate. I cannot make the universal “no scenario” claim airtight; I can make a strong case that this paper is not a computational continuization candidate.
