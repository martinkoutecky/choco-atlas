The continuous mirror question

Given finite complete valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), good kinds \(\mathcal{K}\) with rational per-household supplies \(q_g\), a conflict graph \(H\) on \(\mathcal{K}\), and rational additive values \(v_t(g)\), can one compute in time polynomial in the explicit input size and bit lengths a finite-support allocation \(x_{t,b}\)? Each bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{\mathcal{K}}\) must have independent positive support in \(H\); copies of the same kind do not conflict. Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\), and pairwise EF1 across every positively used type-bundle and every positively used target bundle, where EF1 is evaluated on the integral bundles. For the Theorem 5 mirror, restrict to \(\rho=\sum_g q_g\le 2\) and \(\delta=\max_g\sum_{h:gh\in E(H)}q_h\le 2/3\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of households represented by finitely many complete valuation types and their masses; per-capita supplies of repeated good kinds; and a mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\) over integral, conflict-feasible bundles. The goods remain indivisible for each household, and the allocation must use all supply and satisfy EF1 across its support.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 5 and 2. It leaves the approximation result in Theorem 3, the algorithms in Theorems 4 and 6, and the paper's other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a compact algorithm construct a finite-support EF1 mass allocation without expanding a common denominator into individual households and good copies?
  • Does the tiered supply threshold remain sharp in the mass model?
  • What guarantees hold outside the bounds \(\rho\le 2\), \(\delta\le 2/3\), and the corresponding tiered regime?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s complete EF1 allocation problem. The agents become a distribution over complete valuation types; each agent still receives an integral bundle of indivisible goods, and conflict constraints still apply to that bundle. Only the population and the supplies are measured in mass.

Here is a concrete regime: a large relief programme distributing goods to households. A good kind might be a particular item, supplied in many copies; some kinds cannot be packed together. There may be millions of households but relatively few complete valuation profiles, determined by recurring need or dietary categories. The regime is credible when the number of households is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of profiles, and the supplies of good kinds scale with the population.

Formally, let \(T\) be the finite set of household types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(K\) be the set of good kinds, with per-household supplies \(q_g\ge 0\). Let \(H\) be a conflict graph on \(K\): every copy of kind \(g\) conflicts with every copy of kind \(h\) when \(gh\) is an edge. Copies of the same kind do not conflict, so a household may receive multiple copies. Each type \(t\) has a nonnegative additive value \(v_t(g)\) for kind \(g\). Write \(\rho=\sum_g q_g\) for total supply per household and \(\delta=\max_g\sum_{h:gh\in E(H)}q_h\) for the largest conflict load.

A bundle is an integer vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{K}\) whose positive-support kinds form an independent set in \(H\). A proposed allocation is a finite list of masses \(x_{t,b}\ge0\): type-\(t\) households of total mass \(x_{t,b}\) receive the *actual integral bundle* \(b\). Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\) for every good kind, so all supply is allocated. Require EF1 across the allocation’s support: for every \(x_{t,b}>0\) and every bundle \(b'\) used by positive mass, either \(b'\) is empty or there is a good \(g\) in \(b'\) such that \(v_t\cdot b\ge v_t\cdot(b'-e_g)\). This is pairwise EF1 for the actual bundles of all agents represented by positive mass. The mass allocation is not a lottery giving each person a fractional bundle.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: for arbitrary additive valuations, when \(m\le2n\) and \(\Delta\le2n/3\), Algorithm 4 runs in polynomial time and returns a complete EF1 allocation. The mirror question is: Mass-Complete-EF1 — given \(T,\mu,K,H,q\), and the valuation vectors, construct such an \(x\), with running time polynomial in the explicit type and good-kind data and their binary bit lengths, when \(\rho\le2\) and \(\delta\le2/3\). It is the underlying allocation problem, not an encoding problem.

For rational inputs, choose a common denominator \(D\) and make a finite instance with \(D\mu_t\) households of type \(t\) and \(Dq_g\) copies of each good kind \(g\). Then \(m=D\rho\) and \(\Delta=D\delta\). Theorem 5 therefore guarantees that a feasible mass allocation exists throughout this regime. I would expect the compact construction problem to be Class A: the paper’s matching and envy-cycle structure offers a plausible route to compressing the finite construction. This is a conjecture about the compact algorithm, though; expanding to \(D\) households can take time exponential in the binary input length of \(D\).

A second, complementary anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here: for valuations with common tiers of size \(n\), Algorithm 1 runs in polynomial time and returns a complete EF1 allocation when \(m\le n\lfloor n/\Delta\rfloor+n-\Delta\). Its mirror, Tiered-Mass-EF1, uses the same allocation definition, with good kinds partitioned into tiers whose supplies sum to \(1\) in each full tier and at most \(1\) in the last; every type values every good in an earlier tier at least as much as every good in a later tier. For \(\delta>0\), ask for an EF1 mass allocation when \(\rho\le\lfloor1/\delta\rfloor+1-\delta\). This follows the paper’s threshold after normalizing \(\Delta/n=\delta\) and \(m/n=\rho\). I would also expect Class A here, with the shared tier structure making a compact matching-based construction especially plausible.

These anchors are proved for explicit finite instances, not for the compact binary-mass input above. They establish existence for rational mass instances by scaling, but they do not settle whether a solver can construct a short mass allocation in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|K|\), and the input bit lengths. That is the computational question worth handing to a prover. Follow-up questions include whether the compact algorithm can avoid denominator expansion, whether the tiered bound remains sharp in the mass model, and what happens beyond the stated supply and conflict-load regimes. The paper’s Proposition 4 is a useful stress test, but I would not count it as a computational anchor: it gives a finite \(\alpha\)-EF1 impossibility, and whether its extra item has a meaningful counterpart when normalized supply changes by only \(1/n\) is open in this mirror.

The main weakness is the regime. Grouping repeated goods into kinds imposes clone structure on their conflicts and values; this is plausible for recurring supplies, but narrower than arbitrary item-by-item conflict graphs. The explicit finite algorithms may also expand a compact instance by its denominator. Still, this mirror preserves the paper’s central question—complete allocation of indivisible goods under conflicts with EF1 fairness—and changes the population and supply representation in a way that fits a large, repeated-profile setting. It covers Theorems 5 and 2, not the rest of the paper. The related-work NP-hardness result concerns maximal *partial* EF1 allocations and is cited from elsewhere, so it is not an anchor for these complete-allocation questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that the finite theorems do not yet give algorithms for the compact mass input. Scaling rational masses by a common denominator recovers a finite instance, but that denominator can be exponentially large in its bit length. EF1 also depends on which bundles occur in the allocation’s support, so the result is not automatically a small LP over masses. For Theorem 5, the mirror needs both recurring valuation profiles and recurring good kinds with clone-like conflict relations. That is a meaningful restriction: the paper permits each item to have its own conflict neighborhood, while a fixed set of good kinds imposes repeated structure. Theorem 2’s mirror is narrower still, requiring shared preference tiers and roughly one unit of supply per full tier.

Those are real limits on the mirrors, but they do not defeat them. Theorem 5 is an explicit computational result, and large populations receiving repeated kinds of goods under recurring household valuations are credible. The clone model is a natural way to scale both population and supply; allowing item types to proliferate until every item is unique would lose the compact regime, but a narrower regime can still be worth studying. The unresolved task—constructing a compact allocation without expanding the denominator—is precisely the high-multiplicity computational question. The finite theorem supplies existence after expansion, not its compact complexity.

Theorem 2 survives the same challenge. Tiered preferences and per-capita supplies may be restrictive, but they define a coherent setting for recurring goods. Its EF1 guarantee follows from the tier structure, leaving a question about assigning mass to conflict-compatible integral bundles. That could simplify the problem, but an easy or tractable answer would still be an answer about computation over a continuous population.

So I cannot make a strong universal case against these mirrors. The paper has named algorithmic anchors, and neither fails for lack of a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The honest negative point is that the proponent has established finite-instance existence under scaling, not a compact algorithm; that is a caveat to the proposed research case, not a reason to reject it. I would not support a red verdict on this basis.
