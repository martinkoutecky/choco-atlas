The continuous mirror question

Given two continuous populations of total mass \(1\), each represented by finitely many types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), and shared rankings over mutually acceptable opposite-side types with ties only in the final rank block, find a maximum-mass stable coupling. A coupling is a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding type masses; for each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), stability requires at least one of \(b_i(j)=\mu_i-\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}\) and \(b_j(i)=\nu_j-\sum_{\ell\succeq_j i}x_{\ell j}\) to be zero. Determine the exact, approximation, and parameterized complexity of maximizing \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\) as a function of the type supports and input bit length.

The model it lives in

Two equal-mass, nonatomic sides have finite supports of preference types. Each type has a rational mass and a complete description of acceptability and rankings over opposite-side types, including terminal ties. Variables \(x_{ij}\) record matched mass, each type’s total allocation is bounded by its mass, and no acceptable pair may have positive mass on both sides that is unmatched or strictly prefers the other type. The objective is to maximize total matched mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror uses Theorem 22’s R2T approximation hardness. It leaves the paper’s R1T \(5/4\)-approximation, integrality-gap bounds, and other results outside the proposed problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a maximum-mass stable coupling be found in time polynomial in the type support sizes and mass bit length?
  • What approximation guarantees or hardness thresholds hold for maximum-mass stable coupling with terminal ties?
  • Does the stability disjunction admit a useful formulation or parameterized algorithm in the number of types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest anchor is Theorem 22, proved in this paper by modifying the reduction from [35]. It says that MAX SMTI with two-sided ties confined to the ends of preference lists (R2T) is NP-hard to approximate within any factor below \(33/29\), and is hard under the unique games conjecture to approximate within any factor below \(4/3\).

A natural mirror is R2T Maximum Stable Mass Matching. There are two finite sets of types, \(I\) and \(J\), with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), each side’s masses summing to \(1\). A type gives its members’ complete preference list over acceptable types on the other side; any ties are confined to the final rank block. The input size depends on the support sizes \(\tau_I,\tau_J\) and the masses’ bit length \(L\), not on a separately enumerated population.

A solution is a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), for mutually acceptable pairs, with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\). It records the type-by-type histogram of a one-to-one matching: mass can be split across different counterpart types, but each member of the population gets at most one partner. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\), the matched mass. Define
\[ b_i(j)=\mu_i-\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}, \qquad b_j(i)=\nu_j-\sum_{\ell\succeq_j i}x_{\ell j}. \]
These are the masses of types \(i\) and \(j\) that are unmatched or strictly prefer the other type to their current partners. The coupling is stable when, for every mutually acceptable pair \((i,j)\), at least one of \(b_i(j)\) and \(b_j(i)\) is zero. Otherwise positive mass on both sides could form a blocking pair.

The resulting prover question is: for this problem, can one find a maximum stable coupling in time polynomial in \(\tau_I+\tau_J\) and \(L\), or approximate its matched mass within a specified factor? In particular, do the \(33/29\) and \(4/3\) barriers from Theorem 22 persist, or does divisible population mass permit better algorithms? My working expectation is Class C: maximizing mass while satisfying these pairwise no-blocking conditions seems likely to retain combinatorial difficulty over the type pairs. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 22.

The regime I have in mind is a large centralized matching market with many interchangeable candidates and positions. Members of a type share the same rankings, acceptability, and tie policy; a large population may therefore have \(N\) agents but far fewer than \(N\) distinct types. One plausible pattern is that each side ranks preferred categories strictly and treats its final acceptable fallback pool as tied. This keeps the problem recognizable as maximum-cardinality stable matching with ties and incomplete lists, while making the population—not the matching outcome—the continuous object.

The weakest point is that Theorem 22’s discrete approximation lower bounds do not automatically apply here. A type’s mass can be matched across several types, and stability is defined by whether positive masses can block; the theorem’s reduction has not been shown to survive that change. The mirror also applies the R2T restriction to rankings over types, which is a deliberate high-multiplicity formulation. Those are real gaps, but they are the questions the mirror puts on the table. I would limit this case to Theorem 22; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s R1T approximation result or its integrality-gap results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 22’s hardness construction is built from named agents and their individual preference neighborhoods. In a market with genuinely few types, those neighborhoods must become rankings over counterpart *types*. That is a sensible market model, but it no longer preserves the reduction’s individual-level structure. If instead each distinct ordering is a separate type, the support can grow with the population, and the high-multiplicity advantage the mirror is meant to study largely disappears. The paper’s approximation barriers therefore give little evidence about the compressed-type regime.

The proposed mass problem also does not inherit the paper’s LP shape. Its stability condition is \(b_i(j)=0\) or \(b_j(i)=0\) for each type pair, so maximizing matched mass means optimizing over a union of feasible regions with a disjunction at every pair. Divisible mass does not remove that combinatorial choice. This weakens any expectation that the continuous-optimization tools in the programme will help, though it does not make the resulting complexity question uninteresting.

There is substantial neighboring work: stable allocation, also called ordinal transportation, already studies real-valued assignment quantities and algorithms for finding stable allocations. That makes feasibility and the basic mass model familiar territory, but it does not settle the proposed maximum-matched-mass problem with terminal ties. I would treat this literature as support for the model’s plausibility, not as a novelty collision. [Baïou and Balinski on stable allocation](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.27.3.485.310)

I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large market of interchangeable worker and employer cohorts with rankings over counterpart categories is plausible, and the type-level coupling has a clear population interpretation. The negative case is limited to the gap between the paper’s identity-rich hardness construction and that compressed regime, plus the fact that the continuous problem retains nonconvex stability choices. Those are reasons to be cautious about this anchor, not enough to defeat it.
