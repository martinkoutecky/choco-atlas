The continuous mirror question

Given ordered types \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(m\) identical resources, a common strictly increasing continuous latency \(f\), and a specified family \(C\) of intervals of types, find an allocation \(x_{t,i}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_i x_{t,i}=\mu_t\) such that no \(I\in C\) can reassign its mass among the resources, with outsiders fixed, so that almost every coalition member weakly lowers their latency and positive mass strictly lowers it.

The model it lives in

An atomless population consists of ordered origin-band types with masses \(\mu_t\); each unit of mass uses one resource, represented in aggregate by \(x_{t,i}\). Resource \(i\) has load \(\sum_t x_{t,i}\) and latency \(f(\sum_t x_{t,i})\). Coalitions are specified intervals of types and may reassign their mass while outsiders remain fixed. The uniform allocation \(x_{t,i}=\mu_t/m\) gives equal loads and is stable against every coalition.

The objection that survived

The atomless model lets a coalition split type mass fractionally, unlike the paper's indivisible-player deviations, so the discrete construction and its combinatorial role for \(C\) do not carry over.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.12's identical-resource contiguous-equilibrium existence result and construction; it leaves the laminar and centralized results and the nonexistence results for other RSG regimes untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this mass-stable construction be rounded to finite \(n\)-agent instances with an explicit bound on any resulting stability loss?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 3.12, proved here. It says that in an identical-resource resource selection game, every given contiguous coalition structure \(C\) admits a \(C\)-stable allocation. The proof also gives a linear-time construction. The paper has no NP-hardness or other complexity-hardness result to anchor on, so this is the algorithmic route. [Theorem 3.12 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04648)

A plausible regime is a large population of commuters divided into \(\tau\) ordered neighborhood or origin bands, with many commuters in each band. People in a band share the same costs for using parallel routes, and designated coalitions are consecutive stretches of bands, reflecting communication or coordination along the corridor. Here \(\tau\) is small relative to the number of commuters. Type \(t\) records the band and its coalition memberships; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of commuters in that band.

I would ask the prover to study Interval-Stable Nonatomic Resource Selection. An instance has ordered types \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), \(m\) identical resources, a common strictly increasing rational piecewise-linear latency function \(f\), and a specified family \(C\) of intervals of consecutive types. A feasible allocation is \(x_{t,i}\geq 0\), where \(x_{t,i}\) is the mass of type \(t\) using resource \(i\), and \(\sum_i x_{t,i}=\mu_t\). Resource \(i\)’s load is \(q_i=\sum_t x_{t,i}\), so its users incur cost \(f(q_i)\). A coalition interval may reassign its members’ mass among resources. The question is to find an allocation for which no such reassignment weakly lowers the cost of almost every coalition member while strictly lowering it for positive mass.

I expect this problem to be tractable, for a simple reason: set \(x_{t,i}=\mu_t/m\). Every resource then has load \(1/m\), so everyone pays the same cost. A profitable deviation cannot put coalition mass on a resource whose final load exceeds \(1/m\); such a resource would make those members worse off. But noncoalition mass on any resource is at most its original load, \(1/m\). So no resource can end overloaded. Since total mass is \(1\), all final loads must remain \(1/m\), leaving no strict improvement. This construction is \(O(m\tau)\) and is stable against every coalition, not just the specified intervals.

The mirror covers only Theorem 3.12’s identical-resource contiguous-equilibrium result. Its weakest point is also clear: atomless, divisible mass erases the one-agent load differences around which the finite proof is built. Equalizing the routes makes contiguity irrelevant, and the resulting stability is stronger than the theorem’s. The authors may recognize it as the same resource-selection and coalition-stability question in a continuum population, but may regard this special case as too easy to capture the substance of their discrete construction. A useful next question is whether this balanced allocation can be rounded with a stability guarantee for finite populations, or whether heterogeneous route costs preserve any nontrivial interval-stability result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case has to concede a real anchor: the identical-resource contiguous-equilibrium theorem gives a linear-time construction for a named existence problem. The paper does not classify the complexity of finding equilibria, but this result is enough to make “there is no computational result here” an unreliable dismissal.

The strongest mirror would keep many commuters in ordered bands, make each band a complete type, and allow resource costs to differ. Even then, as long as the paper’s RSG assumption is preserved—every user of resource \(i\) pays the same increasing cost \(f_i(q_i)\)—the continuum version loses the coalition structure that drives the theorem. At a nonatomic Wardrop allocation, every used resource has the same cost \(p\). Since \(f_i(0)=0\), strict increase ensures that all resources are used. Consider any coalition, including an interval of bands, that reassigns its mass and weakly benefits every member. On any resource receiving coalition mass, its final load must be at most its original load: otherwise those members would pay more than \(p\). On a resource receiving none, its final load is just the outsiders who were already there, also no greater than the original load. Total mass is conserved, so every resource’s load must remain unchanged. No coalition can strictly improve. This argument works for arbitrary increasing resource costs and every family of coalitions, not just the identical-resource interval case.

That points to a modeling obstacle for the proposed mirror: in the paper, coalition membership counts and indivisible load changes make the choice of \(C\) matter. In the mass model, a stable allocation can be found without using \(C\) at all. The proposed commuters and bands are plausible, but they do not preserve the paper’s central computational object. The paper’s nonexistence results do not supply an escape route for the same reason: they too concern homogeneous RSG users.

One could make coalition structure matter again by giving types different resource costs, access, or other payoff-relevant traits. That is a plausible high-multiplicity model, but it becomes a player-specific congestion game; the paper’s named RSG results provide no anchor for it. So the negative case is persuasive for the results actually proved here, while falling short of the requested universal claim. A heterogeneous-type extension may be a worthwhile project, and I cannot rule it out from this paper.
