The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that when \(a_2\le a_3\) and \(b_1\le b_3\), each closed-list subgame has tie equilibria in which either candidates \(1\) and \(3\) or \(2\) and \(3\) contest the second seat, or two pairs separately contest it: \(1\) and \(3\) with \(2\) and \(4\), or \(1\) and \(4\) with \(2\) and \(3\). In the full game, candidate \(4\) is always ranked first and wins a seat with high probability, possibly with certainty. Propositions 1 and 2, also proved here, likewise characterize tie-equilibrium outcomes under SNTV and open-list PR.

But none of these propositions asserts a computational result such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or an algorithmic bound. They are equilibrium characterizations. So there is no eligible computational anchor in this paper, and I cannot honestly present one of its numbered results as a complexity result.

There is a plausible population-continuum question suggested by Proposition 3: take voter types \(a,b,c\) as complete utility types, with rational masses \(\mu_a,\mu_b,\mu_c\) summing to one. Let each type choose mass shares over its permitted ballots; let parties choose list orders to maximize expected seats; and retain the paper’s common close-race beliefs and best-response conditions. Given those inputs and a rational threshold \(q\), ask whether a subgame-perfect close-race equilibrium exists in which candidate \(4\) wins with probability at least \(q\). A scenario with many voters in three shared preference blocs makes the high-multiplicity interpretation plausible.

That would be a new computational question suggested by the paper, not a mirror of a computational result it proves. Its soft spot is substantial: the paper’s consistency conditions depend on finite-voter randomization producing possible ties. In an atomless population, independent ballot randomization averages into deterministic mass shares, so the tie probabilities may vanish or change meaning. The authors might accept the bloc-based population model, but preserving their equilibrium concept in the continuum needs justification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes the SNTV tie equilibrium; Proposition 2 classifies open-list PR tie equilibria and gives conditions for them; Proposition 3 characterizes closed-list PR equilibria and the parties’ ranking choices. Those are equilibrium results, not complexity claims or algorithmic bounds. Turning Proposition 3 into a decision problem about whether candidate \(4\) wins with probability at least \(q\) is a plausible new question, but it is not a continuous version of a computational problem this paper proves.

The proposed bloc interpretation is sensible, and the authors could recognize voter types with rational masses. But for this paper’s fixed model—four candidates, three types, a finite set of ballots and list orders—the resulting equilibrium question has bounded dimension. A meaningful complexity landscape would require scaling the candidates, types, or rules beyond the propositions’ setting. That might be worthwhile research inspired by the paper, but it would need a new model and would not rescue any of the three propositions as computational anchors.

There is also a deeper modeling difficulty. In the paper, an individual vote matters through possible ties, and close-race beliefs are tied to those tie events. In an atomless society, one voter’s deviation changes no mass; with independent ballot mixing, aggregate ballot shares become deterministic, so the paper’s positive-probability tie events no longer ground its belief-consistency conditions. A finite-population limit could retain those events, or correlated mass-level behavior could create aggregate lotteries, but either choice adds assumptions the propositions do not settle.

So the case against is strong as a verdict on this paper as a source of ChoCo computational mirrors: none of Propositions 1–3 supplies an eligible anchor, and the proposed threshold problem is newly formulated. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous-computational project could arise in this model is weaker. The type-bloc setting is credible; what the paper lacks is a computational result whose continuous mirror the programme could chart.
