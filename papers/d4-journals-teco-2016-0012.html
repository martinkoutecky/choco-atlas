What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 3.4’s \(e/(e-1)\) lower bound; the paper’s upper bounds, other valuation classes, bid-dependent auction results, and multi-unit auction results remain outside it.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a continuum version of the paper’s first-price auction inefficiency problem. There is a strict qualification: none of the paper’s numbered results classifies a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or similar. Its results are price-of-anarchy bounds. So Theorem 3.4 is a strong anchor for a continuous *performance* question, but it is not a qualifying complexity-theoretic anchor under the narrowest reading of your rule.

Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper, says that the price of anarchy of simultaneous first-price auctions with full information and OXS valuations is at least \(e/(e-1)\). In other words, some mixed Nash equilibrium has expected welfare at most \(1-1/e\) times optimal welfare, even when all bidders’ valuations are OXS.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large ad marketplace with many small advertisers, grouped into a fixed number \(\tau\) of segments. A type is an advertiser’s complete valuation function over bundles of ad slots; \(\mu_\theta\) is the share of advertisers of type \(\theta\). The regime is \(N\gg\tau\), with many advertisers sharing each type. To keep the market meaningful as \(N\) grows, specify each slot’s supply per advertiser, \(\kappa_j\): a finite market with \(N\) advertisers has roughly \(N\kappa_j\) copies of slot \(j\). This is a co-modification of supply forced by scaling the population.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. An instance gives \(m\) slot types, a finite support \(\Theta\), rational type masses \(\mu\), OXS valuations \(v_\theta\), rational supplies \(\kappa_j\), and the simultaneous first-price rule. The population has mass one. Each individual submits an integral bid vector in \(\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m\); the type-level strategy is a distribution \(\sigma_\theta\) over bid vectors. Each slot’s supply goes to the highest bidders, with ties rationed proportionally, and winners pay their bids. Thus individuals receive integral bundles while aggregate type allocations can be fractional.

The welfare benchmark is the maximum of \(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}v_\theta(S)\), subject to \(\sum_S x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S:j\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le\kappa_j\). The continuous problem, Finite-Support Continuum FPA PoA, is: given such an instance, compute the supremum of optimal welfare divided by expected welfare over its type-symmetric mixed Nash equilibria, returning the value and a worst-case equilibrium (or a sequence approaching the supremum). The action variable is the family of bid distributions \((\sigma_\theta)_{\theta\in\Theta}\); the objective is the welfare ratio.

I would investigate this as Class C, provisionally: exact equilibrium computation involves best-response constraints over distributions on a continuous bid space. The paper gives no hardness evidence for that problem, so this is a research direction, not a claim that hardness transfers. The natural follow-up is whether the \(e/(e-1)\) inefficiency survives this continuum model, and how the answer depends on \(\tau\). There is some support for the high-multiplicity regime: Appendix A groups \(n\) bidders into \(d\) identical valuation classes plus a dummy type, and for fixed \(d\) its construction still has a nontrivial limiting welfare ratio, \(1/(1-e^{-1-1/d})\). That construction is not a numbered result, and it does not establish the exact \(e/(e-1)\) bound with fixed support.

I would scope the mirror to Theorem 3.4. The paper’s subadditive lower bound in Theorem 4.1 is a weaker fit for high multiplicity: its construction uses only two bidders. The soft spot in my case is that the anchor concerns equilibrium inefficiency, not computational complexity, and its sharpest construction has a growing number of valuation types. Appendix A helps make a fixed-type regime plausible, but it does not close either gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would vote against the proposed mirror under ChoCo’s current anchor rule, though the stronger claim that no useful continuous auction question exists is too broad.

Theorem 3.4 establishes a price-of-anarchy lower bound. It gives no complexity classification or algorithm for a computational task. “Compute the worst equilibrium” is a possible new problem, but it is not a problem whose computational complexity this paper establishes. That is the cleanest objection to treating this result as a ChoCo computational anchor.

The advertiser-segment setting is credible: a type can specify a complete valuation, and many advertisers can share it. Appendix A supports that regime with a fixed number of valuation classes and a nontrivial limiting welfare ratio, although its item set grows with the population and it does not establish the exact \(e/(e-1)\) bound. So the negative case should not rest on claiming that repeated types are implausible.

The proposed *exact* continuum equilibrium problem has a more basic difficulty. With mass \(\mu_\theta\) of each type and per-capita supply \(0<\kappa<1\), the aggregate bids determine a market-clearing cutoff \(p\) for a slot. An individual advertiser has zero mass, so changing their bid does not move \(p\). A bid above \(p\) wins; the advertiser can lower it while staying above \(p\), improving utility. At \(p\), a marginal bidder is rationed or loses, while bidding \(p+\epsilon\) wins for nearly the full surplus when \(v>p\). A bid below \(p\) can also be improved by bidding just above it. Thus a bidder with positive surplus generally has no best response. This is a consequence of losing individual price impact in the atomless limit; changing the tie rule alone does not restore it. Treating an entire type cohort as a strategic player could restore impact, but that changes the strategic unit.

A limit of finite-population equilibria, or an \(\epsilon\)-equilibrium notion, is the natural repair. Either could yield a worthwhile large-market result. It would ask how equilibria behave as \(N\) grows with type proportions and supply held fixed, rather than compute the PoA over exact equilibria of the proposed atomless game. That is a credible adjacent research direction, but it sits closer to equilibrium-limit analysis than to ChoCo’s stated gap in algorithms and complexity for continuous societies.

So the best negative verdict is narrow: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and the proposed exact equilibrium mirror is generally ill-posed at the atomless limit. The case is weak against a broader programme that welcomes approximate or limiting equilibrium questions; Appendix A gives that programme a plausible starting point.
