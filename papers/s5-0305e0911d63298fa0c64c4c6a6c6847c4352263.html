The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with \(m\) item kinds each supplied at \(1/m\) per capita, does there exist a uniformly specified family of finite-round distributed protocols for every compatible replication \(q\), with \(q\mu_t\) applicants of type \(t\) and \(q/m\) copies of each item, whose honest outcome is ordinally efficient and satisfies strong equal treatment, and under which every honest applicant’s expected item lottery weakly dominates its honest-execution lottery against every fail-stop coalition of at most \(q/2\) applicants?

The model it lives in

A rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types describes replicated applicant populations; each compatible finite replication has \(q\mu_t\) individual applicants of type \(t\), \(q/m\) identical copies of each item kind, integral assignments, and an individual-level protocol and adversary. The question is whether one uniform protocol family preserves the paper’s fairness, efficiency, and maximin-security requirements across those replications.

The objection that survived

The mass vector and allocation marginals alone do not capture transcript-dependent abort strategies or which individual participants are honest; the protocol-family question must model those explicitly.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s joint ordinal-efficiency, strong equal-treatment, and maximin-security feasibility question in replicated markets; it leaves the constructive protocol results and the uniform-dominance and truthfulness results unexamined.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the four-ranking profile from Theorem 4.1 remain an obstruction for every compatible replication?
  • How does feasibility depend on the maximum adversarial fraction and on the protocol family’s round and communication bounds?
  • Can transcript-level security be characterized using the ranking masses and symmetric protocol structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar complexity classification. Its strongest relevant result is a protocol-feasibility impossibility, so the positive case is for a continuous mirror of that question—not a claim that the paper already supplies a conventional complexity anchor.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in the paper. It says that there is a four-player preference profile for which *no mechanism* that is both ordinally efficient and strongly equal-treatment can be realized by a bounded-round maximin-secure protocol against fail-stop adversaries controlling at least \(n/2\) players. The proof reduces such a protocol to two-party coin flipping and invokes Cleve’s impossibility result. In the paper’s example, the four rankings each have mass \(1/4\): \(m_1\succ m_3\succ m_2\succ m_4\), \(m_1\succ m_4\succ m_2\succ m_3\), \(m_2\succ m_3\succ m_1\succ m_4\), and \(m_2\succ m_4\succ m_1\succ m_3\).

The mirror I find plausible is High-Multiplicity Ordinal-Assignment Security Feasibility. A society has a finite support \(T\) of complete item rankings and rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each item kind has per-capita supply \(1/m\). For any \(q\) divisible by the mass denominators and by \(m\), this describes \(q\mu_t\) applicants of type \(t\) and \(q/m\) copies of each item. A type-level allocation variable \(x_{tj}\) records the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) assigned item \(j\); it must satisfy \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}=1/m\). The per-person lottery for type \(t\) is \(p_{tj}=x_{tj}/\mu_t\). The aggregate may be fractional, while each applicant still receives one integral item.

For a given \((m,T,\mu)\), the question is whether there is a uniform family of finite-round distributed protocols for these replicated populations whose honest outcome is ordinally efficient and satisfies the paper’s strong equal-treatment condition, and which is maximin secure against fail-stop coalitions of at most half the applicants. Security means that each honest applicant’s expected item distribution under any such attack weakly dominates their distribution under honest execution, according to their ranking. A solution is a protocol family with its resource bounds stated; there is no scalar objective to optimize because the original result is a feasibility claim. The focal instance is precisely the four-ranking mass profile above, scaled to \(q=4k\) applicants and \(k\) copies of each item.

This is a population mirror rather than a mirror of fractional outcomes alone: it scales the population and item supply together, while preserving integral assignments for individuals. A large school or housing clearinghouse with many applicants but relatively few complete preference types is a credible regime. The type must include every feature the protocol uses; this scenario therefore needs an anonymous protocol in which applicants with the same ranking have the same protocol role. Otherwise identity-specific roles could make the type support grow with the population.

I would expect the four-player impossibility not to transfer automatically. Its coin-flipping reduction uses a small number of players and assignment outcomes that encode a single coin; with many copies of each type, mass can be split across assignments and the aggregate need not encode that coin. For fixed support size, the allocation constraints themselves are finite-dimensional and linear, so a tractable type-level criterion is a reasonable Class A conjecture. Whether the security constraint also admits such a characterization is open; the paper does not establish it.

The weakest point is exactly that gap: Theorem 4.1 does not prove anything about replicated populations, and the individual security guarantees may resist aggregation even when the allocation marginals compress cleanly by type. The mirror also extends a protocol-security paper into a high-multiplicity feasibility question; authors might accept the population model while declining to treat it as a complexity result. The useful follow-up questions are whether the obstruction survives as \(q\) grows, how the answer depends on the adversarial mass threshold, and whether allowing Byzantine deviations changes it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is the right result to test, but the proposed mirror only becomes a population question by replacing its finite cast of protocol participants with a sequence of larger finite casts. That is a plausible research direction in secure mechanism design; it does not yet give ChoCo a continuous computational problem.

The theorem is about whether a mechanism can be realized by a bounded-round distributed protocol, under a particular adversarial model and ideal cryptographic assumptions. It gives no running-time, approximation, or parameterized-complexity classification for an algorithm on a society description. Fix \(q\), and the proposed profile is still a finite protocol instance. Let \(q\) grow, and the central object is a uniform family of protocols whose participants, transcripts, and adversaries all grow with \(q\). The masses \(\mu_t\) do not replace that scale parameter. The proponent’s construction therefore asks a potentially interesting asymptotic protocol question, but it does not put the paper’s central question into the continuous-optimization or high-multiplicity algorithmic framework.

The obstacle is not that rankings cannot be grouped. They can describe type-level assignment marginals. It is that the theorem’s security guarantee concerns actual participants: who commits, who can withhold a message, which coalition rushes or aborts, and whether each honest person’s lottery is harmed. A type-level allocation \(x_{tj}\) records none of the transcript or deviation structure that determines whether those lotteries can be implemented securely. Adding those protocol roles and histories to the types would make the protocol state, rather than the population distribution, the object being modeled; keeping only rankings leaves out information the security question uses.

There is also a limit issue. In an atomless model, a single participant has zero mass, so an aggregate guarantee for type \(t\) need not express the paper’s guarantee to every honest member of that type. Replacing it with a guarantee for average type satisfaction changes maximin security. Replacing each type by a single mass participant changes the adversary too: one mass player does not have the independent commitments, messages, and possible aborts of many protocol participants. Either repair returns to finite \(q\), or studies a different security notion. The linear assignment constraints in the proposal may describe feasible marginals, but they do not bridge that gap.

So the strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror’s meaningful content remains in finite-player protocol families, while its continuous allocation model omits the mechanism that Theorem 4.1 studies. I would not claim that no large clearinghouse scenario could interest these authors: replicated applicants and interchangeable item copies are credible. If the authors count asymptotic protocol feasibility as part of their computational agenda, the mirror could be worthwhile. That is the honest weak point in the negative case. But as a continuization of this paper’s computational result, the proposal changes the research question more than it continuizes the population.
