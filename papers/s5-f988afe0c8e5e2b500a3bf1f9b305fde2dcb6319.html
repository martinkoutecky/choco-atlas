The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), fixed \(k\ge4\), a tie-breaking order, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete rankings \(v_t\), explicit menus \(\mathcal A_t\) of allowed \(k\)-approval ballots for nonfocal types, and a focal type \(\theta\) of mass \(\rho>0\), let \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) allocate each nonfocal type's mass among \(S\in\mathcal A_t\). For focal ballots \(A,B\in\binom{C}{k}\), define the deterministic \(k\)-approval winners from the resulting normalized scores. Decide whether some \(B\) continuously weakly dominates \(A\): for every feasible \(x\), the focal ranking weakly prefers the winner under \(B\) to that under \(A\), with strict preference for at least one feasible \(x\). If no such \(B\) exists, \(A\) is a continuous level-2 strategy; comparing \(A\) with the truthful top-\(k\) ballot gives the improving variant.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity voting game with ranking-and-menu types carrying masses \(\mu_t\); nonfocal action masses \(x_{t,S}\) may split fractionally, while a coordinated focal bloc of mass \(\rho\) chooses one \(k\)-approval ballot. The objective is to test weak dominance of focal ballots under deterministic winner selection, not to fractionalize outcomes.

The objection that survived

A positive-mass focal type that must coordinate on one ballot is a coalition player rather than the paper's individual voter; allowing its mass to split changes the compared object, while a measure-zero focal voter has no electoral influence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9's level-2 and improving-strategy questions for fixed \(k\ge4\), but leaves the paper's Plurality and \(2\)-approval algorithms, structural characterizations, minimality variants, and \(3\)-approval questions untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Whether the coordinated focal-bloc interpretation is an acceptable high-multiplicity semantics for the paper's level-2 agency.
  • Whether the fractional formulation remains polynomial when menus are implicit or \(k\) is part of the input.
  • Whether the original \(X3C\)-style hardness can survive through type-specific menus despite fractional mass splitting.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity, type-level version of the paper’s weak-dominance problem—not a continuum of outcomes or a probabilistic voting rule. The winner remains one candidate under deterministic \(k\)-approval; only the electorate and its action profiles become continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper. It states that, for every fixed \(k\ge4\), given a GS-game \(G=(V,R_k,(A_i)_{i\in N})\) and a strategy \(v\) of voter \(1\), deciding whether \(v\) is a level-2 strategy is NP-hard, while deciding whether \(v\) is an improving strategy is coNP-hard. The proof is by reduction from Exact Cover by 3-Sets: the construction is given for \(k=4\), then extended to larger fixed \(k\) with dummy candidates. The theorem does not claim NP- or coNP-completeness; the paper’s Remark 1 gives only Boolean-hierarchy and DP upper bounds. The hardness construction also does not establish hardness when other voters are restricted to minimal manipulations.

The scenario I would use is a large approval election in which voters are organized into homogeneous constituencies, party networks, or media-defined cohorts. A type records a complete ranking \(v_t\) and the same cognitive information: which level-1 ballots the voters regard as possible. Millions of voters may share one such type, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct ranking/menu types remains moderate. This is not the claim that every election is high-multiplicity; it is a specific regime in which many voters are interchangeable in precisely the sense required by the programme.

The focal “voter 1” becomes a positive-mass homogeneous bloc. Its members share a ranking and use one common tactical approval ballot, perhaps because a party or constituency coordinates its recommendation. This is the necessary type-level co-modification: an infinitesimal individual cannot change a continuum election, whereas a coordinated positive-mass type can.

I would call the resulting problem Continuous CH-\(k\)-Approval Level-2 Strategy\(_\infty\).

An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a fixed \(k\ge4\), a tie-breaking order \(>\), a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and for each type \(t\) a ranking \(v_t\) and a finite menu \(\mathcal A_t\) of allowed \(k\)-approval ballots. A ballot can be represented by its approved \(k\)-set \(S\subseteq C\), since ballots with the same top \(k\) candidates are equivalent under \(k\)-approval. Nonmanipulating types have singleton menus; manipulating types have menus containing their truthful ballot and selected level-1 ballots, just as the paper’s GS-game specifies. One type \(\theta\) is distinguished as the focal bloc, with mass \(\rho=\mu_\theta>0\).

For every nonfocal type \(t\), let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of that type using ballot \(S\in\mathcal A_t\). Thus \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) and \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal A_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\). If the focal bloc uses ballot \(A\), candidate \(c\)’s normalized score is \( \sigma^A_c(x)=\rho\mathbf 1[c\in A]+\sum_{t\ne\theta}\sum_{S\in\mathcal A_t}x_{t,S}\mathbf 1[c\in S] \). Let \(W_A(x)\) be the \(k\)-approval winner under these scores and the fixed tie-breaking order.

For two focal ballots \(A\) and \(B\), say that \(A\) continuously weakly dominates \(B\) if, for every feasible mass allocation \(x\), the focal ranking \(v_\theta\) weakly prefers \(W_A(x)\) to \(W_B(x)\), and for at least one feasible \(x\) strictly prefers it. The question is:

Given \(A\), is there any other focal ballot \(B\in\mathcal A_\theta\) that continuously weakly dominates \(A\)?

If the answer is no, \(A\) is a continuous level-2 strategy. The continuous improving variant asks whether \(A\) weakly dominates the truthful ballot \(A^0=\operatorname{top}_k(v_\theta)\). This is one mirror of both parts of Theorem 9; the level-2 version is my lead question.

The question remains recognizably theirs. It retains \(k\)-approval, fixed tie-breaking, sincere rankings, GS-manipulator menus, level-1 possibilities, weak dominance, level-2 strategies, and improving strategies. The only substantive change is that a finite profile of identical voters is represented by masses, and the finite product of individual choices becomes the set of feasible mass allocations. This is the natural high-multiplicity relaxation: a discrete profile corresponds to allocations whose coordinates are multiples of \(1/N\).

I expect this mirror to be Class A, despite Theorem 9’s discrete hardness. For fixed focal ballots \(A,B\), and fixed candidate winners \(p,q\), the conditions “\(p\) wins under \(A\)” and “\(q\) wins under \(B\)” are linear inequalities in the variables \(x_{t,S}\). Therefore a counterexample to dominance can be found by a linear program. Enumerating \(p,q\in C\) handles all possible winner pairs, including the tie-breaking inequalities. A second LP test checks whether strict improvement occurs somewhere. For fixed \(k\), there are only \(\binom{m}{k}\) \(k\)-approval-equivalence classes, so testing all possible competing focal ballots is polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the explicitly listed menus, and the input bit length.

This suggests that the paper’s X3C hardness is driven by population integrality: in the finite construction, each manipulator either uses its strategic ballot or does not. In the continuum, a type’s mass may split between those actions, turning the exact-cover choice into linear allocation. The discrete hardness therefore need not transfer; its dissolution is precisely the computational phenomenon the continuization programme is meant to expose. The improving version is even simpler: compare only against the truthful focal ballot, and the same LP reasoning applies.

The weakest point is the positive-mass focal bloc. The paper studies an individual boundedly rational voter, whereas this mirror studies a homogeneous constituency choosing one common ballot. If the authors insist that agency must remain attached to a single infinitesimal voter, the continuum limit becomes trivial: that voter has zero electoral influence. I think the type-level bloc is the honest repair, because it preserves the paper’s underlying question—whether one strategic action is robustly better than another—while giving that action a nonzero mass effect.

A secondary modelling question is whether level-1 menus should be supplied as part of the type, as in the paper’s GS-game, or recomputed from a continuum notion of pivotality. I would retain the menus as explicit cognitive-type data for this mirror. Recomputing them for infinitesimal voters would change the cognitive model before testing its complexity, whereas the proposed problem isolates exactly the population continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is an agency mismatch. Theorem 9 concerns one focal voter choosing one ballot, while every other voter independently chooses one action from an allowed menu. The focal voter’s ballot matters because that individual vote is potentially pivotal.

A literal continuum destroys that object. A representative voter has measure zero, so changing their ballot changes no candidate’s score; every ballot becomes outcome-equivalent, and the strict-improvement clause in weak dominance disappears. That is a genuine degeneration, not merely a technical issue.

The proposed repair gives the focal type mass \(\rho>0\) and requires the entire type to coordinate on one ballot. But this is no longer the paper’s level-2 voter. It is a coalition, party, or constituency acting as a single strategic player. High multiplicity makes agents interchangeable; it does not make them coordinate. If the focal type is allowed to split its mass across ballots, then the object being compared is no longer an individual strategy \(A\) versus \(B\), but two aggregate allocations. If it is not allowed to split, the model has added a coordination institution that the paper never studies.

Theorem 9’s reduction reinforces this concern. Each manipulator corresponds to a different set in the Exact-Cover instance and has a menu encoding that particular set. The combinatorial players are therefore distinct cognitive/menu types. Repeating them creates a large electorate, but does not make the original focal voter non-negligible. Merging them into common types destroys the set-specific information. The proposed mass-splitting model is consequently a new fractional coalition game, not simply the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s game.

The LP argument is nevertheless mathematically sound conditional on accepting that new model: winner comparisons are linear, and fractional mass allocations can be tested by LP. But that is not a negative argument; it is precisely a possible Class A result. Nor does it rescue the mirror’s identity. The same objection applies to the improving-strategy variant: it is either trivial for an infinitesimal individual or a collective-action problem for a positive-mass bloc.

Thus the negative case can reject the literal individual-voter mirror, and can reasonably demand that the paper’s authors regard coordinated bloc voting as a substantive extension rather than a population replacement. It cannot honestly establish the requested universal conclusion. A party, union, constituency, or homogeneous electoral bloc with a common ballot is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, and the resulting fractional weak-dominance problem is recognizably related to Theorem 9. The proponent’s anchor therefore survives, provided it is labelled as a coordinated type-level or coalition version rather than as a literal continuum of individual level-2 voters.
