What the mirror covers

The proposed mass formulation targets Theorem 2; no result survives as a continuous mirror, and the simulations and other claims are not covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of Theorem 2, the paper’s algorithmic result. Printed as “(Informal),” it says that Algorithm 1 converges to a local maximal value under weak Pareto optimality and the existence of a stable coalition. The paper proves this in §IV-A: its argument is that accepted switches make the coalition value non-decreasing until the algorithm reaches a stable partition. This is proved in the paper, though informally. It is an algorithmic anchor, not a theorem that the problem is in P or has a formal complexity classification; the paper’s complexity discussion appears separately in prose.

A plausible regime is a large deployment of similar IoT devices: for example, many smart meters or environmental sensors with the same data-generating profile, privacy policy, data contribution, participation cost, and availability. A type is the complete bundle of those attributes that affects participation or payoff. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) types present, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of devices of type \(t\). The intended regime has many devices but relatively few supported types. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that IoT populations are always homogeneous.

Here is a precise candidate question, Continuous MAJP Stable Coalition. Its input is a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a number \(K\) of coalition groups, and binary-encoded parameters for each type: its data type and marginal contribution, sample volume, privacy preference, dissimilarity, and relevant costs. The input also gives finite descriptions of the pricing, coalition-return, and opportunity-cost functions used in the paper’s objective, along with the budget and dissimilarity thresholds.

Choose coalition mass vectors \(z^1,\ldots,z^K\), where \(z_t^k\) is the fraction of the whole society of type \(t\) assigned to group \(k\), with
\[ \sum_{k=1}^{K} z_t^k=\mu_t. \]
Choose \(a_t^k\in[0,1]\), the fraction of that type’s mass in group \(k\) that participates, and a per-capita price \(p_t^k\geq 0\). The participating devices still trade as whole devices; \(a_t^k\) is a cohort participation rate. Maximize the mass-weighted extension of the paper’s coalition value:
\[ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T} z_t^k a_t^k \left[ p_t^k(n_t)+f_{z^k}(\phi_t,a^k) -p_t^k c_{\Delta\phi_t}(z^k)-c(z^k) \right]. \]
Require the paper’s budget, individual-rationality, opportunity-cost, and average-dissimilarity constraints in type-mass form. A solution is a feasible mass partition, participation rates, and prices attaining the maximum, with no feasible type-mass switch between groups that improves the coalition value while respecting the same constraints. Its decision version asks whether such a solution has value at least a given rational threshold.

This retains the paper’s computational question: how to form coalitions and set participation and prices to improve data-market value while controlling leakage and costs. It changes the population representation, as intended, and makes participation fractional at the type level. In a deployment of many similar devices, that is a sensible object; it does not require any individual device to be divisible. The paper’s authors could recognize the formulation as their coalition-value and stable-switch problem.

I would tentatively expect Class A for the natural subfamily where the encoded coalition returns and constraints yield a concave, piecewise-linear mass optimization problem. The paper already assumes concavity for \(f_S\), and the continuum makes cohort participation and coalition composition mass variables. That points toward convex-optimization methods rather than enumerating device-level partitions. The key open technical question is whether the paper’s full value expression and stability conditions actually preserve the needed concavity, and whether the implicit search over coalition compositions has an efficient pricing or separation procedure. Without that, tractability is only a hypothesis.

This case covers Theorem 2 and the underlying coalition-formation objective. It does not treat the paper’s simulation results as complexity results, nor does it continuize outcomes, probability, or time. There is no hardness anchor here from which to claim that hardness transfers.

The weakest point is that the paper’s proof is about discrete switches among named devices and gives only an informal local-convergence guarantee. It does not establish existence, convergence, or tractability for the mass problem above. Also, genuine IoT deployments may have too many distinct types once every relevant privacy preference, price, cost, and availability difference is included. Those are substantial questions for the mirror, but they do not erase the plausible large-fleet regime or the close match between the proposed mass problem and the paper’s central coalition-formation task.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a weak anchor for a computational mirror. It is an informal claim about Algorithm 1 reaching a local maximum, not a complexity result. Its proof does not establish the key step: accepted device moves are said to make the *aggregate* coalition value non-decreasing, but the paper does not show that individual preference improvements imply this. Even granting that step, the inequality is non-strict, so finite many partitions do not rule out cycling among equal-value states. The argument also assumes a stable coalition exists. That leaves little established algorithmic content for a continuum theorem to inherit.

The proposed mass formulation then faces a choice. If devices of each type must stay together, the states are still discrete assignments of types to groups; replacing counts by proportions does not create the continuous coalition problem proposed. If type mass can split among groups, the finite-switch argument no longer applies. In an atomless society, one device has no effect on coalition value or price. A deviation that matters must move a positive mass in coordination. The proponent’s “no feasible type-mass switch improves total value” condition is therefore a new blocking rule, not the paper’s device-level preference or switch condition. Defining which masses may coordinate, how coalition surplus is shared, and what stability means would be new model design.

There is also a tension between the paper’s central externality and a small type space. Coalition costs depend on pairwise correlation terms \(g_{ij}\). A complete type that preserves each device’s relevant relations may be unique to that device, making \(\tau\) grow with the population. A fixed number of types becomes plausible if correlations are exchangeable within a few blocks, but that is an additional symmetry assumption; the paper does not establish that its market setting has this structure. Nor does its assumption that \(f_S\) is concave in data-type information establish concavity in coalition masses. The tractable subfamily in the proposed mirror is imposed, not derived.

So I would not treat Theorem 2 as evidence that this paper already contains a promising continuous computational problem. The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror changes both the deviation model and the stability concept, while the original theorem supplies no sound complexity guarantee to motivate that change. This does not prove the universal claim: a block-exchangeable IoT fleet and a carefully defined atomless coalition game could be a coherent, interesting project. But that would be a new continuum game, not a continuization of the result Theorem 2 actually establishes.
