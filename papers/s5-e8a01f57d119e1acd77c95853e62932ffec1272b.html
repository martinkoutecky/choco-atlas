The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor check yields no qualifying named result. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition that classifies a computational problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. Section 4.4’s “Numerical Propositions” are simulation-based regression predictions, not numbered propositions. The Jackson–Rogers stochastic-dominance result is cited from outside the paper; the authors test its prediction rather than prove a complexity result.

The strongest substantive support for a mirror is Table 4: its pairwise comparisons find that when hamlet \(I\)’s degree distribution first-order stochastically dominates \(J\)’s, \(I\) is less likely to have a higher information-ranking error rate, with the reverse pattern when \(J\) dominates \(I\). The direction appears for both consumption and self-assessment measures and in the simulated outcomes. That is a paper finding, but it is not a qualifying anchor under your rule.

A concrete candidate for a continuation would be Finite-Type Continuum Information Aggregation. Consider a large population divided among \(\tau\) complete types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\). A type records the relevant social role, contact behavior, initially known facts, and forgetting rate. Give the instance a symmetric rational contact-rate matrix \(\kappa_{tu}\), a transmission probability \(p\), a forgetting rate \(\delta\), a finite set \(A\) of poverty facts, and a finite horizon \(H\). Contacts between types \(t\) and \(u\) occur at rates determined by \(\kappa_{tu}\); an informed person can pass each fact to a contact with probability \(p\); nonpersistent facts are forgotten at rate \(\delta\). Let \(x_{t,S}(h)\) be the fraction of type \(t\) that knows exactly the subset \(S\subseteq A\) after time \(h\). These fractions evolve by the induced type-level infection and forgetting rates.

For rational comparison weights \(\omega_{t,ab}\), define the predicted ranking error at time \(H\) as
\[ E_H=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{\{a,b\}\subseteq A}\omega_{t,ab} \left(1-\sum_{S\supseteq\{a,b\}}x_{t,S}(H)\right). \]
This uses the paper’s convention that a ranker errs if they lack either fact needed to compare a pair. The computational question is: given this instance and rational \(\varepsilon>0\), return \(\widehat E_H\) with \(|\widehat E_H-E_H|\le\varepsilon\). The state distribution \(x\) is the evolving quantity; the requested output is the population-level error, not an encoding.

A plausible regime is a census-sized municipality or program area with many households but a few recurring contact and information types, so \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\) and masses express percentages of residents. For fixed \(|A|\), bounded rates, and fixed \(H\), I would expect additive approximation to be tractable: the state has \(\tau 2^{|A|}\) coordinates and can be evolved numerically. That is a conjecture for a new problem, not a result of this paper. The candidate covers model-based information aggregation and the error outcome behind Tables 4 and 5; it does not cover the paper’s empirical regressions or targeting experiment as causal-inference problems.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a continuous-population version of their transmission-and-forgetting model. The weakness is substantial: their paper emphasizes local graph structure and explicitly avoids mean-field approximation. Replacing individual networks with a type-level contact matrix can erase path dependence, clustering, and local information patterns that they consider central. The paper’s villages also average only about 53 households, so the high-multiplicity regime comes from a different application. Thus there is a reasonable model-extension case, but no anchor-compliant case tied to a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the missing anchor: the paper states no computational problem whose complexity or algorithms it studies. Table 4 is its clearest paper-specific result, but it is an empirical comparison testing a prediction from Jackson and Rogers (2007). Tables 5–7 report further empirical and simulation-based associations. None asks for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm. So the proposed continuation is not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The finite-type SIS proposal also loses what makes Table 4’s question specific. Table 4 compares networks by stochastic dominance of their degree distributions. The proposed contact-rate matrix \(\kappa_{tu}\) does not preserve that network comparison; it replaces each network with a type-level diffusion model. That replacement matters here: the paper expressly keeps local graph structure, and its Table 3 finds that reachability and distance to the particular households being ranked predict accuracy. An ODE over type masses does not retain those paths.

A more faithful population limit runs into a sharper problem. The facts being transmitted concern particular households: the nine sampled households’ individual wealths. In a continuum, each household has zero mass. With bounded contact rates and finite horizon \(H\), a fact seeded at one household reaches a vanishing fraction of the population as the population grows. The continuum state for that fact therefore degenerates. To avoid that, one could seed each fact across positive mass, give the tagged household exceptional broadcasting power, or let the number of facts grow with population size. Each choice changes the model substantially. Positive-mass seeds make the information about a household initially common; exceptional broadcasting changes the transmission process; growing the fact set removes the fixed-dimensional state that made the proposed computation tractable.

The better type model would include each household’s relevant network position, neighborhood, and wealth information. In the paper’s sparse, locally clustered networks, that refinement can require retaining enough of the graph to recover the paths information follows. If the type space expands to preserve those details, the finite-\(\tau\) ODE no longer provides the claimed compression. If types instead share poverty categories and contact profiles, the model can sensibly study how information about categories spreads, but it no longer measures whether villagers can rank particular households or select a list of named beneficiaries.

There is a possible mass-level targeting problem here: for example, optimizing an intervention over recurring household types in a much larger program. That could be worthwhile. But it would add a new intervention choice and a new population model; the paper’s randomized comparison of targeting methods does not itself supply that computational question. Likewise, the proposed fixed-\(|A|\), finite-\(H\) error calculation looks like numerical evaluation of a standard finite-dimensional diffusion system, rather than a complexity question emerging from the paper’s results.

This is a strong case against the proposed mirror, but it does not establish the universal claim that no scenario could justify one. A large program with repeated types could support a useful typed diffusion or intervention problem. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and its most plausible continuation either smooths away the local, household-specific information it studies or introduces a different research problem.
