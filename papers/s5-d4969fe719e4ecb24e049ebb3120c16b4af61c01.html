The continuous mirror question

Given colleges, resources and their regions, rational per-capita seat and resource caps, and a finite set of student types with rational masses, complete preferences over college-resource bundles and unmatched status, and a priority tier at each college, compute a feasible allocation \(x_{t,c,r}\) and unmatched mass. Require that no positive mass can improve to a desired contract by displacing lower-priority mass at that college while preserving feasibility when the desired resource is the incumbent’s resource or the empty resource; and require every feasible positive-mass improvement without displacement to be dominated, meaning that carrying it out creates a direct-envy block that was absent before.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population \(\mu\) over complete preference and priority-tier types; individuals still receive integral bundles, while \(x_{t,c,r}\) records the fraction of each type receiving each bundle. Seat and regional resource caps scale per capita, and the task is to find any feasible direct-envy-stable allocation.

The objection that survived

Priority tiers create ties that can change which assignments block and which waste is dominated under the paper’s strict-priority definitions; the mass version needs explicit tie-aware rules.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns Proposition 6’s construction of direct-envy-stable outcomes. It leaves the other stability guarantees and impossibility results, strategy-proofness results, and simulation comparisons aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every such tiered-priority mass market admit a direct-envy-stable allocation?
  • Can one be computed in time polynomial in the numbers of colleges, resources, and types and the rational input bit length?
  • Which precise positive-mass exchange and domination rules preserve the intended blocking notion?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a continuous version of the paper’s direct-envy-stable matching problem. The anchor is Proposition 6, proved in this paper: the three cutoff mechanisms IRC, IMC, and IDC always produce a direct-envy-stable matching, and each runs in \(O(N^3)\) time, where \(N=\max\{|S|,|C|,|R_0|\}\). This is an algorithmic, polynomial-time anchor; the paper has no numbered NP-hardness result of its own. Its cited NP-completeness result concerns checking stable-matching existence under the related regional-caps model, so it is not an anchor for this RRC mirror.

A plausible regime is a large centralized college-admissions cohort allocating seats and shared housing across programs or campuses. Suppose students choose from a standardized menu that yields a relatively small number \(\tau\) of preference and eligibility profiles, with \(n\gg\tau\). A type records a student’s complete ranking of college-resource bundles and unmatched status, together with their priority class at each college. Colleges have strict priorities over types; people within a type are interchangeable. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types. College seat quotas and regional resource caps are expressed as fractions of the cohort, so they scale with population size. That scaling is part of the matching model: each student still receives at most one college-resource bundle, and fractional \(x_{t,c,r}\) means a fraction of type \(t\) receives that whole bundle.

I would call the problem RRC Direct-Env y-Stable Population Matching. An instance gives finite sets of colleges and non-empty resource types, their regions, rational type masses summing to one, each type’s ranking, each college’s priority order over types, and per-capita seat and resource quotas. A solution is a feasible allocation \(x_{t,c,r}\) plus unmatched mass for each type. It must satisfy the college and regional caps and assign no type to an unacceptable contract. The task is to find any such allocation satisfying the paper’s direct-envy-stability condition; there is no welfare objective in Proposition 6, so adding one would change the problem.

For stability, use the paper’s blocking definitions with mass exchanges. A direct-envy block exists if some positive mass of a type can be moved from a less-preferred current assignment to a desired contract by replacing the same positive mass of lower-priority students at that college, while preserving feasibility; for a direct block, the desired resource must be the displaced students’ resource or the empty resource. A waste block exists if positive mass can move to a preferred contract without displacing anyone and feasibility is preserved. Every waste block in a solution must be dominated in the paper’s sense: resolving it would make another contract directly envy-blocking. No direct-envy block may remain. Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the type-level allocation is fractional.

I would tentatively put this mirror in Class A. Proposition 6 gives a constructive mechanism, and the paper’s cutoff approach suggests a route to an algorithm whose work depends on the number of types and the rational input size rather than on the number of students. With finite support, changes in which types are assigned should occur at finitely many type-mass breakpoints or quota-binding points. That is a plausible starting point for an exact polynomial-time algorithm in \(|C|,|R|,\tau\), and bit length \(L\), but the paper does not prove such a continuous result. The prover would need to establish existence under type-level priority ties, give the algorithm and its bit complexity, and show that its output meets the mass-based stability definition. A further question is whether stable allocations of large replicated finite markets converge to solutions of this problem.

This mirror covers Proposition 6’s construction of direct-envy-stable outcomes. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s simulations, strategy-proofness claims, or every other stability notion. The paper’s own admissions and shared-dormitory setting makes the population interpretation credible; scaling quotas with the cohort is a natural co-modification.

The weak point is priority compression. The paper gives each college a strict ranking of individual students. If those priorities are genuinely idiosyncratic, recording them in each student’s type can make \(\tau\) grow almost as fast as \(n\), defeating the high-multiplicity benefit. This case therefore relies on settings with shared priority classes and interchangeable people within each class—plausible in some admissions regimes, but a real restriction on the paper’s strict-priority model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 6 is a real computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time construction of direct-envy-stable matchings. I can’t dismiss it on the ground that the paper has no algorithmic result, or that a continuous answer might be easy.

The strongest objection is that the proposed compression loses information the anchor’s stability test uses. Each college has a strict ranking of individual students, and direct envy depends on whether one student outranks another. Even at a single college, every student therefore has a distinct priority position. A type that preserves all those comparisons must encode that position, so in the paper’s stated model the support can grow with the population: \(\tau\) need not be moderate. Replacing individual ranks with shared “priority classes” fixes the support problem, but it introduces ties. Then students in the same class cannot displace one another under the paper’s strict comparison, changing which contracts block and which wastes are dominated.

There are plausible repairs. Admissions systems can use genuine priority tiers, and the authors might recognize direct-envy stability with tied priorities as a natural extension. A continuum of priority scores is another option, though it needs a different input model than a finite support of rational type masses. Neither repair makes the mirror worthless; each leaves a computational problem to formulate and solve. The proposed mass version also needs a precise rule for the amount exchanged in a block and for when resolving a waste creates a new direct-envy block. Those choices matter, but they are modelling work, not a decisive obstruction.

So the negative case rests on a real limitation: Proposition 6’s most faithful high-multiplicity regime is hard to obtain while preserving strict individual priorities. It does not defeat the better tiered-priority version. Since that version is plausible and could yield a meaningful mass-allocation problem, I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
