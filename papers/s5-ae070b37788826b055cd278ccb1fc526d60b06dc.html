The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), per-institution external cash flows \(e_a\), and nonnegative rational exposure densities \(\ell_{ab}\) from debtor type \(a\) to creditor type \(b\), define \(\tau_a=\sum_b\mu_b\ell_{ab}\). Under proportional allocation, compute the per-type payments \(p_b\) satisfying \(p_b=\min\{\tau_b,\ e_b+\sum_{a:\tau_a>0}\mu_a\ell_{ab}p_a/\tau_a\}\), assuming every positive-exposure risk orbit has positive mass-weighted external cash flow.

The model it lives in

An atomless population distributed over finitely many institution types, each with its own external cash flow and exposure template. The decision variables are per-type clearing payments \(p_a\); there is no optimization objective, and the task is to compute the fixed point of the proportional-clearing equations under the stated regularity condition.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers regular-network clearing and fictitious-default computation for proportional allocation over cohort exposure templates. It leaves arbitrary named-counterparty topologies, other bankruptcy rules, and irregular networks outside its algorithmic claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which explicitly represented strongly monotonic bankruptcy rules beyond proportional allocation admit polynomial-time exact computation for finite-support cohort networks?
  • What computational guarantees remain for finite-support cohort networks that are irregular and may have multiple clearing vectors?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Lemma 3, proved in this paper as a generalization of the Eisenberg–Noe fictitious default procedure. It says that for a regular financial structure and a strongly monotonic bankruptcy rule, the sequence \(p^0=\tau,\ p^j=f(p^{j-1})\) decreases to a clearing payment vector in at most \(n\) iterations. Here \(f(q)\) is obtained by holding at full payment the agents who are not in default under \(q\), then finding the unique fixed point for the remaining agents. This is a genuine algorithmic result, though not a polynomial bit-complexity theorem: its finite iteration bound counts calls to \(f\), whose cost the paper does not analyze.

A concrete population mirror is Finite-Support Continuum Clearing. The instance has a finite set of institution types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_a>0\) summing to \(1\). A type completely specifies an institution’s per-institution external cash flow \(e_a\), liability pattern, and bankruptcy rule. Let \(\ell_{ab}\) be the exposure density from a type-\(a\) debtor to type-\(b\) creditors: with type-\(b\) mass \(\mu_b\), the debtor’s total obligation to that cohort is \(\mu_b\ell_{ab}\). Its total obligations per institution are \(\tau_a=\sum_b\mu_b\ell_{ab}\). For the proportional rule, a type-\(a\) debtor paying \(p_a\) gives each type-\(b\) creditor \(\rho_{ab}(p_a)=\ell_{ab}p_a/\tau_a\) when \(\tau_a>0\).

The requested solution is the vector of per-institution payments \(p\) satisfying

\[ p_b=\min\left\{\tau_b,\ e_b+\sum_a\mu_a\rho_{ab}(p_a)\right\} \quad\text{for every }b. \]

There is no optimization objective: the task is to find the clearing payments, with each payment capped by obligations and determined by the institution’s incoming cash. Require the type-level liability graph to be regular: every risk orbit \(O(a)\), following positive exposures, has positive aggregate external cash flow \(\sum_{b\in O(a)}\mu_b e_b>0\). The output is the exact rational payment for each type.

This is a population limit with a direct high-multiplicity interpretation. In a finite realization with \(N\) institutions and \(n_b\) institutions of type \(b\), take \(\mu_b=n_b/N\) and let a type-\(a\) institution’s claim on each type-\(b\) institution be \(\ell_{ab}/N\). Repeating the types then gives the stated aggregate obligations and receipts. A plausible regime is a large clearinghouse or payment network with many institutions following a finite catalog of balance-sheet and exposure templates: \(N\) might be in the hundreds of thousands while the number of templates \(\tau\) is in the tens or hundreds. Those figures are illustrative, not a claim about a particular network.

The resulting problem is to compute this type-level clearing vector. For proportional allocation, each fixed-default-set subproblem in the lemma’s procedure is a rational linear system. The lemma supplies at most \(\tau\) outer iterations; solving each system by exact rational linear algebra gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\), independent of \(N\). That complexity conclusion is my inference for this explicit mirror, not a complexity theorem stated in the paper. I expect this version to be tractable (Class A). The paper’s broader monotonicity result also suggests asking which other explicitly represented bankruptcy rules make each subproblem efficiently computable.

This mirror covers the paper’s regular-network clearing and finite fictitious default computation under strongly monotonic rules, with proportional allocation as the clean algorithmic case. It does not attempt to mirror every discussion of other rules or extensions. Natural next questions are whether efficient computation persists for other strongly monotonic rules, and what can be computed on irregular networks where clearing payments need not be unique.

The weakest point is the network abstraction. Real bilateral liabilities can distinguish institutions that otherwise look alike; if those named counterparties must be part of an institution’s type, then the number of types may grow with \(N\), and the high-multiplicity benefit disappears. The proposed regime instead assumes exposures are to institution cohorts or pools. That is a substantive modeling choice, but it preserves the paper’s central clearing question—how payments and defaults interact under a bankruptcy rule—rather than making the task easy by changing it to a different allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Lemma 3. It is an algorithmic result: the fictitious default procedure reaches a clearing vector in at most \(n\) outer iterations, although the paper does not analyze the cost of computing each iteration. So “there is no computational result to mirror” would be wrong. The proposed proportional-rule mirror also has a sound tractability argument: for a fixed default set, the equations are rational linear equations, and the number of outer iterations can be bounded by the number of types \(\tau\).

The strongest objection is that a financial institution’s type must capture its role in the liability network, not just its balance sheet or its aggregate exposure to broad cohorts. For an arbitrary liability matrix, preserving that role can require distinguishing institutions by their counterparties and network position. Then \(\tau\) may grow with the population, and the proposed compression brings no high-multiplicity benefit. Keeping \(\tau\) small requires strong symmetry: institutions of a type must have the same exposure pattern to each type. The finite realization in the proposed mirror achieves this with pool-like, broadly distributed claims. That is a coherent model, but it restricts the liability topology that the paper’s theorem is designed to address.

A more general continuum network could retain heterogeneous exposures through a liability kernel over a continuum of institutions. That raises a different computational problem: the finite-support type equations and Lemma 3’s \(\tau\)-iteration argument do not by themselves give an algorithm or a complexity bound for that model. Approximating it with finitely many cohorts would also need an error analysis before the finite-type result could stand in for the original network.

This is the best case against the anchor: the mirror’s exact and efficient version covers a symmetric, pool-exposure regime, while preserving arbitrary network structure seems to forfeit finite-type compression. But the case does not establish that the symmetric regime is unworthy. A fixed-template network can have many institutions, a small number of exposure types, and a type-level risk graph that retains the paper’s regularity and clearing question. That is a plausible population model, and the proposed algorithm appears to handle it. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in that scenario; the negative case is weakest precisely where the advocate’s more careful construction is strongest.
