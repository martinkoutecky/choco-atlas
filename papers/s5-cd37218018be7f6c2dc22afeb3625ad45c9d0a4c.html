The continuous mirror question

Given finite complete agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), good types \(J\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), binary valuations \(v_{tj}\), and a symmetric block-adjacency relation \(W\), decide whether there are finite-support rational bundle distributions \(x_{t,\mathbf b}\) over integral bundles \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\) such that \(\sum_{\mathbf b}x_{t,\mathbf b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,\mathbf b}x_{t,\mathbf b}b_j=q_j\), goods are allocated only to valuers, and \(v_t\cdot\mathbf b\ge v_t\cdot\mathbf b'\) for every \(W_{tu}=1\) and every bundle \(\mathbf b,\mathbf b'\) in the respective supports.

The model it lives in

A population of repeated roles with masses \(\mu_t\), a block network \(W\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\); each person receives an integral bundle, while \(x_{t,\mathbf b}\) records the mass of role \(t\) receiving that bundle. Feasibility requires exact supply use, non-wastefulness, and local envy-freeness across every linked pair of roles.

The objection that survived

The block relation makes each linked pair of cohorts mutually adjacent, so local envy-freeness imposes comparisons across every bundle in those cohorts; this is a narrow fit to the paper’s motivating picture of individual friendships, though the proposed organizational-role scenario partly answers that concern.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s E-GEFA existence question for replicated role classes with block networks; it does not establish that the theorem’s NP-hardness transfers, and it leaves the other E-GEFA hardness results and the proportionality results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Continuous Block-EGEFA have a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of roles, good types, and input bit length, or is it computationally hard?
  • Can its bundle-distribution formulation be solved through an efficiently priced configuration LP?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s graph envy-free allocation problem. It needs a block-network setting: many people share each role in the social network, and goods scale with the population. That is a natural regime for large organizations allocating benefits across cohorts whose members compare allocations within and between departments.

My hardness anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that deciding whether there is a locally envy-free, Pareto-efficient allocation is NP-complete even when agents have binary valuations and there are only two distinct valuation vectors. The reduction is from CUTTING \(\ell\) VERTICES, whose NP-hardness the paper cites from [11]. The theorem’s “two agent types” means two valuation types. For ChoCo’s stricter notion of type, network role must also count: agents with different patterns of social ties are not interchangeable.

I would hand a prover this lead problem, Continuous Block-EGEFA. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(J\) of good types with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), binary additive valuations \(v_{tj}\), and a symmetric relation \(W\) on \(T\). The relation specifies the network: if \(W_{tu}=1\), every member of type \(t\) is connected to every member of type \(u\); a diagonal entry specifies whether distinct members of the same type are connected. Thus a type includes both its valuation and its role in the block network.

Each good copy remains indivisible, and each person receives an integral bundle \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\). A solution is a finite-support distribution of bundles \(x_{t,\mathbf b}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), where \(x_{t,\mathbf b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(\mathbf b\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{\mathbf b}x_{t,\mathbf b}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,\mathbf b}x_{t,\mathbf b}b_j=q_j\ \text{for every }j. \]
Goods must go only to agents who value them, and every good type is assumed to have a valuer. This gives completeness and non-wastefulness, hence Pareto efficiency under the paper’s binary-valuation assumption. Finally, for every \(t,u\) with \(W_{tu}=1\), and every two bundles in the supports of their distributions,
\[ v_t\cdot\mathbf b\ \ge\ v_t\cdot\mathbf b'. \]
The question is whether such a distribution exists. The condition compares an agent’s utility for their own integral bundle with their utility for every neighbor’s bundle, just as graph envy-freeness does in the paper.

The intended regime is a population of \(N\) people with \(N\mu_t\) members of each type and \(Nq_j\) copies of each good type. For a fixed role template, \(N\) can grow far beyond \(|T|\). A rational solution distribution can be realized in a sufficiently large finite blow-up of that template. Mass describes how many people receive each bundle; it does not make any person’s bundle fractional. This preserves the problem the authors study—local envy-freeness and Pareto efficiency—while asking what changes when each network role is repeated many times.

My tentative expectation is Class A: allowing each cohort’s mass to split among integral bundles may dissolve the per-person selection that drives discrete hardness. A route worth testing is to express the bundle distributions as a configuration LP, with pricing over bundles constrained by the binary valuations and envy comparisons. That is a hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 3.

The question also generates a concrete boundary test: does this mass-splitting model admit a polynomial algorithm in the number of roles, good types, and input bit length, or does the arbitrary role graph keep it hard? A useful companion result would characterize when solutions for the continuous block model can be rounded to finite blow-ups with a bounded loss or a controlled integrality gap.

The weak point is substantial. The reduction in Theorem 3 has one network role per constructed agent, so its complete-type support grows with the source graph; its two valuation types do not establish a small number of complete types. Replacing each role by a large cohort changes the instance and lets that cohort divide across bundles. The theorem therefore anchors a plausible continuous question, but it does not show that its NP-hardness transfers—or that the mass relaxation is tractable. My case rests on the block-network regime being a sensible version of the authors’ underlying problem, not on pretending their two valuation vectors already give a two-type high-multiplicity instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real computational anchor, so the strongest grounds for a categorical rejection do not apply. It asks whether a locally envy-free, Pareto-efficient allocation exists and proves NP-completeness. The serious objection is that its “two agent types” are only two valuation vectors. Its reduction has \(2n+1\) agents built from an arbitrary graph, and their network positions generally distinguish them. Under ChoCo’s definition, those positions are part of an agent’s type. The theorem therefore gives no hardness result for a population with a small number of complete types, and its reduction does not identify multiplicity as the source of difficulty.

The proponent’s block-network construction addresses that problem, but at a substantial modeling cost. Repeating each role and connecting every member of one role to every member of an adjacent role makes each person’s neighborhood grow with the population. Local envy-freeness then requires every bundle held by one cohort to compare favorably with every bundle held by each neighboring cohort. That is a coherent allocation problem, but it turns person-to-person social ties into cohort-wide comparisons. It is a narrow fit for the paper’s motivating picture of agents envying their friends, and the paper’s reduction does not supply a reason to privilege this dense network regime.

A sparse-network version would preserve individual friendships more closely, but mass by valuation and role would not record which particular bundles meet along edges. One could add a graph limit or a distribution over local network environments and impose consistency conditions on allocations. That may be a worthwhile project, but it is a more elaborate networked limit model; replacing actual-neighbor comparisons with expected comparisons would change the fairness question. Neither route establishes that the paper’s result has a natural, finite-type continuous mirror.

That is the best case against this anchor: the paper’s hardness result is driven by an arbitrary network whose roles proliferate, while the obvious fixed-type continuation imposes dense, cohort-wide ties. It weakens the claim that Theorem 3 itself motivates ChoCo’s usual mass-over-types framework. It does not, however, defeat the improved block-EGEFA question. Repeated organizational roles with shared cohort-wide comparison relationships are conceivable, and the authors could reasonably recognize that as their problem. The negative case is therefore weak against the user’s universal burden: I cannot honestly conclude that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror.
