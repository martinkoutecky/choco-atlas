What the mirror covers

No result is covered: the theorems characterize hiring rules, and the propositions assess their properties, without computational claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive signal is the setting: a large public-service hiring pool is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Workers could be grouped by shared score profile, protected-group status, and role eligibility; mass would mean their fraction of the eligible pool. A continuous extension of sequential adjusted minority reserves could assign hiring mass across types over a sequence of vacancy rounds.

But this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 2, the closest candidate, says that any rule satisfying static minority rights, static minority fairness, and aggregation independence must produce the same outcomes as sequential adjusted minority reserves for every worker set and hiring sequence. Theorem 3 characterizes when common top, aggregation independence, and permutation independence force a single-priority rule. Both are axiomatic characterizations proved in the paper, not claims about complexity or running time. Propositions 1–4 likewise assess rule properties. None establishes that a named problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

There is also a real weakness in the population story: the paper assumes distinct scores, with tie-breaking folded into a strict ordering. If those distinctions are part of a worker’s complete type, the number of types may grow with the pool. More decisively, a continuous version of Theorem 2 would be an axiomatic extension, not a computational mirror. So the honest positive case is a plausible population regime, but zero qualifying anchors and no per-anchor computational questions. Any algorithmic study of hiring from a type distribution would be a new research direction, not a continuation of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The public-service pool is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Workers could be grouped by score, protected-group status, and eligibility, with mass representing their share of the pool. I would not reject the mirror on the ground that workers must remain individually named.

The decisive problem is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes the unique rule extending static fairness and minority rights while satisfying aggregation independence. Theorem 3 characterizes the rules satisfying common top, aggregation independence, and permutation independence. The other propositions assess rule properties. None states a complexity bound or analyzes an algorithmic problem.

A continuous version of Theorem 2 could characterize mass-valued hiring rules; a continuous version of Theorem 3 could characterize priority rules over types. Those may be coherent questions, but they remain axiomatic characterizations. One could separately ask how to compute a rule’s outcome from a type distribution, but that would introduce an algorithmic problem the paper does not formulate or study. It would be a new research direction, not a computational mirror of either theorem.

So the proponent’s population setting survives, but it does not rescue an anchor. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, the paper offers no result for which a continuous computational mirror can be posed.
