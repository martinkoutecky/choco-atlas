The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of original voter types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s strict ranking of acceptable partner types, and a rational per-capita budget \(B\), is there a redistribution \(z_{t,r}\) of each type’s mass among rankings reachable by adjacent swaps, together with a mass matching, such that the matching is stable and the total swap cost \(\sum_{t,r} z_{t,r} d_K(\succ_t,r)\) is at most \(B\)? Stability means that for every mutually acceptable pair of post-intervention types, the mass on at least one side who are unmatched or prefer that type to their assigned partner is zero.

The model it lives in

Types are original preference profiles with masses \(\mu_t\); post-intervention types are pairs \((t,r)\) with masses \(z_{t,r}\). The decision variables are the mass split across rankings and a matching flow \(x\) between post-intervention types, respecting available mass and acceptability. Each unit of mass receives an integral partner; stability rules out positive mass on both sides of any pair who prefer each other to their assignments. Swap cost is the mass-weighted Kendall distance, bounded by a per-capita budget.

The objection that survived

The mirror replaces strict rankings of named partners and individual blocking pairs with rankings of partner categories and positive-mass blocking; the case gives no evidence that the authors would accept this changed stability convention as their problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the CSR-Swap-\(\exists\)SM result in Theorem 4.6; it leaves the theorem’s other bribery cases and the paper’s remaining action–goal results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fractional mass allocation erase the Independent Set hardness reduction, or does the pairwise stability disjunction preserve a hard choice structure?
  • Can the minimum-cost problem be solved in time polynomial in the number of alternatives, support size, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is the paper’s Theorem 4.6, specifically its result that CSR-Swap-\(\exists\)SM is NP-complete. In full: given a Stable Roommates instance \(I\) and a budget \(\ell\), decide whether at most \(\ell\) swaps of adjacent entries in agents’ preference lists can produce an instance with a stable matching. The paper proves this result; the proof is in Appendix C.5 and reduces from Independent Set. The theorem also establishes three related cases, but I would use only this one.

A plausible setting is a large co-housing exchange. Millions of people enter the market, but many share the same complete profile: the same acceptable partner categories, the same strict ranking of those categories, and the same intervention costs. The input is a distribution \(\mu\) over these finitely many types, with binary-rational masses and support size \(\tau\). The regime of interest has population size \(N\) far larger than \(\tau\). A platform can offer small incentives that move some participants up or down one place in their rankings, and wants to know whether its interventions can make a stable pairing of the population possible.

Call the resulting decision problem Mass-Swap-\(\exists\)SM. Its instance consists of the type set \(T\), the masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s acceptable partner types and ranking, and a rational budget \(B\). For every original type \(t\) and every ranking \(r\) of its acceptable types, let \(z_{t,r}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving ranking \(r\), with \(\sum_r z_{t,r}=\mu_t\). The intervention cost is

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_r z_{t,r}\,d_K(\succ_t,r), \]

where \(d_K\) is Kendall distance, the minimum number of adjacent swaps needed to obtain \(r\).

A solution also specifies a mass matching \(x\) among the post-intervention types \((t,r)\). It must respect their available masses and mutual acceptability. For a post-intervention type \(a\), let \(q_{a\to b}\) be the mass of its members who are unmatched or matched to a type they rank below \(b\). The matching is stable when, for every mutually acceptable pair \(a,b\), \(q_{a\to b}=0\) or \(q_{b\to a}=0\): there cannot be positive masses on both sides who strictly prefer each other to their current assignments. At the type level, this is the natural weak-blocking convention when individuals within a category are interchangeable. The question is whether some rational \(z\) and \(x\) achieve stability within cost \(B\).

The authors should recognize this as their problem in a population regime: the intervention is still adjacent-swap bribery, its cost is still the number of swaps, and the goal is still existence of a stable roommates outcome. The model lets an intervention reach a fraction of a type, just as a high-multiplicity version should; it does not replace population continuity with fractional outcomes alone. It also makes explicit a natural computational question for their setting: can one find the cheapest mass of preference changes that restores stable matching?

I would provisionally expect Class C: hard for continuum-specific reasons. The paper’s NP-hardness does not itself transfer: fractional mass changes might erase the discrete reduction. But stability imposes a disjunction for every type pair—one side’s willing mass must be zero—and those choices can carry combinatorial structure even when masses are divisible. Whether that structure actually yields hardness, or instead admits a polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\), is the question. Further directions include whether fractionalization dissolves the Independent Set reduction, whether a pricing or separation method can avoid enumerating all rankings, and how the mass optimum relates to discrete instances with many repeated agents.

The mirror covers only Theorem 4.6’s Swap-\(\exists\)SM result, not the paper’s other actions or goals. Its weakest point is the move from named individuals and strict rankings over individuals to rankings over partner categories and mass matchings. That is credible for a quota-based large market where both preferences and outcomes are recorded by cohort, but it is not a faithful mirror of a small market whose goal depends on exact named pairs. The case rests on that high-multiplicity regime being one the authors would accept as a natural instance of their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4.6’s NP-completeness result for CSR-Swap-\(\exists\)SM. The strongest case against mirroring it is that named identities are built into both the swap action and the stability test. Each agent has a strict ranking of particular other agents; one swap exchanges two adjacent names on one person’s list; a blocking pair is a particular pair of names. The theorem’s reduction uses that structure to encode an Independent Set instance.

The proposed mass model replaces partner names with partner categories. That is a coherent typed-market model, and the proposed blocking condition makes sense for it. But it changes what a swap does: swapping two categories moves whole groups relative to one another, whereas swapping two named partners changes one person’s list. It also treats everyone in a category as interchangeable when checking for blocks. Keeping the original named-partner rankings avoids that loss, but then identities remain in the type descriptions; a growing market may need a growing type space, and “adjacent” swaps still depend on each finite named list. A continuum of partner identities would need a new definition of adjacency and swap cost.

This is more than the usual change from counting people to measuring population mass. The paper’s conclusion itself lists ties and richer matching models as directions for future work. The proposed mirror may therefore be a worthwhile study of continuous control in a typed matching market, but its connection to this theorem depends on the authors accepting a change in preference and intervention semantics.

That is the best negative case I can make, and it is not decisive. The co-housing scenario is plausible, the mass problem is well-defined, and the goal \(\exists\)SM avoids the paper’s explicitly named-agent objectives. An author might recognize the typed version as a natural population-scale instance of their problem. I would challenge the mirror’s fidelity, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every scenario.
