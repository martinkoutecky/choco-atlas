The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying anchor for a continuous *computational* mirror. Theorem 2.16, proved here, bounds a party’s seat count under the coin-flip outcome: it is within \(3/2\) of the \(k\)-split geometric target and within \(2\) of the geometric target. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says that with geometric constraints the LRY outcome can be arbitrarily far from the geometric target. These are results about fairness of protocol outcomes; neither classifies an underlying problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise computationally complex.

The nearest positive extension would take a voter type to be a grid cell and party affiliation, with mass recording the fraction of the population of each type. A district map would partition the cells into equal-population districts subject to the paper’s contiguity and compactness constraints; its outcome would be the number of seats each party wins and its deviation from the geometric target. A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a very large population distributed across a fixed grid and a relatively small set of cell-affiliation types. The construction behind Theorem 3.2 suggests that an unbounded fairness gap could persist there.

That is a concrete population-continuous extension, but it is not an anchor-based complexity question from this paper. Calling it one would overstate the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 2.16 bounds a fairness deviation, and Theorem 3.2 exhibits a fairness failure. The paper does not analyze the complexity of finding a districting, evaluating the protocol, or computing either bound. That makes this a poor paper to nominate for the programme’s computational-mirror exercise.

For Theorem 2.16, a natural high-multiplicity model can put rational population masses on voter types while keeping the number of districts fixed. But its central calculations already reduce to each party’s total support on either side of a split: the paper gives formulas such as \(\min\{\lfloor 2x\rfloor,k\}\). Replacing counts with scaled masses preserves that arithmetic and its fairness conclusion. Computing the bound from those aggregates is not a new algorithmic problem; it is a continuous restatement of a structural theorem.

Theorem 3.2 offers the more promising route. Grid cell and party affiliation make plausible types, and a large population over a fixed grid is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The example’s bad fairness gap could persist there. But that persistence is still a fairness result. To get a computational question, one must add a task such as optimizing over contiguous, compact district partitions or searching for splits that maximize the gap. With indivisible cells, that is a weighted spatial-partitioning problem. If districts may cut through cells, it requires a further geometric model. Either could be worthwhile, but neither is a computational problem this paper formulates or classifies. The gap’s persistence alone does not supply the missing algorithmic question.

So I would reject both theorems as anchors for a continuous *computational* mirror. That is a solid reason to keep the paper out of this programme’s mirror queue. It does not, however, establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: a continuous, weighted districting problem based on this protocol could be an interesting independent project. The honest negative case is strong on “not a mirror of the paper’s computational results,” and weak on “no scenario could be worth studying.”
