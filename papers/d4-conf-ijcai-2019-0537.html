The case FOR (proponent)

I see a plausible population-level extension, but there is a hard limit on the case I can make: this paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its Tables 1–3 report empirical accuracy and runtime, not complexity classifications. The paper says exact Kendall tau distance is computationally hard and cites [41], but that is an unnumbered statement about an external result. Its claim that finding an optimal outcome is NP-hard is also attributed to [8], and is a different problem. Neither can honestly serve as one of the required anchors. So I cannot present a compliant anchor-based case from this paper.

The strongest candidate I can offer is CP-net Population Transport Distance. Fix a shared feature schema and the paper’s convention of acyclic, minimal CP-nets. A type is a complete CP-net, including its dependency graph and cp-tables. Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be two distributions over these types, with rational masses and supports of sizes \(\tau_\mu\) and \(\tau_\nu\). Mass is the fraction of users in each cohort with each CP-net type. Using the paper’s normalized Kendall tau distance \(d_p(A,B)\) between the partial orders induced by CP-nets \(A\) and \(B\), ask for the minimum-cost coupling:

\[ \min_{x_{A,B}\ge 0}\ \sum_{A,B} d_p(A,B)x_{A,B} \]

subject to \(\sum_B x_{A,B}=\mu_A\) for every \(A\), and \(\sum_A x_{A,B}=\nu_B\) for every \(B\). An instance gives the feature schema, the two supported distributions, and the fixed penalty parameter \(p\). A solution is an optimal rational coupling and its cost.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large product-recommender user base where many users share one of a modest catalogue of CP-net preference templates. The users’ individual preferences and outcomes remain discrete; the continuous object is the distribution of users across those types. The paper already motivates comparing CP-nets as user preferences and discusses recommender systems and social choice, so comparing two user populations by transporting preference mass is a natural extension of its target distance. It covers the paper’s Kendall tau distance task, not the neural architecture or its empirical learning results.

If the pairwise distances are supplied, the coupling is an ordinary polynomial-size linear program. When they must be computed from compact CP-nets, the hard part may instead be the ground-distance calculations. The expected outcome is inherited hardness if exact pairwise KTD is hard: singleton-supported distributions reduce the transport problem to one KTD computation. But this paper does not state a numbered result establishing the complexity class, so that expectation is not a theorem-level conclusion from its text. Further questions include whether small support enables efficient or certified approximate transport, and whether learned distance estimates can yield guarantees for the population-level value.

The weakest point is that population transport is a new cohort-comparison problem, not one the authors formulate. Its connection to their work is the ground metric and its user-preference motivation; a referee could reasonably call it an extension rather than *their* continuous analogue. Without an eligible named anchor, that concern cannot be answered by this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with the paper’s contribution: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Its main results are empirical comparisons of CPMETRIC with I-CPD. The hardness claims in the text are attributed to earlier work and are not results of this paper. So there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous mirror.

The proposed CP-net population transport problem is coherent. A large recommender user base with many users sharing a modest set of preference templates is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; treating a type as the complete CP-net is consistent with the programme’s definition. The weakness is that transport adds a generic aggregation layer to the paper’s pairwise distance. Once the \(\tau_\mu\tau_\nu\) ground distances are available, the problem is an ordinary finite transportation LP. If they are not available, computing them calls the original CP-net distance procedure for each supported pair. That may be a useful cohort-comparison application, but it supplies no result from this paper for the mirror to continue.

A closer population version of the paper’s own task would learn a distance predictor by minimizing expected loss over pairs of CP-net types drawn from a population distribution. That is a legitimate distribution-level question, but with explicit support the expectation is a weighted sum over at most \(\tau^2\) pairs. It reweights the metric-learning data and raises ordinary statistical-learning questions; it does not expose a new population-side computational problem in the paper’s results.

This is a strong case against a result-anchored mirror, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The product-recommender population is plausible, and transport between such populations could be valuable on its own. Calling it worthless would overstate the evidence. The defensible negative conclusion is narrower: the proposed transport problem is an adjacent extension, and this paper offers no named computational result that makes it a compelling continuization target.
