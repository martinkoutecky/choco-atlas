The continuous mirror question

Given a finite task set \(\mathcal J\), service set \(\mathcal S\), robot types \(\mathcal R\subseteq 2^{\mathcal S}\), rational type masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one, and rational service requirements \(q_{js}>0\), compute a feasible allocation or report that none exists. An allocation has masses \(x_{rjs}\ge 0\) assigned from type \(r\) to task \(j\) for service \(s\), and unassigned masses \(x_{r\emptyset}\), with \(\sum_{j,s}x_{rjs}+x_{r\emptyset}=\mu_r\), \(x_{rjs}=0\) when \(s\notin r\), and \(z_{js}=\sum_r x_{rjs}\le q_{js}\). Each assigned unit’s reward is \(\rho_{js}(x)=\exp(1-z_{js}/q_{js})\), and the unassigned role has reward zero. Stability means there is no feasible deviation in which a positive mass of one type changes roles, or equal positive masses of two types exchange roles, and every deviating unit strictly improves its reward after the change.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fleet with a finite menu of complete service-capability types, represented by rational population masses. Decision variables assign each type’s mass to eligible task-service roles or leave it unassigned; task-service loads determine rewards through the paper’s exponential function, scaled by rational service requirements.

The objection that survived

Positive-mass exchanges replace the paper’s two-robot deviations with a block-stability notion, and there is no canonical minimum block size; the proponent acknowledges the atomicity shift, but choosing all positive masses remains a modeling choice.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s pairwise-stability guarantee for Algorithm 2. It leaves aside the separate runtime and communication bounds, empirical utility results, and comparisons with auction baselines.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every feasible instance admit a stable mass allocation under the specified unilateral and pairwise mass deviations?
  • Can one compute such an allocation, or report nonexistence, in time polynomial in the number of tasks, types, and input bit length?
  • How do stable mass allocations relate to stable outcomes of finite fleets as fleet size grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: under the reward function specified by Eq. 4.2 as printed (apparently the preceding Eq. (3)) and \(u_{js}=|S_s|\), Algorithm 2 produces a pairwise-stable solution. The paper defines that stability as the absence of a pair of robots who can benefit by changing coalition membership, individually or together. The theorem is a constructive stability guarantee, not a numbered complexity classification. Section 4.1 gives the pairwise step’s running time in prose and says the overall complexities match GRAPE-S; I would not present those bounds as part of Theorem 1 itself.

A continuous mirror would study pairwise-stable service allocation for a robot population. Let \(\mathcal S\) be a finite service set, \(\mathcal J\) a finite task set, and \(\mathcal R\subseteq 2^{\mathcal S}\) the robot types, where a type records the services a robot can perform. The input gives rational type masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one and rational task-service requirements \(q_{js}>0\), measured as fractions of the population. Let \(x_{rjs}\) be the mass of type \(r\) assigned to task \(j\) to perform service \(s\), and let \(x_{r\emptyset}\) be its unassigned mass. Require type-mass conservation, allow \(x_{rjs}>0\) only when \(s\in r\), and cap each task-service coalition at its requirement, as in Pair-GRAPE-S’s capped implementation. Write \(z_{js}=\sum_r x_{rjs}\).

The per-agent reward in the mirror is
\[ \rho_{js}(x)=\exp\!\left(1-\frac{z_{js}}{q_{js}}\right), \]
the paper’s reward with robot counts scaled by population size and \(u_{js}=|S_s|\). Give the unassigned role reward zero. The question is: find a feasible rational allocation \(x\) with no profitable unilateral mass move or pairwise mass exchange. A deviation moves a positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of one type, or equal masses of two types, between eligible task-service roles and/or the unassigned role; it must preserve feasibility, and every deviating mass must strictly improve its per-agent reward after the move. A solution is the allocation \(x\), together with a certificate that no such deviation exists.

The regime I find convincing is a large, staged fleet of standardized robots: hundreds or thousands of units drawn from a small number of hardware configurations, each with the same service vector and task eligibility. Then \(n\) is large while \(\tau=|\mathcal R|\) is small and independent of \(n\). Mission planning under uniform communication assumptions is the cleanest setting; a model that distinguishes robots by location, battery state, or communication links may have too many types for useful compression. Mass represents a share of each configuration, while each actual robot still receives one integral task-service assignment.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class A, though that is a research hypothesis, not a result. The feasible allocations form a finite-dimensional polytope over types and task-service roles, and the exponential reward comparisons reduce to comparisons of \(z_{js}/q_{js}\). That may permit a type-level separation procedure for deviations. The key open questions are whether such a procedure is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\); whether stable allocations always exist; and whether discrete Pair-GRAPE-S outcomes converge to, or can be rounded from, stable mass allocations. If the pairwise-deviation constraints resist a compact algorithm, the problem could instead be continuum-specifically hard.

The scope is narrow: this mirrors Theorem 1’s stability guarantee, not the paper’s empirical runtime or utility claims. The paper’s statement that ST-MR-IA task allocation is NP-hard cites [3,4], but it is not a numbered result proved here, so I would not use it as an anchor.

The weak point is that pairwise stability is defined for individual robots, whereas a continuous population invites positive-mass deviations. That changes the stability notion, and the paper’s distributed algorithm also depends on robot-to-robot communication absent from this allocation model. The mirror still has a credible connection to the authors’ problem because their service rewards and task constraints are already expressed through counts of robots in task-service coalitions. The mass version asks what their stability result means when those counts are population shares.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1: Algorithm 2 produces a pairwise-stable allocation under the paper’s reward function. It is a correctness guarantee, not a complexity classification; the running-time bound appears separately in prose. That weakens the case for this as a direct complexity mirror, but does not by itself make the algorithmic question disappear.

The obvious high-multiplicity regime is convincing. The paper already studies fleets with hundreds of robots and a fixed menu of service capabilities. A staged fleet of standardized robots could have many units but few capability types, with task requirements scaled as shares. Robot identity is irrelevant to the static allocation and reward calculation in that model. I don’t see a sound argument that this regime is inherently implausible.

The stronger objection is that the theorem’s defining object is atomic: one robot changes roles, changes coalition counts by one, and may create a strict gain across the satisfied/unsatisfied boundary. In an atomless population, an individual or pair has zero mass and cannot change \(z_{js}\), so infinitesimal pairwise stability loses that content. It becomes a Wardrop-style condition. Since \(e^{1-z_{js}/q_{js}}\) ranks roles solely by \(z_{js}/q_{js}\), the uncapped version is a standard separable congestion allocation with a convex potential. Capping roles and allowing positive-mass exchanges preserves more of the paper’s story, but replaces two-robot deviations with mass-block deviations. The paper supplies no natural minimum block size; preserving one would restore the discrete granularity the continuum was meant to remove.

That is a reason to question whether this theorem yields a distinctive complexity problem: the faithful infinitesimal limit sheds pairwise stability, while the positive-mass version changes its meaning and may reduce to standard allocation machinery. But it does not defeat every plausible mirror. The service-type regime is credible, and the mass-exchange variant is close enough to the paper’s allocation model that the authors might recognize it. On the supplied material, the negative case is weak; I would not claim this anchor is conclusively defeated.
