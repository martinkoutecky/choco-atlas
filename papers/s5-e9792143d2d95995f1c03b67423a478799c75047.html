The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible anchor: the supplied text contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Section 5.4 states two unnumbered monotonicity properties; Tables 2 and 3 report pilot outcomes. Neither is a named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires.

The strongest positive candidate is a new mirror of the mediator’s package-selection step in Section 5.2, rather than a mirror of a named result. Call it Continuous Budgeted Negotiative Alignment. An instance has criteria \(K\), base designs with scores \(s^0_{bk}\), and \(q\) optional improvements, each with a binary-rational cost \(c_j\) and nonnegative score increments \(d_{jk}\). It also has a budget \(B\) and a distribution \(\mu\) over stakeholder types \(T\), with support size \(\tau\). Each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) and a normalized weight vector \(w_{tk}\) describing its priorities. A package is a base design \(b\) and a selection \(z\in\{0,1\}^q\) with \(\sum_j c_jz_j\le B\); its score is \(s_k(b,z)=\min\{5,s^0_{bk}+\sum_jd_{jk}z_j\}\). The question is to return a feasible package lexicographically maximizing
\[ \left(\min_{t\in T}\sum_{k\in K}w_{tk}s_k(b,z),\quad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{k\in K}w_{tk}s_k(b,z)\right). \]
The first term raises the floor across supported types; the second rewards average utility across the population.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a municipality choosing upgrades for a corridor affecting many thousands of residents. A type is a complete shared evaluation profile—its weights and any package constraints the model uses—and mass is the fraction of affected residents with that profile. The number of residents could be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles. That scenario is not established by this study’s sample; it is a plausible setting for extending the mediator’s model.

This is recognizable as the paper’s underlying package-choice question: its mediator enumerates budget-feasible packages, computes stakeholder utilities, and maximizes the minimum utility before the sum. Replacing the six equally counted pilot personas with population masses is a defensible co-modification, though it changes the sum into a population-weighted mean. I expect the general problem to be hardness-transferring, not continuum-specifically hard. With one supported type and one criterion, the objective reduces to maximizing capped project gains under a budget; a knapsack instance can be encoded by setting project gains proportional to item values and asking whether the optimum reaches the cap. That is a hardness argument for this proposed generalization, not a result proved in the paper. The pilot’s fixed menu of four upgrades is small; natural follow-ups include complexity in the number of upgrades, parameterization by \(\tau\), and approximation guarantees.

This mirror covers only the mediator’s package-optimization step. It does not cover the study’s empirical findings about disagreement, its ratings and rankings, or the LLM agents’ dialogue and ratification protocol. That is also its weakest point: turning a negotiation among role-played agents into a static population optimization may strip away what the authors regard as central. So the case is credible as a research question motivated by their formal operator, but it cannot be presented as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper-specific case against a mirror is strong: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about the complexity of a computational problem. Section 5.2 applies an enumerating mediator to one small pilot; Section 5.4 gives unnumbered monotonicity properties. Neither studies an algorithm’s complexity or establishes a computational result to continue.

The proposed package optimizer is the best candidate, but it is a new problem family, not a result the paper proves. The paper’s pilot has three designs and four upgrades. Generalizing that menu and adding a population distribution may be useful, but the resulting knapsack-style problem comes from those newly introduced choices. It cannot supply the missing paper anchor.

There is also a substantive change in what the optimizer represents. The six agents are deliberately chosen stakeholder roles, not a sample from a population with known group frequencies; the paper explicitly limits its empirical claims to a small, depth-oriented sample. Weighting the objective by \(\mu_t\) therefore changes the paper’s equal-standing treatment of stakeholder roles. Keeping its minimum-utility objective makes any type with positive mass count equally toward the floor, however small its share. A mass-weighted lower quantile would avoid that feature, but the paper reports its twentieth-percentile metric diagnostically; it does not optimize it.

That defeats the proposed anchor as a mirror of this paper’s computational results. It does not establish that no future urban-planning model could make a worthwhile continuous-population problem: a municipality planning for residents with recurring preference profiles is plausible. Calling that impossible would overstate the negative case. The narrower conclusion is that this paper motivates a possible new ChoCo problem, but does not itself provide the computational result the proposed mirror is supposed to continue.
