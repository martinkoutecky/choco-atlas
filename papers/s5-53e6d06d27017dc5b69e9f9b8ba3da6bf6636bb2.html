The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(U\) and \(V\) with positive rational cross-distances \(d_{ij}\) from a metric, rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\ge 0\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and rational \(\alpha\ge 1\), find a rational coupling \(x\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\nu_j\), such that no \(i,i'\in U\) and \(j,j'\in V\) satisfy \(x_{i,j'}>0\), \(x_{i',j}>0\), and \(\alpha d_{ij}<\min\{d_{i,j'},d_{i',j}\}\). Is there an algorithm polynomial in the number of types \(\tau=|U|+|V|\) and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A two-sided population of metric types with rational masses; the decision variable is a transport matrix \(x\) assigning each unit of mass one whole partner. Find any feasible \(\alpha\)-stable coupling; \(C(x)=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}d_{ij}\) is descriptive and is not optimized by this anchor.

The bipartite question may be settled by a routine type-level deferred-acceptance algorithm, and adjacent continuum matching work already has a deferred-acceptance analogue; this limits novelty, though it does not settle the two-sided metric mass problem ([Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow but credible: the paper’s bipartite metric matching problem has a direct high-multiplicity version. I would use Lemma 4.4 as the anchor. Proved in this paper, it says that Greedy takes a minimum-cost matching in a metric graph, processes the edges in nondecreasing weight order, and flips unstable edges to produce an \(\alpha\)-stable matching in \(O(n^2\log n)\) time. The paper gives no hardness result to mirror; its main price-of-anarchy and price-of-stability theorems are efficiency bounds, not complexity classifications.

Call the mirror Typed-Mass \(\alpha\)-Stable Matching. An instance has two finite sets of types, \(U\) and \(V\), embedded at distinct points in a finite rational metric space; every cross-side distance \(d_{ij}\), for \(i\in U,j\in V\), is positive. Each side has rational type masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\ge 0\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_j\nu_j=1\), and the instance gives a rational \(\alpha\ge1\). The input size is measured by \(\tau=|U|+|V|\) and the binary encoding length \(L\).

The decision variable is a rational matrix \(x\ge0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type-\(i\) agents matched to type-\(j\) agents. It must satisfy \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}=\nu_j\). Each agent still receives one whole partner: mass records how much of a type is assigned to each other type. The search objective, as in Lemma 4.4, is to return any feasible \(\alpha\)-stable matching. Its social cost is \(C(x)=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}d_{ij}\); the anchor does not ask the algorithm to minimize that cost.

Here is the stability condition precisely. There must be no types \(i\in U,j\in V\) and incumbent partner types \(j'\in V,i'\in U\) such that \(x_{i,j'}>0\), \(x_{i',j}>0\), and \(\alpha d_{ij}<\min\{d_{i,j'},d_{i',j}\}\). Those positive assignment masses supply agents on both sides who would strictly gain by matching to one another under the paper’s \(\alpha\)-stability rule. This keeps individual-pair blocking meaningful even though each individual has zero mass in a continuum.

A plausible regime is a large two-sided placement market: many applicants and positions, but only a few standardized location-and-specialty types, with each side preferring closer matches in the same metric. The type must include everything that determines an agent’s costs and preferences. One might have hundreds of thousands of people but only dozens of distinct types. This is a population model: fractional entries in \(x\) describe how cohorts divide across assignments, not fractional partners.

I expect this search problem to be Class A, although the paper does not establish that for the mass input. In the bipartite case, a type-capacitated deferred-acceptance procedure is a plausible route: process rational batches by type, and obtain an ordinary stable mass matching, which is also \(\alpha\)-stable for every \(\alpha\ge1\). The computational question is whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). Expanding the masses into a finite market and running Lemma 4.4 would not suffice: a common denominator can be exponentially large in \(L\). A compressed algorithm is the point of the mirror.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is unusually clean here. If the masses have denominator \(D\), a mass assignment with entries on that grid expands into a matching of \(D\) agents per side, and aggregating such a discrete matching recovers its mass matrix. The blocking condition is also preserved: a positive assignment cell yields agents in a sufficiently large replication. That gives a concrete dictionary between the high-multiplicity discrete problem and the mass problem.

The mirror covers only the construction of an \(\alpha\)-stable matching in the paper’s bipartite metric setting. It does not claim that the paper’s tight price-of-stability trade-off transfers with \(n\) replaced by \(\tau\). A natural follow-up is to minimize \(C(x)\) over stable mass assignments and determine whether the worst-case ratio depends on support size rather than population size.

The weakest point is that this restriction loses the paper’s all-pairs matching setting and its most distinctive efficiency analysis; the bipartite feasibility problem may also be approachable by established deferred-acceptance methods. Still, the mirror preserves the paper’s cost-based preferences, individual blocking notion, metric structure, and whole-partner assignments. That makes it a plausible continuous form of Lemma 4.4, even if its eventual answer is comparatively simple.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that the proposed anchor lands on the paper’s easiest special case. For \(\alpha\ge1\), an ordinary stable matching is already \(\alpha\)-stable. In the bipartite mass model, a type-capacitated deferred-acceptance procedure can make proposals in batches: each type has only \(\tau\) possible counterpart types, and accepted mass moves monotonically toward better-ranked proposals. That points to a direct algorithm polynomial in the number of types and the rational input length, without expanding a large common denominator into individual agents. The continuous question is coherent, but it looks like a compressed form of a classical stable-marriage construction, rather than a mirror of the paper’s flip analysis or its price-of-stability trade-off.

That objection does not rule out a worthwhile follow-up. Minimizing cost among stable mass assignments is a distinct computational question, and the paper’s price bounds could also be restated for mass populations. Those questions are not defeated by observing that the bipartite search problem has an easy algorithm.

The stronger mirror would use the paper’s all-pairs metric setting and ask how the price of anarchy or stability behaves for a distribution over exact metric types. This has a real modeling cost: when most vertices have distinct metric profiles, each is its own type and \(\tau\) grows with the population. A compressed regime needs large groups of exact twins. In a location model that can mean repeated locations; if agents in a group must have positive distance from one another, the model also needs an explicit within-type distance. Such cohorts are more restrictive than general metric populations, but they are mathematically coherent. A metric can contain many vertices with the same distances to every other type and a positive, common distance within the group. Their mass matching problem could still have a nontrivial complexity landscape.

There is already adjacent continuous stable-matching work with an algorithmic deferred-acceptance analogue, including a continuum model with finite capacities. Its market structure does not settle the proposed problem with mass on both sides and the paper’s metric-based stability and efficiency questions, so I would not call it a collision. [A continuum model of stable matching with finite capacities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)

So the negative case can demote the bipartite Lemma 4.4 mirror: it is likely a routine high-multiplicity reformulation with limited reach into the paper’s distinctive results. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The all-pairs mass version survives as a plausible, if more specialized, research question.
