The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) over a finite set \(T\), per-capita supplies \(q_r\) of item classes \(r\in R\), and an adversarial measurable arrival stream containing total mass \(q_r\) of each class, each type \(t\) has a nonnegative, possibly nonmonotone diminishing-returns-submodular utility \(F_t\) on integral bundle-count vectors. Does there exist an efficient deterministic causal mass-flow policy that routes arriving item mass among cohorts of agents while every agent holds an integral bundle, and guarantees final welfare at least \(\frac14\) of the offline optimum for every stream, or can this guarantee be ruled out?

The model it lives in

A society consists of type masses \(\mu_t\); each type-\(t\) agent holds an integral bundle \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{R}\) valued at \(F_t(z)\). Item class \(r\) arrives as mass \(q_r\), and the policy causally transfers agent mass between bundle states or discards arriving items. Maximize \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}F_t(z)\) against the offline optimum under the same supplies, and determine whether a deterministic policy guarantees a \(\frac14\) ratio with an efficient value-oracle implementation.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 may make the \(1/4\) guarantee a direct mass-splitting lift, but the proponent identifies proving that lift and its compact oracle implementation as the task, so this limits novelty rather than killing the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers adversarial online SW at high multiplicity, anchored to Theorem 2 and drawing on Theorem 1; leaves the random-order results in Theorems 3 and 4 and the general matroid approximation analysis aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the randomized strategy behind Theorem 1 be implemented as a deterministic causal cohort flow with a compact value-oracle representation?
  • How does the continuous policy relate quantitatively to finite markets as the number of agents and repeated item copies grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a high-multiplicity version of adversarial online Submodular Welfare. The population is the bidders: for example, a large pool of advertisers falls into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete valuation types. Placements arrive online in a few interchangeable classes, with each class’s supply scaling with the advertiser population. This is plausible when many advertisers share the same diminishing-returns valuation for recurring kinds of inventory.

Here is the model. Let types \(t\in T\) have rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(R\) be a finite set of item classes. Class \(r\) has supply \(q_r\) per unit population. A type-\(t\) agent’s valuation is a nonnegative, possibly nonmonotone submodular function \(F_t:\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{R}\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) of its bundle counts. The adversary gives an arrival stream \(\rho:[0,Q]\to R\), where \(Q=\sum_r q_r\) and class \(r\) occupies total stream mass \(q_r\).

At each arrival, the policy routes the item to an agent or discards it. In a population of \(N\) agents, there are \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_r\) copies of class \(r\); each copy is unsplittable, and every agent holds an integral bundle. The continuous state \(x_{t,z}(s)\) records the mass of type-\(t\) agents holding bundle \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{R}\). A causal policy moves this mass between bundle states as items arrive. Its final per-agent welfare is
\[ W=\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}(Q)F_t(z), \]
compared with the offline optimum using the same supplies. The mass split is across agents holding integral bundles; it does not give an individual a fractional bundle.

I would name the lead question HM-Deterministic Online SW at \(1/4\): for this model, construct a deterministic causal mass-flow policy that guarantees \(W\ge \frac14\mathrm{OPT}\) for every valid arrival stream, or prove that no such policy exists. The policy and its competitive guarantee are the solution. Its efficiency in the value-oracle model is a further part of the algorithmic question.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper in §2.2: “For every \(M>0\), no deterministic algorithm can achieve a competitive ratio better than \(1/M\) for online SW in the adversarial setting with general (not necessarily monotone) utilities.” The proof uses one bidder and two items, extending the utility function according to the deterministic algorithm’s first decision. The theorem establishes that deterministic algorithms have no positive uniform ratio in the finite model.

The continuous question asks whether that barrier survives when many bidders share types and each has a population-scaled supply of arriving items. My expectation is Class A for the \(1/4\) guarantee: a continuum of agents can realize action proportions as deterministic mass flows. The manuscript’s Theorem 1, proved in §2.1, provides a concrete route: it gives a randomized polynomial-time algorithm with competitive ratio \(1/4\) for adversarial online SW with general utilities. A flow lift could route successive copies among type-and-bundle cohorts in the proportions that the randomized algorithm would choose. That would turn its random choices into deterministic aggregate routing. Establishing the lift, especially with a compact value-oracle implementation, is the work the mirror poses.

This covers the adversarial online allocation results around Theorems 1 and 2. It does not try to continuize the random-order analysis in Theorems 3 and 4, or the paper’s general-matroid approximation result. An arrival coordinate here records high-multiplicity supply; the question remains online allocation and welfare.

The weakest point is that Theorem 2’s lower-bound instance has one bidder and two one-off items. It does not itself supply a many-agent regime. The mirror instead asks about repeated bidder types and repeated item classes, a substantive but natural high-multiplicity setting. That is also why the hardness may dissolve: the population can spread risk across agents while each agent still receives whole items. The exact best ratio, whether \(\frac14\) remains tight in this regime, and how the continuous policy approximates finite markets as \(N\) grows are natural next questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a poor anchor for a continuous lower bound because its adversary exploits a single indivisible choice: take the first item or leave it. A population flow can hedge that choice by serving only part of the population. In the two-item example, let \(x\) be the mass that receives the first item. If the second item has value \(0\), the ratio can be \(x\); if it has singleton value \(M\) and conflicts with the first item, the ratio is \(1-x+x/M\). The adversary can choose the worse case, but the best hedge keeps that ratio near \(1/2\) as \(M\) grows. The paper’s \(1/M\) obstruction does not carry over.

The stronger mirror proposed here also looks largely settled by Theorem 1. Under its fluid assumptions, a deterministic flow can route each arriving item mass among type-and-bundle cohorts in the proportions used by the randomized algorithm. Cohort states encode the changing marginal values, and splitting mass implements the algorithm’s random choices in aggregate. The paper’s \(1/4\) proof then supplies the competitive guarantee. Formalizing that lift and its oracle implementation may be useful, but it is unlikely to uncover a new complexity boundary; the advertised deterministic-versus-randomized question is already answered by the paper’s algorithm once randomization becomes mass splitting.

There is a dilemma for richer versions. Keeping arbitrary item-specific valuations while scaling the market makes complete bidder types grow with the item universe, so the support parameter can lose its high-multiplicity advantage. Fixing a small set of repeated item classes and bidder types gives a sensible market model, but makes the flow lift especially direct. This is a reason to doubt the research value of this particular anchor, not a general objection to continuous online allocation.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case stick. The advertiser-and-inventory scenario is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and formalizing the discrete-to-flow correspondence could itself be worthwhile. My strongest case against is narrower: Theorem 2’s hardness does not survive as stated, and the proposed \(1/4\) replacement appears to be a routine lift of Theorem 1 rather than a substantial new computational question.
