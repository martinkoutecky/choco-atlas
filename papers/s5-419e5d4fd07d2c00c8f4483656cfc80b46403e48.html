The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), an explicit support \(T\) of ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), transitively closed precedence constraints \(W\), and a pair \((x,y)\), define \(\Delta_\mu(u,v)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t(\mathbf{1}[u\succ_t v]-\mathbf{1}[v\succ_t u])\) and \(Z=C\setminus(L_y(W)\cup R_x(W)\cup\{x,y\})\). Decide whether \(\Delta_\mu(x,y)>\min_{a\in[0,1]}\sum_{z\in Z}\max(0,a\Delta_\mu(y,z)+(1-a)\Delta_\mu(z,x))\). A yes answer certifies that \(x\) precedes \(y\) in every minimizer of \(K_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_{KT}(\pi,t)\); a no answer only means this test does not certify the pair.

The model it lives in

The society is a rational-mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\). The computation forms pairwise margins from the support, then minimizes the stated piecewise-linear expression over \(a\in[0,1]\) and tests the strict inequality; it takes \(O(|C|^2)\) arithmetic operations after margins are formed, with \(O(|T||C|^2)\) operations to form them.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover αMOT pair certification and exact \(M\)-finest Condorcet partitioning; they do not cover the approximate greedy methods or solve the full Kemeny-median problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether \(x\) precedes \(y\) in every Kemeny median, beyond deciding whether the αMOT test certifies it?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is an election with many respondents ranking the same slate of candidates or proposals. A type is one complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of respondents with that ranking. For a large association or online consultation, one could plausibly have \(10^5\)–\(10^7\) voters but only \(10^2\)–\(10^4\) distinct ranking types. The continuous instance is the explicit support of \(\tau\) rankings with binary rational masses summing to one. Its Kemeny objective is \(K_\mu(\pi)=\sum_t \mu_t d_{KT}(\pi,t)\). This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: agents sharing a type are indistinguishable in the problem’s terms.

Lead: Continuous \(\alpha\)-MOT Certificate Query. Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that verifying a candidate pair \((x,y)\) in the \(\alpha\mathrm{MOT}\)-SinglePair algorithm takes \(O(|Z_k|^2)\) time, where \(Z_k=Z_k(x,y)\) is the interference set defined by the current constraints and \(|Z_k|\leq n-2\). The calculation is the minimum, over \(a\in[0,1]\), of a piecewise-linear sum; Theorem 1 supplies its meaning: when the strict \(\alpha\mathrm{MOT}\) condition holds, \(x\) precedes \(y\) in every Kemeny median. Theorem 2 is proved here, rather than cited.

For the continuous problem, give the prover \(C\), the distribution \(\mu\), transitively closed known precedence constraints \(W\), and an ordered pair \((x,y)\). Define the population margin \(\Delta_\mu(u,v)=\sum_t\mu_t(\mathbf{1}[u\succ_t v]-\mathbf{1}[v\succ_t u])\), and set \(Z=C\setminus(L_y(W)\cup R_x(W)\cup\{x,y\})\). Ask whether
\[ \Delta_\mu(x,y)>\min_{a\in[0,1]}\sum_{z\in Z}\max\{0,\ a\Delta_\mu(y,z)+(1-a)\Delta_\mu(z,x)\}. \]
The solution is an exact yes/no answer to that predicate. A “yes” certifies that \(x\) precedes \(y\) in every minimizer of \(K_\mu\); “no” means this test did not certify the pair, not that the reverse order is forced. The linearity of the Kemeny score in voter weights makes Theorem 1’s guarantee carry over. With the margin matrix supplied, the query takes \(O(m^2)\) arithmetic operations; from the explicit support, computing margins adds \(O(\tau m^2)\), with exact rational bit complexity polynomial in \(L\).

Second anchor: Continuous \(M\)-Finest Condorcet Decomposition. Theorem 8, also proved here, states that Algorithm M-XCC has time complexity \(O(n^2)\) for every input constraint set \(M\) and cumulative ranking matrix \([\delta_{xy}]\), with \(n=|C|\). The algorithm computes the \(M\)-finest Condorcet partition. Theorems 5 and 7 establish the relevant uniqueness and correctness when \(M\) is anti-symmetric and anti-reflexive.

The continuous version takes \(C,\mu\), and such an \(M\), consisting of known precedence constraints compatible with a Kemeny median. Return an ordered partition \(C=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_k\) with the largest possible number of blocks, subject to every earlier/later pair \(x\in X_i,y\in X_j\), \(i<j\), satisfying either \((x,y)\in M\), or \(\Delta_\mu(x,y)>0\) and \((y,x)\notin M\). The partition is the solution. Theorem 8 gives \(O(m^2)\) time on the margin matrix, plus \(O(\tau m^2)\) to form it from the type distribution. This mirrors the paper’s exact decomposition step, not the whole task of finding a Kemeny median.

Both mirrors should be tractable: they are precisely the paper’s pair-certification and decomposition computations with population shares replacing vote counts. They cover the exact preprocessing and partitioning results, not the approximate methods as a whole. A natural next question is whether exact forced-pair detection, beyond the sufficient \(\alpha\)-MOT test, is tractable for rational-mass societies. The broader continuous Kemeny-median problem also embeds every discrete profile: set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and \(K_\mu\) is the discrete score divided by \(N\). So the NP-hardness cited in the introduction transfers to that broader problem. I do not count it as an anchor because this paper states it as cited background, not as a numbered theorem.

The soft spot is that the paper’s algorithms already operate on the cumulative ranking matrix. Turning the electorate into a mass distribution gives a sensible high-multiplicity object, but does not itself produce a new algorithmic advantage or dissolve the core Kemeny hardness. The positive case is narrower: the paper’s central preprocessing and decomposition results have direct, exact population mirrors that make sense for large societies with relatively few ranking types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both proposed anchors have already discarded the population and run on the same sufficient statistic: the cumulative ranking matrix. For a continuous society, set
\[ \Delta_\mu(x,y)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[x\succ_t y]-\mathbf 1[y\succ_t x]\bigr). \]
The \(\alpha\)-MOT test uses only these margins. Its right-hand side scales by the same positive factor as \(\Delta_\mu(x,y)\), so normalizing margins to population shares changes neither the test nor its certificate. Theorem 2’s algorithm already takes the margin matrix as input; the support list merely adds the routine cost of forming it. The proposed yes/no query is therefore the paper’s existing test with a population interpretation. Its “no” answer also certifies nothing about whether the pair is actually forced, so it does not extend the result to exact forced-pair detection.

The M-fine Condorcet partition has the same problem. It depends on the signs of the margins and on \(M\); positive rescaling leaves its output unchanged. Theorem 8 already gives the algorithm on a cumulative ranking matrix, and the paper explicitly says its methods apply to weighted tournaments. Giving the algorithm a rational type distribution instead does not create a population-sensitive computational problem. The proposed \(O(m^2)\) algorithm is the same partition computation after aggregation.

This criticism also applies to a stronger mirror of Theorem 10: its score bound is built from margins and pair-order indicators, so replacing vote counts by rational shares rescales the bound without changing its content. Across these results, the paper already solves the margin-level computations that the continuous formulations ask for. What remains new is primarily the encoding of the electorate as a support list, not an algorithmic question that depends on that support.

That is a substantial objection to these particular anchors, but it does not honestly establish the requested universal negative. A society with many respondents sharing a relatively small set of rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and continuous Kemeny ranking is a coherent problem for it. The brief explicitly treats discrete-to-continuous embeddings and transferred hardness as worthwhile outcomes. So the defensible negative case is narrower: the two named mirrors add little beyond the paper’s already margin-based results. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror of the paper.
