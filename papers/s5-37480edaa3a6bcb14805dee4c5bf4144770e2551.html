What the mirror covers

No result is covered: the cited results characterize allocation methods and their properties, without computational guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: each voter’s type is the party they vote for, \(\mu_i\) is that party’s share of a large electorate, and the decision is to allocate an integer house of \(h\) seats among the parties. Many voters and relatively few parties make this a sensible population model.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror to. Its numbered results are about properties of apportionment methods: for example, (6) characterizes balanced, house-monotone, uniform methods as Huntington methods; (9) characterizes the quota method by house monotonicity, quota consistency, and satisfying quota. Those are axiomatic results, not complexity results or computations of an underlying decision problem. The recursive rule for the quota method does not supply a complexity anchor either.

So I can make a plausible case for the population regime, but I cannot honestly write the required prover-ready continuous problem per anchor. Doing so would mean inventing a computational task around this paper’s axiomatic results, which the requested scope excludes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed electorate of many voters divided among a few party choices is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But in this paper, it does not produce a new continuous mirror: the formal input is already the vector of party vote masses. Writing \(P=\sum_i p_i\) and \(\mu_i=p_i/P\), the quota is \(h\mu_i\), divisor priorities \(p_i/d(a_i)\) are unchanged up to a common factor, and a party merger combines the corresponding masses. The paper’s allocation and coalition questions therefore already operate on the same distribution over party types that the proposed continuization would use. Normalizing the vote totals changes the notation, not the population model.

The paper’s numbered results do not supply a computational anchor. Results such as (6) and (9) characterize apportionment methods through axioms; they do not establish the complexity of a computational problem. The quota method’s recursive definition makes it natural to ask how efficiently it can be computed, but that would be a new algorithmic question about an allocation rule the paper already defines on vote masses. It would not be a computational consequence of making the population continuous.

The strongest repair is to study exact computation for the quota or Huntington methods when party shares are binary rationals, with running time measured in the number of parties and input bit length. That could be a worthwhile project in its own right. Still, its society input is already the paper’s \(p\)-vector up to normalization; adding a computational analysis would extend the paper’s agenda, not give one of its results a continuous mirror. Enriching types with region or other voter characteristics would change the apportionment problem rather than rescue the mirror.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo: this paper already formulates its population through aggregate vote masses, and it proves axiomatic results rather than computational ones. It does not prove that no adjacent algorithmic study could be worthwhile; it does make a continuous-population mirror of this paper difficult to distinguish from the paper’s existing model.
