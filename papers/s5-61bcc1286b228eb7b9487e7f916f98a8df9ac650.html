The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) interval courses, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), binary demand sets \(D_t\), and rational per-capita seat supplies \(q_j\), compute the maximum total assigned course mass. Each type’s mass may be distributed among whole schedules \(S\subseteq D_t\) whose courses do not overlap, subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\) for every course \(j\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete binary course-demand sets with mass \(\mu_t\); supplies are per-capita seat capacities \(q_j\). Decision variables \(x_{t,S}\) distribute each type’s mass over integral, conflict-free schedules \(S\), and the objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}|S|\).

The objection that survived

The paper’s Northwestern cohort does not establish that real course systems have few complete preference types and seats that scale with cohort size; the proponent names plausible large standardized programs, but does not provide evidence about their prevalence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7’s uncapped social-welfare regime and Theorem 15’s capped social-welfare regime. It leaves the max-min, EF1, EFX, and arbitrary-conflict-graph results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bounds relate the continuous optimum to finite-population optima as functions of population size \(N\), support size \(\tau\), and the rational input bit length?
  • How efficiently can continuous solutions be converted into finite assignments when type counts and seat capacities are fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s interval-course allocation problem itself. In a large university system or standardized training program, many learners may have the same set of desired courses and the same credit cap. Treat that complete preference-and-cap description as a type \(t\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the population. If course \(j\) has \(s_j\) seats for a population of size \(N\), its per-capita supply is \(q_j=s_j/N\). This is a sensible regime when the program and its course capacity grow with the cohort, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct preference types stays far below \(N\).

Lead: the continuous uncapped CAUTC-SW problem, anchored by Theorem 7. Theorem 7, proved in this paper using a reduction from circular-arc coloring, states that CAUTC-SW is NP-hard when utilities are binary, credit caps are infinite, and all courses have uniform credit counts. The problem still has the paper’s interval time conflicts.

Here is a continuous version of that exact regime. An instance gives \(m\) interval courses, a list of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita seat supplies \(q_j\). Type \(t\) has a binary demand set \(D_t\subseteq M\). A feasible schedule \(S\) for that type is any subset of \(D_t\) whose courses do not overlap. Choose nonnegative masses \(x_{t,S}\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the whole, integral schedule \(S\), subject to
\[ \sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j \ \ \text{for every course }j. \]
Maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}|S|\). A solution is a feasible distribution of type mass over schedules attaining the maximum.

I expect this continuous problem to be tractable. It is a configuration LP: there may be exponentially many schedules, but its pricing question for a type is a maximum-weight independent set among that type’s desired interval courses. That can be solved in polynomial time by weighted interval scheduling; the ellipsoid or column-generation machinery then gives an exact polynomial-time optimization method in \(m,\tau,L\). A basic solution can be reported using only polynomially many type-schedule pairs. The further question is how large the gap can be between this optimum and the normalized finite-population optimum, and whether rounding bounds depend mainly on \(N\) or also on \(\tau\). If conflicts cease to be interval-structured, the pricing problem may change the complexity picture.

A finite-credit companion: the continuous capped CAUTC-SW problem, anchored by Theorem 15. Theorem 15, proved here, states that Algorithm 2 obtains a \(1/2\)-approximation to CAUTC-SW in \(O(n^2)\) time when there are \(n\) students, utilities are binary, course credits are unit, and credit caps are arbitrary. Its continuous counterpart uses the same types and per-capita supplies, but each type has an integer cap \(C_t\). Feasible schedules are independent sets of desired interval courses of size at most \(C_t\); the objective and mass constraints are as above. I expect this version to be tractable as well: its pricing problem is maximum-weight interval scheduling with a cardinality bound, solvable by dynamic programming. This is the more convincing institutional regime, since students ordinarily do have credit caps. It also raises the rounding question for the paper’s finite \(1/2\)-approximation guarantee, alongside whether analogous mass models support its fairness objectives.

For either problem, the type support is the list of positive-mass types, not all \(2^m\) possible demand sets. Each student still receives a whole, feasible schedule; only the aggregate mass can split across different schedules. This is the population continuization, not fractional courses or a continuous outcome. A finite assignment embeds by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(q_j=s_j/N\); conversely, rational mass assignments can be implemented by replicating the cohort and scaling seat supplies. The continuous optimum is therefore a high-multiplicity relaxation of the finite problem, not a claim that every particular finite instance has the same optimum.

Theorem 7 is my lead because it supplies the paper’s hardness result, and the continuous pricing argument gives a concrete reason that hardness might dissolve. Theorem 15 reinforces the case in the more realistic capped regime. The case covers these two CAUTC-SW settings; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s max-min, EF1, EFX, or arbitrary-conflict-graph results. The continuization claim is about the computational questions over population masses, not about novelty of continuum models or LP methods in general.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. Exact preference-and-cap types and course supplies that scale with the cohort may fit a large structured program better than every university; if \(\tau\) grows nearly as fast as \(N\), the high-multiplicity benefit also weakens. The uncapped restriction in Theorem 7 is narrower still. The capped Theorem 15 mirror helps with plausibility, but neither resolves those limits.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors rely on a special scaling regime: a fixed course catalog, a small set of complete preference-and-cap types, and seat capacity growing with the cohort. In an ordinary university, course requests may distinguish most students, and the actual sections and seats are fixed. Then the support can approach the population size, while per-capita capacity shrinks. That makes the high-multiplicity interpretation a narrow fit for the paper’s motivating setting. A large standardized or online program with expanded capacity could fit it, though, so this is a limit on scope rather than a decisive objection. The paper itself studies social welfare alongside its fairness objectives, making the proposed target a real computational question.[Published paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.FORC.2023.8)

The uncapped mirror anchored by Theorem 7 survives the more fundamental objections. For each type, it assigns mass to whole, feasible course schedules; only the population mass splits. The model forgets no identity that social welfare uses, and positive per-capita capacity prevents the objective from vanishing. Its configuration LP has exponentially many schedules, but pricing is maximum-weight independent set on interval courses, solvable by weighted interval scheduling. That makes the continuous question tractable and gives a clear reason the discrete hardness might disappear.

The capped mirror anchored by Theorem 15 survives too. Adding an integer cap \(C_t\) to each type’s schedules leaves the pricing problem as cardinality-constrained weighted interval scheduling, which a dynamic program solves in polynomial time. The paper’s \(1/2\)-approximation is not itself reproduced as a continuous guarantee, but the underlying social-welfare problem has a legitimate continuous version. Finite instances map to rational masses and per-capita supplies; rational mass solutions can in turn be represented after scaling to a sufficiently large cohort. The remaining question—how well a solution rounds back to a finite enrollment—is substantive programme work, not a failure of the mirror.

One can argue that these versions are narrow, and that a paper merely writing down the standard configuration LP would be a modest contribution. That lowers the expected payoff; it does not show that the question is ill-posed or already answered. The negative case cannot honestly defeat either anchor. Its strongest point is that the useful regime needs evidence: whether real or plausible course systems have few complete student types and capacity that scales with cohort size. If the case must be that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario, the available grounds do not support it.
