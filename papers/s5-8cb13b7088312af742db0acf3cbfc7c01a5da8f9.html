What the mirror covers

The paper’s results concern axiomatic characterizations, integrity-constraint preservation, and query semantics; none supplies a named computational result for the proposed mass-based mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive scenario is a large federation of data providers—say, municipal registries or hospital systems—where \(N\) sources fall into only \(\tau\) recurring database types, with \(N \gg \tau\). A type is a complete relational instance over the shared schema, and its mass is the fraction of sources with that instance. A natural continuous quota aggregator includes a tuple when the mass of types containing it reaches a specified threshold. That is a sensible high-multiplicity model of the paper’s quota rules.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor the required mirror. Its numbered results characterize axioms and constraint preservation. For example, Lemma 21 proves that union commutes with queries in the positive existential fragment; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s general statement that some aggregators are polynomial to compute is not a numbered complexity result either.

So there is a plausible continuous population setting, but no qualifying anchor from which to state one continuous problem per result and predict its complexity. Treating Lemma 21 or the axiomatic characterizations as such an anchor would misstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s hospital and municipal federation is a plausible high-multiplicity story, but it supplies no computational anchor. The paper’s numbered results are axiomatic characterizations, integrity-constraint preservation statements, and query-commutation results. Lemma 21, for example, establishes an identity for union and positive existential queries; it does not formulate a computational problem or classify its complexity. The paper’s general observation that some aggregators are polynomial to compute does not provide a named complexity result to mirror.

The federation story also strains the paper’s notion of a type. A type must be a complete database instance, including its tuples and their identities. Providers sharing a schema will usually have different records, so their whole databases are different types. Treating renamed or structurally similar databases as one type can discard identity information used by joins, queries, and integrity constraints. Repeated replicas could give a small type support, but they do not turn the paper’s results into computational questions.

The strongest repair is to give each complete database type a mass and aggregate a tuple when its supporting mass crosses a threshold. That is a sensible continuous version of quota aggregation, but its natural paper anchors—such as Lemma 1 or Proposition 15—are characterizations of axioms and preservation, not computational results. Computing tuple support from a supplied finite list of types would be a new routine for the aggregator; it would not mirror a complexity problem the paper studied. The same issue holds for Lemma 21: with union, the query result depends on which types have positive mass, not on their masses. Replacing union with a mass threshold changes the aggregator and leaves the query identity as a different question.

This is not a proof that no later computational question about weighted database aggregation could be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest at the paper boundary: its results do not identify such a question, and the plausible mass-based repairs either preserve support behavior or introduce a new algorithmic topic. Replicated feeds keep the modeling possibility alive, but they do not rescue an anchor in this paper.
