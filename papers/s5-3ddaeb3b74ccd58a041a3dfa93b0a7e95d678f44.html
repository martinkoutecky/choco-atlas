The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a case that hybrid school choice has a natural large-population regime, but I can’t give it a qualifying computational anchor. The numbered results are Proposition 1 (proved here: the shift choice function is substitutable but does not satisfy the law of aggregate demand), Proposition 2 (proved here: generalized DA is individually rational, non-wasteful, and within-grade-fair), Proposition 3 (proved here: with more than two shifts, no mechanism has all three properties and is strategy-proof), and Proposition 4 (proved here: with two shifts, generalized DA is strategy-proof). None asserts a complexity classification or analyzes the running time of a computational problem. Proposition 3 is an impossibility result about mechanism properties, not computational hardness.

The strongest setting for a mirror would be a large school district: many students, but relatively few combinations of grade and schedule preference. A candidate type could also include a priority tier. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of students of type \(t\), and let \(x_{t,m,c}\) be the mass assigned to shift \(m\) and classroom \(c\), with remaining mass assigned to online learning. Capacities and grade reservations would be scaled with enrollment. That is a recognizable population-allocation model.

The closest candidate question would ask whether, for every such mass profile, there is a mechanism producing feasible mass assignments that are individually rational, non-wasteful, within-grade-fair, and strategy-proof. Proposition 3 is the natural lead for that question; Proposition 4 suggests a two-shift counterpart. But this is only a candidate analogue, not a sound mirror of those anchors: with a distribution over types, an individual has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness is not captured by changes to the distribution alone. Requiring strategy-proofness against a positive-mass cohort instead changes the claim to a coalitional one. Preserving the paper’s strict within-grade priorities also requires priority information in the types, which can defeat the small-support regime.

So the setting is plausible, and the model can be written down, but the paper does not supply the kind of named computational result this programme requires. The main weakness is not merely that the continuous question is unproved: it is that the paper’s central results concern axiomatic mechanism design, while the proposed mirror would need to resolve a different strategic question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. Proposition 1 is about substitutability and the law of aggregate demand; Proposition 2 proves fairness and efficiency properties of generalized deferred acceptance; Proposition 3 is an impossibility result about strategy-proof mechanisms; and Proposition 4 proves strategy-proofness for two shifts. None gives a complexity bound, algorithmic guarantee, or hardness result. That leaves no qualifying anchor for a ChoCo mirror.

The strongest rescue is to ask how efficiently generalized deferred acceptance can be computed for a mass population. That is a coherent new question, but the paper does not analyze the running time of its procedure or present computation as its contribution. Naming the procedure does not turn its axiomatic theorems into computational results. Nor would adding a complexity question to a mass version make it a mirror of a computational result the paper never claimed.

A large district with many students and few grades and schedule preferences is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But the paper’s strict within-grade fairness depends on each student’s position in a strict priority order. To preserve that information, priority position must be part of the student’s type; with a strict order, positions are distinct, so the support can grow with the population. A model using a few priority tiers could compress the population, but it would change the fairness structure. A continuous priority percentile and mass allocation might be a worthwhile extension, but it would need its own formulation and computational results.

Propositions 3 and 4 do not supply those results through strategy-proofness. In a distribution-only model, a single student’s report changes no mass; treating a positive-mass group’s report as pivotal changes individual strategy-proofness into a coalitional property. One could instead define individual assignments at priority cutoffs and ask about unilateral reports there. That may be mathematically meaningful, but it is a new incentive model and still an axiomatic question, not a computational mirror of either proposition. Proposition 2’s fairness guarantees and Proposition 1’s choice-function properties face the same anchor problem.

So the negative case is strong as a rejection under the programme’s computational-anchor standard: none of the proposed proposition-based anchors survives. Its limit is equally clear: it does not show that continuous school choice is uninteresting, only that a continuous school-choice project would be a new research direction rather than a computational mirror of this paper.
