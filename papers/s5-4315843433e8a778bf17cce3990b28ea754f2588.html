What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 have direct mass-based structural analogues; Lemma 1 and the preliminary definitions receive no separate computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case on the positive side is that the paper’s voting model has a natural population version. Imagine a large federation or association whose members rank policy options, with many members sharing each complete ranking. A type is a weak order over the alternatives; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members with type \(t\); and a quota \(q_{\{x,y\}}\) becomes a share \(\alpha_{\{x,y\}}>1/2\). One could ask for the social ranking obtained by taking the transitive closure of the pairwise support relation. That is a plausible continuous analogue of the paper’s subject.

But it is not an eligible anchored mirror here. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that if \(q\ge r\) pairwise, then at every finite preference profile the transitive closure under \(q\) is a coarsening of the transitive closure under \(r\). Corollary 1, derived from Theorem 1, says that every absolute qualified majority rule gives a coarsening of the simple-majority rule’s ranking. These are structural results about aggregation rules. Neither asserts a complexity classification or presents an algorithmic result. Computing the continuous version I described would amount to summing pairwise masses and taking a graph’s transitive closure; that is a routine computation, not a complexity question the paper studies.

So I have no qualifying anchor and no per-anchor problem to state. The paper’s reference to continuum voters in Dasgupta and Maskin is prior work cited in a footnote, not a computational result of this paper. The positive case is therefore limited to the naturalness of a continuous population model; its weakest point is that the paper offers no named computational result for that model to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest possible mirror is a continuous version of Theorem 1. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of each weak-order type, and let \(p_{xy}\) be the mass that strictly prefers \(x\) to \(y\). For pairwise thresholds \(\alpha_{\{x,y\}}>1/2\), draw an edge from \(x\) to \(y\) when \(p_{xy}\ge\alpha_{\{x,y\}}\), then take the transitive closure. One can ask whether \(\alpha\ge\beta\) makes the resulting ranking under \(\alpha\) a coarsening of the ranking under \(\beta\), with the simple-majority corollary as a special case. That is a natural continuous-population question.

But it still does not mirror a computational result. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish structural properties of aggregation rules; Lemma 1 is structural too. None states a complexity bound or studies an algorithm. Turning the continuous rule into an input-output problem—say, computing its ranking from rational masses—would pose a new computational question, but it would not be a continuous counterpart of a computational result in this paper. The paper’s contribution is the coarsening relationship itself.

I would not make the negative case by claiming the population model is implausible. A large federation with many members sharing rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s reference to continuum voters does not amount to a computational treatment of the question. The case against a mirror is therefore strong on the programme’s stated anchor requirement, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be asked: the model is natural; the missing piece is a computational result in this paper for the mirror to continue.
