The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) types with qualities \(\theta_i\), rational masses \(H\mu_i\) where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and the paper’s learning and utility parameters, choose participating masses \(z_i\in[0,H\mu_i]\), contributing masses \(x_i\in[0,z_i]\), and total sample supplies \(y_i\ge 0\). For \(x_i>0\), let each contributor of type \(i\) supply \(q_i=y_i/x_i\); require \(y_i=0\) if \(x_i=0\). Set \(N=\sum_i y_i>0\), \(\vartheta=N^{-1}\sum_i\theta_i y_i\), and \(\ell(N,\vartheta)=-a[R_0^\star+\epsilon(\vartheta,N)]\); type \(i\)’s outside utility is \(u_i=-a[R_0^\star+\epsilon(\theta_i,n)]-cn\), with \(n\) from Proposition 1. Require \(\ell\ge u_i\) for participating noncontributors and \(\ell-cq_i\ge u_i\) for contributors. Find an additive-\(\eta\) welfare-maximizing allocation for objective \(\sum_i[z_i\ell-cy_i+(H\mu_i-z_i)u_i]\), with the empty coalition also available.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population of interchangeable learner types carries mass \(H\mu_i\). The planner chooses participation mass \(z_i\), contributing mass \(x_i\), and total samples \(y_i\) per type; welfare and individual rationality use the paper’s risk bound and outside option.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the public-information first-best allocation in Theorem 1, using Proposition 1’s outside-option sample count; it leaves the private-information unravelling result in Theorem 2 and the verification mechanism in Theorem 3 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact optimum retain a quality-cutoff structure when each type has positive mass and participation can be fractional?
  • Can an additive-\(\eta\) optimal allocation be found in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length, \(\log H\), and \(\log(1/\eta)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires: none of its numbered results establishes that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its theorems characterize welfare allocations and equilibria. So there is no fully compliant complexity anchor here.

The strongest positive candidate is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under H1–H3, the full-information welfare-maximization problem has a unique solution: everyone joins the coalition, and contributions follow a quality cutoff. In the paper’s ordering, there is an \(L^{\mathrm{opt}}>0\) such that each of the first \(L^{\mathrm{opt}}-1\) agents contributes the maximum allowed by their individual-rationality constraint; the \(L^{\mathrm{opt}}\)-th agent may contribute any amount up to that maximum; and every worse-quality agent contributes zero.

Here is a continuous-population version I would take to a prover. Let the population have total mass \(H\), with a finite support of \(\tau\) types and type masses \(H\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A type specifies the learner’s sampling distribution \(P_i\), its quality \(\theta_i\), and the common sampling cost and utility parameters used in the paper. Consider large-client collaborative learning settings—such as many devices using a small number of standard sensor or data pipelines—where \(H\) is large and \(\tau\ll H\).

For type \(i\), let \(z_i\) be the mass that joins, \(x_i\le z_i\) the mass asked to contribute samples, and \(y_i\) the total samples supplied by that contributing mass. When \(x_i>0\), its per-agent contribution is \(q_i=y_i/x_i\). Set \(N=\sum_i y_i\) and, for \(N>0\), \(\vartheta=\sum_i\theta_i y_i/N\). The collaborative model gives utility \(\ell(N,\vartheta)=-a[R_0^\star+\epsilon(\vartheta,N)]\) before contribution costs, where \(\epsilon\) is the paper’s bound. An outside type-\(i\) agent receives \(u_i=-a[R_0^\star+\epsilon(\theta_i,n)]-cn\), with \(n\) as in Proposition 1.

Mass-Continuum First-Best Collaboration takes \(H\), the rational masses \((\mu_i)\), the ordered qualities, and the paper’s utility and learning-bound parameters as input. It asks for the welfare-maximizing allocation \((z_i,x_i,y_i)\), subject to individual rationality: a participating noncontributor of type \(i\) must get at least \(u_i\), and a contributor must get at least \(u_i\) after paying \(c\,y_i/x_i\). Its welfare is
\[ \sum_i\bigl[z_i\ell(N,\vartheta)-cy_i+(H\mu_i-z_i)u_i\bigr]. \]
The solution is the optimal mass allocation and its welfare; the empty coalition is an available option. For a complexity formulation, ask for an additive-\(\eta\) optimal allocation in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), \(\log H\), and \(\log(1/\eta)\).

I expect this problem to be Class A, as a candidate rather than an established result: Theorem 1’s sorted cutoff suggests that the number of distinct types, rather than the number of learners, controls the allocation, and cohort mass makes fractional participation explicit. The key open step is proving the continuous threshold structure and showing the resulting optimization can be solved with the stated dependence on \(\tau\) and \(L\).

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem because it scales their first-best collaboration objective and participation constraints over interchangeable learners. Continuity concerns the population: \(z_i\) and \(x_i\) are masses of learners, not fractional prediction outcomes. The scenario also has a concrete high-multiplicity interpretation: many providers share the same data pipeline, quality, costs, and preferences.

The scope is deliberately limited to Theorem 1’s public-information benchmark. It does not claim to mirror Theorem 2’s private-information unravelling or Theorem 3’s verification mechanism. Those invite a further question: in a continuum of learners, does the naive mechanism unravel to the empty coalition, or can a positive mass of the worst-quality cohort remain?

The weakest point is that Theorem 1 is not a complexity result, and its proof assumes strictly distinct individual qualities. Repeated-type cohorts change the allocation granularity, so the theorem does not transfer automatically; the threshold structure needs a fresh proof. That weakens the claim that the paper already establishes a computational result for the continuous model, but it does not make the proposed welfare-optimization question artificial: it is a direct population-mass version of the paper’s own first-best problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational-complexity result. It characterizes the welfare-maximizing contribution scheme; it gives no running time or complexity classification. The paper’s sample counts are already real-valued, so the proponent’s formulation changes the population axis and then introduces a new computational task: finding the best mass allocation. That may be a worthwhile problem, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named complexity result of this paper.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror. Many learners sharing a small number of data pipelines is a credible high-multiplicity regime. If type includes the features that affect the model—distribution, quality, costs, and preferences—then grouping identical learners loses no relevant information. Repeated quality values also do not look fundamental: the paper calls strict ordering convenient, and a mass formulation can allocate contributions within a cohort. Keeping total mass \(H\) preserves the scale relevant to sample volume. The resulting optimization may be simple, but simplicity would be an answer to the computational question, not a reason the question is ill-posed.

The possible extensions to Theorems 2 and 3 are not developed into anchors. One genuine issue there is that an individual deviation in an atomless population changes no aggregate, so the paper’s unilateral Nash analysis would need a suitable continuum formulation. But that does not defeat the first-best mirror, and group deviations could still motivate a separate question.

So the case against is weak. The paper supplies no compliant computational-complexity anchor, and the proposed first-best mirror is a new algorithmic formulation rather than a direct continuation of one. But I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous-population problem: the mass version of the first-best objective remains coherent under the programme’s type and high-multiplicity criteria.
