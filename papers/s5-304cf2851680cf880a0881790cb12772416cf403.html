What the mirror covers

The proposed continuous restatements concern Theorem 11's Pareto property and Theorem 17's strategy-proofness; neither is a computational result, and the remaining results are not covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper an eligible computational anchor. It has named results, but none classifies the complexity of a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest is Theorem 17, proved here: the skeptical aggregation operator is strategy-proof when agents have IUO Hamming-set preferences. Theorem 11, also proved here, says the skeptical operator is Pareto optimal among admissible labelings that are no more committed than every participant’s labeling, even when participants have heterogeneous preferences drawn from all eight classes considered in the paper. Both are mechanism-property results, not computational-complexity results.

The strongest plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a very large public deliberation over one fixed argumentation framework. A type could specify an agent’s preferred legal labeling and preference class; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the population of each type. In such a regime, the number of people could be enormous while the number of distinct types stayed small. That makes the population story credible.

But the paper defines a profile as a set of submitted labelings, so multiplicities do not affect its operators. Keeping the operators unchanged makes the mass values irrelevant. Making mass matter would require a different aggregation rule. Likewise, a single agent’s lie becomes a coalition or mass-manipulation question in a continuum; an infinitesimal agent may have no effect at all. Those could be worthwhile new computational questions, but they are not mirrors of the paper’s named results.

So the honest positive case is limited: the paper offers a plausible setting for large populations, and its theorems might extend to population distributions through their support. It supplies no named computational anchor for the required continuous problem, however, and I would not present an unanchored extension as evidence that this paper fills ChoCo’s complexity-theoretic gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 11 proves a Pareto property of the skeptical operator; Theorem 17 proves its strategy-proofness under a particular preference class. They classify no running time, complexity, or algorithm for an optimization problem. That is a decisive mismatch with ChoCo’s stated aim of mapping computational questions.

The proposed setting itself is credible: a very large deliberation about one fixed argumentation framework, with types specified by agents’ ideal labelings and preference classes. For Theorem 11, one can replace the finite electorate with a distribution over those types and define Pareto improvement to mean weak improvement for almost everyone and strict improvement for a positive mass. But the paper’s operators take a *set* of submitted labelings as input, so the distribution’s masses do not enter the result. The continuum version is a well-defined reformulation of an axiomatic claim, not a computational question of the kind ChoCo targets. Making masses affect the outcome through weighted welfare would instead replace the operator and ask a different question from the theorem.

Theorem 17 has a more basic problem in the continuum. A single agent in an atomless population has measure zero, so changing their report cannot change the distribution: individual strategy-proofness is vacuous. Treat a group as the manipulator and the question becomes coalition manipulation, which the theorem does not address. With the paper’s set-based operator, introducing any positive mass of a previously absent report can change the input regardless of how small that mass is. A minimum-mass manipulation can therefore have infimum zero; adding thresholds, report costs, or a mass-weighted rule to avoid that degeneracy would again change the studied problem.

One could still study the complexity of computing these operators or finding manipulations in an argumentation framework. But those would be new computational questions about the finite operators, with continuous masses either ignored or made relevant by changing the mechanism. The honest limit of this negative case is that the large-deliberation scenario is sensible, and a continuous restatement of Theorem 11 is possible. If ChoCo chose to include computational work adjacent to axiomatic papers, that could motivate follow-up research. Under the programme’s stated scope, though, the paper supplies no named computational anchor to mirror.
