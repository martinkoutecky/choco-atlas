The continuous mirror question

Given an agenda of \(m\) alternatives, a finite support of \(\tau\) complete three-tier ballot types with binary rational masses summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\), does there exist an ordered partition \(P\) into three nonempty tiers such that \(\sum_{a\succ_P b}\Delta_\mu(a,b)\ge q\), where \(\Delta_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a])\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete three-tier ballot types. The decision variable is the collective ordered three-tier partition \(P\), and the objective is to maximize \(\sum_{a\succ_P b}\Delta_\mu(a,b)\).

The objection that survived

The paper already analyzes winner determination through the same weighted-tournament objective, so the distributional input may change the encoding more than the computation; the proponent acknowledges normalization but does not explicitly address that existing analysis.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4 for \(j=3\) and \(k=3\). It leaves the theorem’s other parameter ranges, Theorem 3’s tractability results, and the paper’s max \(k\)-OP results outside the stated population model.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a population version of \((j,k)\)-Kemeny winner determination. The type is a complete ballot: either a weak order with exactly \(j\) tiers or, when \(j=\mathcal L\), a linear order. A society is a finite support of such types, with binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For each pair of alternatives, define its population margin by
\[ \Delta_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_t a]\bigr). \]
Given \(k\ge3\), a solution is an ordered partition \(P=(P_1>\cdots>P_k)\) of the alternatives into nonempty tiers maximizing
\[ F_\mu(P)=\sum_{a\succ_P b}\Delta_\mu(a,b). \]
Call this Mass-\((j,k)\)-Kemeny Winner. Its decision version adds a rational threshold \(q\) and asks whether some \(P\) has \(F_\mu(P)\ge q\). The input is the agenda, the \(\tau\) supported types and their masses; the relevant parameters are \(m,\tau,L\), and \(k\).

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that determining a winning ordering for \((j,k)\)-Kemeny is NP-hard when \(j\ge3\) and \(k\ge3\), and also when \(j=\mathcal L\) and \(k\ge3\); in particular, winner determination for \((3,3)\)-Kemeny is NP-hard. The paper proves this by realizing the weighted tournaments used in its hardness argument through profiles of \(j\)-chotomous orders.

The population mirror preserves the underlying question exactly. If a finite profile has \(n_t\) ballots of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Then \(\Delta_\mu(a,b)\) is the paper’s pairwise margin divided by \(n\), so every output ordering has the same relative score and the same maximizers. Grouping ballots into types is polynomial in the explicit profile size. I therefore expect this mirror to be hardness-transferring, with the hard combinatorics in the agenda and the output ordering. It does not replace the authors’ aggregation problem with a tractable surrogate.

A plausible regime is a large consultation on a shared agenda of proposals or candidates, where many participants use the same three-tier rubric. With a fixed small agenda, the set of possible three-tier ballots is finite and may be modest, so a very large population can have \(\tau\ll n\). A type captures everything this model uses about a participant: their placement of every alternative. The output remains a collective tiered ordering; only the population becomes a distribution. That is a natural high-multiplicity form of the paper’s problem, and I would expect the authors to recognize it.

The weak point is that for rational masses this is also a normalized representation of a finite profile. It does not reveal a new continuum effect, and Theorem 4 does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is bounded or otherwise especially small. Its value is more specific: it asks whether the complexity landscape changes when the input records type frequencies rather than voters individually. The theorem suggests that agenda-driven hardness survives. Further questions include whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and which restrictions on the aggregate margins—such as the paper’s acyclic cases—restore tractability in the mass representation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the population distribution adds no independent computational content to winner determination. The masses enter only through the pairwise margins
\[ \Delta_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_t a]\bigr). \]
Choose a common denominator \(D\) for the rational masses and set \(w(a,b)=D\Delta_\mu(a,b)\). Then the continuous objective is exactly the paper’s weighted-tournament score, scaled by \(1/D\), so its maximizing orderings are unchanged. Computing \(w\) from the supported types takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). The paper also shows that arbitrary integer edge weights can be realized, up to a common factor, by profiles of three-tier orders. Thus the hardness result transfers in both directions through the same aggregate object.

That makes the proposed mirror look like a new encoding of the paper’s max \(k\)-OP problem, whose weighted-tournament complexity the paper already studies. Different ballot distributions with the same margins are indistinguishable to this rule; one can discard the type list before determining the winner. There is no population-level decision variable here, and the continuous model does not create the kind of mass-allocation or intervention problem where continuous optimization might supply new machinery. The most defensible objection is therefore low incremental value: this is close to a collision at the level of the problem’s computational sufficient statistic.

The usual objections do not work. Theorem 4 is a named computational result; a large consultation on a fixed agenda is a plausible repeated-type setting; ballots capture everything this model uses about participants; and neither a vanishing-agent argument nor “the hardness is in the alternatives” defeats the mirror. Nor does the rational-mass encoding itself disqualify it: high multiplicity is expressly a reason in its favor.

This negative case is limited. The paper’s weighted-tournament input is not literally a continuous society, and questions about support-sensitive complexity—such as parameterization by \(\tau\)—could still be worthwhile. Under the programme’s stated standard, I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror survives. The strongest conclusion I can support is that this particular winner mirror may add little beyond the paper’s existing aggregate formulation; I expect that to be a weaker case than the proponent’s.
