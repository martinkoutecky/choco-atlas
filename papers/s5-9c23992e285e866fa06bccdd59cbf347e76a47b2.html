The continuous mirror question

Given colleges \(C\), a finite set \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita upper quotas \(q_j\), compute a student-optimal H-stable assignment. Each type’s mass receives one assignment as a block, or remains unmatched; college \(j\) receives mass at most \(q_j\). H-stability is defined by college score cutoffs: each type goes to its most-preferred listed college whose cutoff it meets, and no positive cutoff can be lowered while maintaining the quotas, so tied score groups are admitted or rejected together.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference-and-score types. Assignment variables choose one college or no assignment for each type, while the mass assigned to each college respects its per-capita quota. Cutoff stability preserves equal treatment, and the objective selects a student-optimal H-stable assignment.

The objection that survived

The cited finite student-proposing algorithm already computes the analogous solution, and the mirror retains blockwise cutoff decisions without showing that continuous-optimization tools help; the type-based complexity question remains open.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the H-CAPT upper-quota student-optimal assignment anchored by Lemma 6. It leaves aside the paper’s lower-quota, common-quota, and paired-application cases, as well as its computational experiments as a separate result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the student-optimal H-stable assignment be computed in time polynomial in the number of colleges, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • What is the precise correspondence with finite H-CAPT instances when type masses and per-capita quotas are scaled to integral counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s student-optimal assignment problem with ties and the equal treatment policy. The paper contains no numbered theorem classifying this core problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its strongest anchor is Lemma 6, proved in this paper: for CAPT, maximizing the rank-weighted objective with \(\alpha_1>\cdots>\alpha_r\), over integer solutions satisfying the applicant constraints (each applicant gets at most one college), college quotas, and constraint (4), yields the student-optimal H-stable matching. Constraint (4) says that if an applicant is assigned to a college, every applicant with at least as high a score there must be assigned to that college or to one they prefer more. The paper computes with this formulation as its Optimal Envy-Free IP model. The lemma is an optimization-formulation result, not a polynomial-time complexity result.

Here is the corresponding problem to hand to a prover: H-CAPT\(_\infty\) Student-Optimal Assignment. An instance has a finite set of colleges \(C\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) applicant types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita upper quotas \(q_j\) for each college \(j\). A complete type \(t\) specifies its strict preference list and its score at each college on that list. Find an assignment \(a:T\to C\cup\{\varnothing\}\). Each type’s mass goes to one college as a block, or remains unmatched, and the mass assigned to \(j\) must be at most \(q_j\).

The assignment must be H-stable in the paper’s cutoff sense: there must be a score cutoff for each college such that each type gets its most-preferred listed college whose score clears that cutoff, if there is one; and no positive cutoff can be lowered while keeping all college quotas feasible. Thus the cutoff cannot split a tied score group. Among H-stable assignments, return a student-optimal one: every type gets the best college it can receive in any H-stable assignment. The mass-weighted version of the paper’s objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j\alpha_{r_t(j)}x_{tj}\), where \(x_{tj}=1\) when \(t\) is assigned to \(j\), and \(r_t(j)\) is its rank for \(j\). The underlying question is which assignment is student-optimal; the IP is the paper’s way of computing it.

A plausible regime is a large centralized admissions system with a constrained catalogue of ranked programme choices and common, integer score bands. A type is the complete combination of an applicant’s submitted list and score vector. For example, \(100{,}000\) applicants represented by a few thousand recurring profiles is a coherent instance regime, though not a claim about the measured support of the Hungarian system. College capacities scale with the population, so \(q_j\) is the natural unit. Individual outcomes remain assignments to whole colleges; it is the population that is represented continuously by its masses. That keeps ties and equal treatment central to the problem instead of replacing them with fractional seats or lotteries.

The authors could recognize this as their problem: the mirror preserves H-stability, the score ties, the quota rule, and the student-optimal objective. The paper cites Biró and Kiselgof (2015) for a student-proposing extension of Gale–Shapley that finds a student-optimal H-stable solution in the finite model. I expect the mass version to be tractable as well, likely by processing types and score groups directly, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), rather than in the represented population size. That would be a Class A answer for this mirror; it is an expectation about the continuous-input version, not a complexity theorem proved by this paper.

The scope is the paper’s ties-plus-equal-treatment-policy problem with upper quotas. It does not cover the paper’s future combined case with lower quotas, common quotas, or paired applications. Further questions include whether a type-level algorithm works for arbitrary binary-encoded masses and quotas, and how its output relates to the finite problem when \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq_j\) are integral.

The weakest point is that a complete type includes both the full preference list and exact scores, so in real admissions data the support \(\tau\) could approach the number of applicants. Also, equal treatment makes the type-level decision integral even though masses and capacities are rational, so this mirror may yield high-multiplicity compression without an LP-based tractability gain. The case is positive but deliberately narrow: the population distribution is a sensible object here, while the paper supplies no named hardness result whose dissolution in the continuum could be claimed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Lemma 6 is a narrow anchor: it proves that a particular rank-weighted IP objective selects the student-optimal H-stable matching. It does not classify a computational problem’s complexity. The paper’s computational contribution is mainly an IP formulation and solver experiments; it already points to an extended Gale–Shapley algorithm for the same student-optimal solution. So this result offers little evidence of a complexity gap that continuization could resolve.

The strongest version of the mirror is still recognizable. Define each type by its full preference list and score vector, scale college quotas with population mass, and apply the same cutoff rule. Equal treatment makes a type’s applicants move together: the mass is assigned to the best eligible college or remains unmatched. Rational masses change the arithmetic of quota checks, but the algorithm can process types and score groups directly. Allowing a type’s mass to split across colleges would generally contradict that cutoff-based equal-treatment rule. This makes the mirror a weak fit for the programme’s continuous-optimization motivation: the matching decisions remain blockwise, and the paper supplies no hard or complexity-theoretic result for the mirror to probe.

That is a case against prioritizing this paper, not a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large admissions system with many applicants sharing complete preference-and-score profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and rational population shares and per-capita quotas give a coherent question. The type support might be too large in practice, but that is an empirical limitation, not a fundamental modeling failure. Under the programme’s stated standard, the mirror survives; I cannot honestly make the universal negative case.
