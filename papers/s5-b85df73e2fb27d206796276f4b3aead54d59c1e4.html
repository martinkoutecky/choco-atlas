The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bid set \(A\), exact rational ballot shares \(q_{r,a}\) for each round, the elimination and tie rules, and a distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings, determine every winner under sincere exhaustive-ballot voting that is compatible with the observed shares and the paper’s assumptions about first-round choices and revealed strategic behavior.

The model it lives in

Use complete rankings \(\pi\) as types with mass \(\mu_\pi\), and mass-valued ballot histories constrained to match each observed share \(q_{r,a}\). For each feasible distribution and history, apply sincere voting by each type’s highest-ranked remaining bid, with the specified tie-breaker; return the set of possible winners.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can make is for a continuous version of the paper’s reconstruction of sincere outcomes from exhaustive-ballot tallies. There is a limit to the claim, though: the paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as being in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Propositions 1–7 establish properties of these particular elections and their compatible preference profiles; the vote-buying counts are unnumbered. So none is a qualifying complexity anchor under your stated rule.

The closest named finding is Proposition 7, proved here. It says that for the preference profiles in Table 8, the sincere winner could be South Korea, Japan, or Qatar. If more than 11 voters prefer the USA to Japan, or exactly 11 do and the FIFA president is among them, Japan is eliminated in round 2 and the result stays Qatar. Otherwise, four cases remain: if at least one USA-first voter ranks Qatar second, Qatar wins; if all three relevant voters rank Japan second, Japan wins only when 11 voters prefer Japan to Qatar and include the president, otherwise Qatar wins; if two rank Japan second and one ranks South Korea second, Japan or South Korea can win under the corresponding 11-vote preference and presidential tie-break conditions stated in the proposition, with the exactly-11 Japan–South Korea tie depending on the president; and if two or three rank South Korea second and at most one ranks Japan second, South Korea wins only if 11 voters prefer it to Qatar and include the president, otherwise Qatar wins. This is a substantial instance-level computation, but it is not a complexity result.

My lead candidate mirror is Continuous Exhaustive-Ballot Reconstruction. An instance has a finite bid set \(A\), exact rational ballot shares \(q_{r,a}\) for each observed round \(r\) and active bid \(a\), the elimination and tie rules, and the preference of any designated tie-breaker. A type is a strict ranking \(\pi\) of all bids. The unknown society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings; \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of the electorate of type \(\pi\). For each type, introduce mass variables for possible ballot histories through the observed rounds. Histories must reproduce every \(q_{r,a}\). Minimize the total mass whose ballots differ at any round from that type’s highest-ranked available bid. Among all minimum-deviation solutions, return the possible winners when each compatible society votes sincerely. A solution is the exact minimum strategic mass, the set of possible sincere winners, and rational witnesses for the compatible distributions.

That is the paper’s underlying question—what sincere outcomes are consistent with aggregate round results and limited strategic behavior—expressed for a population rather than 22 named voters. A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a large federation or membership organization repeatedly choosing among a fixed set of host sites or policy bids. Members can share ranking types because they belong to blocs with common priorities; the electorate size \(n\) can be large while the number of supported rankings \(\tau\) stays small relative to \(n\). The input tallies are shares, and the chair’s tie-breaking preference remains a separate institutional parameter. Proposition 7’s thresholds then have natural mass counterparts, such as a tie at one-half.

I expect this problem to be tractable when the number of bids \(m\) is fixed: one can enumerate the finitely many elimination paths and test the resulting linear constraints on type and ballot-history masses. Whether it remains tractable when \(m\) grows is open; there the ranking and elimination-order combinatorics may require a pricing or separation algorithm, or may retain hardness. The mirror covers Proposition 7’s sincere-winner identification, not the paper’s entire historical analysis or its unnumbered vote-buying calculations. Further questions include whether discrete hardness transfers as the agenda grows, and whether the optimal strategic-mass calculation has a compact LP formulation.

The weak point is the regime shift. The paper studies a deliberately small executive committee, and its conclusions depend on sparse, person-specific evidence and a particular chair. A large delegate assembly with repeated preference types is plausible, but it is not the FIFA committee. Also, aggregate tallies cannot identify members’ true preferences uniquely; the continuous problem returns a set of compatible outcomes, not a verdict about what any individual privately believed. That leaves a sensible mirror question, but the paper supplies no qualifying complexity anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper contains no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Propositions 1–7 reason about two fixed elections and the preference profiles compatible with their observed tallies. The vote-buying section likewise calculates thresholds for those elections. None classifies a general problem’s complexity or gives an algorithm whose complexity could be carried into a population model.

Proposition 7 is the closest anchor, but it is a case analysis for the preference profiles in Table 8: under specified rankings and tie-breaking conditions, which of Qatar, Japan, or South Korea wins sincere voting? The proposed “Continuous Exhaustive-Ballot Reconstruction” makes that into a general computational problem, but the general input class and its objective are new. In particular, minimizing the mass that votes insincerely is a reasonable formulation, but the paper does not pose or solve that optimization problem. Asking for all compatible sincere winners instead stays closer to Proposition 7, but still turns one finite reconstruction into a new family of instances.

That family could be modeled coherently. A large association or delegate assembly might have many members, a small support of complete ranking types, and rational vote shares by round. Ballot histories can be represented by mass flows, and a chair’s tie-breaking preference can remain an institutional parameter. The single chair does not invalidate the model, and using types does not lose information the model needs. Those are not sound objections to this mirror.

The remaining case against is about whether this paper is the right source for that research programme. The paper’s specific contribution is an inference about two historical elections with 22 eligible voters, using their particular tallies to characterize possible sincere outcomes. A general algorithmic study of reconstruction from aggregate round-by-round shares may be worthwhile, but that would be a newly formulated computational problem inspired by the paper, not a computational result the paper itself establishes.

So I would reject Proposition 7 as a qualifying anchor and would not select this paper on the strength of the other analyst’s case. I cannot honestly support the broader claim that no scenario could make a continuous version worthwhile: the large-assembly model is plausible, and a well-posed reconstruction problem survives. The negative case is strong on the absence of a computational anchor, but weak as a universal claim about every possible mirror.
