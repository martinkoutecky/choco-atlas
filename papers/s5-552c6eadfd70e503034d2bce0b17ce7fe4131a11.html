The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires: it proves no algorithmic upper bound, hardness result, or parameterized-complexity result. So I cannot give a compliant per-anchor mirror.

The strongest structural support is Theorem 4.3, proved in the paper: for fixed buyer and seller sets, the Shapley value is the unique solution on assignment situations satisfying submarket efficiency—the payoffs in each submarket sum to its worth—and valuation fairness—when one buyer–seller valuation changes, the changes in those two players’ payoffs are equal. That is a central result, but it is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. It cannot honestly serve as the required complexity anchor.

There is still a plausible follow-on continuous problem. Consider a wholesale market with many buyers and sellers but a fixed, small set of repeated valuation profiles. A buyer type records its valuations across seller types; a seller type records the corresponding column of valuations. Let each type have a rational mass, with total mass \(1\), and let \(a_{bs}\) be the value of matching buyer type \(b\) to seller type \(s\). For any subpopulation with masses \(z\), define its worth by the maximum-surplus mass matching:
\[ v(z)=\max_{x\ge 0}\sum_{b,s}a_{bs}x_{bs} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_s x_{bs}\le z_b,\qquad \sum_b x_{bs}\le z_s. \]
Here \(x_{bs}\) is matched mass; it permits fractional type-level averages while each individual still receives at most one match.

A concrete candidate is Replica Shapley Evaluation for Finite-Type Assignment Markets. Given the rational type masses and valuation matrix, replicate the population by a factor \(q\) for every \(q\) that makes the type counts integral. In that finite assignment game, all agents of a type have the same Shapley payoff. The task is to determine whether each type’s per-agent payoff converges as \(q\) grows and, if so, return an \(\epsilon\)-approximation to the limit for every type. The payoff being computed is the expected marginal contribution in a uniformly random player order. The regime is \(q\to\infty\) with the number \(\tau\) of types fixed.

This is a credible high-multiplicity setting: millions of market participants may share a modest number of complete valuation profiles. The Shapley allocation is the paper’s central object, so the authors could plausibly recognize its computation in such a market as a natural extension of their assignment-game problem. The paper, however, does not establish that the replica limits exist or characterize their complexity. On regular instances, LP sensitivity suggests tractability; degenerate matching ties may need separate analysis. I would leave the overall complexity classification open and ask whether the limits exist, how to compute them, and whether submarket efficiency and valuation fairness survive in the limit.

The weakness is decisive for the requested grading: this candidate is a proposed computational extension, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of the paper. The paper’s actual contribution is axiomatic, and continuizing that contribution alone falls outside ChoCo’s stated scope.

If the limit is tractable, that would be a useful follow-on; it would not turn the paper’s axiomatic characterization into a computational anchor.
