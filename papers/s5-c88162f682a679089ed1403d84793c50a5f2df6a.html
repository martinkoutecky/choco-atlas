The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\) summing to one, endowments \(e_A=(1,0)\) and \(e_B=(0,1)\), positive proposed bundles \(x_A,x_B\), a queried type \(i\), and a finite union \(K_i\) of rational polyhedral regions of coalition mass profiles, decide whether there exist \(q\in K_i\) and bundles \(y_A,y_B\in\mathbb R_+^2\) such that \(q_i>0\), \(q_Ay_A+q_By_B\le q_Ae_A+q_Be_B\), every type \(t\) with \(q_t>0\) weakly prefers \(y_t\) to \(x_t\), and type \(i\) strictly prefers \(y_i\) to \(x_i\), for \(u(z)=\log z_1+\log z_2\). Such a profile and allocation are a known feasible blocking deviation, so their existence means reject; otherwise accept.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic exchange economy with two types, masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\), shared type-level bundles, and type-specific knowledge regions \(K_i\) over coalition mass profiles; decide whether a profile known to type \(i\) admits an aggregate-feasible deviation that weakly benefits each participating type and strictly benefits \(i\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is conditional on treating Proposition 1’s decidability result as a computational anchor. It is the paper’s only plausible one: for any finite TU game \(G=(N,v)\), coalition set \(\mathcal S\subseteq 2^N\), payoff proposal \(x\), and agent \(i\), exactly one of \(\vdash\Gamma_G(\mathcal S)\Rightarrow C_i(x)\) and \(\vdash\Gamma_G(\mathcal S)\Rightarrow\neg C_i(x)\) holds. Here \(\Gamma_G(\mathcal S)\) gives complete, accurate feasibility information for the coalitions in \(\mathcal S\), and \(C_i(x)\) says that no coalition containing \(i\) can feasibly make its members weakly better off while making \(i\) strictly better off. The proposition is proved in this paper.

A plausible regime is a large replica exchange economy: \(n=2k\) agents belong to just two types, distinguished by endowment, with agents of each type sharing preferences and information. In the continuum version, \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\) are the fractions of the population in those types. A coalition is described by its mass profile \(q=(q_A,q_B)\), where \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\). This is a population continuization: bundles remain individual outcomes, while the society and coalitions are represented by mass.

One concrete mirror is Mass-Profile Acceptance. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\); the two endowment types from the paper’s exchange example; a proposed bundle \(x_t\) for each type; a queried type \(i\); and a finite union \(K_i\) of rational polyhedral regions describing the coalition mass profiles whose feasibility information type \(i\) knows. Use the common preference \(u(z)=\log z_1+\log z_2\), and give every participating member of a type the same bundle \(y_t\). The question is whether there is a known profile \(q\in K_i\), with \(q_i>0\), and a feasible deviation satisfying
\[ q_Ay_A+q_By_B\le q_Ae_A+q_Be_B, \]
such that \(u(y_t)\ge u(x_t)\) for each type with \(q_t>0\), and \(u(y_i)>u(x_i)\). If such a profile and bundles exist, the answer is “reject” and they are the witness; otherwise it is “accept.” Requiring strict improvement for positive mass gives the individual acceptance criterion a meaningful population counterpart.

For this explicit two-type model, I would expect Class A: after setting \(z_t=q_ty_t\), the resource and Cobb–Douglas utility conditions have conic formulations, so the search over each knowledge region reduces to conic feasibility. A natural next question is whether a minimum-cost description of the knowledge regions can recover the core, and how the answer changes as the number of types grows. The mirror covers individual acceptance under partial coalition information; it does not cover the paper’s minimal-coverage characterization or its replica burden count.

The weakness is substantial. Proposition 1 establishes proof-theoretic decisiveness, not a running-time bound or a classification such as P or NP-hardness. Theorem 1 and Proposition 5 are structural and counting results, respectively, rather than computational-complexity results. So under a strict ChoCo reading that requires a named complexity result, this paper has no qualifying anchor. The model above is a credible follow-up question grounded in the paper’s replicated economy, but its tractability would be a new result, not one this paper supplies. It also replaces exact coalitions with mass profiles, so it may not preserve the identity-specific information burden that drives the paper’s replica count.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only anchor, and its “decidability” result does not give the computational result ChoCo is looking for. It says that, under the paper’s complete positive-or-negative information convention, exactly one of two sequents is provable. It gives no input encoding, running time, complexity classification, or approximation or parameterized algorithm. Theorem 1 characterizes which coalitions must be covered; Proposition 5 counts them. Neither supplies a computational problem with a complexity result to mirror. That is the strongest case for a negative verdict on this paper.

The proposed Mass-Profile Acceptance problem is coherent enough to study, but it changes the object that Proposition 1 studies. Its input includes regions \(K_i\) describing what a type knows about coalition mass profiles, and its question searches for a blocking deviation over those profiles. The paper instead gives an agent information about feasibility facts for particular coalitions and asks whether a specified sentence follows in its proof system. The conic formulation may yield an algorithm for the new problem; that would be a new result, rather than a continuous counterpart to Proposition 1’s theorem.

The more faithful population model runs into the paper’s central unit of information. Theorem 1 concerns coverage of individual coalitions: some member of each nonempty coalition must know its feasibility. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, and a mass profile identifies a whole class of coalitions with the same composition. Giving a type knowledge of a profile can therefore cover many coalitions at once, erasing the identity-sensitive assignments that Theorem 1 characterizes and Proposition 5 counts. One could retain those assignments by making information patterns part of agent types, but then the number of types may grow with the coalition structure rather than remain small in the high-multiplicity limit. Alternatively, one could measure the burden of mass profiles by volume or another continuous quantity, but the paper gives no canonical measure for doing so.

A stronger follow-up could still ask how partial information about feasible mass profiles affects blocking or core membership in a continuum exchange economy. It could be computationally interesting. The case against is that this question requires a new information representation and a new burden measure, and the tractability of its optimization would not supply the missing complexity result in this paper. So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s anchor requirement, but it does not prove that every neighboring continuous optimization problem would be unworthy.
