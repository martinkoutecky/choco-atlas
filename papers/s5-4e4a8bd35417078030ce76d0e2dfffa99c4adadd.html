The continuous mirror question

Given a finite proposal set \(X\), a rational irreducible transition matrix \(P\), and a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), utilities \(u_t(x)\), and rational acceptance quotas \(q_x\), find a main two-step plan and a punishment two-step plan for each \(x\in X\) that induce a collective equilibrium. Each type has a binding delegate who casts the entire type’s mass. At every history \(h\) ending with proposal \(x\), let \(V_{h,t}\) be type \(t\)’s expected continuation utility if \(x\) is rejected. The induced rule must accept when \(\sum_{t:u_t(x)>V_{h,t}}\mu_t\ge q_x\), and reject when \(\sum_{t:u_t(x)\ge V_{h,t}}\mu_t<q_x\); in the remaining cases, the delegates’ votes must be consistent with the quota and equilibrium conditions. State the search complexity in \(|X|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the binary input length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A population distribution \(\mu\) over types, with one strategic delegate per type. The delegates’ yes-votes carry their types’ masses, and proposal \(x\) is accepted when yes-vote mass reaches \(q_x\). Proposals follow \(P\); rejection continues the stopping game, and the task is to find an equilibrium-inducing tuple of two-step plans.

What the mirror covers

Covers the existence and search for a two-step-game-plan collective equilibrium corresponding to Theorem 9.1, including the finite-threshold refinement in Lemma 9.13; leaves the paper’s other equilibrium, robustness, and application results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is equilibrium search fixed-parameter tractable in \((\tau,|X|)\) for binary-encoded rational inputs?
  • How does this delegate model relate to finite electorates with the same type proportions, and can the paper’s equilibrium incentives survive without binding bloc votes?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. The paper has no named theorem asserting an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. Its closest algorithmic anchor is Theorem 9.1, because the paper gives a finite construction for the existence result. If “computational result” here requires a formal complexity classification, then this paper has no qualifying anchor.

Theorem 9.1, proved in the paper, says that \(T\) is nonempty: every collective stopping game in the paper’s model has a collective equilibrium induced by two-step game-plans. By Theorem 6.3, such an equilibrium yields a subgame-perfect equilibrium in pure, action-independent strategies. The proof constructs one by iteratively eliminating alternatives and game-plans; the construction can be restricted to finitely many candidate thresholds. This is an existence and computability result, not a polynomial-time bound.

The mirror I would hand to a prover is Weighted-Type Collective-Stopping Equilibrium Search. An instance has a finite proposal set \(X\), a rational, irreducible Markov transition matrix \(P(y\mid x)\), and a finite set \(T\) of constituency types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type has a rational utility \(u_t(x)\geq 0\) for every proposal and a delegate who casts its bloc’s vote. For each \(x\), acceptance requires yes-votes representing at least a rational quota \(q_x\) of the population. The input size is measured in \(|X|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the bit length \(L\).

The requested output is any tuple of two-step game-plans: a main plan and a punishment plan for each proposal. A plan has sets \(A\subseteq B\subseteq X\) and a threshold \(k\): accept proposals in \(A\) during the first \(k\) periods, then proposals in \(B\). After a proposal \(x\) is rejected, let \(V_{x,t}\) be type \(t\)’s expected continuation utility under the induced strategy. At every history ending in \(x\), the strategy must accept if
\[ \sum_{t:\,u_t(x)>V_{x,t}}\mu_t\geq q_x, \]
and must reject if
\[ \sum_{t:\,u_t(x)\geq V_{x,t}}\mu_t<q_x. \]
In the remaining cases, indifferent types can make either decision consistent with the quota. A solution is a plan tuple satisfying these conditions at every history; the task is to find one, with no additional optimization objective.

A plausible regime is a large association, union, or political organization whose members fall into a small number of stable preference groups, each represented by a delegate with a binding bloc vote. The society might have millions of members but only a few dozen types, with \(\mu_t\) recording each group’s share. Proposals arrive through an exogenous agenda process. This keeps the paper’s finite proposal set and stopping-game incentives while making population mass determine which coalitions can pass a proposal.

This is a recognizable extension of the authors’ collective-choice and coalition-formation model: the weighted quota defines a nonempty, monotone family of decisive coalitions, and the equilibrium question remains theirs. The mass-based quota is the necessary co-modification when the represented population becomes continuous. My expectation is that the fixed-support version belongs on the tractable, Class A side, with an FPT algorithm in \((\tau,|X|)\) as a useful target. The paper’s construction supports computability, but it does not establish that parameterized bound—or any polynomial running time. Binary-encoded masses could still make the full problem harder.

The weakest point is the bloc-voting assumption. The theorem’s strategic players are individuals, while this mirror gives each type’s delegate control over a positive mass of votes. If members vote individually in an atomless population, a single person’s vote has no effect on acceptance; the paper’s pivotal-voter incentives may disappear. The mirror is most plausible for institutions where groups really do vote through binding representatives. Further questions are whether the search problem is FPT in \((\tau,|X|)\), whether finite electorates with the same type proportions approximate its equilibria, and whether an individual-voter continuum can retain the paper’s incentives under a suitable equilibrium concept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole plausible anchor is Theorem 9.1. It gives a constructive existence result: a finite procedure finds a two-step equilibrium. But it gives no running-time bound in the number of players, alternatives, or input bit length. The proposed equilibrium-search problem therefore asks a new question about the theorem’s construction; it does not mirror a complexity result the paper establishes. That question could be worthwhile, but its relevance to a programme about continuous-population computation needs a further argument.

That argument runs into a problem with voting incentives. In an atomless society, one voter’s ballot has zero mass, so changing it cannot affect whether a proposal meets the quota. The individual deviations that discipline behavior in the paper’s equilibrium concept then cease to do so. Ballots can be chosen to produce different aggregate acceptance decisions, but the equilibrium conditions no longer explain why voters choose them.

The proposed fix—one delegate per type, casting the type’s entire mass—restores pivotality by making each type a strategic bloc. But that changes the strategic units: members who were individual voters in the paper now act as a coordinated group. For that institutional model, the paper’s arbitrary monotone decisive-coalition framework already accommodates the weighted quota, and Theorem 9.1 gives the existence conclusion. A search-time bound in the encoding of the weights could still be a new result, but it would concern a finite weighted voting game. The continuum supplies the weights; it does not supply a new equilibrium mechanism.

This is the strongest negative case, not an airtight one. A polity with binding, weighted delegates is plausible, and authors studying collective choice and coalition formation might recognize its equilibrium-search problem. So I cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. The case against is that the natural atomless mirror loses the paper’s incentive structure, while the version that preserves it relies on an added bloc-delegation institution and leaves only a finite weighted-game complexity question.
