The continuous mirror question

Given item kinds \(X\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supplies \(q_x\in[0,1]\), and rational additive pairwise values \(d_{t,u}(x)\), find a mass allocation \(z_{t,B}\ge 0\) to integral bundles \(B\subseteq X\) satisfying \(\sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B\ni x}z_{t,B}=q_x\). For every two occupied cohorts \((t,B)\) and \((u,C)\), require subsets \(S_B\subseteq B\) and \(S_C\subseteq C\) with \(|S_B|+|S_C|\le K\) such that \(d_{t,u}(B\setminus S_B)\ge d_{t,u}(C\setminus S_C)\); copies of the same kind in the two bundles count separately. Does a deterministic algorithm, polynomial in \(|X|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length, always return a polynomial-size allocation with \(K=\lceil C\sqrt{\tau}\rceil\) for a universal constant \(C\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic high-multiplicity fair-division model: type mass \(\mu_t\) is split among integral bundles, item kind \(x\) has per-capita supply \(q_x\), and \(d_{t,u}\) measures type-\(t\)'s pairwise envy toward type \(u\). The objective is to minimize the worst pairwise number of physical copy occurrences that must be deleted.

The objection that survived

The proposed draft counts deleted item kinds, whereas Theorem 3 counts physical items; the mirror should count each copy occurrence in the two bundles separately.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive allocation guarantee of Theorem 3 and its externalities-model corollary. It leaves the finite-population lower bound, the EF1 counterexamples, and the consensus and truthfulness results open.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can every such instance be allocated in polynomial time with worst-pair relaxation \(O(\sqrt{\tau})\), independent of population scale?
  • For fixed \(\tau\), is the required relaxation bounded by a constant, and what is its sharp dependence on \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a type-compressed, nonatomic version of the paper’s externalities model. Imagine a very large research consortium distributing standardized equipment or grant lots among researchers in a small number of fields. A researcher’s type records their own value for each item and how they value it going to researchers of each field. Researchers of the same type have identical valuations; the number of agents can grow while the number \(\tau\) of types stays small. To make the continuum meaningful, item supplies scale with population: \(q_x\) is the mass of agents receiving one copy of item kind \(x\). Each individual still receives an integral bundle.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper using a cited multicolor discrepancy theorem and its polynomial-time algorithm. It says that for any \(n\)-agent asymmetric-envy instance with additive valuations, including signed valuations, there is a partition of the items into \(n\) bundles such that, for every ordered pair of distinct agents, envy can be eliminated by discarding \(O(\sqrt n)\) items from their two bundles. Such a partition can be computed in deterministic polynomial time. Proposition 1 connects this envy formulation to the paper’s externalities model.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Type-Compressed Continuum EF Relaxation. The input is a finite set \(X\) of item kinds, a finite set \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_x\in[0,1]\), and rational additive pairwise values \(d_{t,u}(x)\). The value \(d_{t,u}(x)\) is the gain to a type-\(t\) agent from having \(x\) in their own bundle rather than in the bundle of a type-\(u\) agent; it can be represented in the paper’s externalities model as \(V_t(t,x)-V_t(u,x)\).

A solution assigns mass \(z_{t,B}\ge 0\) of type \(t\) to each integral bundle \(B\subseteq X\), with
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq X} z_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T,\,B\ni x}z_{t,B}=q_x. \]
For every two occupied cohorts \((t,B)\) and \((u,C)\), the solution must provide a set \(S\subseteq B\cup C\) of at most \(K\) item kinds such that
\[ d_{t,u}(B\setminus S)\ge d_{t,u}(C\setminus S). \]
The computational question is whether a deterministic algorithm, polynomial in \(|X|,\tau\), and the input bit length, can return a finite, polynomial-size cohort allocation satisfying this condition with \(K=\lceil C\sqrt{\tau}\rceil\) for some absolute constant \(C\).

I expect this problem to be tractable, in the sense of a Class A result: adapt discrepancy or continuous-optimization methods to the weighted supplies and cohort masses. The reason to expect \(\sqrt{\tau}\) is that the relevant envy constraints are indexed by types and directed type pairs, rather than by a population whose size can grow without bound. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 3: its coloring argument assigns individual items to a finite set of agents, and does not directly produce a mass allocation respecting per-capita supplies.

The mirror covers the constructive EF-\(c\) allocation question of Theorem 3. It does not carry over the paper’s \(\sqrt n\) bound as stated; it asks what replaces \(n\) when many agents share a small support of types. Natural next questions are whether an \(\Omega(\sqrt{\tau})\) lower bound exists in this regime, or whether fixed \(\tau\) permits a constant relaxation independent of population size.

The weakest point is the resource co-modification. Scaling item supplies and allowing each type’s mass to split among integral bundles is essential to define a nontrivial nonatomic allocation with per-capita resources. It is plausible for the consortium scenario, and preserves the paper’s swap-based envy test, but the authors may see it as a distinct fair-division model. Also, the paper’s Theorem 4 is a lower bound on the relaxation needed in some finite \(n\)-agent instances, not a computational-hardness result. I would not present it as an NP-hardness anchor; its useful role here is to motivate the question of whether the lower bound tracks type support or disappears in the continuum regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed mirror’s fit, not to the existence of any mirror. Theorem 3 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for an EF relaxation. But its model allows arbitrary pair-specific externalities \(V_i(j,x)\). A complete type for agent \(i\) must therefore describe how \(i\) values items assigned to every recipient. Keeping the number of such types fixed as the population grows requires externalities to become constant across recipient classes. That may fit the consortium story, but it is a real restriction on the paper’s model.

The proposed resource scaling adds another change: copies of each item kind grow with the population. And the paper counts discarded physical items, while the proposed condition counts item kinds. If two compared bundles contain separate copies of the same kind, deleting that kind from both may remove two physical items. Defining the relaxation over copies would repair the mismatch, but it underscores that this is a newly specified allocation economy, not a direct continuization of Theorem 3. Its algorithmic question is still coherent; the theorem’s coloring proof, which uses \(n\) agent colors and pairwise valuation constraints, does not itself establish a bound in \(\tau\).

Theorem 4 is weaker support for the proposed regime. Its discrepancy construction uses \(\Theta(n)\) valuation sets, so it does not establish a lower bound for fixed type support. Asking whether a lower bound tracks \(\tau\) is a reasonable follow-up, but the paper’s construction does not make that question more or less compelling.

Those points make the proponent’s expected \(O(\sqrt{\tau})\) bound a hypothesis, not a consequence of the paper. They do not defeat the better version of the mirror: a finite-type population with recipient-class externalities and per-capita supplies is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and Theorem 3 gives it a clear computational question. I can make a case that this mirror needs substantial model work, but I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists.
