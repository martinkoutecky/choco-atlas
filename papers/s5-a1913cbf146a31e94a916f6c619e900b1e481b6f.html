The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest assessment is that this paper has no qualifying anchor: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition, and makes no complexity claim such as NP-hardness or polynomial-time solvability. Its computational results are finite calculations of power indices, reported in Tables 3–8 and performed with AL.EX4 and ipdirect. There is therefore no named computational result here from which to give the required one-mirror-per-anchor account.

The closest reported result is Table 8, computed in this paper. For the December 2014 Presidential election, the authors use the weighted game \(G=[180;129,71,33,20,18,17,12]\) and report Coleman’s power-to-prevent values \(0.02, 0.36, 0.06, 0.23, 1, 0.11, 0.11\) for KKE, SYRIZA, DIMAR, PASOK, ND, ANEL, and GD, respectively. They interpret these figures as showing that the enhanced \(60\%\) quota gives the opposition substantial blocking power. This is a relevant computational finding, but it is a table of results for one seven-party game, not a numbered complexity result.

A plausible adjacent question, motivated by that blocking concern, is Presidential Blocking Control\(_\infty\). Consider a very large assembly where many delegates share a small number \(\tau\) of complete types: each type specifies the delegate’s current voting stance and the cost per unit of mass to switch from yes to no. The instance gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\), a quota \(q\), a required safety margin \(\varepsilon>0\), and costs \(c_t\). Choose how much yes-voting mass \(x_t\) to switch, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) for types currently voting yes, to minimize
\[ \sum_t c_t x_t \]
subject to the remaining yes mass being at most \(q-\varepsilon\). The solution is the transfer vector and its cost. A large convention or union congress with many delegates in recurring caucus and voting-cost types gives this a plausible high-multiplicity regime: the number of delegates is large while \(\tau\) stays moderate. The resulting optimization is a linear program, so I would expect this question to be tractable.

That is a credible continuous blocking-control question, and the paper’s \(60\%\)-quota discussion gives it a substantive foothold. It covers the concern in Section 5 about the opposition’s ability to block an action. The Coleman index itself is harder to mirror: it counts pivotal coalitions, and the paper gives no canonical way to assign a probability or measure to coalitions in an atomless population. The proposed control problem does not reproduce that index calculation or the 2012 power rankings. Its weakest point is therefore also decisive under the requested standard: it is an extension inspired by the paper’s application, not a continuous version anchored by one of the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest foothold is Table 8, but it is a computation for one fixed seven-party game, not a theorem or algorithmic result. The paper states no complexity classification for computing power indices. More importantly, its closest proposed mirror, Presidential Blocking Control\(_\infty\), changes the question: it adds a campaigner, switching costs, and an intervention. Coleman’s index instead counts how often a party is pivotal across winning coalitions. Minimizing the cost of moving enough yes-voting mass to defeat a quota does not preserve that coalition-based measure of power.

A more faithful mirror would ask for continuous versions of the paper’s power indices. That runs into a modeling choice the control proposal sidesteps. If parties remain the players, there are still seven distinct actors; their seat shares are weights, not a high-multiplicity population. If MPs become the population, splitting party blocs lets their members join coalitions independently, which changes the paper’s party-level bargaining game. And in an atomless version, a single member has zero mass: adding them cannot swing a quota. Coleman’s finite counts of swings and coalitions no longer directly define an index. One can choose a coalition-sampling rule or a finite-game scaling and study the resulting limit, but that choice supplies new semantics rather than carrying the table’s calculation into the continuum. Continuum-player Shapley value theories do exist, including constructions based on limits of finite games, but they do not by themselves select a continuum Coleman index or reproduce the paper’s coalition counts. ([Aumann and Shapley, *Values of Non-Atomic Games*](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/values-of-non-atomic-games); [Neyman, *Values of Games with Infinitely Many Players*](https://ratio.huji.ac.il/files/dp247.pdf).)

So the proposed control problem is a plausible new continuous voting-control project, and a deliberately designed asymptotic power-index problem could also be worthwhile. Neither is a continuous computational result this paper establishes: the first changes its object, and the second requires a new definition before its computational question is set. The negative case is strongest against calling either one a mirror of this paper. It is weaker as a universal claim: a large assembly with repeated delegate types could make continuous power measurement a sensible follow-on, and the authors might recognize it as related work. I would reject the proponent’s anchor as stated, but I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuation of the paper’s subject.
