The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of surplus schools \(S\), deficit schools \(D\), and teacher types \(T\), each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), origin \(o(t)\), and acceptable destinations \(A_t\subseteq D\). Given per-capita release capacities \(\alpha_s\) and initial deficits \(\beta_d\), choose transfer masses \(x_{t,d}\geq 0\) for \(d\in A_t\), subject to \(\sum_{d\in A_t}x_{t,d}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t:o(t)=s}\sum_{d\in A_t}x_{t,d}\leq\alpha_s\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,d}\leq\beta_d\). For residual deficits \(h_d=\beta_d-\sum_t x_{t,d}\), compute a feasible transfer whose residual vector Lorenz-dominates every other feasible residual vector.

The model it lives in

A capacitated flow network with one node per teacher type, source-to-origin capacities \(\alpha_s\), type capacities \(\mu_t\), arcs to acceptable deficit schools, and sink capacities \(\beta_d\). The decision variables are transfer masses \(x_{t,d}\); the objective is to minimize every sorted partial sum of the residual-deficit vector in the Lorenz-dominance sense.

The objection that survived

The paper already computes the fractional optimum efficiently, so the type-level formulation appears to be an exact aggregation of a step in its algorithm and leaves out the integer-rounding and strategy-proofness results.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lorenz-optimal deficit computation over mass transfers. It leaves out the paper’s integral rounding result and Proposition 4’s strategy-proofness result.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s Lorenz-optimal deficit computation. It has a plausible large-market interpretation, and the paper itself already develops the real-flow problem this mirror needs. The case is modest, though: it does not continue the paper’s distinctive integer-rounding result.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2, printed as “The MDR algorithm produces a Lorenz dominant transfer.” Its algorithmic foundation is attributed to Dutta and Ray (1989) and Megiddo (1974); the paper’s Appendix A proves the extension it needs, showing Lorenz dominance against all achievable deficit vectors, not only those arising from maximum flows. In the paper’s real-flow stage, the result means that the algorithm produces a feasible, generally fractional post-transfer deficit vector whose sorted partial sums are no larger than those of any other feasible vector. The theorem does not itself state a complexity class. The unnumbered Remark 1 says the computation is efficient, using a lexicographically optimal flow algorithm and maximum flow. So this is an algorithmic anchor, with the polynomial-time claim supplied by the remark.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Fix finite sets of surplus schools \(S\) and deficit schools \(D\). The input gives a finite support \(T\) of teacher types. A type \(t\) records the teacher’s current surplus school \(o(t)\) and her complete set \(A_t\subseteq D\) of acceptable destination schools; add qualifications or other relevant attributes to the type if the instance uses them. The input also gives each type’s mass \(\mu_t\), each surplus school’s releasable mass \(\alpha_s\), and each deficit school’s initial deficit \(\beta_d\), all as binary rationals in a common population scale.

Choose mass transfers \(x_{t,d}\geq 0\) for \(d\in A_t\), subject to \(\sum_{d\in A_t}x_{t,d}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t:o(t)=s}\sum_{d\in A_t}x_{t,d}\leq\alpha_s\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,d}\leq\beta_d\). The residual deficit is \(h_d=\beta_d-\sum_t x_{t,d}\). The question is to compute a feasible transfer \(x\) such that \(h\) Lorenz-dominates every other feasible residual-deficit vector: for every \(k\), the sum of the \(k\) largest entries of \(h\) is no greater than the corresponding sum for any other feasible vector. A solution is the transfer masses and their resulting residual-deficit vector.

The regime I find credible is a large education authority or comparable redeployment system with many teachers but relatively few recurring combinations of origin school, acceptable destinations, and qualifications. For example, tens of thousands of teachers might fall into hundreds or a few thousand such types. The mass of a type is the fraction of the workforce in that category; capacities and deficits are measured in the same per-capita units. Because the type population is nonatomic, \(x_{t,d}\) describes how much of that group is assigned to \(d\): each individual teacher still goes to one school and receives a whole assignment. The type must include everything about a teacher that affects feasibility or preferences; this model does not erase relevant individual differences.

I expect this problem to be tractable: it is a rational capacitated-flow problem with a Lorenz-optimal objective, computable through lexicographically optimal flow methods. The paper’s own fractional-flow stage makes the connection particularly plausible: its authors already compute such an aggregate deficit vector as part of their solution. The mirror changes the representation from a node per named teacher to a node per type with capacity \(\mu_t\). It does not make a teacher fractionally appointed.

This covers the aggregate Lorenz-optimal deficit computation in Sections 2–3. It does not cover the paper’s integer rounding result or Proposition 4’s strategy-proofness result. Further questions include whether finite populations approximate the continuous optimum with a useful deficit-error bound, and whether any meaningful incentive property survives when reports and transfers are modeled at the type-mass level.

The weak point is that the paper’s operational problem insists on indivisible transfers, and its contribution includes carefully rounding a fractional solution while preserving Lorenz dominance. The mirror removes that difficulty. It is therefore a credible continuous-population analogue of the paper’s aggregate optimization step, but a less compelling continuation of its central integer result—and it does not expose discrete hardness that might dissolve in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper already solves the proposed continuous problem. The regime is plausible: many teachers can share a type consisting of their origin school and acceptable destinations. But in that regime, replacing individual teachers with type masses does not create a new optimization problem.

Sections 3.1–3.4 explicitly allow real-valued flows and construct a Lorenz-dominant fractional deficit vector \(h^\star\). Appendix A proves that it dominates every achievable fractional deficit vector, and Remark 1 says the computation is efficient. If \(n_t\) teachers share type \(t\), their individual flow nodes can be merged into a type node with capacity \(n_t\): any feasible aggregate flow can be distributed among those identical teachers, and individual flows can be summed back into a type flow. Scaling rational masses, capacities, and deficits to a common denominator gives the same network problem. The resulting Lorenz ordering is preserved by scaling, and the compressed network has one node per type. Thus computing a Lorenz-dominant mass transfer is the paper’s fractional-flow computation in type-level notation.

The paper also carries that computation into its discrete problem: it uses the fractional optimum to construct an integral Lorenz-dominant transfer. So a continuous mirror of the aggregate deficit computation would repeat a step already inside the paper’s algorithm, while leaving out the distinctive rounding result. Calling that step a population-continuum model is a reasonable interpretation, but it does not make the computational question new.

This defeats the proposed anchor as a worthwhile *new* mirror. It does not support the stronger universal claim that no scenario could yield one. The paper’s repeated teacher types make high multiplicity quite credible, and extensions involving new constraints or objectives could pose fresh questions. A continuum version of individual strategy-proofness is a less promising extension: a single teacher has zero mass and cannot change the outcome, so individual truthfulness risks becoming vacuous. Overall, the negative case is strong for the stated aggregate-optimization anchor, but too narrow to establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
