There is still a plausible *unanchored* mirror in the paper’s optimal-split subproblem. Consider many agents in a few role types, with type \(t\) making up rational mass \(a_t\). Agents of types \(t,u\) share a pairwise synergy \(w_{tu}\), and the interaction graph is complete, as in the paper’s evaluated setting. Define
\[ q(z)=\sum_{t<u}w_{tu}z_tz_u+\frac12\sum_t w_{tt}z_t^2. \]
The continuous split problem takes \((a,w)\) as input and asks for \(x\in[0,a]\) maximizing \(q(x)+q(a-x)\); \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) sent to one side, and \(a_t-x_t\) is the mass sent to the other. A solution is an optimizing mass vector. The objective is the paper’s sum of pairwise synergies, normalized by the square of population size. This mirrors the underlying split decision, not its QUBO encoding.

A plausible regime is a large organization forming teams from many repeated role classes, where pairwise synergy depends on roles. Then \(n\) can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of types, and splitting a role class by mass is meaningful. That is a credible ISG regime, but it is not the paper’s sampled benchmark regime: independently sampled edge weights generally give agents distinct interaction profiles, so \(\tau\) may grow with \(n\). The proposed model covers only the split subproblem, not the full coalition-structure algorithm or its hardware runtime and approximation claims. I would not assign it to the tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hard class from this paper alone; its cited discrete hardness does not show that indivisibility-free mass splits remain hard.

The weak point is therefore decisive for an anchor-based case: the paper offers a recognizable continuous candidate, but no qualifying named result to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative begins with the anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational guarantee. Its NP-hardness statements are attributed to earlier work; its \(O(n^2)\) runtime and approximation figures are experimental claims about a particular quantum device. So there is no named computational result here for ChoCo to continuize.

The proposed optimal-split mirror is still a real candidate, but it depends on a much narrower model than the paper’s general weighted graphs. For role types to determine the mass-level objective, every pair of agents in the same two role classes must have exactly the same synergy. In a generic graph, an agent’s interaction profile is part of its complete type, so the number of types can grow with the population. Coarsening agents by role then changes the game. The paper’s independently sampled edge weights do not support that coarsening, and its sparse-graph setting adds connectivity constraints that a type-mass vector alone does not capture. A complete graph with role-based weights is conceivable, but it is a constructed special case.

That special case also does not continuize the paper’s hardware claims: the measured runtime and solution quality concern encoding finite binary assignments on a particular annealer. The recursive coalition-structure problem raises a further issue. With divisible mass and no minimum coalition size, negative same-type synergies can reward splitting mass into ever smaller coalitions; a full continuum version may need extra constraints to ensure an optimum exists. Those constraints would define a different problem. The one-step split avoids that degeneracy, but remains an unanchored subproblem rather than a continuization of the paper’s reported algorithmic guarantees.

This is a solid case against treating GCS-Q as a strong, paper-anchored target for ChoCo. It does not establish the universal negative the task asks for: a society with repeated roles and deterministic role-pair synergies makes the proposed split model coherent. On the supplied paper alone, the honest negative is therefore limited: it weakens the case for this paper, but cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror in that regime.
