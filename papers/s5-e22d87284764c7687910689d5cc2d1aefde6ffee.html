The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(I\) and \(J\) on the two sides, rational masses \(p_i,q_j\), strict rankings of counterpart types and being unmatched, and a communication relation \(H\subseteq I\times J\), choose match masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\). Require row and column sums to respect type masses, and \(x_{ij}=0\) unless the match is mutually acceptable. Let \(u_i=p_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=q_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\); for each \((i,j)\in H\), define \(b^I_{ij}=\mathbf 1[j\succ_i\bot]u_i+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(b^J_{ij}=\mathbf 1[i\succ_j\bot]v_j+\sum_{\ell:i\succ_j\ell}x_{\ell j}\). Require \(b^I_{ij}=0\) or \(b^J_{ij}=0\), and maximize \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). Under UGC, is there no polynomial-time algorithm achieving approximation ratio \(3/2-\epsilon\) for every constant \(\epsilon>0\), with time measured in the type counts and mass bit length?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-sided market with finitely many preference-and-access types, rational mass per type, and complete communication blocks between type pairs in \(H\). A feasible mass matrix \(x\) respects supplies and mutual acceptability; for each channel pair, at least one side has zero mass preferring the other type to its assignment. The objective is total matched mass, and the input size is measured by the number of types, \(|H|\), and the binary encoding length of the masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers maximum-cardinality socially stable matching, including continuous analogues of Theorem 3.2, Theorem 4.3, and Corollary 4.4. It leaves out markets with arbitrary individual-specific communication edges and the paper's proposed edge-removal variant.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-level mass problem remain NP-hard when assignments may split rational mass?
  • Does a polynomial-time \(3/2\)-approximation extend to this mass model?
  • Does the UGC-based \(3/2-\epsilon\) inapproximability survive divisible assignments?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the exact maximum socially stable mass matching problem, anchored by Theorem 4.3. In the paper, Theorem 4.3 proves that Soc-Stable is NP-hard, via a reduction from Independent Set and Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2. That result is proved here. The mirror I would ask is whether the optimum mass matching can be computed efficiently when each side of a large matching market is described by a small number of preference and communication types.

Take finite sets of types \(I\) and \(J\), one for each side. Type \(i\) has mass \(p_i\), type \(j\) has mass \(q_j\), and both masses are binary rationals. A type specifies a strict ranking over counterpart *types*, including the option of being unmatched, and its pattern of access to the other side. Let \(H\subseteq I\times J\) be the type pairs with a direct communication channel: every member of type \(i\) can communicate with every member of type \(j\) exactly when \((i,j)\in H\). Split types as needed so preferences and channel access are the same for everyone in a type.

A solution is a nonnegative matrix \(x_{ij}\): \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\). Its row and column sums cannot exceed the respective type masses, and \(x_{ij}=0\) unless the match is acceptable to both types. Write \(u_i=p_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=q_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\) for unmatched mass. For a channel pair \((i,j)\), let
\[ b^I_{ij}=\mathbf 1[j\succ_i\bot]u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}, \qquad b^J_{ij}=\mathbf 1[i\succ_j\bot]v_j+\sum_{\ell:\,i\succ_j\ell}x_{\ell j}. \]
These are the masses on each side who would prefer the other type to their current assignment. Require \(b^I_{ij}=0\) or \(b^J_{ij}=0\) for every \((i,j)\in H\). With complete access between the two types, positive mass on both sides would supply an actual social blocking pair. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). Because the populations are nonatomic, any such matrix can be realized by matching individual agents, even though the type-level assignment is fractional.

The Theorem 4.3 question is the threshold version: given this instance and a rational \(B\), is there a socially stable \(x\) with \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\ge B\)? The continuous version should be studied in time as a function of the number of types, the channel pairs, and the mass bit length. I expect exact optimization to remain hard, likely because the social type graph still forces combinatorial choices and the no-blocking constraints are disjunctive. I would call that a plausible Class C obstruction, not transferred hardness: the paper’s integral reduction does not by itself prove hardness when type masses can split.

Two results sharpen the same question. Corollary 4.4 says that, assuming the Unique Games Conjecture, Soc-Stable cannot be approximated within \(3/2-\epsilon\) for any constant \(\epsilon>0\). The corollary is derived in this paper from its reduction and the UGC-based Independent Set hardness in cited reference [4]. Its continuous mirror asks whether, for every such \(\epsilon\), the mass problem also has no polynomial-time algorithm guaranteeing at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/(3/2-\epsilon)\). I expect a type-graph hardness barrier may survive, but the fractional assignments could destroy the reduction’s gap; a gap-preserving proof is needed before claiming the same bound.

Conversely, Theorem 3.2, proved here, says that the paper’s algorithm returns a socially stable matching of size at least two-thirds of maximum, giving a \(3/2\)-approximation. The mirror asks whether a polynomial-time algorithm in the type-count and input bit length can return a socially stable mass assignment of value at least \(2\mathrm{OPT}/3\). I expect an aggregate deferred-acceptance approach may yield this bound, though the paper’s individual-agent algorithm does not establish that directly. Together, these anchors pose a useful continuous complexity landscape: exact optimization may stay hard, a \(3/2\)-approximation may remain attainable, and the UGC barrier may or may not persist.

A plausible regime is a large annual placement market with many applicants and positions but relatively few recurring profiles: applicants share rankings over position categories and referral-channel patterns; position types share rankings over applicant categories and access patterns. For example, the market might have hundreds of thousands of applicants and slots but only dozens or hundreds of distinct types. Mass then means a share of the cohort. This is a high-multiplicity question about the population itself: fractional \(x_{ij}\) records what proportion of each type is paired, while each actual agent still receives one match or remains unmatched. It does not make the question continuous by fractionalizing the outcome alone.

The mirror covers the paper’s maximum-cardinality socially stable matching problem and its approximation and hardness results. It does not claim that every market fits the regime. Its weakest point is the coarsening: the paper ranks named agents and allows an arbitrary social graph, while this mirror ranks counterpart types and assumes all-or-none access between a pair of types. That is a substantive model change. The case for it is that preferences and communication access are part of a type’s complete description, so a large market with repeated profiles is a coherent high-multiplicity instance regime; matching fractions are exactly the aggregate representation of its many interchangeable agents. A direct follow-up is whether bounded-degree or tree-structured type-channel graphs make the mass problem tractable, and whether the paper’s Independent Set reduction can be made robust to divisible mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that multiplicity erases the paper’s defining input. Its social graph is arbitrary, and its reduction from Independent Set puts the source graph’s structure into agent-specific communication edges and preferences. In a type-based mirror, a channel between two types means every member of one type can communicate with every member of the other. That replaces a sparse graph of individual contacts with complete bipartite blocks. A type that preserves an individual’s exact neighborhood will usually contain only one agent in a social graph without many repeated neighborhoods. The proposed placement-market scenario can make block access plausible, but it describes a restricted network model rather than a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s general one.

That weakens Theorem 4.3 as an anchor. Its reduction does not establish hardness for the mass problem: divisible assignments can split the mass associated with a gadget, so the reduction’s all-or-nothing choices may disappear. The theorem does motivate asking whether a type-level mass problem is hard, but that is a new question about a restricted, coarsened family. It is not a continuous version whose computational character is supported by the paper’s proof.

Corollary 4.4 inherits the same gap. Its UGC inapproximability comes from the Independent Set reduction, so it gives no approximation barrier for divisible mass unless a separate gap-preserving argument rules out fractional solutions. The corollary supplies no independent evidence for that stronger mirror.

Theorem 3.2 is the most plausible mirror and the least persuasive reason to choose this paper for the programme. The alternating-path argument suggests a route to a mass version, and deferred acceptance has natural aggregate formulations for groups with common preferences. That route still needs an algorithm with running time polynomial in the number of types and mass bit length: expanding rational masses into individual copies may take exponential time. So the mirror is well posed, but the paper offers a likely blueprint rather than a result that settles it.

The best repaired mirror would retain a continuum population while representing communication through structured blocks, or through a finite description of heterogeneous connection patterns. That can be a sensible model, and it avoids the objection that matching tracks individual identity: the objective is total matched mass. Yet its relevance depends on the market. Dense, repeated communication blocks may fit some cohort systems; the paper’s motivating examples of friends, prior transactions, and personal contacts more naturally produce sparse, individual-specific edges. A richer network limit could represent those cases, but it would need its own finite input language and complexity parameters.

Nearby continuum matching work does not close the question. Azevedo and Leshno study stable outcomes in a continuum matching market, but their framework does not address maximum socially stable matching with graph-restricted blocking pairs. [Their continuum matching framework](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/) is evidence that continuous matching models are sensible, not evidence that this computational problem is already solved.

So the negative case can make the three anchors look weaker than the proponent suggests: the hardness and UGC claims do not transfer, and the approximation mirror may be a direct aggregate lift. It cannot honestly establish the requested universal conclusion. A plausible finite-type channel model still gives a computational question about maximum matched mass, and the paper contains no continuous treatment that answers it. The case against is strongest as a warning about model fit and novelty, and weak as a claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
