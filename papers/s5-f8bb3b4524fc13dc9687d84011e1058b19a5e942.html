Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 7 — survived: The continuous version is a well-posed core-membership problem over fractional masses of retailer types, and its violation test is an LP.

The continuous mirror question

Given a population distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) retailer types, where each type \(t\) has a rational nonnegative demand \(d_t(\omega)\) in each finite scenario \(\omega\) with rational probability \(q_\omega\), and a rational allocation rate \(a_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t \mu_t a_t=C(\mu)\), decide whether there is a coalition mass vector \(x\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) such that \(\sum_t x_ta_t>C(x)\), where \(C(x)=\min_{y\ge0}2\sum_\omega q_\omega\left|\sum_t x_td_t(\omega)-y\right|\).

The model it lives in

A society is the distribution \(\mu\) over complete scenario-demand types. A coalition chooses mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type; its cost is \(C(x)\), and the violation objective is \(\sum_t x_ta_t-C(x)\). For the anchored cost parameters, optimizing this objective is an LP.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 7, for the newsvendor setting with \(c=0\) and \(p=h=2\). It leaves the paper’s core-existence results and their constructive allocation methods outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do polynomial-time core-membership algorithms extend to nonzero ordering costs or transportation costs when the society has small type support?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It states that TMOC is NP-hard: given a cooperative newsvendor game and an efficient allocation \(l\), decide whether some coalition \(S\) has \(\sum_{j\in S}l_j>C(S)\), so the allocation is outside the core. The reduction uses the especially simple setting \(c=0\), \(p=h=2\), and two equally likely demand scenarios. It constructs an allocation with \(n+1\) equal charges and grand-coalition cost \(1\); a violating coalition exists exactly when the Subset Sum instance has a zero-sum subset. This is a hardness result about finding a coalition of indivisible retailers, not about a complicated inventory model.

My mirror is Continuous Newsvendor Core Violation. Consider a population of retailers with total mass \(1\), divided among \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\). A type is a retailer’s complete per-unit demand profile across a finite set of shared market scenarios \(\Omega\); all retailers have the anchor’s costs, \(c=0\) and \(p=h=2\). Let \(q_\omega\) be the rational probability of scenario \(\omega\), and \(d_t(\omega)\) the demand per unit mass of type \(t\). A coalition is any measurable subpopulation, represented by masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Its cost, normalized per unit of total population, is
\[ C(x)=\min_{y\ge 0} 2\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}q_\omega \left|\sum_{t}x_t d_t(\omega)-y\right|. \]
Given a rational allocation rate \(a_t\) per unit mass, with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=C(\mu)\), ask whether the allocation is in the core: does every \(x\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) satisfy \(\sum_t x_ta_t\le C(x)\)? A solution to the violation version is a mass vector \(x\) that breaks this inequality, or a certificate that none exists.

This continuous question is an LP. Maximize \(\sum_t x_ta_t-2\sum_\omega q_\omega u_\omega\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(y\ge0\), and
\[ u_\omega\ge \sum_t x_t d_t(\omega)-y,\qquad u_\omega\ge y-\sum_t x_t d_t(\omega). \]
Its optimum is positive exactly when a violating coalition exists; it is always at least zero because \(x=0,y=0\) is feasible. Thus core membership is decidable in polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(|\Omega|\), and the input bit length. The expected classification is tractable. For a finite retailer instance with type-symmetric charges, assigning each retailer mass \(1/n\) gives this per-population cost game after dividing costs and total charges by \(n\).

The population regime is plausible: a large retailer network can have many outlets but comparatively few complete demand and cost profiles. The paper itself notes that newsvendor cores have been studied even with infinitely many retailers, citing Montrucchio and Scarsini [22]. That supports this population version as a sensible object; it does not answer its computational core-membership question. The shared-scenario demand representation also fits the paper’s allowance for correlated demands and its own two-scenario hardness construction. I expect the authors would recognize this as their core-stability question for a continuum of retailers, with measurable subpopulations allowed to secede.

The scope here is deliberately just Theorem 7; I would not claim to have mirrored the paper’s duality-based core-existence results. The main follow-up is whether similar algorithms survive nonzero ordering costs, transportation, or richer demand models, and whether the constructive allocation results also extend.

The weak point is exactly where the tractability comes from: this mirror lets a coalition take a fraction of a type, while Theorem 7’s reduction depends on choosing each retailer in or out. If indivisible retailers are essential to the intended question, this is a relaxation. But for a genuinely continuous population, divisible subpopulations are the natural coalition notion, and removing that subset-selection integrality is the point of the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can make a case that this is a narrow ChoCo fit, but I cannot honestly defeat the Theorem 7 mirror. The paper is in stochastic operations research, and its type would be a retailer’s full scenario-contingent demand profile and cost parameters, not a preference ranking. Such profiles may be nearly all unique in some networks, leaving support size \(\tau\) close to the number of retailers. And the reduction uses the special case \(c=0\), \(p=h=2\). Those are real limits on the mirror’s reach, but none rules out a plausible high-multiplicity regime with many retailers sharing a small number of profiles.

For the stated newsvendor case, the proposed continuous question is well formed. A type can specify its demand in each finite scenario, and a coalition can take mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type. Its cost is
\[ C(x)=\min_{y\ge0}2\sum_{\omega}q_\omega\left|\sum_t x_t d_t(\omega)-y\right|. \]
For a given allocation rate \(a_t\), core violation is the maximization of \(\sum_t x_ta_t-C(x)\). Replacing the absolute values with epigraph variables gives the stated LP: maximizing over \(y\) is correct because subtracting the minimum cost is equivalent to maximizing the negative cost over \(y\). With rational finite input, it is solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\), the number of scenarios, and the bit length.

The reduction’s discrete subset choice becomes divisible mass in that model. That changes the answer to the membership question, but it is the point of a continuous-population mirror, not a reason to reject the question. Nor does the use of \(c=0\) make the direction vacuous: with a shared order quantity and linear per-unit ordering cost, adding \(cy\) to the cost preserves the LP structure. Repeated retailer profiles are also a credible regime, and the paper’s cited infinite-retailer newsvendor work supports that regime rather than colliding with the computational membership question.

The prior continuous newsvendor results establish core existence; they do not settle whether a *given allocation* belongs to the core. So they do not defeat this anchor. The strongest negative verdict I can support is that the mirror is specialized, and its benefit depends on instances with moderate profile support. I cannot support the requested universal claim that there is no worthwhile mirror: this one survives, and under the programme’s stated criteria it is a credible tractable mirror of Theorem 7.
