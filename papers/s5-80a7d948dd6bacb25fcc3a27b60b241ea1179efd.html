The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), distribution \(\mu\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\) of item kinds \(g\in G\), additive valuations \(v_\theta(b)=\sum_g w_{\theta,g}b_g\), and a target type \(\theta\), compute the largest \(z\) such that for every \(0\le\kappa<1\) and every removable mass distribution over integral configurations \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{G}\) of total mass \(\kappa\), supported on configurations with \(v_\theta(b)<z\), the residual supply can be assigned to mass \(1-\kappa\) using only integral configurations with value at least \(z\). In the bounded-catalogue regime, output \(z\) or a certificate allocation.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fair-division market with type mass \(\mu\), integral configurations \(b\), per-capita supply \(q\), and mass-allocation variables \(x_{\theta,b}\ge0\); the objective is the maximum residual-feasible value threshold \(z\), with supply constraints \(\sum_{\theta,b}b_gx_{\theta,b}\le q_g\).

The objection that survived

The exact equivalence between finite RMMS and the proposed mass-residual definition is asserted rather than proved, and the target-type threshold can be independent of \(\mu\); a formal limit theorem must establish that this is the intended high-multiplicity object.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition \(3.13\)'s RMMS-value and feasible-partition tasks, but not the paper's structural existence theorems, EEFX+EFL results, or restricted-additive \(O(m^2)\) allocation algorithm.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(\operatorname{RMMS}_\infty\) equal the limit of finite RMMS instances with \(Q\) agents and \(Qq_g\) copies of each item kind?
  • Is exact continuous RMMS computation polynomial-time for bounded \(|G|\), and what is its complexity when \(|G|\) is unbounded?
  • Can residual feasibility be represented by a compact configuration-LP separation oracle?
  • What additive loss results when a rational mass allocation is rounded to \(Q\) actual agents?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity, continuous-population version of exact RMMS computation, anchored in Proposition 3.13. I would regard this as a qualified but genuine mirror of the paper’s computational contribution.

Proposition 3.13 is proved in this paper. It states that, for additive valuations, computing the RMMS value is weakly NP-hard, and that computing a feasible \(n\)-partition is likewise weakly NP-hard. The proof reduces from the weakly NP-hard Partition problem: when \(n=2\), RMMS equals MMS, and for larger \(n\) the construction adds \(n-2\) exceptionally valuable items. Thus the hardness is not caused by a large number of named agents; it already appears with two agents and many indivisible goods.

I would call the continuous problem Continuous Residual-Maximin Share. Its regime is a large allocation market—say, a university course-allocation system—with \(N\) students, \(N\) large, but only \(\tau\) recurring valuation types, where \(\tau\ll N\). A type is a complete additive valuation vector over a finite catalogue of item kinds: two students have the same type exactly when they value every course or resource identically. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types.

Because a fixed finite set of goods cannot serve a continuum of agents, the natural co-modification is to give each item kind \(g\) a rational per-capita supply \(q_g\). In a finite realization with \(Q\) students, there are \(Qq_g\) copies of \(g\). Goods remain indivisible for each individual student: a student receives an integral bundle, never \(0.37\) of a course. What becomes fractional is only the mass of identical students receiving each integral bundle.

Formally, a configuration is an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\), and a population allocation uses variables \(x_{\theta,b}\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,b}b_gx_{\theta,b}\le q_g\) for every item kind \(g\). Thus \(x_{\theta,b}\) is the mass of type-\(\theta\) agents receiving bundle \(b\).

For a target type \(\theta\), define \(v_\theta(b)=\sum_g w_{\theta,g}b_g\). Given a threshold \(z\), call \(z\) continuously residual-self-feasible if, after removing any mass \(\kappa<1\) of bundles whose value to \(\theta\) is strictly below \(z\), the remaining item supply can be assigned to mass \(1-\kappa\) using only integral bundles of value at least \(z\) to \(\theta\). The Continuous RMMS Value problem asks for the largest such \(z\), denoted \(\operatorname{RMMS}_\infty(\theta;q)\). Its certificate version asks, for a given \(z\), for a mass partition into integral configurations satisfying the threshold; the case \(\kappa=0\) is the direct analogue of the feasible \(n\)-partition in Proposition 3.13.

This is recognisably the paper’s problem. The paper defines RMMS by asking whether, after removing low-valued bundles, the remaining goods can still be partitioned into sufficiently many valuable bundles. The mirror replaces the integer number of removed agents by removed population mass and replaces a finite partition by a distribution over integral bundles. It preserves additive valuations, indivisible individual bundles, the RMMS threshold, and the residual-feasibility invariant. It does not replace fair division by divisible-goods allocation.

The population regime is plausible. Large student cohorts, employees receiving bundles of benefits, or residents receiving combinations of scarce services often contain many agents with a small number of repeated preference profiles. For rational \(\mu\) and \(q\), multiplying by a common denominator gives an ordinary finite high-multiplicity instance, and every finite allocation induces a rational mass allocation. Conversely, a rational mass allocation can be implemented by sufficiently many agents. This gives the required finite-to-continuous dictionary rather than merely an analogy.

My lead prediction is that the fixed-catalogue version, with a bounded number of item kinds, should be tractable: its natural formulation is a configuration LP, and the relevant configuration subproblems can plausibly be handled by dynamic programming, column generation, or fixed-dimension integer optimization. In that regime, Proposition 3.13 would provide a meaningful Class A experiment: the discrete Partition obstruction may disappear because a positive-mass subset of identical agents can receive one integral bundle while another subset receives a different integral bundle.

The unrestricted exact version is more delicate. With binary-encoded values and an unbounded number of item kinds, pricing configurations resembles knapsack, so weak hardness may remain. If it does, that would be a continuum-specific Class C boundary: the named-agent multiplicity has disappeared, but combinatorics in the indivisible goods and the residual condition remain. Either outcome is informative. A polynomial algorithm would show that the paper’s weak NP-hardness was an artefact of finite-agent integrality; a hardness result would identify precisely where population continuization stops helping.

The main follow-up questions are whether \(\operatorname{RMMS}_\infty\) has an exact polynomial algorithm or only an FPTAS, whether residual feasibility admits a compact separation oracle, how to round a mass allocation to \(N\) actual agents with bounded additive loss, and whether the paper’s simultaneous EEFX+EFL existence theorem survives for atomless type distributions.

My weakest point is that the paper defines RMMS for a finite number \(n\) of agents, whereas the residual-mass definition above is a genuinely new extension. An opponent can argue that fractionalizing the population changes the combinatorial object too much. The answer is that this is precisely the intended high-multiplicity move, while keeping bundles and goods integral at the individual level. Still, the extension is not forced uniquely by the paper, so I would present this as a serious continuous research question anchored by Proposition 3.13—not as a result already implicit in the authors’ theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3.13 is the strongest possible anchor for the affirmative case, but it is also where the negative case is clearest: the hardness being mirrored is not population hardness.

The proposition already uses \(n=2\). Its reduction is the indivisible one-copy-per-good Partition obstruction: can a fixed collection of weighted goods be split into two equal-valued bundles? There is no large electorate, repeated agent type, or interaction among many agents in that construction. If one holds \(M\) fixed and lets the population grow, the mirror degenerates: once \(n>|M|\), every partition has an empty bundle, so \(\operatorname{MMS}=0\) and hence \(\operatorname{RMMS}=0\).

The proposed repair—introducing per-capita supplies \(q_g\) and repeating every good proportionally—is coherent, but it is not determined by the paper. It replaces the paper’s finite-good problem by a new asymptotic resource-packing model. Different choices of \(q\) produce different limits, and the original instance supplies no canonical \(q\). The rational finite-to-continuous dictionary only shows that this invented model can be implemented by replicated instances; it does not show that it is the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s RMMS problem.

The best version of the proposed mirror still has this difficulty. Its input is effectively \((q,v)\), while the population distribution \(\mu\) is irrelevant to the RMMS value for a target type. The continuous society is therefore not the object being computed over. What remains is a fractional configuration or bin-packing problem: mass may be distributed among integral bundles, while item kinds are supplied fractionally across the population. That is a legitimate research problem, but it is a new high-multiplicity packing problem rather than a population-continuization of the paper’s computational result.

This distinction matters because the finite RMMS benchmark is about partitioning one fixed indivisible set of goods among named agents. The proposed limit simultaneously changes the number of agents, the number of goods, their multiplicities, and the allocation from a partition to a distribution over configurations. Those changes may produce an interesting Class A or Class C boundary, but the paper gives no computational theorem whose population axis is being relaxed.

So the negative case is not airtight: the \(q_g\)-model is mathematically sensible and could be worthwhile on its own. What it cannot honestly claim is that Proposition 3.13 provides a strong continuous-population mirror. At best, it motivates a new asymptotic configuration-packing problem inspired by RMMS. That is a substantially weaker case for ChoCo’s programme.
