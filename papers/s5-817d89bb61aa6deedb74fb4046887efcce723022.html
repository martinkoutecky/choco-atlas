The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(x\in C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported first-choice-and-approval types \((f,S)\), with \(f\in S\subseteq C\), determine the exact set of \(k\geq 1\) for which \(x\) is a \(\beta(k)\) winner, including ties.

The model it lives in

Represent the population by masses \(\mu_{(f,S)}\) over ballot types. For each candidate \(C_j\), compute the first-choice share \(P_j=\sum_{(f,S)}\mu_{(f,S)}\mathbf{1}[f=C_j]\) and approval share \(A_j=\sum_{(f,S)}\mu_{(f,S)}\mathbf{1}[C_j\in S]\); its score is \(A_j+(k-1)P_j\). Return the target’s interval of winning \(k\)-values by intersecting its pairwise score inequalities.

What the mirror covers

Covers the exact \(k\)-ranges for which candidates can win, corresponding to Theorem 3.6; it does not mirror the paper’s other voting criteria or results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow one: the paper’s Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper, gives an exact winner range as the first-choice weight \(k\) varies. The paper has no named theorem asserting a complexity class such as NP-hardness or membership in P. Theorem 3.6 is a computationally useful characterization, though, and it is the best anchor here.

In full, Theorem 3.6 assumes a set \(Y=\{C_1,\ldots,C_r\}\) of candidates who can win for some \(k\geq 1\), with \(r>2\), ordered so that their plurality scores satisfy \(p_1<p_2<\cdots<p_r\) and their approval scores run in reverse order, \(a_1>\cdots>a_r\). It says that candidate \(C_w\) wins exactly when
\[ 1+\frac{a_{w-1}-a_w}{p_w-p_{w-1}} \leq k \leq 1+\frac{a_w-a_{w+1}}{p_{w+1}-p_w}. \]
For \(w=1\), omit the lower bound; for \(w=r\), omit the upper bound. The bounds are inclusive, so ties count as winning. The theorem supplies the range from the neighboring candidates’ score differences.

A plausible mirror is Continuous \(\beta\) Winner-Parameter Range. There are \(c\) candidates and a finite set of voter types \(T=\{(f,S): f\in S\subseteq C\}\), where \(f\) is the voter’s first choice and \(S\) is the set of candidates they approve. An instance is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the supported types and a target candidate \(x\). The mass \(\mu_{(f,S)}\) is the fraction of the population with that ballot. Define
\[ p_j=\sum_{(f,S)\in T}\mu_{(f,S)}\mathbf{1}[f=C_j], \qquad a_j=\sum_{(f,S)\in T}\mu_{(f,S)}\mathbf{1}[C_j\in S]. \]
The task is to return the exact set of \(k\geq1\) for which \(x\) is a \(\beta(k)\) winner, with ties included. Its score is \(a_j+(k-1)p_j\). Each candidate’s winning set is therefore an interval, possibly empty or unbounded; its endpoints can be given exactly from the rational input.

For the ordered candidates in Theorem 3.6, the mirror asks for precisely the same adjacent-score interval, now with \(p_j\) and \(a_j\) computed from population masses. More generally, the target’s interval can be found by intersecting its pairwise score inequalities. This takes polynomial time in \(c\), the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\); that tractability claim is an inference from the formulation, not a complexity result stated by the authors. I would expect this mirror to be Class A, with a direct affine-inequality calculation rather than a difficult optimization step.

The regime is a large election with a modest candidate slate: for example, a national or organizational election with many voters but far fewer distinct first-choice-and-approval patterns than voters. Voters sharing a type are indistinguishable for the winner-range question, and mass expresses percentages directly. This fits the paper’s own interest in elections with many voters and few candidates. Its authors should recognize the problem: the paper explicitly studies which candidates can win and the ranges of \(k\) for which they do, given approval and plurality information.

This covers the paper’s parametric winner-range result in Section 3, not its voting-criteria claims or its discussion of possible strategies. A further question is how the range changes when a small amount of population mass shifts between ballot types. That would be a separate robustness problem.

The weak point is substantial: the paper does not establish a hardness result, and this mirror does not test whether continuous populations dissolve discrete hardness. Since the original range calculation already depends only on aggregate scores, replacing vote counts with normalized masses largely preserves the same calculation. The case for a mirror is that this is a genuine continuous-population formulation of the paper’s own central question; it is not a case that the paper opens a new complexity frontier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is the paper’s strongest computational anchor, so the negative case should focus on it. Its continuous mirror is sensible: a large election can have repeated first-choice-and-approval ballots. That makes this a weak case for the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.

The problem is that the proposed mirror is already answered by the theorem’s calculation. If \(p_j,a_j\) are the count scores and \(P_j=p_j/n,\ A_j=a_j/n\) are the corresponding population shares, then
\[ A_j+(k-1)P_j=\frac{a_j+(k-1)p_j}{n}. \]
Dividing every candidate’s score by \(n\) preserves all winners and every \(k\)-threshold. The proof of Theorem 3.6 compares affine score functions; it does not rely on scores being integers. For rational population shares, its result therefore carries over directly. Computing the shares from \(\tau\) supported types is just summation.

Even the stronger version that handles every candidate, without Theorem 3.6’s ordering assumptions, reduces to intersecting pairwise inequalities:
\[ (k-1)(P_x-P_j)\ge A_j-A_x \quad\text{for each candidate }j. \]
That gives the exact winning interval, including ties and equal slopes. It takes polynomially many rational operations in the support size and candidate count. There is no pricing problem or hidden search over ballot types. This makes the proposed mirror a change from counts to proportions, rather than a new computational question the programme needs to investigate.

A more ambitious proposal might ask how much population mass must move between ballot types to make a candidate win. With an explicit transfer-cost table, that is a linear program over the type-to-type transfers and candidate score margins. It is a valid continuous problem, but it adds a new intervention and cost model that the paper does not study. In this paper’s finite ballot space, the resulting formulation is already an explicit LP; its difficulty would come from added constraints or a richer cost model, not from the winner-range result.

So the best negative case is narrow: the sole anchor is a named characterization, but its continuous version is already implicit in its real-valued score calculation, and the obvious extensions either repeat that calculation or add a different problem. I cannot honestly make that into a strong case against *every* possible continuous mirror. The high-multiplicity election regime is plausible, and a distinct continuous intervention model could be worth studying. The case against this particular anchor is that its mirror offers little new computational work.
