A type’s mass is its fraction of the electorate, and the aggregate margin is the mass for \(1\) minus the mass for \(-1\). The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

That distinction is decisive here. Theorem 2.1, Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.1, Theorem 4.2, and Theorem 5.3 characterize voting rules by axioms. They do not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. The simulation chapter reports comparisons, but supplies no numbered computational result either.

The three-type population is a sensible high-multiplicity model: each voter has one of three ballot types, and the society can be represented by their masses. But that makes a new computational question *possible*; it does not turn an axiomatic characterization into one. The strongest version would scale the dissertation’s fixed vote margin with population size, giving a threshold on \(\mu_1-\mu_{-1}\). That is a valid population mirror of the rule’s definition, but computing its outcome is just a weighted sum and threshold check. Asking how much mass must move to change the winner would be a new robustness or campaigning problem, not a result this dissertation establishes.

The infinite-voter theorem is an even poorer computational anchor. Its classification uses cardinality distinctions such as finite versus infinite support and co-finite abstention. A distribution of total mass one does not retain those distinctions; finitely many individual votes have zero mass. Scaling the threshold into a per-capita quantity could yield a sensible new rule, but it would no longer mirror the theorem’s cardinality-based characterization. The fuzzy-voting theorem has a related problem: its intensities vary continuously, but there are still finitely many individually indexed voters, and the result is axiomatic. Replacing them with a mass distribution over intensities yields an integral threshold rule, not a computational result from the paper.

The simulation is the nearest candidate, but its probability of disagreement comes from sampling finite voter profiles. In a fixed continuous society, the type masses are already specified, so the vote totals are deterministic. Keeping random fluctuations would make the question about noise in sampled profiles; introducing random state-level populations or mass transfers could be interesting, but each adds a new problem rather than mirroring a named result here.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: it offers no direct, result-anchored computational mirror, and its most natural continuous reformulations lead to axiomatic questions or newly invented computational ones. I cannot honestly prove the universal claim that no worthwhile adjacent question could be devised; the three-type model makes several imaginable. But those possibilities do not rescue a continuous computational mirror of this dissertation’s stated results.
