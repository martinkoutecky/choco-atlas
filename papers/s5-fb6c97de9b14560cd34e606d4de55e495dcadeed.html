The continuous mirror question

Given two candidates \(a,b\), finitely many ordered stages, rational masses of cohorts \(t=(r,p,w,\sigma)\) with \(\sigma\in\{a,b\}\), a revealed history, and budget \(B\), where future ballot masses are adversarially revealed subject to known stage, price, and weight masses: does there exist a nonanticipating policy that, after each revelation, transfers mass between ballots, pays \(p\) per transferred unit while preserving its weight \(w\), and guarantees that \(a\) is a weighted-Plurality co-winner for every future revelation?

The model it lives in

A finite-stage high-multiplicity online election over types \(t=(r,p,w,\sigma)\), with mass \(\mu_t\), integral ballots for each mass unit, transfer variables \(x_{r,p,w,\sigma,\sigma'}\in[0,\mu_t]\), cumulative objective \(\sum p\,x\le B\), and a nonanticipating policy required to guarantee the final weighted-Plurality outcome.

The objection that survived

The mirror batches \(M\) identical voters into a single revelation and bulk intervention, so it is not the literal individual-by-individual \(M\)-copy game; an exact individual-arrival limit would require a separate measure-valued policy model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the natural Partition-based priced weighted Plurality and Approval hardness results, especially Theorems \(5.4\) and \(5.7\). It leaves the general \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\) and \(\Pi^p_{2k+1}\) results, artificial election systems, most unpriced variants, and the Veto complexity gaps untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-stage two-candidate mirror be solved exactly by an \(\mathrm{LP}\) for arbitrary finite type sets and histories, including the flexible desired-set objective?
  • What formal limit connects cohort revelation to the individual-arrival game, and can policies be rounded with additive loss?
  • Which richer voting rules or price-weight structures yield transferred hardness rather than the expected Class \(A\) behavior?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s priced-and-weighted online bribery problems. The lead anchor is Theorem 5.4.

I would call the mirror Continuous Online Plurality \(\$\)-Weighted Bribery\(_\infty\). Fix two candidates, an ordered set of \(R\) voting stages, and cohort masses \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_R\) with \(\sum_r\lambda_r=1\). A type is a complete tuple \(t=(r,p,w,\sigma)\): stage \(r\), per-unit bribe price \(p\), per-unit voting weight \(w\), and intended ballot \(\sigma\). The mass of type \(t\) is \(\mu_t\). Each individual unit of mass still casts an integral ballot; only the number of interchangeable agents is continuous.

The briber knows the order, cohort masses, prices, and weights. When stage \(r\) arrives, the current cohort’s ballot distribution is revealed. The briber may transfer a mass \(x_{t,t'}\) from its revealed type \(t\) to any output-ballot type \(t'\), paying \(\sum_{t,t'}p(t)x_{t,t'}\). The transfer must be chosen from the current revelation only, and the cumulative cost must not exceed \(B\). Future ballot distributions remain unknown and are chosen adversarially, exactly as future votes are universally quantified in the paper.

The question is whether there exists a nonanticipating mass-transfer policy that guarantees that the designated candidate is a Plurality co-winner for every admissible future revelation. The destructive version asks whether the policy guarantees that a specified candidate is not a winner. A solution is the policy, not merely a final population profile.

A particularly faithful finite-type version takes each cohort to consist of \(M\) identical copies of one paper-voter type. The continuous action \(x_r\in[0,\lambda_r]\) is the limit of bribing \(Mx_r\) of those copies. Thus the regime is, for example, a large party convention or union roll call with many near-identical delegates in each ordered district or delegation. With \(R\) cohorts, the population has \(MR\) agents but only \(O(R)\) realized types; \(M\) can be very large. The order of stages remains finite and discrete. It is time and the outcome space that stay discrete; the population is what becomes continuous.

The paper’s Theorem 5.4, proved in this paper, states that \(\mathrm{online}\)-Plurality-\(\$\)Weighted-Bribery and its destructive counterpart are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with two candidates. The constructive hardness reduces from Partition by giving voter \(i\) price and weight \(s_i\), with \(\sum_i s_i=2S\), and asking whether a set of whole voters of total price at most \(S\) can supply enough weight to change the winner.

This is an excellent continuization target because the source of hardness is visibly indivisibility. In the mirror, the corresponding variables are \(x_i\in[0,\lambda_i]\), rather than \(x_i\in\{0,\lambda_i\}\). On the worst-case branch, every not-yet-bribed voter supports the opponent. If \(\Delta_0\) is the current weighted score margin of the desired candidate, the robust constructive question reduces to whether there are masses \(x_i\) satisfying \(0\le x_i\le\lambda_i\), \(\sum_i p_i x_i\le B\), and \(\Delta_0-\sum_i w_i\lambda_i+2\sum_i w_i x_i\ge0\). This is a fractional covering problem, equivalently a linear program or fractional knapsack problem, and is solvable in polynomial time for rational input.

So my expectation is Class A: the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness of Theorem 5.4 disappears in the continuous population model. The online aspect does not become vacuous. The policy still observes the current cohort before acting, cannot see future revelations, and must survive the worst case. What disappears is the Partition obstruction caused by having to buy an entire weighted voter. A favorable future revelation only helps, so the all-opponent revelation is the critical policy path.

The second anchor is Theorem 5.7, also proved here. Its first bullet states that unpriced and one-dimensional online bribery for Approval—unpriced, destructive unpriced, priced unweighted, destructive priced unweighted, weighted unpriced, and destructive weighted unpriced—are all in \(\mathrm{P}\). Its second bullet states that priced weighted Approval-Bribery and destructive priced weighted Approval-Bribery are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. I use the second bullet as the additional hardness anchor.

The corresponding problem, Continuous Online Approval \(\$\)-Weighted Bribery\(_\infty\), has the same cohort-and-policy structure, except that a ballot is an approval vector in \(\{0,1\}^{m}\). A bribe transfers mass to any approval vector. Constructively, the policy must guarantee that some candidate in the briber’s preferred set is a co-winner; destructively, it must guarantee that no candidate in the hated set wins.

For a fixed intended winner \(g\), the paper’s structural observation gives the continuous formulation. Bribed mass approves every desired candidate, while adversarial nonbribed mass approves every relevant undesired candidate and does not approve \(g\). If \(x_i\) is the bribed mass of cohort \(i\), then the score comparison against an undesired candidate \(h\) has the form \(S_g^0+\sum_iw_ix_i\ge S_h^0+\sum_iw_i(\lambda_i-x_i)\), together with \(\sum_i p_ix_i\le B\) and \(0\le x_i\le\lambda_i\). Try each possible \(g\); each case is a linear program. The destructive version uses strict score inequalities against the hated candidates.

I would therefore expect this mirror also to be in \(\mathrm{P}\), with the discrete \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness again attributable to whole-voter selection. This is not merely a fractional outcome model: every mass unit receives an integral approval vector, while aggregate approval scores are fractional because the society contains many interchangeable agents.

The authors should recognize both mirrors as their problems. The order of revelation, use-it-or-lose-it intervention, known prices and weights, adversarial future information, budget, weighted winner rule, and constructive/destructive objectives are all retained. The only substantive change is that a large population of indistinguishable voters can be acted on by mass rather than only one named voter at a time. That is exactly the high-multiplicity question: does the problem remain hard when \(M\) copies of each relevant voter type are present and bribes can be allocated at population scale?

This does not claim to mirror the whole paper. The general PSPACE and \(\Pi^p_{2k+1}\) results in Theorems 4.1, 4.4, and 4.5 are driven by alternating information revelation and, in the lower bound, an artificially engineered election system. The strongest continuous case concerns the natural systems in Section 5, specifically the hardness results whose reductions use indivisible price-weight pairs.

The weakest point is that batching a homogeneous cohort may be judged to alter the online game: the original paper makes a separate decision for every named voter, whereas the mirror permits a fractional decision over a cohort. I think this is defensible because the cohort model is the limit of \(M\) sequentially revealed identical voters, and the programme explicitly treats the removal of indivisibility as the phenomenon to investigate. But the mirror should state that correspondence openly, and should separately study the variant where mass is revealed one infinitesimal unit at a time. The natural further questions are whether discrete policies can be rounded with additive loss, exactly which price-weight structures yield an LP, and whether increasing the candidate set or using more complex voting rules restores hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a modelling objection, but it does not support the universal conclusion.

Theorem 5.4’s Partition reduction relies on a separate, indivisible decision for each voter. If each voter \(i\) is replicated \(M\) times, the paper’s online game has \(M\) separate use-it-or-lose-it decisions, hence \(M\) times as many alternating revelations and interventions. The proponent’s model instead reveals an entire cohort and permits a transfer \(x_i\in[0,\lambda_i]\). That is not merely a high-multiplicity encoding of the paper’s game: it changes the temporal granularity, reveals many votes simultaneously, and permits a collective intervention unavailable to the original briber.

The literal individual-by-individual limit does not repair this cleanly. A single arriving agent has vanishing mass, weight, and cost. To obtain a nonzero intervention one must specify a measure-valued, nonanticipating policy over a continuum of arrival times. That is a continuous-time adversarial control problem, not the paper’s finite sequential election and not merely a continuous population. Alternatively, retaining finitely many stages requires cohort revelation and bulk bribery—the proponent’s different model. Thus the proposed LP is best understood as a fractional relaxation of the Partition construction, not as the high-multiplicity limit of online bribery.

The same objection applies to Theorem 5.7. Its priced-weighted Approval hardness is inherited from the same indivisible price-weight pair construction. Once a cohort’s mass can be fractionally bribed, the Partition obstruction disappears, but that says only that a different bulk-intervention problem has been defined. Moreover, the proponent’s “try each desired candidate \(g\)” formulation does not generally capture the paper’s flexible constructive objective: an adaptive policy might guarantee that some desired candidate wins without guaranteeing the same \(g\) on every revelation branch. A singleton-target restriction repairs that defect, but leaves the timing mismatch untouched.

This is nevertheless not enough to sustain the requested universal negative. A better mirror can legitimately define a fixed number of sequential cohorts, reveal each cohort’s aggregate ballot distribution, and allow mass transfers after revelation. The stages remain discrete, the population is genuinely continuous, and the problem retains prices, weights, budget, adversarial future information, and a nonanticipating policy. For two-candidate Plurality, the worst future revelation is monotone, so the resulting problem really does reduce to a fractional covering or knapsack LP. Approval admits an analogous fixed-target formulation. These are coherent high-multiplicity problems, and the authors would recognize them as online bribery at population scale.

Their answers being easy is not a negative argument: a Class A result is precisely one of the programme’s intended outcomes. Nor is the use of high-multiplicity work an objection. The honest conclusion is therefore that the written mirrors are vulnerable as claimed continuizations of the paper’s exact individual-level game, but the repaired cohort versions survive. I cannot honestly defeat every worthwhile mirror of Theorems 5.4 and 5.7; the universal “no continuous mirror” case is weak.
