The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no anchor: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about the complexity of a computational problem. Its main results are finite calculations of Banzhaf and Shapley–Shubik indices, coalition-class counts, and a normal approximation. Those are mathematical analyses, but they do not pose the algorithmic questions ChoCo is organized to classify. The suggestion to study high-multiplicity councils is therefore a new direction, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

That direction is the strongest candidate. Its basic obstacle is that the paper’s agents are the member states; their populations are voting weights. Replacing them with a continuum of citizens changes who votes. A more plausible model would use many interchangeable council members grouped by weight. But for a member of a type to be interchangeable, its weight and all other relevant attributes must match its peers. As the number of members grows, each member’s share of total weight goes to zero. A single member’s exit then has vanishing effect on the quotas and on the other members’ power, under ordinary smoothness conditions.

Banzhaf power has a sharper limit problem. A member is pivotal only when the coalition’s weight lies in an interval whose width is that member’s weight. As individual weights shrink, that pivotal probability goes to zero; in the nonatomic model, one member changes no mass at all. The normalized Banzhaf index then requires a limiting convention, since its underlying individual swing probabilities vanish. One can define a rescaled, type-aggregate power measure from a sequence of finite councils. That may be a useful asymptotic voting-power project, but it depends on the sequence, quota rounding, and the chosen rescaling. It no longer answers the paper’s question about how the power of a remaining state changes when one named state exits.

The finite structure is central to the reported effects: there are only 28 states, the quota changes discretely, and six states account for over 70% of the population weight. Replicating weight types until each member has negligible weight smooths away those features. Keeping a few states with positive limiting weight preserves them, but leaves a finite set of pivotal agents outside the high-multiplicity limit. A positive-mass cohort exit is another defensible model; it asks about a bloc leaving and its aggregate influence, rather than the single-state exits analyzed here.

So the negative case is strongest on scope: no computational-complexity result in the paper supplies a ChoCo anchor, and the best proposed high-multiplicity extension loses the single-member pivotality that drives its subject. The boundary is real: a carefully chosen asymptotic power measure or bloc-exit model could be worth studying. The paper itself gives little reason to expect that work to be a continuous mirror of its results.
