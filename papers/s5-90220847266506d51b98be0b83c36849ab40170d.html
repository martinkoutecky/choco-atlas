The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational mass distribution \(\mu\), complete values \(a_{tq}\) over a finite good catalogue \(Q\), exact MIV predictions \(p_t\), and a finite adaptive stream of per-capita batches \((q_r,\nu_r)\), compute or decide feasibility of a nonanticipating policy that visibly transfers mass \(y_{t,B}\) between integral bundle states \(B\) and \(B\cup\{q_r\}\). At the end of every allowed stream, every positive-mass state must satisfy \(a_t(B)+\max_{g\in G\setminus B}a_t(g)\ge\alpha V_t(G)\), where \(V_t(G)=\sum_r\nu_ra_{tq_r}\); maximize \(\alpha\) or determine whether a prescribed \(\alpha\) is achievable.

The model it lives in

A finite-type, high-multiplicity online fair-division model with population mass \(\mu\), integral microscopic bundles, a measure-valued state \(x_{t,B}\), irrevocable mass transfers \(y_{t,B}\), exact MIV predictions, and an individual rather than type-average \(\mathrm{PROP1}\) objective.

The objection that survived

The mass state \(x_{t,B}\) may require exponentially many bundle-history configurations, and the cases do not settle whether aggregate batch observation preserves the paper's per-item adaptive adversary. This could invalidate the claimed finite-type tractability, but it does not invalidate the well-posed individual-integral mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the exact-MIV \(19/30\)- and \(n/(n+\kappa)\)-\(\mathrm{PROP1}\) guarantees of Theorem 4.1. It leaves the fully online impossibility, simultaneous envy results, random-rule bounds, and chore results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the potential argument of Theorem 4.1 lift to observable per-capita batch transfers while preserving individual \(\mathrm{PROP1}\)?
  • Can policies be computed in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|Q|\), the horizon, and the input bit length despite the bundle-history state?
  • What rounding theorem relates continuum policies to finite \(N\)-agent realizations, and do the \(19/30\) and \(1/(1+\sigma)\) guarantees survive?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified Class A mirror anchored in Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. I would not use Theorem 3.1 as the formal anchor: it is an important online-fairness impossibility, but not a complexity classification in the programme’s usual sense.

Theorem 4.1 says that, with exact maximum-item-value predictions \(p_i=\max_g v_i(g)\), the paper gives a deterministic online algorithm against adaptive adversaries, without knowing the horizon, that always returns a \(19/30\)-\(\mathrm{PROP1}\) allocation. Its rule maintains normalized slacks \(s_i\) and assigns each arriving good to an agent maximizing the potential decrease
\[ f(s_i+\rho-\rho z_i)-f(s_i+\rho+(1-\rho)z_i), \]
with \(\rho=19/(30n)\) and \(z_i=v_i(g)/p_i\), subject to the special treatment of the first maximum-valued good. The theorem also proves that, if an upper bound \(\kappa\) on the number of agents positively valuing each good is supplied, a reciprocal-potential rule gives \(n/(n+\kappa)\)-\(\mathrm{PROP1}\); choosing between the two rules gives \(\max\{19/30,n/(n+\kappa)\}\)-\(\mathrm{PROP1}\). The theorem is proved here, not merely cited.

My lead mirror is \(\mathsf{MIV\text{-}PROP1}_\infty\). Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type specifies its value \(a_{tq}\in[0,1]\) for every good class \(q\) in a finite catalogue \(Q\). Thus two agents of type \(t\) are indistinguishable for every valuation and prediction relevant to the problem. Before the stream starts, the algorithm receives an exact prediction \(p_t\) for every type, where \(p_t\) is the largest value of any good class that will actually arrive.

An adaptive stream presents batches \((q_r,\nu_r)\), where \(q_r\in Q\) and \(\nu_r\) is the mass of identical indivisible copies arriving at stage \(r\). Supplies scale with the population: in an \(N\)-agent realization, the batch contains \(N\nu_r\) copies. At each stage the policy must allocate that batch irrevocably. Its state is \(x_{t,B}\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents whose current integral bundle is the multiset \(B\). An action is a mass transfer \(y_{t,B}\) satisfying \(0\le y_{t,B}\le x_{t,B}\) and \(\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}=\nu_r\); it moves mass \(y_{t,B}\) from \(B\) to \(B\cup\{q_r\}\). Every microscopic agent receives either the whole good or nothing. Only the aggregate action is continuous.

Write \(V_t(G)=\sum_r\nu_r a_{tq_r}\). The policy must guarantee, for every adaptive stream and every occupied state \(x_{t,B}>0\), that almost every agent in that state satisfies
\[ a_t(B)+\max_{g\notin B}a_t(g)\ge \alpha V_t(G). \]
The maximum is over actual goods outside that agent’s bundle, so this is genuinely \(\mathrm{PROP1}\), not a type-average relaxation. The computational task is to decide whether such a nonanticipating mass policy exists for a prescribed \(\alpha\), and, if so, compute a succinct policy or maximize \(\alpha\). A natural support parameter is
\[ \sigma=\max_q\sum_{t:a_{tq}>0}\mu_t, \]
the mass analogue of \(\kappa/n\); the expected continuous counterpart of the theorem’s second guarantee is \(1/(1+\sigma)\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s own applications: millions of users, tenants, or departments sharing a small catalogue of valuation profiles, while ad impressions, recommendation slots, compute jobs, or service requests arrive in per-capita quantities. One could have \(N\) in the millions and \(\tau=|T|\) in the tens or hundreds. The type contains the complete valuation profile over all relevant good classes, so the model does not incorrectly treat named agents with different prices as identical.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. Online arrival, adaptive information, irrevocability, additive values, exact MIV predictions, and the \(\mathrm{PROP1}\) objective are unchanged. The only co-modification is the necessary per-capita scaling of goods. Without it, a continuum population would make every single good have zero mass and the problem would become vacuous. We also do not fractionalize bundles: the continuous variable records which mass of agents receives each indivisible copy.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the finite-type, finite-good-catalogue regime. The proof of Theorem 4.1 already uses a potential that is a sum over agents. In the mirror, that sum becomes
\[ \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}f(s_{t,B}+\rho). \]
The recipient choice becomes a threshold or water-filling transport problem over current bundle states. With rational masses, denominator clearing gives a finite high-multiplicity instance; conversely, finite allocations induce empirical mass states. A configuration-LP or column-generation formulation could therefore compute the aggregate policy independently of the population denominator. The main prediction is that the \(19/30\) guarantee survives exactly, while the \(\kappa\)-variant becomes \(\max\{19/30,1/(1+\sigma)\}\).

The mirror generates worthwhile follow-up questions: can the policy be represented in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|Q|\), the horizon, and the bit length, rather than in the population size? Can one prove an \(O(1/N)\) or additive rounding theorem from mass policies to finite populations? Is \(19/30\) optimal in the continuum, and does the exact MIV prediction permit \(\mathrm{PROP1}\) itself? More ambitiously, does the fully online impossibility of Theorem 3.1 disappear precisely because a batch can be split across agents with different current slacks?

The weakest point is that the lifting from Theorem 4.1 is not automatic. The theorem allocates one arriving item to one named agent, whereas the mirror allocates a per-capita batch across a distribution of bundle states. Establishing that the potential argument survives this mass-flow operation is a genuine new theorem. If a referee rejects per-capita batches as fractionalizing goods, the mirror fails; if fairness is weakened to type-average fairness, it also becomes too easy. The case stands only under the stricter interpretation above: integral bundles for every microscopic agent, continuous mass only in the population and its aggregate allocation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror does not yet establish a worthwhile continuous problem. Its central difficulty is that Theorem 4.1 is not really a theorem about a static population of types. It is a theorem about the evolving allocation history of each named agent.

The potential uses \(B_i^t\) and \(s_i^t\). Two agents with the same initial valuation type cease to be interchangeable as soon as one receives a good and the other does not. In the proposed mirror, the relevant state is therefore not merely the type mass \(\mu_t\), but \(x_{t,B}\): the mass of type-\(t\) agents currently holding each possible bundle \(B\). With \(|Q|\) good classes and \(H\) arrivals, the number of possible bundles can already be \(\prod_q(H_q+1)\), and the support of \(x\) can be exponentially large. The continuous object has become a measure over allocation histories, not a finite-dimensional society distribution.

Keeping that state preserves the original individual guarantee, but then the mirror is essentially an anonymous execution of the finite algorithm. For finite \(N\), masses are multiples of \(1/N\), and the proposed policy can be simulated by running Theorem 4.1 on \(N\) agents. Passing to \(N\to\infty\) gives a fluid limit, but not a new finite-type LP or a new separation problem. A configuration LP would have columns indexed by bundle histories; the claim that it is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|Q|\), and the horizon has not been justified and is generally implausible.

Nor does the potential proof lift by simply replacing sums with integrals. For one arriving good, the paper proves an inequality of the form \(\sum_i d_i\le\max_i\Delta_i\), then assigns that single good to one recipient. A batch must be distributed among many bundle states. Allocating it according to an averaged potential only controls an aggregate quantity; it does not ensure that every positive-mass subpopulation retains its individual PROP1 slack. Sequentially processing the copies may recover the guarantee, but then one has returned to the original online process, merely represented in bulk.

The proposed batching also changes the adversary. In the paper, the adversary may react after each recipient is observed. If an entire batch is presented and its internal split is hidden from the adversary, the improvement comes from weaker adaptivity or bulk arrivals, not from continuizing the population. If the adversary observes every mass transfer, the state-history objection returns.

The other possible repair is to collapse each type’s bundles into an average allocation or fractional item holdings. That produces a much cleaner continuous optimization problem, but it no longer mirrors indivisible online fair division. For example, with \(3N\) identical goods and \(N\) identical agents, assigning all goods to half the agents gives the type-average value \(3\), exactly the proportional target, while the other half receive value \(0\) and fail PROP1: adding one good only gives value \(1<3\). Average fairness therefore cannot substitute for the paper’s individual guarantee.

So the best version of the mirror faces a strict dichotomy. Preserve indivisible bundles and individual PROP1, and the state is an exponentially rich measure over histories; or compress the state, and one has fractionalized the outcome or weakened fairness. Neither route yields the claimed finite-type computational mirror.

This is a serious objection to the proposed anchor, but the universal negative is not airtight. A carefully defined fluid model with per-capita batches, integral microscopic bundles, and a new rounding or measure-valued algorithm could still be worthwhile. The honest conclusion is therefore that the proponent has not shown a useful mirror—not that no such mirror can exist in any scenario.
