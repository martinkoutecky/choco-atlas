The continuous mirror question

Given a finite graph \(H=(V,E)\) and positive rational type masses \(\mu_e\) summing to \(1\), let \(x_e\in[0,\mu_e]\) describe a subpopulation and let its cost be \(\tau(H[\operatorname{supp}(x)])\). Decide whether there are nonnegative per-unit densities \(p_e(x)\), for every nonzero \(x\) and each \(e\in\operatorname{supp}(x)\), such that \(\sum_{e\in\operatorname{supp}(x)}x_ep_e(x)=\tau(H[\operatorname{supp}(x)])\) and \(p_e(x)\ge p_e(y)\) whenever \(x\le y\) and \(e\in\operatorname{supp}(x)\); in the yes case, give a concise evaluator for the densities.

The model it lives in

A fixed-charge, nonatomic edge-request cost-sharing model: each edge \(e=uv\) is a request type with mass \(\mu_e\); any positive mass activates that edge in the endpoint-cover cost \(\tau(H[\operatorname{supp}(x)])\). The decision variables are per-type cost densities \(p_e(x)\), whose mass-weighted charges must be efficient and cross-monotonic.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers PMAS existence, recognition, and the constructive allocation rule of Theorem 8 and Corollary 9. It leaves aside integral PMAS results, including the stable-matching characterization and enumeration in Theorems 13–14 and Corollary 15, and the complexity of outputting the full coalition-indexed scheme.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Corollary 9, proved in this paper. It says that, given a finite graph \(G\), one can decide in polynomial time whether its vertex cover game admits a population monotonic allocation scheme (PMAS), and construct one if it does. The structural result behind it is Theorem 8, also proved here: the game admits a PMAS if and only if \(G\) contains no \(K_3\), \(C_4\), or \(P_5\) as a subgraph. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor instead. Its conclusion does caution that writing out a full PMAS takes exponential space; I therefore take polynomial-time recognition as the core claim, and treat construction as returning the paper’s rule for evaluating allocations rather than listing every coalition’s allocation.

A continuous version I find credible is Continuous Edge-Demand PMAS Recognition and Synthesis. Its instance is a finite simple template graph \(H=(V,E)\), with each edge type \(e\in E\) assigned a positive rational mass \(\mu_e\), where \(\sum_e\mu_e=1\). A type is an endpoint pair: every agent of type \(e=uv\) is an otherwise indistinguishable request that can be served by covering either \(u\) or \(v\). For a coalition, let \(x_e\in[0,\mu_e]\) be its mass of type \(e\), and let \(\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{e:x_e>0\}\). Its cost is
\[ \gamma(x)=\tau\bigl(H[\operatorname{supp}(x)]\bigr), \]
the minimum number of endpoint facilities needed to cover its active request types.

The question is whether there is a cross-monotonic cost-density scheme \(p_e(x)\), defined for every nonzero subpopulation \(x\) and each \(e\in\operatorname{supp}(x)\), such that
\[ \sum_{e\in\operatorname{supp}(x)} x_e p_e(x) =\tau\bigl(H[\operatorname{supp}(x)]\bigr), \qquad x\le y,\ e\in\operatorname{supp}(x) \ \Longrightarrow\ p_e(x)\ge p_e(y). \]
Here \(p_e(x)\) is the charge per unit of type-\(e\) mass; agents of the same type receive the same charge. A solution is YES or NO, and in the YES case the paper’s allocation rule can be given as a concise evaluator for any coalition \(x\). It should not be interpreted as a polynomial-size list of allocations for all coalitions.

This is a high-multiplicity regime of the paper’s game: many requests arrive over a fixed or modest set of endpoint pairs, so the number of agents \(N\) can be much larger than the number \(r=|E|\) of types. Repeating a type creates parallel requests but does not change the cost of covering that edge type, just as duplicating an edge player does not change the vertex-cover value. The cost is a fixed activation cost: any positive mass of a request type requires that type to be covered. That makes the support-based coalition value a plausible continuation of the authors’ model, rather than a replacement of the problem by fractional outcomes or probabilistic ballots.

The Theorem 8 characterization extends directly to this question. For a PMAS allocation \(b_e(S)\) on the discrete game over active edge types \(S\), define \(p_e(x)=b_e(\operatorname{supp}(x))/x_e\). Efficiency follows from the discrete scheme’s efficiency. If \(x\le y\), then both the discrete charge and the reciprocal mass move in the direction needed for cross-monotonicity. Conversely, if a forbidden subgraph occurs, choose a common small mass \(\epsilon>0\) for each edge in that obstruction. Restricting the continuous scheme to those equal-mass subcoalitions would give a discrete PMAS on the obstruction, contradicting the paper’s proof. Thus the expected answer is tractable, by the same forbidden-subgraph test and the paper’s allocation construction. In ChoCo’s trichotomy this is a tractable mirror, though not an example of discrete hardness disappearing: the anchored discrete recognition problem is already polynomial-time solvable.

The authors should recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime. Their players are edges, and their coalition value depends on which edges are present; the mirror replaces each edge player with a mass of identical players of that edge type. It covers the PMAS-existence recognition result and its constructive characterization, not the paper’s integral-PMAS/stable-matching results. A natural next question is whether those stable-matching characterizations have a useful analogue when individual charges are densities over mass. Other useful follow-ups are whether the scheme can be evaluated and checked efficiently for arbitrary rational \(x\), and how the result changes when serving a type’s demand has a cost that scales with its mass.

The weakest point is that this is a thin continuization: the coalition’s cost depends only on support, and the structural theorem already does nearly all the computational work. Fractions affect the per-unit charge, but not which combinatorial obstructions matter. It therefore gives a defensible population mirror and a clean complexity question, but little evidence that continuous optimization technology unlocks a problem the discrete community could not already solve.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The Corollary 9 anchor survives. Many identical requests can plausibly share an endpoint-pair type, with covering a type incurring a fixed facility cost. For that model, the proposed density conversion is exact: from a discrete PMAS \(b\), set \(p_e(x)=b_e(\operatorname{supp}(x))/x_e\). Conversely, for any continuous scheme, choose \(\epsilon>0\) small enough that \(x^S=\epsilon\mathbf{1}_S\) is feasible for every coalition, and set \(b_e(S)=\epsilon p_e(x^S)\). Efficiency and cross-monotonicity carry across both conversions. Theorem 8 therefore answers the mirror’s existence question, and Corollary 9 answers recognition.

The strongest case against is about how much computational substance the mirror adds. Its cost depends only on which types have positive mass: changing their masses has no effect on the existence question. At a type’s disappearance, the cost can jump, and the corresponding charge density can grow without bound as \(x_e\) approaches zero. Recognition is therefore still forbidden-subgraph detection on the support; the continuous magnitudes contribute no new algorithmic structure. A mass-sensitive variant could avoid that thinness, but adding throughput costs or capacity constraints would introduce assumptions the paper’s vertex-cover game does not supply.

That objection limits the mirror’s likely payoff; it does not defeat its validity. Support-based costs are plausible for fixed facility charges, and the programme allows a well-posed mirror whose answer is already known. I can’t honestly make the universal negative case: the proponent has a credible population regime and a continuous question that the authors would likely recognize. My case against is weak precisely at that anchor.
