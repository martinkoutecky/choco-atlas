What the mirror covers

Theorem 3 was the proposed hook, but no population mirror survives; the proponent leaves Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 1 without continuous questions.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid positive ChoCo computational mirror from this paper: it contains no numbered result classifying an underlying problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Its theorems concern mechanism design and welfare guarantees, not computational complexity.

The closest hook is Theorem 3, proved here. It says that for every \(\delta>0\), \(\varepsilon>0\), every \(\varepsilon\)-basic family \(K\subseteq K^\delta\), and every possibly randomized mechanism giving each player finitely many strategies, there exist candidate sets \(K\), true valuations \(\theta\in K\), and undominated strategies \(s\) such that
\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SW}(\theta,M(s))] \le \mathrm{MSW}(\theta)-2\delta m(1-1/n)+\varepsilon. \]
This is a real asymptotic welfare impossibility result, and the paper’s \(n\) players could suggest a large-market setting with many bidders sharing a small number of valuation-uncertainty types. But “finite” here means finite strategy sets, not a finite or high-multiplicity population, and the theorem’s bad instance singles out one bidder among \(n\). Replacing that bidder with a positive mass of identical agents changes the instance; in an atomless model, an individual’s report may have no effect at all.

Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Corollary 1, and Theorem 3 are named results, but none supplies the required computational anchor. A continuous-population welfare or implementation problem could be proposed as new follow-on work, but presenting it as the continuous version of a computational result in this paper would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the closest candidate, but it gives a welfare impossibility, not a computational result. It says that finite mechanisms can suffer a particular worst-case loss; it gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or computational problem whose complexity could be mirrored. Here “finite” describes each player’s strategy set, not a high-multiplicity population. Theorem 1 is also an incentive impossibility, Theorem 2 characterizes undominated reports, and Corollary 1 bounds welfare loss. None supplies the computational anchor ChoCo requires.

A stronger version of the proposed mirror might put a distribution \(\mu\) over bidder uncertainty types and scale the supply with the population, so welfare per capita remains meaningful. But an anonymous mechanism that sees only \(\mu\) cannot see a single atomless bidder’s deviation: that report changes the distribution by zero. The individual incentive comparisons underlying Theorems 1–3 then lose their force. To preserve them, one could make positive-mass cohorts coordinate their reports, or retain individually indexed reports and allocations. The former changes who acts—from individual bidders to blocs—and the latter gives up the type-distribution representation. Scaling supply also turns the finite-unit Vickrey auction into a different, continuum-market model.

One could still study algorithms for welfare or mechanism design in such a model. That may be worthwhile follow-on work, but Theorem 3 does not anchor its computational questions; they would need a new formulation and motivation. The case against a mirror is strongest under ChoCo’s stated criteria. It cannot prove that no continuous auction problem would ever be worth studying.
