The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, so it has no anchor that meets your stated requirement. It does make unnumbered complexity claims: computing a Slater order is NP-hard via minimum feedback arc set on tournaments [14, 15]; approximating Young score is NP-hard [18, 19]; and computing a Dodgson winner is NP-hard [20, 21]. The paper cites those claims rather than proving them as named results here. Its equations and Table 1 do not supply qualifying anchors.

The strongest *unanchored* candidate is a continuous Slater problem. Consider a procurement setting with many evaluators ranking the same finite set \(A\) of subcontractors. A type is a complete ranking \(\pi\) of \(A\); the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, with mass \(\mu_\pi\) representing the fraction of evaluators of type \(\pi\). For each pair \(a,b\), direct an arc \(a\to b\) when \(\sum_{\pi:\,a\succ_\pi b}\mu_\pi>1/2\). The problem is to return a linear order of \(A\) minimizing the number of these majority arcs it reverses; its first alternative is a Slater winner. This preserves the paper’s underlying rule and its output, while making the population the distribution over ranking types.

A plausible regime is a large procurement body evaluating a common slate of bids, where many evaluators share one of relatively few ranking types, so the evaluator count is much larger than the support size \(\tau\). The paper itself motivates the analogy by treating each criterion ranking as an electoral vote and each subcontractor as an alternative. I would expect hardness to transfer: finite ranking profiles embed as rational mass distributions, and the combinatorics lie in ordering the alternatives. The weakest point is the scenario shift: the paper’s criteria are distinct evaluation dimensions, whereas this mirror uses repeated evaluators. That makes the model plausible as a computational social choice problem, but it cannot repair the missing named anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper supplies no qualifying anchor. Its Slater, Young, and Dodgson complexity claims are unnumbered assertions attributed to earlier work; the integer programs and case study do not establish computational results of the paper itself. The proposed continuous Slater problem is therefore an extrapolation from a cited problem, not a mirror of a result this paper contributes.

There is also a real gap between the paper’s setting and the proponent’s repair. The paper selects among offers using a fixed set of distinct criteria. Treating those criteria as votes is its social-choice analogy; it does not make them a population of interchangeable agents. Duplicating a criterion could give that dimension extra influence. A large evaluator panel ranking the same bids is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but it replaces the paper’s criteria-based model with an election among evaluators.

That objection does not defeat the evaluator-panel mirror outright. The paper’s Condorcet framing makes it plausible that its authors would recognize the problem, and Slater’s rule is well-defined on a distribution of evaluator rankings. Young offers an even clearer candidate: retain as much evaluator mass as possible while making a chosen offer a Condorcet winner. With type-level retention variables, that has a natural linear formulation, subject to the chosen tie convention. These are meaningful mirrors of the underlying problems, even though this paper does not prove the results they would study.

So the negative case is strong on the narrower claim that the proponent has not anchored a mirror in a computational result contributed by this paper. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The evaluator-panel setting is plausible, and the paper’s own social-choice analogy gives it a credible bridge. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario.
