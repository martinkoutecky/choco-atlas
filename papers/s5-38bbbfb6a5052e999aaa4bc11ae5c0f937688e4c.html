I can’t identify an admissible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes the TTC rule by individual rationality, pair efficiency, and strategy-proofness; Corollaries 1 and 2 give related characterizations; and Theorem 2 proves an impossibility for rules on a mixed preference domain. Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 establish properties of TTC. These are axiomatic and mechanism-existence results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise complex. The TTC algorithm is described, but the paper has no numbered computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Treating Theorem 1 as an anchor would also make the mirror an axiomatic continuization, which the programme puts out of scope. So there is no valid anchor here, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand a prover.

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s temporary-exchange setting: recurring cohorts of residents might share housing priorities and priorities over who receives their endowments. A plausible population model would give each type a complete description of those preferences and its endowment class, let \(\mu_t\) be its share, and represent allocation as a mass-preserving assignment. But the paper studies which rules satisfy normative properties; it supplies no optimization objective or computational question for that model. Preserving its recipient preferences over named agents also makes a small type set harder to justify unless the model groups recipients into classes. This could motivate an axiomatic study of TTC in a continuum housing market, but it is not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no computational anchors to defeat here. Theorem 1 and Corollaries 1–2 characterize TTC through axioms; Theorem 2 is an impossibility for an axiom bundle; Propositions 1–2 establish TTC’s normative properties. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem. The paper does give a TTC procedure and says it terminates in at most \(n\) rounds, but that is not a numbered complexity result. Under ChoCo’s scope, the absence of an anchor is the strongest objection.

The strongest repair would be a market with recurring cohorts of residents and housing units, grouped into finitely many complete types: each type includes its house class and its rankings over house and recipient classes. A mass-preserving assignment could then represent the continuum market. That may be a sensible new model; the objection is not that housing markets can never have multiplicity or that preferences over recipients must remain tied to individual names. But if the model’s input and output are only type masses and mass assignments, one person’s report changes no aggregate, and swapping two people changes no positive mass. Strategy-proofness and pair efficiency would need substantive new formulations—say, type-level deviations or positive-mass coalitions. Those could motivate an axiomatic study, but they would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated boundary, though it cannot establish that no related continuous housing-market project would ever be worthwhile. The proponent’s cohort example supports a possible model; it does not supply the missing computational question.
