The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible named anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. It proposes FIT-MKCLPT, Lexicographic Ensembles, and Rankdom Forest, then reports empirical comparisons. The observation that arranging many rankers becomes “rapidly intractable” is not a hardness result; the experiments are not complexity theorems. So I can’t honestly give a continuous problem *per anchor*.

The strongest plausible mirror, clearly unanchored, is population-risk learning for pairwise preferences. Let \(O=\prod_i D(A_i)\) be the option domain, and let each type \(\theta\) be a complete preference relation over \(O\). A society is a finite support \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\); \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of users with that preference type. Include a rational query distribution \(q\) over option pairs, a finite encoded family of kernels, and a bound \(B\) on ranker size. Choose an MKCLPT or Rankdom Forest \(r\) of size at most \(B\) to maximize
\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta \sum_{\{a,b\}}q_{ab}\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[r(a,b)=\theta(a,b)\right], \]
where abstentions score zero. Equivalently, the decision version asks whether such a ranker can reach accuracy at least a given rational threshold. The regime is a large panel of users sharing relatively few preference types, such as customers rating pairs of multiattribute products.

This connects closely to the paper’s pairwise-learning task: its CRP measure is exactly the fraction of all training pairs predicted correctly, so the objective has a natural population analogue. The weak point is that the paper treats labeled pairs as training examples, not users as a population; this mirror changes empirical learning into population-risk learning. It covers the MKCLPT and Rankdom Forest contributions, but the paper supplies no named result from which to predict tractability, transferred hardness, or continuum-specific hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that there is no named computational result in the paper to mirror. It proposes learning algorithms and reports empirical performance; it proves no complexity bound for learning MKCLPTs, arranging ensembles, or training Rankdom Forests. So there are no paper results from which to build the requested anchored mirrors.

The proposed population-risk problem is also a substantial change in target. The paper learns a ranker from partial comparisons to predict an underlying preference relation, including on unseen options. A distribution over users with potentially conflicting complete preferences instead asks for a ranker that best represents a crowd. If everyone shares one preference type, the objective reduces to weighted supervised learning: multiplicities become example weights, and the population adds no visible structure for the continuous-optimization tools ChoCo emphasizes. If users have different types, the task becomes collective aggregation rather than the paper’s stated learning task.

That is a real scope objection, but it does not defeat the strongest version of the mirror. A large panel of users with recurring preference types is plausible, the objective is a direct population version of the paper’s CRP measure, and the paper itself mentions combinatorial preference aggregation. Optimizing a bounded-size ranker against that distribution is a well-posed computational question.

So the negative case is weak at its hardest point: it can show that the mirror is unanchored and shifts the paper’s target, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in that crowd-learning scenario.
