The continuous mirror question

Given a shared finite-horizon MDP with rational linear features, a society μ over complete reward types, a focal cohort of mass \(\delta>0\), fixed reports from the remaining mass with mean feature count \(F_-\), and the cohort’s target feature expectation \(\Phi^\star\), compute a feasible trajectory distribution \(q\) minimizing \(\left\|F_-+\delta\,\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim q}[\phi(\omega)]-\Phi^\star\right\|_2^2\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity assistance game with \(\tau\) complete reward types and masses \(\mu_t\); a focal cohort of mass \(\delta\) chooses a distribution \(q\) over feasible trajectories to minimize feature-matching error. An MDP occupancy measure represents the cohort’s fractional aggregate report.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s feature-matching best-response computation; it leaves the Gibbard impossibility result and Propositions 2–5 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • At what rate does the continuous optimum approximate the best empirical report distribution for a finite cohort of \(k\) users?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous feature-matching bloc best response, anchored in Proposition 1, which is proved in this paper. The proposition says that when the AI treats humans as noisily optimal planners with linear features and fits rewards by maximizing the likelihood of their aggregated demonstrations, a human’s best-response trajectory can be computed in polynomial time. The authors derive a least-squares objective and solve it using a linearly constrained occupancy-measure problem. This is a computational result, though not a hardness result: the paper gives no named result showing that one of its problems is NP-hard or hard in another standard complexity class. The Gibbard theorem it cites is a mechanism impossibility result, not a computational hardness result.

Here is a continuous population version of Proposition 1’s feature-matching manipulation problem. Consider a shared finite-horizon MDP \(\mathcal M=(S,A,P,\mu_0,H)\) with a rational linear feature map \(\phi:S\times A\to\mathbb Q^d\). Principals have complete reward types \(t\), including their reward weights over those features and any other attributes that affect their payoffs or feasible demonstrations. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types. Each unit of mass provides a trajectory as its demonstration.

Take a focal cohort of mass \(\delta>0\) whose members share type \(t^\star\), and fix the demonstrations of everyone outside that cohort. Let \(F_-\) be their mass-weighted mean feature count. Let \(\Phi^\star\) be the expected feature count under the policy that is optimal for the cohort’s true reward. The cohort chooses a distribution \(q\) over feasible trajectories to minimize
\[ \left\|F_-+\delta\,\mathbb E_{\omega\sim q}[\phi(\omega)]-\Phi^\star\right\|_2^2. \]
The output \(q\) specifies what fraction of the cohort submits each trajectory. Equivalently, a solution is an occupancy measure \(y\) satisfying the MDP flow constraints and minimizing
\[ \left\|F_-+\delta\sum_{h,s,a}y^h_{s,a}\phi(s,a)-\Phi^\star\right\|_2^2. \]
The occupancy formulation has polynomial size in the MDP description and \(d\); with rational inputs, this is a convex quadratic program. Thus the proposed mirror is tractable. It asks for the best aggregate report by a positive-mass group, not for the encoding itself.

This fits a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large platform collects demonstrations from many users doing a shared task, with relatively few reward-feature types. A coordinated group might be an advocacy campaign or a cohort trying to steer a learned system. The population size \(N\) can be large while \(\tau\) remains modest. In a finite society, if the cohort contains \(k\) of the \(N\) principals, its mass is \(\delta=k/N\), and its empirical reports are represented by a distribution over trajectories. As \(k\) grows, the possible proportions of reports become finer. This follows the paper’s concern with large numbers of strategic inputs, including the Tay example, rather than relying on outcome-space continuity.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a continuous version of their feature-matching manipulation question: it keeps the shared task, linear features, inferred reward, and strategic demonstrations, while replacing counts of users and reports with masses. The extension from one strategic human to a positive-mass bloc is deliberate. Proposition 1 supplies the computational anchor; it does not itself prove this bloc formulation or establish that independent users will coordinate.

That is also the weakest point. The anchor is a polynomial-time result, not a hardness result whose hardness might dissolve under continuization. And a coordinated cohort is a stronger strategic actor than the individual demonstrator in Proposition 1. The mirror is most convincing for organized users who can coordinate submissions. For independent nonatomic users, a single person’s influence vanishes with their mass, so the more relevant follow-up is how much mass is needed to change the learned policy. Other natural questions are whether the formulation remains tractable with the direct demonstration payoffs from Proposition 2, and how accurately finite cohorts approximate the continuous optimum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only strong computational anchor here: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for a human’s best response to a specified feature-matching learner. So the case against cannot rest on the claim that the paper has no computational result.

The difficulty is that the best response depends on a person’s ability to affect the learner’s aggregate feature counts. With \(N\) principals, one report changes their average by order \(1/N\). In an atomless population, a single person’s report has no effect on the learned policy. Under the paper’s model with \(\beta=0\), the person’s payoff then cannot distinguish their reports. The individual manipulation problem has disappeared in the limit.

The proposed bloc mirror avoids that disappearance by giving a positive-mass cohort a coordinated choice over its reports. That makes the cohort a coalition: its members jointly optimize the effect of their mass on the learner. High multiplicity groups agents with the same relevant attributes; it does not make them a single strategic actor or give them a way to coordinate. A bloc manipulation problem may be a sensible extension, and the paper’s Tay example gives it some motivation, but it is not the individual best-response problem in Proposition 1. The occupancy-measure formulation then computes a coalition’s aggregate action, rather than preserving the paper’s strategic unit.

A stronger rescue would study the leading-order effect of one person’s report as their mass tends to zero. That could preserve an incentive signal, but it changes the question into sensitivity analysis of the learner, with the result depending on how its policy responds near ties and other discontinuities. It may be a worthwhile follow-up, but the paper does not formulate that problem.

So the negative case is that the faithful individual mirror loses the strategic effect, while the non-degenerate bloc mirror adds coalition structure. That is a real mismatch, but not a universal rejection: the bloc scenario is plausible enough that the authors might recognize it as their problem. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror survives.
