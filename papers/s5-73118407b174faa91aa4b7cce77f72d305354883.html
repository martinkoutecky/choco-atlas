The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(m\), \(w\), and \(\bar{\sigma}\), determine the worst-case distortion of SNTV over candidate metric spaces with candidate-distance ratio at most \(\bar{\sigma}\) and finite rational-support voter-location distributions \(\mu\). Each type \(t\) has location \(x_t\), mass \(\mu_t\), and truthful favorite \(a_t\); SNTV selects the \(w\) candidates with greatest ballot masses, using a fixed tie-break. The quantity to determine is the supremum of \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(C_{\mathrm{SNTV}})/\min_{|C|=w}\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(C)\) over those instances.

The model it lives in

Types are voter locations \(x_t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) and favorite ballots \(a_t\). The ballot mass for candidate \(y_j\) is \(b_j=\sum_{t:a_t=y_j}\mu_t\); SNTV selects a committee \(C\) from these masses. Its objective is the worst-case ratio of mass-weighted committee cost \(\sum_t\mu_t d(x_t,C)\) to the minimum mass-weighted cost of a size-\(w\) committee, over candidate metrics with \(\sigma\le\bar{\sigma}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's SNTV distortion guarantee. It leaves the universal lower bound and the strategyproofness, Random Sequential Dictator, and Sequential Dictator results outside the computational anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact worst-case distortion of SNTV over these societies be determined as a function of \(m\), \(w\), and \(\bar{\sigma}\), beyond the \(1+2\bar{\sigma}\) upper bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see a defensible but narrow mirror in the paper’s non-strategyproof results. Its usable anchors are approximation guarantees, not named hardness or running-time results; I would not present it as a complexity classification. The theorem numbering below is from the arXiv version matching the text.

Take a finite set of voter-location types \(T=\{x_1,\ldots,x_\tau\}\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is a location in the candidate metric space, together with its truthful favorite if it is tied between candidates. Its mass is the fraction of the population at that location. For candidate \(y_j\), the ballot mass is \(b_j=\sum_{t:\,a_t=y_j}\mu_t\). An anonymous randomized mechanism is a map \(\Phi\) from ballot-mass vectors to distributions over \(w\)-candidate committees. The social cost of a committee \(C\) is the average \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(C)=\sum_t\mu_t\min_{y\in C}d(x_t,y)\); distortion compares its expected cost under \(\Phi\) with the minimum cost of any size-\(w\) committee.

A plausible regime is a very large municipal or organizational electorate whose members fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of location types, with many people per type. The mechanism sees only favorite-candidate totals, while the evaluation uses the underlying locations and distances. Thus \(n\gg\tau\), while the number of ballot categories is at most \(m\). This keeps the committee and ballots discrete; it continuizes the population and the social-cost sum.

My lead is Continuous SNTV Distortion. Given \(m,w\) and a bound \(\bar\sigma\) on the ratio of the largest to smallest distance between distinct candidates, determine the worst-case distortion of the rule that selects the \(w\) candidates with greatest ballot mass, with a fixed tie-break. Its instances range over candidate metric spaces and finite rational location distributions satisfying that bound; a solution is the supremum distortion, or a certified bound on it.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper: SNTV’s distortion is at most \(1+2\sigma\). It directly supplies the proposed continuous bound. The proof’s sums over voters become mass-weighted sums, and the normalization from total population \(n\) to total mass \(1\) cancels in the ratio. [Theorem 2 in the matching arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.09386)

A second question is Continuous Minimax Committee Distortion: for the same \(m,w,\bar\sigma\) and class of societies, determine the best worst-case distortion achievable by any anonymous randomized rule based only on ballot masses. A solution is a rule and a matching worst-case guarantee. Its anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here: no randomized mechanism can achieve distortion below \(1+\frac{w-1}{w+1}(\sigma-1)\). The lower-bound construction uses equal mass on \(w+1\) voter-location types, so its population proportions already have a natural continuous reading. [Theorem 1 in the matching arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.09386)

Theorem 2 is my strongest anchor because it gives an explicit rule on mass inputs. Theorem 1 complements it with a universal lower bound. Both are approximation-performance results; neither establishes NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or a similar complexity classification. For these two mirror questions, I expect the stated guarantees to transfer tractably. In fact, for rational masses, clearing denominators gives a discrete electorate with exactly the same ballot proportions and distortion ratios; conversely, every discrete electorate yields such a distribution. That two-way identity supports the mirror, though it does not create a new complexity result. The exact minimax distortion between the lower and upper bounds, and whether another mass-based rule improves on SNTV, remain follow-up questions.

I would cover only Section 3’s results without strategyproofness. The authors should recognize this as their problem: favorite-candidate ballots, committee outcomes, the social-cost objective, and the information restriction all remain intact; the voter sum becomes an integral over population mass. The committee itself is not made fractional. Strategyproofness results are poor anchors here because an individual in a nonatomic population has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate ballot vector, making unilateral incentives vacuous without a different deviation model.

The weakest point is the same feature that makes this mirror precise: the paper studies distortion guarantees, not the complexity of computing or optimizing over mechanisms. And for these scale-invariant bounds, the rational finite-support model is exactly the high-multiplicity version of the discrete one. This is a credible population mirror of the paper’s approximation question, but only a limited contribution to ChoCo’s complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no named computational problem whose complexity it classifies. Theorem 1 is a lower bound on distortion; Theorem 2 is an upper bound for SNTV. They are meaningful mechanism-design results, but they do not give running times, hardness, or an algorithm for bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness. That leaves no computational result for ChoCo to carry into its complexity landscape.

The proponent’s population model is coherent, and I would not reject it for lack of a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. Let \(\mu\) be a distribution over voter locations, with a favorite candidate specified in ties. The mechanism sees ballot masses \(b_j=\int \mathbf{1}[a(x)=y_j]\,d\mu(x)\); distortion is evaluated using committee costs \(g_C=\int d(x,C)\,d\mu(x)\). So a society can be continuous while ballots and committees remain discrete. But for fixed \(m\) and \(w\), the distribution enters these questions only through \(m\) ballot masses and at most \(\binom{m}{w}\) committee costs. There is no population-sized combinatorial object for a continuous-optimization method to simplify.

That defeats the two anchors as ChoCo contributions. Theorem 2, the SNTV bound, carries over by replacing voter counts with masses; its proof uses weighted sums and the same normalization. Theorem 1, the lower bound, already uses \(w+1\) equally weighted groups, so its construction has a direct mass interpretation. These are good continuous statements, but each is an extremal distortion claim, not an algorithmic question. Computing SNTV from ballot masses is direct, and evaluating committee costs from an explicit finite support is just summation. The proposed minimax question—find the best worst-case distortion over all mass-based rules—is the strongest improvement on the proponent’s framing. It is a legitimate mechanism-design question, but the paper gives no finite representation of the rule space or computational task for finding such a rule. Adding that task would start a different research project, rather than continuize one of this paper’s computational results.

The other sections do not supply a better anchor. Their strategyproofness claims concern a single voter changing one ballot. In a nonatomic society, that deviation has zero mass and leaves the aggregate ballot vector unchanged. A local derivative condition or a positive-mass coalition deviation could be studied, but either changes the incentive question; neither turns the paper’s theorems into complexity results. The deterministic bound’s dependence on \(n\) also does not create a useful population axis for the central results.

So my negative case is narrow but substantial: the mirror is sensible, and the authors would likely recognize it, yet its natural outputs are continuous versions of approximation bounds rather than the computational questions this programme is meant to chart. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous study could be built from this model. I can argue that these results do not provide a strong ChoCo mirror as currently stated.
