The case FOR (proponent)

There is a defensible, but conditional, positive case. The best mirror is the paper’s owner-object special case, not its general priority model. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or approximation result; its results are structural stability theorems. Thus this cannot be a fully compliant ChoCo computational anchor. Still, it supplies a mathematically natural continuous-population problem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that when every agent owns an object, and \(\mu^T\) is the matching produced by TTC, the singleton \(\{\mu^T\}\) is the unique horizon-\(k\) vNM stable set whenever \(k\ge 3\gamma-1\), where \(\gamma\) is the largest number of agents in any TTC cycle. The theorem combines existence of a path to TTC with the owner-consent condition, which prevents any improving path from leaving \(\mu^T\).

The most plausible regime is a large housing-exchange market. There are \(N\) households, \(N\) indivisible homes, and only \(\tau\ll N\) distinct household types. A type records a complete preference order over finitely many home types and the home type it owns. Many households share the same preferences and endowment. For rational masses \(\mu_t\), one can recover a finite market by taking \(N\mu_t\) identical copies of type \(t\). This is genuine population continuization: homes remain indivisible for each household, while \(x_{t,s}\) denotes the mass of type-\(t\) households receiving homes of type \(s\).

The continuous instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), home types \(S\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and an ownership map assigning each type to a home type. Let \(q_s\) be the mass of homes of type \(s\). A mass matching is a matrix \(x=(x_{t,s})\) satisfying

\[ x_{t,s}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_s x_{t,s}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s. \]

The remaining mass is unmatched. Every individual receives either one integral home or nothing; fractional \(x_{t,s}\) records only how many identical households receive each kind of home.

Define a mass-TTC procedure by letting each remaining type point to its most-preferred remaining home type, each home type point to its owner type, and executing every directed cycle at its bottleneck mass. Exhausted type or home masses are removed, while residual mass continues to later rounds. Let \(x^T\) be the resulting mass allocation.

A horizon-\(k\) mass-improving path is a finite sequence of mass matchings. One step transfers a positive mass \(\delta\) of one type from its current home to another home, possibly displacing mass. The moving mass must strictly prefer its assignment \(k\) steps ahead to its current assignment. In the owner version, the owner mass of the destination home must also strictly prefer its assignment \(k\) steps ahead. A set of mass matchings is stable when it satisfies the paper’s internal and external stability conditions with these mass paths.

The resulting problem is Continuous Owner-TTC Horizon Threshold:

Given \((T,S,\mu,P,\text{ownership})\) and an integer \(K\), compute \(x^T\) and decide whether \(\{x^T\}\) is the unique horizon-\(K\) vNM stable set. Equivalently, define

\[ \kappa^*=\min\{k:\{x^T\}\text{ is the unique horizon-}k\text{ vNM stable set}\}, \]

and ask for \(\kappa^*\), or whether \(\kappa^*\le K\). A solution is the TTC mass allocation together with a correct stability or threshold determination.

The expected positive direction is Class A for the sufficient-horizon version. Mass-TTC itself is a finite type-level procedure, and the natural analogue of the paper’s bound is

\[ K\ge 3\Gamma-1, \]

where \(\Gamma\) is the maximum number of positive-mass type nodes in a mass-TTC cycle. The paper’s constructive cycle-by-cycle proof suggests that this bound should remain independent of \(N\), depending instead on \(\tau\). The exact computation of \(\kappa^*\), however, may be continuum-specific: arbitrary submass transfers create infinitely many intermediate allocations, so unrestricted reachability could require a new flow or separation theory. Further questions include the exact threshold, finite-\(N\) convergence, unequal home capacities, and whether a DA analogue has no stable mass set.

The mirror is plausible because the authors’ owner-market interpretation already concerns repeated exchange units, and the rational-mass model is exactly its high-multiplicity form. It also preserves the paper’s central object—TTC farsighted stability—rather than replacing it with a divisible-goods allocation problem.

The weakest point is serious: the paper’s horizon counts one-agent moves, whereas the continuum model must treat a positive mass of identical agents as one type-level move. If literal one-agent moves are retained, a positive-mass cycle may require infinitely many steps and the theorem’s finite horizon becomes vacuous. If mass moves are allowed, the model has been co-modified. Moreover, strict priorities over named individuals do not pass cleanly to an atomless population; the owner-object case avoids most of this difficulty, which is why it is the only convincing mirror.

So the strongest honest conclusion is: a credible continuous-population analogue exists, centred on Theorem 3, and likely yields a Class-A threshold problem; but because Theorem 3 is not a computational result, this paper does not meet ChoCo’s strict anchored-computation criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is decisive at ChoCo’s anchor gate: Theorem 3 is not a computational result. It gives a structural existence and uniqueness statement for a farsighted stable set. The paper specifies no input encoding, complexity measure, decision problem, approximation task, or algorithmic classification. Its constructive proof is not a complexity theorem.

The proponent’s \(\kappa^*\)-problem is therefore new:

\[ \kappa^*=\min\{k:\{\mu^T\}\text{ is the unique horizon-}k\text{ stable set}\}. \]

Nothing in the paper asks for, defines, or analyses this threshold computationally. The proposed continuous problem is consequently not a continuization of Theorem 3’s computational content; it is a new computational project inspired by a structural theorem. The same objection applies to Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollary 1.

The owner-exchange setting is the best possible modelling rescue, but it exposes a second problem. The paper’s contribution is specifically about one-agent-at-a-time deviations under limited farsightedness. The introduction contrasts this with earlier work allowing groups to move together. In a high-multiplicity market, a positive-mass TTC cycle requires either \( \Theta(N) \) individual moves, making any fixed horizon meaningless, or a type-level mass move. The latter is not an innocuous limit operation: it replaces the paper’s defining behavioural primitive with coordinated group movement, essentially returning to the model class the paper distinguishes itself from.

The same issue appears in the object structure. The paper has strict rankings over individual objects and priorities over individual agents. Replacing them by rankings over home types requires either identity-dependent tie-breaking, which destroys finite type multiplicity, or a new anonymous mass-TTC rule with bottleneck transfers. That rule may be sensible, but its cycle bound \(3\Gamma-1\) would be a new theorem about a new game, not a high-multiplicity version established by Theorem 3.

Allowing arbitrary submass transfers creates an additional modelling choice rather than resolving this one. The state space becomes a continuum and the horizon counts aggregate transfers rather than reactions by agents. Restricting transfers to whole type masses, bottlenecks, or a prescribed discretization produces different notions of farsightedness. There is no canonical lift of the paper’s one-agent dynamics.

Thus a mass-TTC model could certainly be worth studying in its own right. It might even yield an interesting threshold or reachability problem. But that is precisely why it fails as this paper’s continuous mirror: the worthwhile computational question has to be invented after changing both the behavioural primitive and the state representation. No choice of housing scenario can repair the absence of a named computational result in the paper. Under ChoCo’s computational-anchor standard, this paper should therefore receive no continuous mirror, while acknowledging that the proponent has identified a plausible new research direction rather than a valid continuation of this paper.
