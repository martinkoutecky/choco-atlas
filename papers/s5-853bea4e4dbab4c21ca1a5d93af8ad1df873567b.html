The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population to continuize: many citizens, conscripts, or committee members each submit a complete assignment that places every item into one of the shared categories. A type would be one such assignment; its mass would be the fraction of the population holding it. The collective output would remain an onto assignment. That is a natural high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s subject.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror on. Its closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: for an onto domain with \(n\ge1\) and either \(m=p\ge4\) or \(m>p\ge3\), every aggregator \(F:X^n\to X\) satisfying Independence and Unanimity is dictatorial. Theorem 1 is cited from Dokow and Holzman; it is also an axiomatic impossibility theorem. Neither result asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm.

One could ask for a continuum version of Theorem 2, with Independence and Unanimity imposed on a map from distributions over assignments to a collective assignment. But that remains an axiomatic question, and dictatorship has no straightforward analogue for a nonatomic population. Turning it into a computational task would add a new problem rather than mirror a result of this paper. So the honest positive case is that its institutional settings admit plausible high-multiplicity populations; the case does not yield an eligible computational anchor or a continuous problem I can defend as the paper’s computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The citizens, conscripts, or committee members in the examples could form a sensible high-multiplicity population: many people may submit the same complete assignment. That establishes a plausible continuous society, but it does not supply a computational result to mirror.

The paper’s sole original anchor is Theorem 2. It characterizes aggregators \(F:X^n\to X\) satisfying Independence and Unanimity: under the stated conditions, such an aggregator is dictatorial. Theorem 1 is an imported axiomatic result, and the lemmas establish conditions for applying it. None asks for an algorithm, classifies a computational problem, or gives an optimization task. The paper’s contribution is a theorem about which aggregation rules can satisfy certain axioms.

The strongest continuous reformulation would map a distribution over complete assignments to a collective assignment, then define distributional versions of Independence, Unanimity, and Supportiveness. But that remains an axiomatic question. In particular, the conclusion that one named individual dictates the outcome has no direct counterpart in a distribution that records only type masses: a single individual has zero mass in a nonatomic population. Keeping a named dictator would preserve an exceptional individual rather than make the society itself the continuous object. Either way, the reformulation does not yield a computational mirror of Theorem 2.

One could add a new computational task—for example, finding an aggregator that minimizes axiom violations—but that adds an objective and an algorithmic problem the paper never studies. The negative case is therefore strong on the programme’s stated criterion: this paper offers no named computational result to continuize, even though its motivating settings can support high multiplicity.
