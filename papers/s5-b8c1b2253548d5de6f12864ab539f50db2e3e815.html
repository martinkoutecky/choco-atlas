The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 7.1

  • Theorem 7.1 — survived: The opponent engaged this anchor, but the cohort-level coloring variables preserve each template's integral conflicts and make the aggregate-load question a recognizable restricted extension.

The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many cohort templates \(q\), each a weighted graph \(H_q=(R_q,E_q)\) with a supplied proper \(\kappa\)-coloring, rational cohort masses \(p_q\) summing to \(1\), and \(k\ge\max\{\kappa/\varepsilon,2\Delta\}\), distribute each template's mass over its integral proper \(k\)-colorings: choose \(x_{q,\sigma}\ge0\) with \(\sum_\sigma x_{q,\sigma}=1\). For role weights \(a_{qr}\), define \(L_i=\sum_q p_q\sum_\sigma x_{q,\sigma}\sum_{r:\sigma(r)=i}a_{qr}\). Find a distribution minimizing \(\max_i L_i/\min_i L_i\), equivalently decide whether all color loads can be made equal in the atomless limit of repeated cohort copies.

The model it lives in

The population consists of infinitesimal mass over finitely many cohort types \(q\); each unit of cohort mass receives one integral proper coloring of its template \(H_q\). The variables \(x_{q,\sigma}\) mix those feasible cohort assignments, and the objective balances the resulting aggregate color loads \(L_i\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7.1's approximate equitable-coloring guarantee in the repeated finite-template regime; it leaves the paper's results for arbitrary graphs, other weight regimes, and partition equitability outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite populations with \(N p_q\) integral, what rounding bound on \(x_{q,\sigma}\) guarantees near-equal loads when \(N\) is not divisible by \(k\)?
  • Which cross-cohort conflict models still admit a finite type description and an efficient balancing algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Atomless Equitable Coloring of Repeated Conflict Cohorts. The paper has no named NP-hardness result to anchor on; its relevant named result is an algorithmic one.

My anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper. It says: given a vertex-weighted graph of maximum degree \(\Delta\) and a supplied \(\kappa\)-coloring, for any \(\varepsilon\in(0,0.1]\) and integer \(k\), if
\[ k\ge \max\{\kappa/\varepsilon,\,2\Delta\} \quad\text{and}\quad \max_{v\in V}w_v\le \varepsilon\, w(V)/k, \]
then the graph has a \((1+7\varepsilon)\)-\(\mathrm{EQ1}\) \(k\)-coloring, computable in time polynomial in \(n\) and \(1/\varepsilon\). Here \(\mathrm{EQ1}\) means that for every pair of color classes \(C_i,C_j\), some vertex can be removed from \(C_i\) so that the remaining weight is at most \(1+7\varepsilon\) times \(w(C_j)\).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a very large population made of repeated, bounded-size conflict cohorts: for example, recurring teams or groups whose members must be assigned to different service pools when they conflict. Each cohort comes from one of finitely many templates \(q\). A template is a graph \(H_q=(R_q,E_q)\), with a rational weight \(a_{qr}\) for each role \(r\in R_q\), and a supplied proper \(\kappa\)-coloring \(\phi_q\). Many copies of each template occur. The person type is the template-and-role pair \((q,r)\), including its weight and its conflict relationships within the cohort. The copy identifier does not change the problem-relevant type. If the number of copies grows while the templates remain fixed, the population grows while the number \(\tau\) of types stays fixed.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. The input is the finite list of templates, their rational copy masses \(p_q\) summing to one, their role weights and supplied \(\kappa\)-colorings, and \(k,\varepsilon\) satisfying Theorem 7.1’s parameter conditions. A unit of cohort mass must receive one integral proper \(k\)-coloring of its template. Equivalently, choose fractions \(x_{q,\sigma}\ge0\) over the proper \(k\)-colorings \(\sigma\) of each \(H_q\), with \(\sum_\sigma x_{q,\sigma}=1\). The load of color \(i\) is
\[ L_i=\sum_q p_q\sum_\sigma x_{q,\sigma} \sum_{r:\,\sigma(r)=i} a_{qr}. \]
The task is to find such fractions minimizing the load ratio
\[ \rho=\max_{i,j} L_i/L_j \]
for positive total weight, or, in the theorem’s decision version, to achieve \(\rho\le 1+7\varepsilon\). This is a continuous population of cohort copies, but every individual cohort still gets a valid discrete coloring, so its internal conflicts are respected.

This mirror has an especially transparent answer. For each template, take its supplied coloring \(\phi_q\). For each shift \(s\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\), assign role \(r\) color
\[ \sigma_{q,s}(r)=1+\bigl((\phi_q(r)-1+s)\bmod k\bigr). \]
Each shifted coloring is proper, and each role receives each of the \(k\) colors in exactly one of the \(k\) shifts. Assign mass \(1/k\) of every cohort type to each shift. Then every color has load exactly \(L_i=W/k\), where \(W\) is the population’s total weight. Thus the mirror is in Class A and achieves \(\rho=1\). In the corresponding finite instance with \(N\) repeated cohorts, the theorem’s low-maximum-weight condition becomes \(a_{\max}\le \varepsilon W_N/k\); for fixed templates and positive weight density, it holds for all sufficiently large \(N\). Removing one individual also has vanishing effect in the atomless limit.

The mirror covers Theorem 7.1’s weighted equitable-coloring guarantee and its low-maximum-weight regime, not every result in the paper. It is plausible that the authors would recognize it: the same objects are being colored, the same local conflicts constrain feasible assignments, and the same class weights are being balanced. The co-modification is that the action is specified per cohort so that conflicts within a cohort remain meaningful, while mass may be split among different integral cohort colorings. That is exactly the kind of fractional type-level average that becomes natural at high multiplicity.

The weakest point is the narrowness of the regime. Repeated finite templates preserve bounded-degree conflicts cleanly, but an arbitrary sparse graph with many distinct, identity-specific neighborhoods may not have a finite type description. Also, the continuous answer uses the ability to spread copies across color shifts; it does not explain how the paper’s finite-instance construction behaves on general graphs. Further questions are whether broader sparse interaction models admit finite-type mirrors, and what quantitative finite-\(N\) rounding bounds recover \(\mathrm{EQ1}\) from exact continuous balance. The paper’s lower-bound theorem on the number of colors is an existence impossibility result, not a computational hardness result, so it is not an additional complexity anchor here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proposed mirror is that its types do not describe individual vertices well enough to determine conflicts. A role \((q,r)\) conflicts with another role \((q,r')\) only when they belong to the same cohort copy. The marginal masses of those role types do not record which agents share a cohort. The variables \(x_{q,\sigma}\) preserve that missing information by treating an entire cohort template as the unit receiving a coloring. That makes this a population of cohort bundles, rather than the paper’s population of graph vertices.

There is also a real change in the fairness condition. \(\mathrm{EQ1}\) compares color classes after removing one vertex; in an atomless population, one vertex has zero mass. The proposed load-ratio objective replaces that condition with balance in aggregate loads. So it does not literally continuize \(\mathrm{EQ1}\).

Those objections identify the mirror’s limits, but they do not defeat its best version under ChoCo’s stated rules. The cohort model preserves integral feasible colorings and their conflicts, has finite repeated types, and asks a computational question about aggregate allocation. Repeated conflict cohorts are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the shift to aggregate loads is a natural mass formulation when single-agent removal vanishes. The allowed co-modification for integral bundles covers much of the change in the unit of assignment.

I therefore cannot honestly support the universal negative claim here. The objections make the mirror narrower than the paper’s general graph-coloring setting, but narrowing to a sensible instance regime is allowed. Theorem 7.1 remains a credible anchor, and the negative case against it is weak.
