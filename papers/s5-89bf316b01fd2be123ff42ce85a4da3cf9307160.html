The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered computational-complexity result that can serve as an anchor. Its introduction says Kemeny median ranking is NP-hard, but that claim is unnumbered and not proved as a result here. QUICK and FAST are heuristic algorithms evaluated experimentally; the paper gives no numbered complexity theorem or formal performance guarantee. So there are zero eligible anchors under the stated rule.

The strongest plausible mirror, considered separately from that failure, is a continuous Kemeny median problem. Given \(m\) objects and a support \(T\subseteq S_m\) of \(\tau\) complete ranking types, each with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), find a ranking \(S^\star\in S_m\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,d_{\mathrm{Kem}}(t,S^\star). \]
A natural regime is a large survey or association election over a fixed shortlist, where many respondents submit the same ranking and the population size \(N\) greatly exceeds \(\tau\). For type counts \(n_t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) preserves the discrete median rankings exactly. Taking the paper’s NP-hardness claim at face value, this mirror should be Class B: the difficulty in the number of alternatives persists. Further questions include complexity parameterized by \(\tau\), approximation, and extensions to tied or partial ranking types. This covers the median-ranking objective, not the empirical accuracy claims for QUICK and FAST.

The main weakness is that the paper already allows weighted input rankings and aggregates them through the combined input matrix. If those weights can be rational, the proposed mirror may already be the paper’s own mathematical formulation after normalization. That makes the model recognizable, but leaves both a collision risk and—decisively—the absence of a qualifying numbered anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s first point is decisive under the stated anchor rule: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary giving a computational complexity result. Its NP-hardness sentence is an assertion in the introduction, while QUICK and FAST are heuristics assessed experimentally, with no formal performance guarantee. So there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The proposed Kemeny mirror also runs into a stronger problem: the paper already formulates the same task over weighted rankings. Let \(W=\sum_k w_k\), and for each ranking type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=\sum_{k:R^{(k)}=t}w_k/W\). Then
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,d_{\mathrm{Kem}}(t,S) =\frac{1}{W}\sum_k w_k\,d_{\mathrm{Kem}}(R^{(k)},S), \]
so the continuous objective has exactly the paper’s minimizers. The paper explicitly uses weighted averages, and its combined input matrix \(c_{ij}=\sum_k w_k r_{ij}^{(k)}\) aggregates the weighted rankings before QUICK and FAST operate. Tied and partial rankings can be treated as ranking types in the same mapping.

A large survey or association vote with many repeated rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. But encoding it by rational type masses would restate the paper’s weighted input, not give it a new continuous-population model. This is not an objection that high-multiplicity work already exists; it is that this paper’s own objective already depends on type weights in precisely the needed way.

A formal complexity study of weighted Kemeny median, perhaps parameterized by support size, could still be worthwhile. That would fill a computational gap for an existing weighted problem, however, rather than provide a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that narrow ground; it does not show that the weighted problem itself lacks research value.
