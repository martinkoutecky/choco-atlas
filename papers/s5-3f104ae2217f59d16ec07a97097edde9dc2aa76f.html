The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror on. Lemmas 1–4 and Propositions 1–6 analyze equilibrium behavior and information aggregation; none establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a comparable class.

The closest results are Proposition 3, proved here, which shows that with five or more experts some expertise configurations do not permit any speech order—even one conditioned on the history—to implement the first-best decision; and Proposition 5, also proved here, which shows that increasing an expert’s information quality can lower the designer’s expected payoff even after optimally reordering the committee. These are substantive results about debate design, but neither establishes the complexity of finding an optimal order.

One could propose a new continuous problem about scheduling mass across expert types in a large committee. But the paper provides no qualifying named computational result for that problem to mirror. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the paper’s most serious weakness as a candidate for ChoCo: it contains no numbered result about computational complexity. Propositions 3 and 5 are the closest anchors. Proposition 3 rules out first-best implementation for some finite committees; Proposition 5 gives a comparative static for optimal debate order. Neither says how hard it is to find an optimal order. A new scheduling problem could be built from either result, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The natural proposal is to replace a finite committee by a distribution over expert types—say, signal quality and prior reputation—and ask for the best measurable speech schedule. The trouble is that speech order operates through individual, finite Bayesian updates. In log-odds, a truthful report by an expert of quality \(\rho\) changes the public belief by \(+r\) or \(-r\), where \(r=\log(\rho/(1-\rho))\). That update does not shrink just because the expert represents a smaller fraction of the committee. A schedule of type-masses therefore does not describe the paper’s sequence of speakers: a zero-mass speaker still changes everyone’s beliefs by a finite amount, and those belief changes determine whether later speakers herd.

There are two apparent repairs, and each changes the model. Keep each report’s finite informational effect, and a continuum schedule has to specify an uncountable succession of discrete updates; ordinary population mass does not supply a well-defined history. Scale each report’s effect down with its mass, and the model’s signal precision and reputational incentives change with that scaling. Alternatively, let a whole cohort report as one unit. That changes who is evaluated and how, and replaces individual sequential debate with a group mechanism. These might support interesting new models, but the paper does not supply a natural continuous limit for them.

A large forecasting panel is the strongest scenario for repeated experts of the same type. It could support a high-multiplicity *discrete* problem: retain the integer number of experts of each type and choose their order. But those counts and atomic speaking slots remain essential to the history. Passing to fractions alone loses them. If the panel instead reports simultaneously, the sequential order problem has disappeared; Section 5 already studies that different mechanism.

So the negative case is not that continuous questions would have uninteresting answers, or that high multiplicity is inherently unsuitable. It is that the paper’s key object is a path of individually consequential reports, while its results are equilibrium and welfare claims rather than computational results. A continuous weak-signal or cohort version might be worthwhile as a new project, but it would need new assumptions about signals, reputation, and timing before it became a recognizable computational mirror. The universal claim remains hard to prove; a sufficiently redesigned model could qualify. The case is strongest against treating any such redesign as a mirror already latent in this paper.
