The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a defensible computational result to anchor a continuous mirror on. Propositions 1–3 concern which strategies survive iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies; Proposition 4 establishes a restricted GUE property. They are named results, but none classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result of the kind this programme needs.

The paper’s \(\mu\) is a prior over the type profile of a finite set of named agents, not a distribution of population mass over agent types. The three-agent example’s fractional probabilities do not make its society continuous. A continuum of contractors grouped by working process and preferences could be an interesting new model, but the paper’s numbered results do not establish anything about its computational complexity. I would therefore invent neither an anchor nor an expected complexity class for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The attached case correctly observes that Propositions 1–4 are not complexity classifications. But that does not establish that no worthwhile computational mirror exists. The paper also defines a concrete optimization problem: the principal chooses a strategy to maximize her minimum utility. Section 5.2 says that, in the finite dynamic model, this strategy is calculated by backward recursion. That gives a better starting point than trying to turn the weak-dominance counterexamples into continuum theorems.

A natural high-multiplicity version would consider a large project workforce drawn from \(\tau\) complete work-process types. Each type includes the process, preferences, costs, and protected utility level; \(\mu_t\) records its share of the workforce. Where the project scales with staffing, the principal chooses accepted mass \(x_t\) of each type, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). Given rational process and payoff data, one can ask for the complexity of computing the principal’s maximin value and strategy, or deciding whether an efficient continuation can meet every type’s per-capita utility guarantee. The parameters could include \(\tau\), the horizon, the process-state description, and the input bit length. That is a computational question about a continuous population of agents, grounded in the paper’s dynamic team model.

The weak-dominance propositions are less promising anchors: in an atomless population, a single agent’s report may have no effect, so their strategic diagnosis can degenerate. Likewise, a continuum of bidders for one indivisible contract risks making winner selection ill-defined. The dynamic maximin problem avoids those obstacles by focusing on a large, interchangeable workforce and a project whose scale can grow with it.

This is a plausible research direction, not evidence that a continuous version is already known to be tractable or that the authors would certainly adopt the mass-scaled project model. That recognition test remains the main uncertainty. Still, “the paper has no numbered complexity theorem” supports the narrower conclusion that it is not itself a computational result; it does not support the universal conclusion that no worthwhile continuous mirror can be formulated.
