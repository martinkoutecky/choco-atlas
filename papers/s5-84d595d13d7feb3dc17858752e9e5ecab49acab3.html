The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is conditional: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it does not contain a qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s formal rule.

The paper’s named results are about epistemic representability, not computational complexity. Lemma 1, cited from elsewhere, gives a necessary weak-reinforcement condition. Propositions 2–5, Theorems 8–10 and 13, Proposition 12, and Corollary 14 prove or refute maximum-likelihood interpretations of PB rules. None asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized tractability, an approximation guarantee, or a computational formulation used algorithmically. Thus no fully compliant ChoCo case can be claimed.

The best salvage is a mirror of Proposition 7, proved in this paper. Proposition 7 states that, on unit-cost instances and under the assumption that the ground truth is exhaustive, both approval-Nash maximization and cost-approval-Nash maximization are maximum-likelihood estimators for the noise model

\[ \Pr_{\mathcal M}(A\mid \pi,I) = \frac{c(A\cap \pi)} {2^{|\mathcal P|-1}c(\pi)}. \]

For unit-cost exhaustive allocations, \(c(\pi)=k\), so the two rules coincide.

A precise continuous problem would be:

\[ \textsc{Continuous Unit-Cost Epistemic PB-MLE}. \]

An instance consists of a finite project set \(\mathcal P\), an integer \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_A)_{A\subseteq\mathcal P}\) over approval types \(A\). Here \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the population submitting ballot \(A\). The decision variable is still an integral \(k\)-project allocation \(\pi\subseteq\mathcal P\), not a fractional project allocation. The task is to return every allocation maximizing the population-normalized log-likelihood

\[ \ell(\pi;\mu) = \sum_{A\subseteq\mathcal P} \mu_A \log\!\left( \frac{|A\cap\pi|}{k} \right), \]

with value \(-\infty\) if some positive-mass type has \(A\cap\pi=\varnothing\). Equivalently, maximize

\[ \prod_{A:\mu_A>0} \left(\frac{|A\cap\pi|}{k}\right)^{\mu_A}. \]

The types are complete approval ballots. Mass is population share. The action is the choice of an integral funded project set. The objective is aggregate epistemic likelihood.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for large participatory-budgeting processes with millions of residents but a much smaller number of recurring approval patterns—say, standardized community, neighbourhood, or stakeholder profiles. It is not a claim that every PB election has few types; the regime is specifically \(n\gg |\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\). For rational masses \(\mu_A=n_A/N\), multiplying the objective by \(N\) recovers exactly the finite-profile log-likelihood:

\[ N\ell(\pi;\mu) = \sum_i \log \Pr_{\mathcal M}(A_i\mid\pi,I). \]

So this is genuinely the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem, not a fractional-outcome reformulation.

I would expect this problem to be Class B rather than continuum-specifically easy: rational finite profiles embed directly, while the funded set remains an integral committee. Any hardness of the corresponding finite Nash-welfare project-selection problem would transfer. But that hardness is not proved in this paper; it would be a new ChoCo result. Natural follow-up questions are winner determination, approximation, and fixed-parameter algorithms in \(k\), the number of positive-mass types, or the number of projects.

The mirror is therefore plausible and the authors would likely recognize it: their entire epistemic framework already treats ballots anonymously and evaluates products of ballot probabilities. The continuous model replaces the empirical profile by its distribution and replaces a product over named voters by an integral, or weighted sum of logarithms, over types.

Its weakness is decisive for this task: Proposition 7 is not a computational theorem. Moreover, the paper’s impossibility results—especially Theorem 8—also transfer immediately to continuous societies because their witnesses use only finitely many rational profiles. Thus the mirror is mathematically sensible, but it turns the paper’s epistemic analysis into a new computational problem rather than continuizing one of the paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s formal gate: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Proposition 2, Proposition 3, Proposition 4, Theorem 8, Theorem 13, and Corollary 14 are representability or impossibility results about maximum-likelihood interpretations. Proposition 7 and Theorems 9–10 identify rule/noise-model coincidences. None defines a computational problem and proves membership in P, hardness, approximation, parameterized complexity, or an algorithm. The paper does not analyse winner determination as a function of the number of projects, costs, types, or encoded profile. Thus there is no computational result here whose continuization could contribute to ChoCo’s complexity landscape.

Proposition 7 is the best attempted rescue, but its proposed mirror is a new problem built on the paper, not a continuous version of one of its computational results. For rational masses \(\mu_A=n_A/N\),

\[ \sum_A \mu_A \log \Pr_{\mathcal M}(A\mid\pi,I) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_A n_A\log \Pr_{\mathcal M}(A\mid\pi,I) = \frac{1}{N}\log L_{\mathcal M}(\profile,\pi,I). \]

The factor \(1/N\) does not affect the maximizer. Hence every rational continuous society is exactly an ordinary finite profile with repeated ballots, represented by type counts rather than by a voter list. The proposed problem is a legitimate high-multiplicity reformulation, but it is not a computational theorem or algorithm implicit in Proposition 7.

The stronger version of the mirror does not improve this diagnosis. Retaining integral project sets, general costs, exhaustive or non-exhaustive ground truths, and arbitrary complete ballot types still leaves a finite project-selection problem whose objective is a weighted log-likelihood. If the masses are rational, it is exactly a finite weighted-profile problem; if they are arbitrary reals, the paper supplies no computational representation or approximation model for those reals. Giving the distribution succinctly would create a new input-encoding and aggregation problem, not a consequence of the paper’s result.

For the particular unit-cost model, the collapse is especially direct:

\[ \ell(\pi;\mu) = \sum_A \mu_A\log |A\cap\pi| -\log k -(|\mathcal P|-1)\log 2. \]

The population distribution merely supplies weights to the same Nash approval objective. There is no mass-transfer variable, continuous feasibility region, or pricing problem of the kind that motivates ChoCo. Making the electorate a distribution changes the representation of repeated observations, while the paper’s decision object remains an integral subset of projects and the paper provides no computational structure for selecting it.

The negative results do not rescue the mirror either. Theorem 8 and Theorem 13 can indeed be restated over rational distributions, but their contradictions already occur on a fixed two-project instance with finitely many ballot types. Their continuous versions are constant-size algebraic witnesses to the absence of a noise model, not complexity results about a continuous society. Likewise, transferring those impossibilities upward would establish no computational boundary.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is not implausible here: large PB elections can naturally contain repeated approval patterns, and voter identity is not central to the paper’s likelihood calculations. That is why the case against cannot honestly rely on “no meaningful multiplicity” or “identity is lost.” The objection is narrower and more decisive: the paper offers an epistemic study of rules, not a computational problem whose population axis can be continuized. The Proposition 7 construction could motivate a worthwhile new project on weighted Nash-style PB winner determination, but it cannot serve as a qualifying ChoCo mirror of this paper.
