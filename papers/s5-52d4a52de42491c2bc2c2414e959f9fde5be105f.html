The continuous mirror question

Given a population distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support of weak rankings of \(n\) arms, accessible by sampling a fresh respondent for each queried pair, what query complexity is necessary and sufficient for an adaptive learner to return an arm of maximum Copeland score with error probability at most \(\delta\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete weak rankings \(t\) of the arms with rational masses \(\mu_t\); a query selects a pair and samples its comparison from a fresh type drawn from \(\mu\). The objective is to return a Copeland winner under the paper's ternary modal-outcome scoring, minimizing or bounding the number of queries.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.1 bounds oracle queries to an unknown population; with explicit masses, pairwise probabilities and a winner are directly computable, so the sample bound does not describe ChoCo's explicit-input task.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the sample-based Copeland-winner identification guarantees in Theorems 2.1 and 3.1 for ranking-generated feedback; it leaves aside explicit-input computation, the transitivity-specific guarantee in Theorem 4.2, and the experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do Theorem 2.1's lower bounds persist when pairwise outcome probabilities must arise from one distribution over weak rankings?
  • Can algorithms exploit the ranking-type support or masses to improve query-complexity bounds over those based only on pairwise gaps?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a real, fairly narrow continuous mirror here. The paper’s human-judgment and voting motivation suggests a large electorate or rating panel: each arm is a candidate or item, and each respondent has a complete weak ranking of the arms, including ties. A type \(t\) is one such ranking; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. The population has rational masses and support size \(\tau\), with many more people than types.

For each duel, the learner draws a fresh respondent from the population and asks them to compare the selected pair. Thus, for example,
\[ P_{\mu,\succ}^{(i,j)}=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t,\qquad P_{\mu,\sim}^{(i,j)}=\sum_{t:\,i\sim_t j}\mu_t. \]
The remaining probability is for \(j\succ_t i\). Fresh independent draws give the paper’s stochastic feedback model, while the Copeland score and winner condition stay exactly the same. The action is still choosing a pair of arms to query; the objective is to identify a Copeland winner with few queries. Nothing about the arms, outcome space, or rounds is continuized.

The concrete regime is a large electorate, or a large online panel of users, sampled afresh for each comparison. It is plausible for \(N\) to be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballot types is modest. This is a scenario-specific case: it fits the paper’s voter and human-preference applications, not necessarily every dueling-bandit application.

I would call the lead problem Continuous-Population Copeland Identification (CPCI-PAC). Its instance is a population distribution \(\mu\) with finite support over weak rankings of \(n\) arms, together with a confidence target \(\delta\). The learner does not see the masses directly; it has an oracle that lets it choose an arm pair and observe the response of a fresh respondent drawn from \(\mu\). The task is to give an adaptive querying policy and stopping rule that returns an arm of maximum Copeland score with error probability at most \(\delta\). A solution is the policy and its correctness and query-complexity guarantee.

Two named results anchor this mirror. Theorem 2.1 is proved in this paper (in Appendix B), and supplies the information-theoretic lower-bound question for CPCI: how many comparisons must any \(\delta\)-correct learner use? For a unique Copeland winner \(i^*\), the theorem gives the following two cases.

  • With no indifference outcomes, let \(L(j)\) be the arms that dominate \(j\), and \(d_j=CP(i^*)-CP(j)\). Define

\[ C_j=\max\left\{ \frac{|L(j)|}{d_j+1}\mathbf 1[|L(j)|\ge d_j+1],\ \frac{|L(j)|-1}{|L(j)|+d_j-2}\mathbf 1[i^*\in L(j)] \right\} \]
and \(D_{j,k}=\mathrm{kl}(P_{\succ}^{(j,k)},1-P_{\succ}^{(j,k)})\). Then every learner that identifies a Copeland winner with confidence \(1-\delta\) has
\[ \mathbb E[\tau]\ \ge\ \ln\frac{1}{2.4\delta} \sum_{j\ne i^*} C_j \min_{k\in L(j)}\frac{1}{D_{j,k}}. \]

  • With indifferences and all three outcome probabilities positive, also let \(I(j)\) be the arms for which indifference is the modal outcome. Define

\[ \Psi(j)=\{(a,\ell):0\le a\le |I(j)|,\ 0\le \ell\le |L(j)|,\ a+2\ell\ge 2d_j+1\} \]
and
\[ C_j=\max_{(a,\ell)\in\Psi(j)} \frac{\binom{|I(j)|}{a}\binom{|L(j)|}{\ell}} {\binom{|I(j)|-1}{a-1}\binom{|L(j)|}{\ell}\mathbf 1[a\ge1] +\binom{|I(j)|}{a}\binom{|L(j)|-1}{\ell-1}\mathbf 1[\ell\ge1]}. \]
For \(p^{(j,k)}=(P_{\succ}^{(j,k)},P_{\sim}^{(j,k)},P_{\prec}^{(j,k)})\), let \(D_{j,k}\) be the maximum of the two KL divergences from \(p^{(j,k)}\) to \((P_{\sim}^{(j,k)},P_{\succ}^{(j,k)},P_{\prec}^{(j,k)})\) and to \((P_{\prec}^{(j,k)},P_{\sim}^{(j,k)},P_{\succ}^{(j,k)})\). The lower bound is
\[ \mathbb E[\tau]\ \ge\ \ln\frac{1}{2.4\delta} \sum_{j\ne i^*} C_j \min_{k\in L(j)\cup I(j)}\frac{1}{D_{j,k}}. \]

The mirror question for this theorem is whether those unavoidable query costs still apply when the ternary probabilities arise from masses of voter types. They do: every distribution over weak rankings induces such pairwise probabilities, and the theorem applies to that induced instance. This is a sample-complexity lower bound, not an NP-hardness result.

Theorem 3.1 is also proved in this paper (Appendix C), using the PPR-1V1 mode-identification result of Jain et al. as a subroutine. It gives the constructive CPCI-PAC question: can one identify a winner with a high-probability sample cap? For POCOWISTA, the theorem states
\[ \Pr\!\left(\hat i\in C(P),\ \tau\le t(P,\delta)\right)\ge 1-\delta, \]
where, as stated in the paper,
\[ t(P,\delta)\le \sum_{i<j}t_0\!\left(p^{(i,j)},\frac{\delta}{\binom n2}\right), \]
and
\[ t_0(p,\varepsilon)= \frac{c_1p_{(1)}}{(p_{(1)}-p_{(2)})^2} \ln\!\left( \sqrt{\frac{2c_2}{\varepsilon} \frac{p_{(1)}}{p_{(1)}-p_{(2)}}} \right), \qquad c_1=194.07,\quad c_2=79.86. \]
Here \(p_{(1)}\ge p_{(2)}\ge p_{(3)}\) are the ordered probabilities of the three possible responses. When every pair has a unique modal response, this is a finite gap-dependent cap; if a pair has a modal tie, the displayed bound supplies no finite cap. Thus the mirror preserves the paper’s actual target: return a COWI quickly and with a fixed confidence, not merely compute Copeland scores from known probabilities.

The two theorems give complementary answers for the same population problem: CPCI-LB asks for a universal lower bound on expected queries, while the lead CPCI-PAC asks for a learner with a high-probability query cap. The expected direction is statistical learnability with costs governed by pairwise gaps, alongside instances where many samples are unavoidable. There is no basis here for a P/NP or Class A/B/C verdict about ordinary running time, and I would not claim one.

The weakest point is the oracle interface. Under ChoCo’s usual explicit-input convention, if the learner is handed the support and rational masses, it can calculate every pairwise probability and the winner directly; the sample-complexity problem disappears. CPCI must therefore treat the population as an unknown source that can be sampled, as in the paper’s learning setup. That makes this a credible population-based mirror of the authors’ problem, but a less direct fit to ChoCo’s standard explicit-distribution computational model. It also covers the ranking-generated subfamily of dueling-bandit instances, rather than every arbitrary collection of pairwise probability matrices.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the input model. Theorem 2.1 is a lower bound on observations from an unknown stochastic environment. Its \(P^{(i,j)}\) are response probabilities, not an encoded society. Interpreting them as marginals of a distribution over weak rankings is plausible, but the theorem still measures how many noisy comparisons a learner needs when it cannot see that distribution. Under ChoCo’s explicit-input model, the learner is given the support and rational masses; the theorem’s sampling uncertainty is absent. So it does not supply the proposed computational lower bound for a known continuous society.

Theorem 3.1 has the same divide. POCOWISTA’s guarantee is a high-probability bound on oracle queries, with costs set by the gaps between response probabilities. If respondents are sampled afresh, this is a coherent statistical-learning problem, and the paper’s result transfers to that setting. But the support and masses are then hidden behind a sampling oracle. If they are given explicitly, the learner can calculate each pairwise probability from the masses and identify a Copeland winner; the paper’s query bound no longer describes the task. Either way, the theorem does not answer an algorithmic question about a society supplied as a finite distribution.

A stronger population-based version could restrict the possible pairwise matrices to those induced by distributions over weak rankings, and ask whether that structure improves winner-identification bounds. But the paper’s algorithms and bounds use only pairwise response marginals; they do not use the latent ranking types, their support, or their masses. That version would be a worthwhile question about learning from sampled voters, but it remains a sampling-complexity question about an unknown population. Theorem 4.2 does not bridge the gap: its transitivity condition and improved query cap are also stated in terms of pairwise probabilities, not an explicit population representation.

The large-electorate or online-panel scenario is credible, and I would not argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. That is where the negative case is weakest. I would argue more narrowly that these anchors do not establish a ChoCo mirror under its stated society-as-input model: they concern uncertainty about an unobserved population, while continuization in the programme takes the population distribution itself as the computational input.
