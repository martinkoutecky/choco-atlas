The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. The strongest plausible positive direction is a new population-level extension. Imagine a large utility program where many households share the same complete energy context: tariff, background load, renewable generation, appliance, and washing deadline. Each cohort has a mass, and households in it have a common but unknown preference weight. Given the distribution of their demonstrated cumulative reward vectors, and a known controller’s response to each weight \(w\in W=\{(w_{\mathrm{cost}},w_{\mathrm{comfort}})\ge0:w_{\mathrm{cost}}+w_{\mathrm{comfort}}=1\}\), one could ask for a distribution \(\Lambda\) of household preferences consistent with those demonstrations. If \(Q(w)\) is the controller’s predicted distribution of reward vectors for weight \(w\), the question is to find \(\Lambda\) such that
\[ \nu=\int_W Q(w)\,d\Lambda(w), \]
where \(\nu\) is the observed population distribution of demonstrations. A solution is a compatible preference distribution.

That is a concrete continuous-population inference problem, and a fleet of similar households is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But it is an extension, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s named result: the study models one agent and uses continuous preference weights, while leaving the population finite and unmodeled. Treating its experimental tables as complexity anchors would overstate the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named result here to anchor a continuous mirror. The paper proves no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. It applies a previously proposed algorithm, then reports experimental preference estimates and simulation comparisons. Those experiments do not define a complexity problem whose population size could be continuized.

The proposed household-fleet scenario is plausible, and the paper’s mention of future multi-agent work gives it some connection to the authors’ interests. But its equation, \(\nu=\int_W Q(w)\,d\Lambda(w)\), changes the target: the paper infers one user’s preference vector from that user’s demonstration; the extension infers a population distribution of preferences from a distribution of demonstrations. To make the extension work, it must introduce repeated households, a shared controller response \(Q\), and assumptions about how demonstrations vary with preferences. If households are identical in all those respects, adding their mass just repeats the original inference. If their preferences vary, the unknown object is the new mixture \(\Lambda\), not the individual preference the paper studies. That may be a worthwhile new problem, but it is not a continuous version of a result in this paper.

The negative case is strongest against a *paper-grounded* mirror: no qualifying computational result was identified, and the proposed extension supplies a new research question instead. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror could arise in any scenario. The text alone cannot establish that, especially given the authors’ stated interest in a multi-agent extension.
