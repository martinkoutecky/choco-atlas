The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor. The paper has no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Its numbered results are structural. For example, Theorem 3.1, proved here, says that for every lower-contour-monotonic RSCF, a full-measure set of prior profiles makes graph-LOBIC equivalent to graph-LDSIC. Theorem 5.1, also proved here, says that for every unanimous RSCF on the unrestricted domain, a full-measure set of priors makes swap-LOBIC equivalent to swap-LDSIC. Those are substantive results, but neither is a computational-complexity result.

There is a tempting continuous formulation: let \(\mu\) be a distribution over ranking types and let a rule map \(\mu\) to a lottery over alternatives. But a unilateral report by an infinitesimal agent leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, making incentive constraints vacuous. Allowing a positive mass of agents to misreport instead gives a coalition-manipulation problem, which is not the paper’s individual incentive-compatibility question. And the paper’s full-measure claims concern prior beliefs in a society with finitely many agents; they do not continuize the population. Its lottery outcomes are likewise outcome randomness, not a continuous society.

So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover under the requested criteria. The potential soft spot is the same as the decisive limitation: one could invent adjacent population-level questions, but none would be anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor, and that is the strongest case against a mirror. Theorem 3.1, Theorem 5.1, and Theorem 8.1 are structural claims about incentive compatibility and the form of rules. The paper gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem whose complexity it establishes. One can invent a task such as checking Bayesian incentive compatibility from a tabulated rule, but its complexity would depend on how the rule and priors are represented. With an explicit table, the table itself contains the profiles to check; a succinct representation would create a new problem the paper does not study.

The best attempt to anchor a continuous question in these results runs into the individual incentive constraint. For an anonymous rule based on the distribution \(\mu\) of voter types, one voter has mass zero. Changing that voter’s report leaves \(\mu\), and hence the rule’s outcome, unchanged. A positive-mass group deviation can affect the outcome, but that asks about coalition manipulation. Making an individual report matter by retaining a named voter or role requires information beyond the population distribution. None of these gives a direct continuization of the paper’s LOBIC or OBIC results.

The paper’s “almost all prior profiles” claims also concern priors over the finite set of other agents’ preference profiles. A continuous-population version would need a new prior space and a chosen notion of genericity there. Even if that modeling work produced an interesting question, it would still lack a computational result in this paper to mirror. A welfare-maximizing search for continuous Bayesian rules could be worthwhile on its own, but it would be a new mechanism-design problem, not an extension of the paper’s computational contribution.

So the negative case is strong under the programme’s anchoring criterion: this paper supplies no computational result for a continuous mirror to take up, and its central individual-incentive claims lose their bite under anonymous population aggregation. The universal claim is less airtight: one could build a useful continuum model of group deviations or rule synthesis. Its weakness is that such a project would be adjacent to the paper’s subject rather than a continuous mirror of its results.
