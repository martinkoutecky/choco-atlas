The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and symmetric rational costs \(\gamma(t,u)\) between types and the source, replicate \(n\mu_t\) agents of each type and play the paper's game with a uniformly random order. Does the limit \(\Lambda_t=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}\mathrm{payment}_i(\pi)\right]\) exist along integers \(n\) for which every \(n\mu_t\) is integral, and how can \(\Lambda\) be computed?

The model it lives in

Use finite replicas of endpoint organizations, with each type \(t\) carrying mass \(\mu_t\) and a complete cost profile \(\gamma(t,u)\) to every type and the source. For each admissible \(n\), retain the paper's finite sequential game and equilibrium concept, then output the limiting expected payment per population member of each type. This defines a limit of atomic games rather than a nonatomic equilibrium.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the type-aggregated, per-capita folk-share outcome in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1; it leaves individual edge assignments, equilibrium refinements, and the complete-implementation discussion aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(\Lambda\) exist for every rational type-mass vector and type-cost matrix?
  • Can \(\Lambda\) be computed efficiently when \(|T|\) varies, or is the variable-type problem hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered complexity result here: the paper proves equilibrium characterizations, but says nothing about polynomial time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. So it cannot anchor a ChoCo complexity classification in the strict sense. The best positive case is narrower: treat the named equilibrium results as results about computing the game’s outcome, then ask for their high-multiplicity limit.

The strongest anchor is Corollary 1, proved here as a consequence of Theorem 1: in the random-order game \(\Gamma\), the folk rule is the unique expected subgame-perfect-equilibrium payoff allocation. The supporting Theorem 1, also proved here, says that for each fixed order \(\pi\), \(\Gamma^\pi\) has a unique subgame-perfect-equilibrium payoff allocation, equal to the marginal-contribution payoff vector of the optimistic game \((N,v_C^+)\) under \(\pi\); moreover, the equilibrium is strong and uses undominated strategies. Neither result is a complexity classification, but together they identify the equilibrium payments to be computed.

A plausible regime is a large network of endpoint organizations connecting to a shared service source. Each endpoint is a distinct node, but its connection costs to other endpoints and the source depend only on its service class. There are many endpoints and relatively few classes. A type is the endpoint’s complete cost profile, including its costs to each type and to the source; mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of endpoints of type \(t\). Nature orders endpoints uniformly at random, each chooses a legal arc joining its current component to another component, and it pays that arc’s cost. Its utility is the negative of that payment. This retains the paper’s strategic action and individual objective; the aggregate output is the type-level payment allocation.

For a precise version, take finite \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and symmetric rational costs \(\gamma(t,u)\geq 0\), including costs to a source \(0\). For each \(n\) divisible by the denominators of \(\mu\), create \(n\mu_t\) distinct agents of each type \(t\), with every pair of distinct agents of types \(t,u\) incurring cost \(\gamma(t,u)\). Play exactly the paper’s game on this replicated cost matrix.

The lead question, Continuum Folk-Share Limit, is: compute, or establish nonexistence of, the vector
\[ \Lambda_t=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\, \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t} \mathrm{payment}_i(\pi)\right], \]
where \(\pi\) is uniform and each payment is the unique equilibrium payment for that order. The solution is the aggregate equilibrium charge per unit population assigned to each type. Corollary 1 makes the finite-\(n\) quantity well-defined through the folk allocation; the continuous question asks what that allocation becomes when the society is specified by masses.

Theorem 1 also suggests a distinct, order-conditioned question, Continuum Ordered Implementation Profile. Add a limiting order schedule \(q:[0,1]\to[0,\mu]\), absolutely continuous with \(q(0)=0\), \(q(1)=\mu\), and \(\sum_t q_t(s)=s\). For any sequence of orders whose type-prefix counts divided by \(n\) converge to \(q(s)\), compute the limiting cumulative type payments
\[ P_t(s)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n} \sum_{\substack{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t\\ \mathrm{rank}(i)\le ns}}\mathrm{payment}_i. \]
Part of the problem is to determine whether this limit exists and is independent of the approximating orders. The output is the type-resolved payment profile through the game, not just the final total. The random-order limit is the lead problem; its typical prefix path is \(q_t(s)=s\mu_t\).

I would expect the random-order limit to be tractable for fixed \(|T|\): with finitely many type-pair costs, the underlying tree-cost calculations appear reducible to finite-dimensional, piecewise-linear structure. That is a research expectation, not a result of this paper. When \(|T|\) grows, the complexity is open. No hardness-transfer or continuum-specific-hardness claim follows from this paper’s results.

The scope is only the equilibrium payment results in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1. This does not mirror the paper’s wider discussion of cost sharing or establish a complexity result for minimum spanning trees. The main weakness is that a nonatomic population does not literally move one agent at a time or form an ordinary spanning tree. These questions define continuity as the limit of replicated finite games, which is a defensible high-multiplicity bridge, but not a direct continuum game. Per-capita normalization also risks discarding finite bridge costs and the fine allocation effects the authors care about. The case survives only if the authors accept the limiting type-level equilibrium allocation as their problem in a large, repeated-agent regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither Theorem 1 nor Corollary 1 states a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium payoffs for a fixed order; Corollary 1 identifies their expectation with the folk rule. The paper does not pose an input-output computation problem or analyze its complexity. Calling a proposed task “compute the limit” does not make it a computational question the paper established. To turn either result into a ChoCo problem, one must add a separate specification: the encoded input, the requested output, and what counts as an exact or approximate answer.

That objection applies to both anchors. The fixed-order theorem does not ask for an algorithm to compute its marginal-contribution vector. The corollary does not analyze computing the expected allocation. The proposed ordered payment profile and random-order folk-share limit are coherent questions, but they are new asymptotic questions about cost allocation, rather than continuizations of computational results in the paper.

The proponent’s best repaired model is persuasive on multiplicity. If many endpoint organizations share a complete cost profile, one can replicate each type, give same-type links positive costs so total cost can scale with population, and study normalized payments along the limiting type-order path. I would not reject that on the grounds that agents have identities, that high multiplicity is implausible, or that per-capita costs must vanish. Nor does prior high-multiplicity work count against it.

That repair also clarifies the limit of the negative case: it does not show that no worthwhile continuous research question exists. The replica model may be a sensible high-multiplicity study of folk-rule allocations. The stronger claim—that this paper supplies a continuous mirror worth adding to ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme—is much less convincing. Once Theorem 1 reduces the strategic game to marginal contributions and Corollary 1 to the folk rule, the proposed questions study how those allocations behave under population replication. That may be worthwhile work, but it is a newly framed computational or asymptotic problem, not a complexity result anchored in this paper. The case against the mirror is therefore strongest on programme fit, and weak as a universal claim against every plausible scenario.
