The continuous mirror question

Given issues \(I_1,\ldots,I_m\) with finite domains \(D_1,\ldots,D_m\), a finite support \(T\) of complete conditional-ballot types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether \(\min_{r\in\prod_j D_j}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\delta_t(r)\le q\). For the Theorem 2 restriction, each conditional type has an in-star dependency graph on three vertices.

The model it lives in

Each type specifies its dependency graph and conditional approvals, and carries mass \(\mu_t\); the decision remains one shared outcome \(r\), minimizing the mass-weighted disagreement \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\delta_t(r)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(\Delta=2\) in-star and \(\Delta=1\) out-star hardness cases of Theorems 2 and 3, plus the binary dichotomy of Theorem 4; extending its group-dichotomous min-cut algorithm to rational weights is an inference. Theorems 1, 5, and 6 are not separately anchored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For continuous CAV with in-star types, can an algorithm improve on the \(d^m\) exhaustive-search bound while respecting the ETH lower bound for support size \(\tau=O(m^2)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is continuous-population CAV: keep the paper’s issues, finite domains, and single shared outcome, but represent the electorate by masses of complete conditional-ballot types.

An instance has issues \(I_1,\dots,I_m\) with finite domains \(D_1,\dots,D_m\), a finite support \(T\) of ballot types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\). A type includes everything the rule uses: its dependency graph and all its conditional approvals. For an outcome \(r\in\prod_j D_j\), let \(\delta_t(r)\) count the issues on which type \(t\) is dissatisfied, and set
\[ \operatorname{Dis}_{\mu}(r)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\delta_t(r). \]
The decision problem asks whether some outcome \(r\) has \(\operatorname{Dis}_{\mu}(r)\le q\); the optimization problem returns a minimizing \(r\). The action is still the paper’s decision—choose one outcome for all issues. Only the population is continuous.

This fits a large participatory-budgeting or public-consultation electorate in which people choose among shared conditional-ballot templates. For example, \(10^5\) participants might use a much smaller set of recurring ballot patterns, so \(n\gg\tau=|T|\). That is a plausible regime, though it depends on the ballot platform encouraging reuse: if everyone writes a bespoke conditional ballot, \(\tau\) could approach \(n\). Each type is a complete description for this rule, so grouping identical ballots loses no information.

This is not fractionalization of the outcome or a noise model. The mass objective is the fraction-weighted disagreement count. A finite profile with \(n_t\) voters of each type gives \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the discrete threshold \(s\) becomes \(q=s/n\). Conversely, a rational mass distribution has integer multiplicities after clearing denominators, though those multiplicities need not be expanded in the input. That makes this a natural high-multiplicity formulation of the paper’s winner-determination problem.

Lead anchor — Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that, assuming ETH, CAV has no \(f(m)d^{o(m)}|P|^{O(1)}\)-time algorithm for any computable \(f\), even when the maximum in-degree is \(\Delta=2\); the result holds in particular when every voter with conditional preferences has an in-star dependency graph on three vertices. The proof reduces from \(k\)-multicolored clique, using one issue per color and a special issue, with one voter for each pair of colors. The reduction and its use of the cited ETH lower bound are given here.

The continuous question is Continuous CAV-In-Star: given the common issue domains and a rational distribution over ballot types, with every nontrivial type dependency graph an in-star on three vertices, decide whether the minimum mass disagreement is at most \(q\). The solution is an outcome \(r\), or a yes/no answer for the threshold version. On the reduction instances, set \(q=0\) and give every constructed voter type positive mass. Then zero disagreement means every type is satisfied, just as in the discrete construction. The reduction has at most \(\binom{k}{2}+1\) voter types; repeating every type \(M\) times makes \(n=M\tau\) arbitrarily large without changing the distribution or the answer. Thus high multiplicity does not remove this hardness. The expected classification is Class B: discrete hardness transfers. More precisely, the same reduction rules out \(f(m)d^{o(m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) time under ETH, where \(L\) is the encoded type and mass data. A useful next question is whether small support or special structure among ballot types helps when the issue count is large.

A second anchor — Theorem 3, also proved here. Assuming ETH, it rules out a \(d^{o(m/\log m)}|P|^{O(1)}\)-time algorithm for CAV even when \(\Delta=1\), in particular when each conditional voter’s graph is an out-star on three vertices. The proof reduces from 2-CSP: each CSP variable becomes an issue, each constraint gets an issue for a satisfying pair of values, and voters enforce consistency between those choices. The \(2\)-CSP lower bound is cited from Marx (2010); the CAV reduction is proved here.

The corresponding Continuous CAV-Out-Star problem is the same mass-minimization question restricted to those out-star types. Its threshold version takes \(q=0\) on the reduction instances, so it asks whether one outcome satisfies every positive-mass type. The answer is again expected to be Class B: the paper’s lower bound carries over to the rational-mass formulation, giving the corresponding \(d^{o(m/\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) barrier under ETH. This anchor shows that the hardness survives even with one incoming dependency at most per issue. It also leaves a worthwhile boundary question: can the logarithmic gap in the dependence on \(m\) be closed for this restricted case?

A tractable boundary — Theorem 4, proved here. For binary issues, it states that group-dichotomous CAV can be optimally solved, while the unrestricted binary case is NP-hard even for \(\Delta=1\). Here, a conditional statement approving \(0_j\) must have an all-zero premise on its in-neighbors; one approving \(1_j\) must have an all-one premise; and one approving both values must have either an all-zero or all-one premise. Unconditional ballots are unrestricted. The proof reduces the restricted case to min-CSP constraints that can be solved by min-cut; its hardness argument uses known min-CSP results.

The continuous counterpart, Continuous Group-Dichotomous CAV, takes binary issue domains and rational masses over types satisfying those same ballot restrictions, and asks for an outcome minimizing \(\operatorname{Dis}_{\mu}(r)\). Its expected class is A: the paper’s min-cut construction should extend to rational type weights, with the weight of each type contributing to its constraints. Rational weights can be scaled to integers with polynomially many bits, so this is a plausible polynomial-time algorithm in the encoded input size. Outside the group-dichotomous restriction, the paper’s discrete NP-hardness instances embed by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); that side is expected to be Class B. The extension of the min-cut algorithm to rational weights is an inference for the mirror, not a continuous result stated in the paper.

The scope is deliberately narrow: these mirrors concern CAV winner determination. They do not continuize issue outcomes, strategic control, time, or axiomatic questions. The strongest plausibility point is that the paper’s objective already aggregates voters by their dissatisfaction counts, so replacing repeated full ballots with type masses preserves exactly the information the rule uses.

The weakest point is also clear: this continuous version may be viewed as normalized, weighted CAV rather than a substantially new optimization problem. Since every rational distribution corresponds mathematically to integer multiplicities after scaling, and the outcome remains a single integral assignment, there is no fractional choice for a continuum to exploit. The paper’s reductions put their combinatorics in the issues and conditional dependencies, not in the indivisibility of the electorate. That weakens any claim that continuization will make these hard cases tractable; it does not weaken the mirror itself. Under the programme’s standard, high multiplicity is the point, and Theorem 4 supplies a credible tractable fragment alongside the hardness boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that all three anchors sit unusually close to ordinary weighted CAV. For an outcome \(r\), the continuous objective is just the weighted sum of the same per-voter disagreement counts. The paper gives voters no actions, interactions, or identity-dependent stakes after their ballots are fixed; the electorate enters only through that sum. So the proposed mirrors may add little modeling structure: they recast a finite-domain optimization problem with voter multiplicities as one with rational weights. That makes this a weaker prospect for ChoCo than problems where a population model changes the feasible operations or brings continuous-optimization methods into play.

That concern is clearest for Theorems 2 and 3. Their reductions use threshold \(0\), so every positive-mass type must be satisfied. The hardness witnesses therefore depend on which ballot types occur, not their masses. Repeating each type to create an arbitrarily large electorate changes nothing. The mirrors are well-defined, and their lower bounds transfer, but those results establish hardness for a support of ballot types rather than showing that population mass itself creates a new computational phenomenon.

Theorem 4 is the best remaining anchor, and the hardest to dismiss. Its group-dichotomous min-cut construction appears to extend to rational type weights, giving a tractable continuous-population question. The negative case is that this looks like a direct weighted version of the paper’s existing reduction and algorithm: rational multiplicities supply capacities or weights, while the optimization still ranges over finite issue assignments. That may be a modest incremental result rather than a substantial continuization result.

But these objections do not defeat the mirrors. The paper’s setting is explicitly elections over shared issues, including participatory budgeting; a large electorate using recurring conditional-ballot patterns is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Complete ballots are valid types, and rational masses are exactly the programme’s intended input. Moving from counts to proportions is not a defect just because the objective remains additive. The zero-threshold witnesses also do not rule out the more general weighted objective, where masses do matter. And an easy weighted extension is still a well-posed computational question.

So the honest negative case is weak. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 may offer limited insight into mass-sensitive behavior, and Theorem 4 may be routine to extend, but I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. In particular, the weighted group-dichotomous question survives the objections.
