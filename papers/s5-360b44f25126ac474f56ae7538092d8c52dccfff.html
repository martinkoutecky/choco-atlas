The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-volume, three-cohort version of the paper’s main truthful-auction lower bound. I would mirror Theorem 4.1, proved in the paper: every deterministic truthful mechanism for three bidders with valuations in \(\SubAdd_m\cup\SingleM_m\) that guarantees a \(\bigl(\frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2}+\frac{12}{\log m}\bigr)\)-approximation requires \(\exp\bigl(\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\log m})\bigr)\) bits of communication. This is the paper’s own lower bound, not a cited result. [Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08241)

A plausible regime is a large procurement platform with many organizations buying from the same catalog: for example, campuses, clinics, or municipal departments with standardized needs. Agents sharing a type have the same complete valuation over bundles, including the same relevant prices or budgets. The population is large while the number of distinct valuation types stays small. The anchor’s hard profiles fit three large cohorts: one single-minded cohort and two subadditive cohorts. That is an instance regime, not a claim that every combinatorial auction has a useful continuum limit.

Here is a concrete mirror, Continuum Truthful Three-Cohort Auction Communication. An instance has \(m\) item categories and a mass distribution \(\mu\) over role-and-valuation types \((r,v)\), where \(r\in\{A,B,C\}\), \(v\in\mathcal V_m=\SubAdd_m\cup\SingleM_m\), and each role has mass \(1/3\). The support has size \(\tau\), and the masses are binary rationals. For the direct counterpart of Theorem 4.1’s inputs, \(\tau=3\): one valuation type of mass \(1/3\) in each role. Think of this as \(N/3\) copies of each role in a large market, with \(N/3\) copies of each item; after normalizing by \(N\), each item has capacity \(1/3\).

A solution is a deterministic, individually truthful mechanism with bundle menus and payments, plus a measurable assignment of agents to integral bundles \(S\subseteq[m]\). If \(q_{r,v,S}\) is the mass of type \((r,v)\) assigned \(S\), feasibility requires \(\sum_S q_{r,v,S}=\mu_{r,v}\) and \(\sum_{r,v,S:\,j\in S}q_{r,v,S}\le 1/3\) for each item \(j\). The \(q\)'s may be fractional because a continuum of agents can be split across bundles; each individual still receives an integral bundle. Welfare is \(\sum_{r,v,S}q_{r,v,S}v(S)\). The communication question is whether menus and an assignment meeting an \(\alpha\)-approximation to maximum feasible welfare can be computed and implemented with polynomial communication in \(m,\tau,L\), or whether a lower bound persists even at \(\tau=3\). Truthfulness is the same underlying incentive question as in the paper: for each agent, reporting their valuation must maximize \(v(S)-p(S)\) against the menu they face.

I think the authors would recognize this as a serious extension of their problem: the item catalog, valuation classes, welfare objective, and truthful menus all remain, while a large population of identical agents replaces each named bidder. Replication gives a natural bridge from the three-bidder setting, and the population-level assignment makes mass the honest quantity. The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s truthful lower bound; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s two-bidder results or its whole separation.

My working prediction is hardness transfers. The lower bound’s combinatorics live in the \(m\)-item valuation and menu structure, rather than in having many bidders, so I would ask whether the \(\exp\bigl(\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\log m})\bigr)\) communication bound survives at fixed support size. The main weakness is that this transfer is not automatic: atomless agents are individually non-pivotal, and a cohort can split across bundles, potentially avoiding the all-or-nothing welfare cases used in the paper’s proof. The prover should therefore first settle whether those type-level splits preserve the menu lower bound. That also raises two natural follow-ups: whether nontruthful mechanisms retain a constant-factor advantage in this market, and how the answer changes as \(\tau\) grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main lower bound is a real named result, but it is a communication-complexity result: it says how many bits three strategic bidders must exchange when each privately holds a valuation. That is the obstacle to mirroring it. ChoCo’s continuous society is an input distribution over types. Once the society is given that way, the theorem’s communication problem is gone; a centralized algorithm receives the distribution. To restore communication, one must add a new account of who knows the distribution and who sends messages.

The proposed three-cohort version has another specific problem: its direct hard profile uses one single-minded cohort and two subadditive cohorts. The paper itself warns that its lower bound does not establish the corresponding result for the restricted domain \(\SingleM\times\SubAdd\times\SubAdd\); its taxation argument needs each bidder’s domain to contain subadditive valuations. Giving every role the full union domain could address that mismatch, but it would not resolve the more basic change in what is being measured.

There are two natural ways to make the communication question precise, and neither preserves the proposed mirror. If each cohort privately knows its valuation and acts as one sender, there are still just three inputs. Replicating each sender’s population changes the market’s scale, but adds no high-multiplicity communication problem. If individuals are the senders, then there are \(N\) private reports; the input is no longer a distribution over three complete types unless we restrict all agents in each cohort to report identically. With that restriction, the communication problem collapses back to three inputs. If the distribution is known centrally, there is no communication game to lower-bound.

The proposed scaled inventory and fractional type-level assignment are sensible co-modifications, but they also change the welfare problem. The paper’s bad-tuple proof reasons about the bundle a bidder receives and uses, for example, the subadditivity bound for two bidders with identical valuations. A replicated cohort may split across integral bundles, so its aggregate allocation is a mixture of outcomes the original bidder could not receive. Those proof bounds do not establish hardness for that mixture model. Asking whether the resulting configuration-allocation problem is hard could be worthwhile; it would be a new algorithmic question, not a consequence or direct mirror of the paper’s communication theorem.

So I would reject this anchor as a ChoCo mirror, including its strongest fixed-support version: either the population does no computational work, or the model changes enough that the paper’s result no longer supports the proposed question. The negative case is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists at all. A separately defined continuum auction with private reports, scaled supply, and explicit truthfulness rules might be interesting. But that would need its own motivation and formalization; this paper alone does not make it a compelling continuization target.
