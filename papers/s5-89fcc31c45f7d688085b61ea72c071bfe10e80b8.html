The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate universe \(U\), initial candidate set \(C\subseteq U\), target \(p\in C\), rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(U\), and an addition or deletion budget \(q\), can at most \(q\) allowed candidates be added or deleted so that, after restricting each ranking to the resulting set \(S\), some rival \(c\ne p\) has \(k\)-approval mass score \(\sum_{\rho}\mu_\rho\mathbf{1}[c\in\operatorname{top}_k(\rho|_S)]\) strictly exceeding \(p\)'s?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(\rho\) of \(U\), carrying masses \(\mu_\rho\); the decision variable is an integral candidate subset \(D\) to add or delete, and the objective is to make a rival beat \(p\) under population \(k\)-approval scores.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover unweighted \(k\)-approval bribery and destructive candidate control, specifically Theorems 8 and 20. They leave weighted and priced variants, edge-cover equivalences, manipulation, voter control, and the remaining results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the Hitting Set reduction yield NP-hardness when the number of ranking types is bounded or parameterized?
  • What is the complexity of the mass-bribery mirror for variable \(k\), type-dependent prices, or swap-distance costs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s unweighted bribery problem, anchored in Theorem 8, which is proved in the paper. The theorem states that, for every \(k\ge3\), constructive bribery in unweighted \(k\)-approval elections with unpriced voters is NP-hard. Its proof reduces X3C to \(3\)-approval bribery: set-voters approve the three elements of an X3C set, the target \(p\) initially trails the element candidates, and \(p\) can be made a winner with at most \(m\) bribes exactly when the selected set-voters form an exact cover.

The corresponding population regime is a large approval election in which millions of voters fall into relatively few relevant ballot types. For \(3\)-approval, a type can be an approved triple \(A\subseteq C\), \(|A|=3\): the ordering within and below the approved triple is irrelevant to the rule and to unpriced bribery. Thus the number of voters may be enormous while the number of types is at most \(\binom{|C|}{3}\), and often much smaller. This is not an assumption about all elections; it is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a large platform or consultation in which recurring approval patterns are common.

Call the mirror \(3\)-Approval-Mass-Bribery\(_\infty\). An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\in\binom{C}{3}\), and a rational mass budget \(\beta\). A solution is a nonnegative transfer \(x_{A,B}\) for \(A,B\in\binom{C}{3}\), satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{A,B}\le\mu_A\) for every source type and \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}\le\beta\). The transfer changes mass \(x_{A,B}\) from voters approving \(A\) to voters approving \(B\). The resulting distribution is \(\mu'_A=\mu_A-\sum_Bx_{A,B}+\sum_Bx_{B,A}\). The question is whether \(p\) is a \(3\)-approval co-winner under \(\mu'\), namely whether \(\sum_{A:p\in A}\mu'_A\ge\sum_{A:c\in A}\mu'_A\) for every \(c\in C\). Equivalently, one may minimize \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}\).

This is recognizably the paper’s bribery problem: the candidates, approval rule, target, arbitrary vote changes, and unit cost per voter are unchanged. Only the electorate has become divisible. If a discrete election has \(a_A\) voters of type \(A\) and \(N\) voters in total, then \(\mu_A=a_A/N\) and a quota of \(q\) bribes becomes \(\beta=q/N\). Discrete briberies are precisely the integral points of this mass-transfer problem.

For fixed \(k=3\), the mirror is a polynomial-size linear program: there are \(\binom{|C|}{3}\) types and polynomially many transfer variables and winner inequalities. Hence I would expect this mirror to be Class A. The X3C reduction exposes why: its integral choice of at most \(m\) set-voters becomes a fractional exact-cover system. The continuous solution may distribute mass among several set-types, so the integrality obstruction responsible for Theorem 8 disappears. That is a substantive computational question about whether the paper’s discrete hardness is population-multiplicity hardness, not a cosmetic replacement of voter counts by percentages.

The paper offers a second, independent hardness anchor in Theorem 20, also proved there. It states that \(k\)-approval elections are computationally resistant to destructive control by either adding or deleting candidates. “Computationally resistant” is defined in the paper as NP-hard. The proof reduces Hitting Set, using buffer and cloned candidates so that a successful candidate-control action corresponds to selecting a hitting set rather than exploiting irrelevant candidates.

The continuous mirror is \(k\)-Approval-Destructive-Candidate-Control\(_\infty\). Its instance contains a finite universe \(U\) of possible candidates, an initial candidate set \(C\), a distinguished candidate \(p\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(U\), and either an addition pool \(A\subseteq U\setminus C\) or a deletion budget. For addition, a solution is \(D\subseteq A\) with \(|D|\le q\); for deletion, it is \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{p\}\) with \(|D|\le q\). After forming \(S=C\cup D\) or \(S=C\setminus D\), each ranking is restricted to \(S\), and \(k\)-approval scores are computed by population mass: \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu,S}(c)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[c\text{ is in the top }k\text{ of }t|_S]\). The question is whether some allowed \(D\) makes another candidate strictly beat \(p\), so that \(p\) is no longer a co-winner.

I would expect this second mirror to be Class B: the Hitting Set hardness transfers. The candidate-control action remains integral, and the combinatorics live in the candidate universe and the agenda rather than in the number of voters. Take the election constructed in Theorem 20 and replicate every ranking type \(L\) times. The resulting electorate has arbitrarily high multiplicity but exactly the same winner comparisons after normalization. Thus a polynomial algorithm for the continuous-population version would solve the paper’s Hitting Set reduction. This also shows that continuization need not make every hard result easy; it separates population-induced hardness from candidate-induced hardness.

These mirrors cover Theorem 8’s \(3\)-approval constructive bribery and Theorem 20’s destructive candidate control. They do not claim to cover the paper’s weighted or priced variants, its edge-cover equivalences, or all manipulation and voter-control results. Natural follow-up questions include whether mass bribery remains tractable for variable \(k\), type-dependent prices, or swap-distance costs, and whether candidate-control hardness persists under parameters such as the number of ranking types.

The weakest point is the bribery mirror’s divisibility assumption. A critic can reasonably say that persuading \(0.37\) of a homogeneous voter type is meaningful only when individuals can be acted on independently; if the “type” is an indivisible organization or bloc, the LP relaxation changes the problem. That is a genuine limitation, not a defect to hide. But it is exactly the high-multiplicity regime the programme is designed to study, and Theorem 20 remains a faithful mirror even under that criticism because its candidate action stays discrete.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly make a strong negative case against these two anchors. The usual objections fail under the programme’s rules.

For Theorem 8, the proposed \(3\)-approval mass-bribery problem is a legitimate high-multiplicity version. Although the paper describes complete rankings, for unpriced \(k\)-approval bribery the top-\(k\) approval set is a complete type: lower-ranked candidates and the order within the approved set affect neither feasibility nor cost. A large electorate with recurring approval patterns is entirely plausible, and arbitrarily many voters of each pattern can be represented by a rational mass.

The fact that the X3C reduction uses one voter per set does not rescue the negative case. Every such voter type can be replicated \(L\) times, with the quota scaled by \(L\), producing an arbitrarily large high-multiplicity election with the same normalized scores. Passing to divisible mass then asks exactly whether the integral obstruction survives fractionalization. That is a genuine computational question, not an illegitimate relaxation.

The best criticism is that, for fixed \(k=3\), this mirror is an elementary LP with \(\binom{m}{3}\) types and polynomially many transfer variables. It has no difficult pricing problem and may be less technically rich than the programme’s preferred examples. But “the continuous answer is easy” is explicitly not an objection: this is precisely a possible Class A result.

Theorem 20 is an even stronger anchor. Fix a universe of potential candidates \(U\), represent the society by a rational distribution over complete rankings of \(U\), and retain the paper’s discrete choice of candidates to add or delete. For a surviving set \(S\), define

\[ \mathrm{sc}_{\mu,S}(c) = \sum_{\rho}\mu_\rho \mathbf{1}\!\left[c\in\operatorname{top}_k(\rho|_S)\right]. \]

This is a population-continuous candidate-control problem. The candidate action need not be fractional: continuization concerns the population, not every other object in the instance. A large consultation, platform, or electorate with recurring complete preference orders is a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

Nor does the construction depend essentially on named individual voters. Replicating every ranking type \(L\) times preserves all winner comparisons after normalization, while making the population arbitrarily large relative to its type support. The Hitting Set structure remains in the candidate universe, so the mirror asks a meaningful question about how population continuity interacts with candidate control. Calling it merely “weighted voting” would be incorrect under the programme’s own rules: high-multiplicity formulations support, rather than collide with, the proposed mirror.

There is no continuous treatment already supplied by the paper, and existing high-multiplicity election literature would strengthen rather than undermine the case. Neither anchor has identity-dependent objectives, vanishing costs, or an absence of plausible repeated types.

Thus the negative case has only modest criticisms: the first mirror may be technically routine, and the second is a hybrid with a discrete candidate-control action. Neither defeats the mirrors. The universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror is therefore not honestly supportable; the proponent’s Theorem 20 anchor in particular survives intact.
