What the mirror covers

The proposal targets Theorems 1 and 2; it leaves Corollary 1's trust characterization, Proposition 1's individual-rationality condition, and Section 6's impossibility discussion aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow. This paper has no named result about whether finding an efficient allocation is in P, NP-hard, or parameterized tractable. Its named results concern mechanism properties, and it gives no running time for computing \(\pi^\ast\). So it does not provide the kind of complexity anchor your programme usually prioritizes.

The best available anchor is the paired characterization in Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says that the efficient allocation rule \(\pi^\ast\) with the post-execution-verification payment rule \(x^{\mathrm{PEV}}\) is ex-post truthful if every agent’s valuation is multilinear in agents’ probabilities of success. Theorem 2 says that if this mechanism is ex-post truthful for all type profiles, then every agent’s valuation must be multilinear in those probabilities. Together, they give an exact boundary for when PEV implements efficient task allocation truthfully.

A continuous mirror I would put to a prover is Mass PEV Allocation. Fix a finite set of roles in a task group, such as driver and passenger roles. A complete type specifies an agent’s role, valuation over every feasible local allocation, and probability-of-success profile. The instance gives a finite type domain, rational masses \(\mu_{i,a}\) of each type \(a\) in each role \(i\), and a finite catalogue of feasible local task configurations. A decision variable \(y_g\geq 0\) gives the mass assigned to configuration \(g\); the masses must match the supplied type totals. Maximize total expected social welfare over these configurations. The requested solution is an optimal mass assignment and, for each type and role, PEV payments per unit mass conditional on success or failure; it must also determine whether those payments make truthful reporting ex-post optimal throughout the stated type domain, or provide a counterexample profile and deviation.

The regime is a large service with many repeated, bounded-size task groups: for example, commuter ridesharing along common routes and time windows. Millions of agents might fall into a much smaller set of complete types defined by trip, vehicle capacity, detour cost, reliability, and preferences over partner types. That is plausible only if those preferences are genuinely shared: preferences tied to named individuals would create many distinct types. The bounded group size fits settings such as ridesharing, but narrows the paper’s general model.

For an explicitly listed configuration catalogue, this mirror is a linear program over the \(y_g\), and the finite valuation tables make the multilinearity condition checkable. I would therefore expect that restricted version to be tractable. If feasible configurations are implicit and exponentially numerous, tractability turns on the pricing problem; this paper gives no result about that problem. The mirror covers the PEV truthfulness boundary in Theorems 1 and 2, not the paper’s separate trust aggregation result or its broader impossibility discussion.

The soft spot is that Theorems 1 and 2 assume an efficient allocation rule; they do not analyze its computational cost. Also, a continuum of type masses does not by itself prove that an individual’s incentive problem carries over unchanged. The repeated, finite-size task-group model makes that extension plausible, but it is a proposed high-multiplicity version, not a result established by the authors. Under a strict requirement that anchors assert complexity-class results, this paper has no qualifying anchor; the case above is the best mechanism-design mirror available, not a complexity result from the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the anchor has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize when an already-defined PEV mechanism is ex-post truthful, conditional on an efficient allocation rule \(\pi^\ast\). They give no algorithm, running time, complexity classification, or input representation for computing \(\pi^\ast\). Theorem 1’s condition and Theorem 2’s boundary are mechanism-design results, not computational results of the kind this programme is meant to chart.

The proposed Mass PEV Allocation problem adds a new optimization question: how to assign mass to task configurations. With an explicit configuration catalogue, it is a generic linear program. With an implicit catalogue, its difficulty turns on a pricing problem that the paper never specifies. Either version could become a worthwhile new project, but neither computational question is supplied by Theorems 1 and 2. For explicit finite valuation tables, the multilinearity condition can also be checked directly; the theorem already settles the truthfulness claim once that condition is known.

The best setting for the mirror is repeated, standardized task groups: for example, many commuters on common routes with shared reliability and preference types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not reject it just because the paper’s ridesharing example names individuals or includes externalities. The obstacle is what happens to the theorem’s incentive question in the continuous limit. A single agent in an atomless population has measure zero. Changing that agent’s report does not change the aggregate mass assignment or its welfare optimum. The paper’s argument turns on an agent’s report changing the chosen allocation and on PEV payments reflecting the resulting externality. In the atomless model, that individual pivot disappears. Treating a positive-mass type cohort as a strategic player restores a nonzero effect, but changes the question to truthfulness for coordinated cohorts rather than the individual truthfulness characterized in the paper.

A finite high-multiplicity version could preserve individual deviations by retaining each person’s positive \(1/n\) share. That may be useful, but the interesting computation would still be the new task of finding and pricing feasible configurations. The paper supplies no complexity result for that task, and its truthfulness characterization does not determine its complexity.

So the negative case is strong against this particular anchor as a computational mirror: the paper proves no computational claim for the continuous model to test. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous research can arise from the paper. A configuration-allocation or pricing problem for repeated task groups could be valuable on its own; the material here does not establish that it is unworthy, only that it is an extension rather than a continuization of the named results.
