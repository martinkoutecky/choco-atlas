The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), fixed \(k>1\), a target \(c\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over a finite support of nonmanipulator partial-order types, and rational manipulator mass \(\alpha>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t+\alpha=1\), can the cohort masses be allocated among rankings extending their partial orders and the manipulator mass among ballots so that \(c\) wins uniquely under \(k\)-approval?

The model it lives in

Use variables \(x_{t,S}\) for mass of type \(t\) completed to a ballot with top-\(k\) set \(S\), and \(y_S\) for coalition mass using \(S\). Require each type’s allocations to sum to \(\mu_t\), coalition allocations to sum to \(\alpha\), and allow \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(S\) is feasible for that partial order. Maximize the minimum score margin of \(c\) over its opponents; a positive optimum means \(c\) can win uniquely.

What the mirror covers

Covers the \(k\)-approval Weak Manipulation question anchored by Theorem 1; it leaves the paper’s other hardness results, Strong Manipulation results, and other voting rules aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize when finite instances of Theorem 1’s single-manipulator setting correspond to positive-mass continuous instances, and when the discrete hardness survives that change.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Weak Manipulation for \(k\)-approval, with a coalition whose size grows with the electorate. The natural regime is a large membership vote or public election where detailed rankings are private, but voters fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of classes with the same known partial order. A coordinated bloc shares a target candidate and can choose its ballots. The electorate may be enormous while \(\tau\) remains small.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every fixed constant \(k>1\), WM under \(k\)-approval is NP-complete even with one manipulator. The proof reduces from the cited NP-complete Possible Winner problem for 2-approval and extends the construction to other fixed \(k\). This is a strong anchor because it identifies hardness in the paper’s central setting: manipulation when the other votes are only partially known.

A continuous version would be Continuous Weak Manipulation for \(k\)-Approval. An instance has candidates \(C\), a target \(c\), fixed \(k>1\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) for a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of nonmanipulator partial-order types, and a rational manipulator mass \(\alpha>0\), with \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t+\alpha=1\). It asks whether the mass can be completed and cast so that \(c\) wins uniquely. A solution assigns each partial-order cohort’s mass across its possible complete-ranking extensions, and assigns the coalition’s mass across complete ballots. Each unit of mass still receives an ordinary integral ranking; only the cohort’s aggregate can split.

For \(k\)-approval, it is enough to track each ballot’s top-\(k\) set. Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) completed to a ranking with top-\(k\) set \(S\), and \(y_S\) the manipulator mass voting with top-\(k\) set \(S\). Require \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_S y_S=\alpha\), and \(x_{t,S}=0\) unless \(S\) can be the first \(k\) candidates of a linear extension of type \(t\)’s partial order. The resulting scores are
\[ \mathrm{sc}(a)=\sum_{t,S:\,a\in S}x_{t,S}+\sum_{S:\,a\in S}y_S. \]
The question is whether these variables can satisfy \(\mathrm{sc}(c)>\mathrm{sc}(a)\) for every \(a\ne c\). This is a decision problem, so there is no cost objective.

I expect this continuous question to be tractable (Class A) for fixed \(k\). It is a linear feasibility problem: there are at most \(O(\tau m^k)\) cohort variables and \(O(m^k)\) coalition variables, and feasible top-\(k\) sets can be checked from the partial order. Strict victory can be handled by maximizing a common score margin. The discrete NP-hardness may disappear because a continuous cohort can divide its mass among completions instead of making indivisible per-voter choices. That is exactly the population-level change this mirror is meant to test.

The paper’s authors should recognize the underlying question: same rule, target, coalition action, partial-vote uncertainty, and winner condition. The change is to ask it for a high-multiplicity population in which cohorts have rational masses. This continuizes the society, not the candidates or outcome; fractional quantities describe how many agents take each ordinary ballot.

My weakest point is that Theorem 1’s hardness already holds with one manipulator, while this mirror gives the coalition positive population mass. A single manipulator’s share tends to zero as the electorate grows, and the theorem does not establish hardness for coalitions proportional to the population. So this is a continuous version of the paper’s WM problem family, not a limit that preserves that theorem’s one-manipulator parameter. The main follow-up is to establish the precise finite-to-continuous dictionary: when does a rational mass solution correspond to a replicated finite instance, and which discrete hard instances lose their hardness once voter counts and completions become divisible? Variable \(k\), where the top-\(k\) sets are no longer polynomially enumerable, is another natural boundary question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proposed anchor is that Theorem 1’s hardness is for one manipulator. In an electorate of size \(n\), that manipulator has mass \(1/n\), which vanishes in the continuum. Giving the coalition a positive mass \(\alpha\) instead studies coalitions whose size grows with \(n\). The paper’s theorem does not establish hardness in that regime, so the proposed LP would not mirror the theorem’s specific complexity result.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror. Weak Manipulation is defined for any nonempty coalition, and a positive-mass bloc with shared aims and a small number of recurring partial-order types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Splitting each type’s mass among feasible completions still describes an actual completion of a nonatomic society: different members can hold different rankings consistent with the same partial order. For fixed \(k\), the resulting \(k\)-approval question is well-posed and has a compact linear formulation. The paper’s existential-completion quantifier survives that change.

So I cannot make a convincing case that this anchor has no worthwhile continuous mirror. The gap is that Theorem 1 alone does not justify claims about the one-manipulator limit; the proposed continuous problem needs its own finite-to-continuous dictionary. But rejecting it on that basis would confuse failure to preserve one theorem’s parameter regime with failure of the broader high-multiplicity question. My negative case is weak here, and this anchor survives.
