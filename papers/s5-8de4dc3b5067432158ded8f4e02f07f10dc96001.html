The case FOR (proponent)

The anchor audit comes up empty under your rules. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its closest result is the unnumbered “Theorem” in Section 4.1: for a fixed conjecture \(Z\), the \(Z\)-core is nonempty if and only if the conjectured worth function \(z\) is balanced. In full, this means that every balanced collection of coalitions, with weights \(\lambda_S\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\ni f}\lambda_S=1\) for each player \(f\), must satisfy \(\sum_S\lambda_S z(S)\le z(N)\). The paper presents this as a recovery of Bondareva–Shapley, not as a complexity result. The six “Characteristics” are not computational results either.

The strongest adjacent positive case would be a continuous version of that core-existence question. Picture a large industry of small firms, with many firms but relatively few complete types: each type records a firm’s vector of market specializations and any merger parameters the model uses. The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types. In each market layer, mass can be divided among merger coalitions, with the paper’s cross-layer effects and a specified conjecture about how the rest of society responds.

A precise candidate problem is: given \(\mu\), a conjecture \(Z\), and a finite rational polyhedral representation of the resulting mass-level conjectured worth function \(z\), decide whether there is a per-unit payoff \(x_t\) for each type \(t\) such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t \le z(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t \nu_t x_t \ge z(\nu) \quad\text{for every } 0\le \nu\le\mu. \]
Here \(\nu\) describes the type composition of a deviating subpopulation; a solution is the payoff vector \(x\). This keeps the paper’s central object—coalitional stability under multiple membership and externalities—while making the population itself continuous. With the stated polyhedral representation, I would expect a Class A result via LP and separation. Further questions include whether a measure-based version of the balancedness theorem holds, and whether computing \(z\) from the underlying layered merger model has an efficient pricing algorithm.

The mirror is plausible as a follow-on to the paper: firms of the same complete type are interchangeable, and large populations over a modest number of types are a credible regime. But this is not an anchored case under the requested standard. The paper neither defines mass-level merger structures nor gives a computational representation of \(z\); the polyhedral input is an added assumption that may hide the hard part. So the defensible positive claim is narrow: the paper’s core-existence theorem suggests a natural continuous computational problem, but the paper itself supplies no eligible computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the proponent’s own audit: this paper gives no computational result to continuize. Its unnumbered theorem says that the \(Z\)-core is nonempty exactly when \(z\) is balanced. It gives no algorithm, input encoding, complexity bound, or pricing problem. The six characteristics are also structural observations, not computational claims. Core existence is a natural question to ask computationally, but that would be a new problem suggested by the paper, not a computational result in it.

The proposed mass-core question does not yet close that gap. In the paper, \(z(S)\) comes from optimizing over feasible coalition structures in every layer and applying a conjecture about how the rest of the finite society responds. Replacing \(S\) with a type-mass vector \(\nu\) does not define those mass-level structures or responses. With multiple membership, it must also say how the same mass of agents is coordinated across overlapping coalitions in different layers. The candidate’s polyhedral representation of \(z\) supplies this missing object rather than deriving it from the paper’s game.

That representation also leaves the computational claim unsettled. If \(z\) is given explicitly by a polynomial-size rational description, the core test may be generic LP or separation. If it is generated from the merger model, its description size and pricing complexity are precisely the unresolved work. So the proposed Class A expectation does not follow from the paper’s theorem.

The firm example does not support a stronger objection that no high-multiplicity regime is sensible: many firms sharing a small set of complete specialization types is plausible. But making that into a nondegenerate continuum model requires specifying how market demand, coalition worths, and cross-layer membership behave as the population grows. Those are legitimate modeling choices under the programme’s scope; they simply are not settled by this paper.

So the negative case is strong on eligibility: there is no anchored computational result here, and the suggested mirror currently assumes away its main computational input. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous model could exist. A carefully specified repeated-type firm model might be worth studying, but that would be a follow-on research programme rather than a mirror of a computational result in this note.
