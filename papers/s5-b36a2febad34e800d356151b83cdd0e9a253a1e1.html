The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\) Boolean base events, supported order types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), and each type’s order parameters, determine whether there is a nonzero accepted-mass vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) such that \(\sum_t x_t a_t(\omega)\ge 0\) for every state \(\omega\in\{0,1\}^k\), where \(a_t(\omega)=-q_t\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in\psi_t}(\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in\phi_t}-p_t)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous society has types given by complete order descriptions and masses μ_t. The decision variable x_t is the mass of that type’s traders whose individually indivisible orders are accepted; the objective is to find any nonzero accepted mass subject to the auctioneer’s state-by-state no-loss constraints.

The objection that survived

For the existence objective, any safe nonzero vector can be scaled below every positive μ_t, so the cohort caps do not matter and the resulting problem is exactly divisible-order matching from Theorem 1.6.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.7’s indivisible-order matching results; its mass formulation coincides with Theorem 1.6’s divisible-order matching, which is polynomial for logarithmically many base events and coNP-complete for unrestricted events. It leaves the market-maker designs and distributed-computation results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the problem’s complexity depend jointly on support size τ, the number of base events \(k\), and the order-formula encoding length?
  • For finite markets with repeated order types, how many traders are needed to realize or approximate a feasible continuous accepted-mass vector?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of indivisible-order matching. It keeps the market’s question intact: can the auctioneer accept some orders without losing money in any possible state?

A type \(t\) is a complete order description: buy or sell, the Boolean claim \(\phi_t\), any condition \(\psi_t\), quantity \(q_t\), and limit price \(p_t\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) order types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of traders submitting that exact order. For a state \(\omega\), an individual order has payoff to its trader
\[ \Upsilon_t(\omega)=q_t\,\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in\psi_t} \bigl(\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in\phi_t}-p_t\bigr), \]
so the auctioneer’s payoff is its negative.

Call the problem Continuous-Population Indivisible-Order Matching. Given the base Boolean events, the supported order types and their masses, and their rational order parameters, choose accepted masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Each individual trader’s order is still accepted whole or rejected; \(x_t\) is the mass of the type’s cohort whose orders are accepted. The question is whether there is a nonzero vector \(x\) such that
\[ -\sum_t x_t\Upsilon_t(\omega)\geq 0 \qquad\text{for every state }\omega. \]
A solution is the vector of accepted masses. Thus the continuous object is the population of traders, and the action remains acceptance of the original indivisible order. The aggregate payoff is fractional because it sums over a continuum of traders.

The anchor is Theorem 1.7. As stated in the chapter, indivisible matching is NP-complete with \(O(\log n)\) base securities and \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete with unrestricted securities; \(n\) bounds the input size and the number of base securities. The chapter omits the proof for space and says its matching analysis follows Fortnow et al. [FKPW05]. The logarithmic-security result is my strongest anchor: it offers a clear question about whether hardness caused by choosing an indivisible subset of orders disappears when a large cohort can be selected by mass.

A plausible regime is a large prediction-market platform or internal forecasting market where many traders use a small set of standardized order templates. Cohorts might share the claim, conditioning event, quantity, and limit price because they follow the same trading policy or hedge the same exposure. The population could be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct templates stays modest. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that every prediction market has this structure.

The continuous problem has a direct LP formulation: maximize \(\sum_t x_t\), subject to \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\) and the no-loss inequalities for all states. If there are \(k=O(\log B)\) base events, where \(B\) is the encoded input length, there are only \(2^k=\mathrm{poly}(B)\) state constraints. The continuous version is then polynomial-time solvable by an ordinary LP. I expect the logarithmic-security hardness in Theorem 1.7(i) to dissolve: the discrete subset choice has become a mass-allocation problem. This is a Class A outcome for that regime.

With unrestricted \(k\), I expect hardness to remain, for a different and population-independent reason. A single type that buys \(S_\phi\) at price \(1/2\) has a safe positive accepted mass exactly when \(\phi\) is unsatisfiable: any satisfying state makes the auctioneer lose on every positive fill, while an unsatisfiable claim is safe in every state. This gives coNP-hardness even when \(\tau=1\). I expect coNP-completeness for the continuous version, with a dual LP certificate for nonexistence of a positive safe fill. That would put the unrestricted case on the Boolean-state, Class B-style side of the boundary; the original \(\Sigma_2^p\) classification need not survive the convexification.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem’s continuous-population form: the auctioneer, contingent orders, and state-by-state no-loss condition are all preserved. The paper itself distinguishes indivisible from divisible orders, so the change in aggregate acceptance is material and visible. My weakest point is exactly that change: the mass variable \(x_t\) produces the same aggregate effect as divisible order filling. A reader could say the proposed mirror therefore tracks the chapter’s divisible variant. The reply is that each trader’s order remains indivisible; it is the population of otherwise indistinguishable traders that admits fractional selection. Whether the authors accept that as the natural high-multiplicity form of indivisible matching is the key plausibility test.

This mirror covers Theorem 1.7’s matching results, not the chapter’s market-maker designs or equilibrium analysis. It also raises useful follow-up questions: how does complexity depend jointly on support size \(\tau\), event count \(k\), and formula structure; which restricted Boolean languages make state separation efficient; and how large must a finite repeated-order market be to represent a continuous solution exactly?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor, Theorem 1.7, runs straight into the chapter’s Theorem 1.6. Write \(a_t(\omega)=-\Upsilon_t(\omega)\) for the auctioneer’s payoff from one order of type \(t\). The proposed continuous decision asks whether some \(x\neq 0\) satisfies
\[ 0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_t a_t(\omega)\geq 0\quad\text{for every }\omega. \]
But the upper bounds \(\mu_t\) do not affect whether such an \(x\) exists. If a nonzero safe vector \(y\) exists, scale it by a sufficiently small \(\epsilon>0\) so that \(\epsilon y_t\leq\mu_t\) for every supported type. The question therefore reduces to whether a nonzero fractional combination of the orders is safe.

That is precisely divisible-order matching. A cohort of identical indivisible orders accepted in some mass has the same aggregate payoff as one divisible order of the corresponding total quantity. The auctioneer’s constraints see only that aggregate payoff, so individual order indivisibility has disappeared. The chapter already gives the matching complexity for divisible orders: polynomial time with logarithmically many base securities, and co-NP-completeness with unrestricted securities (Theorem 1.6). The proponent’s unrestricted one-order satisfiability argument is also the same co-NP-hardness argument used there. So the proposed mirror reproduces a variant the paper already analyzes.

A more useful population objective, such as maximizing safe trading volume, does make cohort supplies matter. But it gives the natural optimization version of divisible matching. The chapter explicitly identifies objectives such as trading volume as extensions of its matching problem, and its polynomial-time proof already maximizes total accepted order fraction in the logarithmic-security case. That could be a worthwhile market-design question in its own right; it does not supply a distinct continuous-population complexity problem.

The plausible high-multiplicity regime—many traders using a small set of order templates—makes the collapse exact. If instead the market has enough distinct formulas, conditions, prices, or quantities to preserve the original order-by-order choices, then the complete type space grows with the order list. There is no repeated-type regime doing the computational work; the continuous variables merely fractionally relax singleton orders. In the unrestricted case, the hardness already occurs with one order type, so population multiplicity cannot be the source of a new boundary.

The other likely computational anchor, Section 1.5’s market computation results, is harder to turn into a population problem. Agents there hold coordinate-specific input bits, and the function may depend on which agent has which bit. Grouping agents just by bit value loses that identity. Restricting to symmetric aggregate functions makes the target depend on a count or mass, but then the chapter’s threshold-function characterization already covers the central computation result. Section 1.4’s market-maker algorithms operate on aggregate security inventories; the number of traders is not their computational parameter.

I cannot rule out every newly invented type space or market objective. The negative case is strongest for the named results: the one credible high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 1.7 becomes the paper’s already-studied divisible problem, while preserving its order-specific combinatorics leaves no useful multiplicity. On this paper, that is a substantial reason not to prioritize a continuous mirror.
