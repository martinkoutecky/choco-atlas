The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, rational masses \(\mu_j\) and observed rankings \(\succ_j\) for finitely many cohorts, with an adversarial hidden common valuation vector \(v_j\) per cohort consistent with its ranking, design a randomized mechanism that queries at most \(q\) values per cohort and returns \(a\in A\), minimizing worst-case distortion \(\max_v \frac{\max_{b\in A}\sum_j\mu_j v_j(b)}{\mathbb{E}[\sum_j\mu_j v_j(\hat a)]}\).

The model it lives in

Finite cohorts \(j\) have rational masses \(\mu_j\) and rankings \(\succ_j\); each hides one common nonnegative valuation vector \(v_j\). A query to cohort \(j\) reveals \(v_j(a)\) for a chosen alternative, with at most \(q\) queries per cohort; the mechanism outputs one integral alternative and minimizes worst-case expected utilitarian distortion.

The objection that survived

The proponent's per-unit query oracle is not a coherent high-multiplicity model: if valuations are included in the input, no queries are needed, while if only ordinal masses are given, the hidden valuation support and query interface are unspecified.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 15 and supports a cohort-level transfer of Theorem 17. It leaves the average-distortion results such as Theorems 1, 6, and 11 aside because finite iid fluctuations are not preserved by a deterministic mass profile, as well as the remaining lower bounds unless separately reformulated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the weighted cohort version of RTSEARCH achieve \(O(\log m)\) worst-case distortion with \(O(\log m)\) queries per cohort?
  • Does the cohort-level model retain the \(\Omega(\log m)\) deterministic query lower bound, and is there a deterministic \(O(\log m)\)-query constant-distortion mechanism?
  • How should cohort queries and rational mass encodings be related to large finite electorates without changing the guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper does support a positive case, although it has no NP-hardness anchor. Its named computational results are query-complexity and distortion bounds. My lead mirror is based on Theorem 15.

Theorem 15, proved in this paper, states that the randomized mechanism RTSEARCH achieves worst-case distortion \(O(\log m)\) using \(O(\log m)\) value queries per agent. Given an agent’s top value \(\nu_i\), RTSEARCH chooses a random threshold scale \(r\), queries the set \(S_{i,r}\) of alternatives whose value exceeds \(\nu_i/2^r\), and returns an alternative maximizing \(\sum_i \nu_i I\{a\in S_{i,r}\}\). The proof shows that the artificial welfare generated by these threshold sets is within a factor of \(2\) of true social welfare, and that averaging over \(O(\log m)\) threshold scales loses only another logarithmic factor.

A natural continuous-population version is Continuous Threshold-Query Distortion.

There are \(m\) alternatives \(A\), and a society is a finite-support probability measure \(\mu\) over complete latent voter types \(\theta=(\succ,v)\), where \(\succ\) is a strict ranking and \(v:A\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) is a valuation vector consistent with that ranking. The mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of society of that type. The mechanism observes only the ordinal mass profile \(\rho_\mu(\succ)=\sum_{\theta:\theta\text{ has ranking }\succ}\mu_\theta\); the valuation components remain hidden behind a query oracle. The finite-support and rationality assumptions give a finite encoding, while the population itself is represented by mass rather than named individuals.

A query policy may ask each unit of population for the value of at most \(q\) alternatives, possibly adaptively. Its output is one integral alternative \(a\in A\). Define

\[ W_\mu(a)=\int v_x(a)\,d\mu(x), \]

and define distortion by

\[ \operatorname{dist}(M,\mu)= \frac{\max_{a\in A}W_\mu(a)} {W_\mu(M(\rho_\mu,\text{query answers}))}. \]

The decision version asks: given \(m\), \(q\), and a distortion target \(D\), does there exist a randomized \(q\)-query mechanism whose worst-case distortion is at most \(D\) over all admissible continuous societies? A solution is the query policy together with the rule selecting the winning alternative.

The direct continuous analogue of RTSEARCH chooses \(r\) uniformly from \(O(\log m)\) scales. For every mass point \(x\), it queries the top-ranked value \(\nu_x\), uses binary search to identify the alternatives whose values exceed \(\nu_x/2^r\), and returns an alternative maximizing

\[ \int \nu_x I\{a\in S_{x,r}\}\,d\mu(x). \]

The proof of Lemma 16 lifts from sums to integrals: for every alternative, artificial welfare remains between its true welfare and twice its true welfare. Thus the expected \(O(\log m)\) distortion and \(O(\log m)\) query rate are very plausible in this continuous model. I would expect this problem to be Class A.

The regime is quite plausible. Consider a national consultation, large customer population, or employee electorate with millions of participants but relatively few recurring preference/value cohorts: perhaps \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of ranking and valuation-response types. Mass is the honest quantity—“\(18.4\%\) of the population values policy \(a\) above its threshold”—while the winner remains a single discrete alternative. Nothing here continuizes the outcome space.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. It retains their ordinal input, hidden cardinal values, limited value queries, utilitarian welfare, and distortion objective. The only substantive modification is replacing \(\sum_i\) by an integral over population mass and requiring a finite-support representation of the relevant types.

A useful companion anchor is Theorem 17, also proved in this paper. It states that every deterministic mechanism achieving constant worst-case distortion must make \(\Omega(\log m)\) queries per agent. More quantitatively, for \(m\ge154\) and \(2\le\lambda\le\log m\), every deterministic mechanism using at most \(\lambda\) queries per agent has worst-case distortion at least

\[ \frac18 m^{1/(3\lambda)}. \]

The corresponding Continuous Query-Budget Frontier asks, for the same mass society model, whether a deterministic mechanism with at most \(q\) queries per unit mass can guarantee constant distortion over every rational finite-support society. A solution is either such a mechanism or a continuous adversarial society witnessing failure.

Under the local-query interpretation—queries are charged per unit of population rather than once per repeated cohort—the lower-bound construction should transfer by replacing each finite agent with a mass slice and replacing sums by integrals. Its cyclic ranking construction uses \(\Theta(m)\) observable ranking types, but one can replicate each slice arbitrarily many times, so the physical population may still satisfy \(N\gg\tau\). I therefore expect the \(\Omega(\log m)\) lower bound to survive as a Class B-style inheritance of the finite information barrier, although it is not an NP-hardness result. Together with Theorem 15, it would give a continuous query-complexity frontier: randomized \(O(\log m)\) queries suffice, while deterministic constant distortion requires \(\Omega(\log m)\) queries.

This second mirror generates several worthwhile questions: whether deterministic \(O(\log m)\)-query mechanisms exist; whether the randomized/deterministic gap survives; whether the lower bound remains true for fixed \(\tau\); and how continuous mechanisms can be rounded to large finite electorates.

I would not use Theorems 1, 6, or 11 as my principal anchors. Their average-distortion results depend heavily on finite-sample fluctuations under impartial culture. In a literal atomless iid population, exact law-of-large-numbers effects can make every alternative have the same aggregate expected welfare, collapsing the average-distortion problem. A nontrivial continuous version would need a measure-valued prior over societies or common shocks, which is a further modelling choice.

The weakest point is therefore the query semantics. If querying one homogeneous type reveals its cardinal information for the entire mass at once, the lower-bound mirror becomes much easier and Theorem 17 may disappear. Conversely, an unrestricted pointwise query oracle over an atomless population may not have a satisfactory finite representation. The proposed finite-support, per-unit-query model is a deliberate bridge between those extremes. Even if the lower-bound companion is rejected, the Theorem 15 mirror remains a concrete and author-recognisable continuous population problem with a credible Class A algorithmic direction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is semantic: Theorem 15 is fundamentally about individually addressable private cardinal information, not about population multiplicity.

RTSEARCH receives a query answer from a named agent \(i\), and its budget is charged separately for each such agent. Replacing \(\sum_i\) by \(\int d\mu\) is therefore not automatically a continuous computational problem. If the full distribution \(\mu\) over \(\theta=(\succ,v)\) is part of the input, then \(W_\mu(a)=\int v(a)\,d\mu\) is already computable and the optimal alternative requires zero queries. If only the ordinal projection \(\rho_\mu\) is supplied, then the hidden valuation component is not part of the finite input at all; it is an oracle or sampling environment. The claimed “finite-support encoding” does not resolve this, since exposing the finite support would expose the cardinal values that queries were meant to reveal.

There are then only two natural query semantics. Under type-level or aggregate queries, one query to a repeated latent type informs the whole mass of that type. This is the correct high-multiplicity interpretation, but it changes the paper’s resource measure from queries per agent to queries per type or cohort. Under pointwise queries, one insists on querying every unit of mass separately. That preserves the theorem’s information model, but introduces an artificial continuum of individually addressable private records and is no longer a high-multiplicity optimization problem. It is a new distributional query-oracle model.

Theorem 17 has the same problem more sharply. Its lower bound relies on distinguishing queried from unqueried named agents and choosing concealed values independently for them. If the cyclic agents are replicated as genuinely identical complete types, a type-level query collapses all replicas and the per-agent adversary no longer applies. If replicas retain independent concealed values, they are different complete types, so replication has not created high multiplicity. Charging queries per infinitesimal unit preserves the lower bound only by stipulation, not because of continuization.

That is a serious objection to the proponent’s exact formulation: its “continuous” model has not specified a coherent relationship between hidden types, observable cohorts, and query cost. The integral inequality behind Lemma 16 is easy; the missing part is a finite, population-level information model.

But the objection does not defeat every better mirror. One can define a legitimate latent-cohort model: the society consists of known cohorts with masses \(\mu_j\) and common rankings, while each cohort has a hidden common valuation vector \(v_j\). A query to cohort \(j\) reveals \(v_j(a)\) for its entire mass. RTSEARCH then becomes

\[ \arg\max_a \sum_j \mu_j \nu_j I\{a\in S_{j,r}\}, \]

and Lemma 16 lifts directly to the weighted sum. Replicating each cohort arbitrarily many times gives a genuine high-multiplicity society. Theorem 17’s construction can likewise be represented by \(m\) positive-mass cohorts, with the same query transcript and lower bound when queries are charged per cohort.

Thus the negative case is real against the proposed per-unit oracle and against any claim that the integral replacement alone is a contribution. It is not, however, an airtight universal rejection. A cohort-level hidden-cardinal model is author-recognisable, population-based, and preserves Theorem 15’s problem. I would therefore judge the negative case weak: Theorem 15 remains a viable green anchor, while Theorem 17 is more sensitive to the precise query semantics.
