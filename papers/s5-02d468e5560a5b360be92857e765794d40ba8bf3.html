The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a narrow one: systemic capital allocation for a large population of similar financial institutions. It uses one application of the paper, not its multi-utility representation results.

My lead anchor is Proposition 5.11, proved in this paper as an application of its general dual representation. Under Assumption 5.10—\(\mathcal A\) is convex and upward closed, \(\Lambda\) is nondecreasing and concave, \(\Lambda^{-1}(\mathcal A)\) is weak-star closed, and the resulting risk set is nontrivial for some input—it states that, for every \(X\in L^\infty(\mathbb R^d)\),
\[ R(X)=\bigcap_{w\in\mathbb R^d_+\setminus\{0\}}\ \bigcap_{\substack{Z\in L^1(\mathbb R^d_+)\\ \mathbb E[Z]=w}} \left\{m\in\mathbb R^d:\langle m,w\rangle\ge \sigma_{\Lambda^{-1}(\mathcal A)}(Z)-\mathbb E[\langle X,Z\rangle]\right\}. \]
If \(\Lambda^{-1}(\mathcal A)\) is a cone, its support function is \(0\) on the positive dual cone and \(-\infty\) otherwise. This is a representation of the acceptable capital injections. The paper compares it with earlier systemic-risk dual representations, including Ararat and Rudloff; I am not claiming that the dual formula itself is new. Proposition 5.11 is a formulation anchor rather than a complexity theorem. The paper does use it in Example 5.12 to obtain an explicit representation for weighted aggregated losses.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation of regional insurers with standardized portfolios and stress-test scenarios. There may be hundreds of thousands of institutions but only a few dozen or hundred distinct exposure profiles. A type comprises an institution’s complete loss vector across the scenarios, its systemic-risk weight, and its cost of receiving capital. Mass is the fraction of institutions of that type. The stress scenarios and their probabilities stay as in the paper; the population of institutions is what becomes continuous.

Here is a precise mirror of the weighted-aggregate case in Example 5.12. Call it Continuous Systemic Capital Allocation. An instance gives finitely many stress states \(s\), with rational probabilities \(p_s\), and a finite type support \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. For each type \(t\) and state \(s\), it gives a rational loss \(x_{ts}\), a rational weight \(\alpha_t>1\), and a positive rational unit cost \(c_t\). Choose a nonnegative per-institution capital injection \(m_t\) for each type to minimize \(\sum_t\mu_t c_t m_t\), subject to
\[ \sum_s p_s\sum_t\mu_t\,\phi_{\alpha_t}(x_{ts}+m_t)\ge 0, \qquad \phi_\alpha(y)=\max\{y,0\}+\alpha\min\{y,0\}. \]
A solution is an optimal type-level injection schedule and its cost. The feasible schedules are the continuum counterpart of the paper’s \(R(X)\), restricted to nonnegative injections.

This stays close to the underlying problem: the decision is still how much capital to inject so the financial system meets its acceptance condition. The paper’s finite sum over entities becomes an integral over institution types, just as \(\sum_i\phi_{\alpha_i}(x_i)\) becomes \(\sum_t\mu_t\phi_{\alpha_t}(x_t)\). This does not continuize the outcome space or the uncertainty. It continuizes the population being aggregated.

The finite model also has a useful high-multiplicity dictionary. If \(N\) institutions have identical type data in groups of sizes \(n_t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Allowing different injections within a type does not improve this model: because \(\phi_{\alpha_t}\) is concave, replacing those injections by their within-type average weakly improves aggregate acceptability in every state, while preserving linear cost. Thus the per-type decision is not imposing an arbitrary restriction on an optimum.

For this separable weighted-loss case, I expect Class A. Since \(\phi_\alpha(y)=\min\{y,\alpha y\}\), the constraint has an exact linear programming formulation. Add variables \(z_{ts}\), impose \(z_{ts}\le x_{ts}+m_t\) and \(z_{ts}\le\alpha_t(x_{ts}+m_t)\), and require \(\sum_{s,t}p_s\mu_tz_{ts}\ge0\). With \(m_t\ge0\), minimizing the stated cost is an LP with size polynomial in the number of types, scenarios, and input bit length. This algorithmic conclusion belongs to the proposed mirror; Proposition 5.11 itself does not establish a running-time bound.

The scope is only the systemic-risk capital-injection problem, especially the weighted-aggregated-loss example. I would not use this anchor to claim a mirror of the paper’s full multi-utility theory, its multi-currency applications, or dynamic trading. Further questions include whether the general systemic aggregation maps in Proposition 5.11 admit similarly efficient formulations over a type continuum, and whether richer intervention rules introduce genuine integrality or pricing problems.

The weakest point is substantial: this paper’s stated contribution is mathematical representation, and its systemic-risk model concerns a vector of financial entities, not a conventional voting or social-choice problem. The continuum extension is natural because those coordinates are precisely the entities being aggregated, but it remains an extension the authors would have to accept. The positive case is strongest as a computational mirror of one systemic-risk application, not as evidence that the paper is already doing continuous computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 5.11 is not a computational result. It gives a dual representation of \(R(X)\); the paper does not define a finite input encoding, an algorithmic task, or a complexity bound. Its dual variables range over \(L^1\), and its assumptions do not provide a finite description from which to compute the represented set. So the proposed allocation problem is not a continuous mirror of a complexity theorem in the paper.

There is a real change of model in the weighted-loss proposal. In the paper, \(d\) is the number of entities in one system, \(m\in\mathbb R^d\) is a common portfolio of eligible capital, and \(R(X)\) is the set of all portfolios that make that system acceptable. The proposal instead indexes interventions by institution type, adds a type-dependent cost objective, and assumes finitely many stress states with rational data. Those are reasonable choices for a new optimization problem, but the LP’s finite encoding and objective do not come from Proposition 5.11. It is a computational extension of the paper’s application, rather than a result the paper already poses computationally.

That objection does not dispose of the strongest version of the mirror. One could omit the added costs and ask for the full feasible set of type-indexed capital schedules, preserving the set-valued nature of \(R\). With finite type support and finitely many stress states, that is a well-posed high-multiplicity model; grouping identical institutions and averaging their injections is justified by concavity. I would not reject it simply because the resulting formulation is an LP. The paper’s conclusion also explicitly names capital allocation as a direction for future research, which makes it plausible that its authors would recognize this question.

So the negative case is strongest against calling Proposition 5.11 itself a computational result: it is not one. But that does not establish the requested universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The systemic capital-allocation extension is close enough to the paper’s application, and its population interpretation is coherent enough, that I cannot honestly rule it out. The case against this anchor is therefore limited: it distinguishes a new computational project from a mirror of a computational theorem, but does not show that the new project is unworthy.
