What the mirror covers

The proposed model extends anonymous-congestion competitive assignment to type masses; it does not provide computational continuations of the top-guarantee, weighted-congestion, or finite-lottery results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of computing a competitive mass assignment for rational piecewise-linear utility inputs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous-population version of the paper’s anonymous-congestion competitive assignment. Think of a large school district with many pupils but a relatively small number of school-and-crowding preference types. A type \(t\) records a pupil’s full utility function \(u_t(a,q)\) for school \(a\) at crowding level \(q\). Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), seek assignment masses \(y_{ta}\) and school loads \(q_a\) such that
\[ \sum_a y_{ta}=\mu_t,\qquad q_a=\sum_t y_{ta},\qquad y_{ta}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ u_t(a,q_a)=\max_x u_t(x,q_x). \]
The solution is a mass assignment in which every type is placed only at a school it most prefers at the resulting crowding. This preserves the paper’s underlying question—fair assignment under congestion—while making the population itself the mass distribution. It does not replace the problem with a welfare objective the authors did not pose.

The closest supporting result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that a lottery implementing the competitive fractional congestion can be supported on deterministic assignments that are each top-\(n\)-guaranteed up to one unit of congestion, give each agent utilities within \(2\delta_i\) across assignments, are \(2\delta_i\)-competitive and \((2\delta_i+\varepsilon)\)-efficient, with stronger utility guarantees when every post has positive fractional congestion. Under per-capita normalization and regular utilities, the one-person rounding error tends to zero as the population grows. That makes the mass assignment a credible limit of the authors’ own competitive model. This is support for the mirror’s fidelity, not a computational-complexity anchor.

On the anchor requirement, the paper provides no qualifying named computational result. Propositions 1 and 2 prove that top-guaranteed assignments exist, with constructive proofs, but state no running-time classification. Propositions 3 and 4 and Lemmas 1–4 establish structural, existence, uniqueness, and implementation properties; Theorem 1 gives approximation guarantees. None states that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper does mention such results in §1.2(3), attributing to Chen et al. [10] a polynomial-time test for existence of a deterministic competitive assignment and NP-completeness for existence of an envy-free assignment meeting the top guarantees. Those are unnumbered claims about prior work, not numbered results of this paper, so they cannot serve as the required anchors.

Thus the continuous problem above is a plausible new complexity question: determine the exact computational complexity of finding a competitive mass assignment from \((\mu_t,u_t)\), measured in \(m,\tau\), and input bit length. The paper does not justify predicting Class A, B, or C for it. Its weakest point is also decisive for this task: its most direct fractional bridge is already developed for finite populations, while the paper supplies no qualifying complexity result for the proposed continuum problem to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive under ChoCo’s stated test: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 establish existence of top-guaranteed assignments; Propositions 3 and 4 and Lemmas 1–4 establish structural properties; Theorem 1 gives approximation guarantees. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The polynomial-time and NP-completeness claims in §1.2(3) are attributed to Chen et al., not proved as results of this paper.

Theorem 1 does support the proposed mass model as a natural extension, but it is not a computational anchor. It concerns lotteries over assignments of a finite, individually indexed population. With per-capita loads and uniformly regular utilities, its one-agent rounding error can shrink as the population grows; that gives a plausible limit. It does not establish the complexity of computing that limit. In fact, for rational type masses, aggregating identical agents in the paper’s randomized model already gives the proposed mass equations. This makes the model faithful, but does not supply a complexity result from the paper to continue.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror would specify a finite set of complete utility types, rational masses, and an encoding such as rational piecewise-linear utilities, then ask for the complexity of finding a competitive mass assignment. That is a coherent computational question. But it is a new equilibrium-computation problem attached to the paper’s characterization, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper contains. The paper’s main contribution is to identify and justify fair assignments, not to study an algorithmic task whose continuous complexity landscape can be carried forward.

So the negative case is strong as a reason not to treat this paper as a ChoCo mirror candidate: it fails the programme’s computational-result gate. It is weak as the broader claim that the mass-assignment problem could not be worthwhile in its own right. The type-mass model is sensible, and a researcher could choose to study its computation; this paper just does not provide the computational anchor that would make that work a mirror of its results.
