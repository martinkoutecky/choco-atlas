The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its sole theorem, attributed to Genest and Schervish, characterizes when Bayesian conditioning reproduces the two-person linear pool \(wq+(1-w)r\). It gives no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, hardness result, or approximation question. The paper’s weight–truth-conduciveness relation is another model-dependent identity, derived under assumptions about one peer’s opinions; its elicitation proposal asks for judgments, not for a computation. The Condorcet jury theorem appears as background, not as a computational result of this paper.

The best population-level proposal would assign mass to complete epistemic types—beliefs, pooling weights, likelihood assumptions, and perhaps interaction context—and ask how the society’s pooled opinion changes. That is imaginable, but the paper supplies no collective pooling process to study. For a fixed one-shot pool, aggregation is just a weighted sum or integral. To pose a harder task, one must add an objective such as choosing weights, changing a mass of opinions, or modeling repeated interactions. Those additions supply the computational problem; they do not mirror a result in this article. The paper itself notes that extending the Bayesian account to multiple peers raises order and dependence choices, so there is no canonical crowd version waiting to be continuized.

This does not show that no useful continuous study of opinion pooling could exist. A population of repeated or similar deliberators is plausible. But that would be a new project in social epistemology or opinion dynamics, not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper: its densities describe one agent’s uncertainty about a peer, not a continuous society. The negative case is strong as a paper-screening verdict; the broader claim that no scenario could ever make such a study worthwhile cannot honestly be established from this text alone.
