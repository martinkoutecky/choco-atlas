The case FOR (proponent)

The closest plausible mirror is exact coalitional manipulation for Baldwin on a continuous society. But the paper has no named worst-case complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. It says in §1.3 that it does not address manipulation’s algorithmic complexity. The paper’s NP-hardness statements are citations to earlier work; its own numbered results concern manipulation conditions and asymptotic rates. I can make a concrete case for the mirror, but I can’t honestly call it an anchored positive case under your rules.

The candidate problem is: given candidates \(C\), a support \(S\) of complete rankings with rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to \(1\), a target \(d\), and the paper’s fixed tie-breaking rule, let \(c\) be Baldwin’s winner under \(\mu\). Decide whether there is a reported distribution \(\nu\), also of total mass \(1\), such that Baldwin elects \(d\) and every ranking \(r\) whose mass decreases (\(\nu_r<\mu_r\)) ranks \(d\) above \(c\). A solution is such a distribution \(\nu\); the objective is feasibility, with no bribery cost. This follows the paper’s own definition of continuous coalitional manipulability.

A plausible regime is a large election with many more voters than distinct rankings: for instance, a population of hundreds of thousands or millions choosing among a small number of candidates. Each type is a complete ranking, and its mass is the population share holding that ranking; typically \(\tau\ll n\), with \(\tau\le m!\). That is a population continuization, not an outcome-space or time continuization.

The nearest supporting result is Theorem 3.15, proved in this paper. Its Baldwin entry says that under Perturbed Culture, for \(m\ge3\), the critical concentration is \(\theta_c=(m-2)/(4m-5)\): as \(n\to\infty\), the coalitional-manipulation rate tends to \(1\) for \(\theta<\theta_c\) and to \(0\) for \(\theta>\theta_c\). The theorem leaves the equality case unstated. This gives a useful bridge: the proof analyzes the continuous expected profile and lifts its behavior to large finite electorates. But it is a phase-transition result, not a complexity classification, and the paper already resolves that particular one-parameter family.

For the arbitrary-distribution decision problem, I would expect parameterized tractability in \(m\): one can enumerate Baldwin elimination orders and the rankings whose reported mass decreases, then test the resulting linear feasibility conditions. That gives a concrete route to an \(f(m)\operatorname{poly}(L)\) algorithm; whether the dependence on \(m\) can be made polynomial is the sharper question. The weakest point is also the main qualification: the paper already defines continuous profiles and continuous manipulation, so the mirror’s novelty is specifically the worst-case algorithmic study on arbitrary rational profiles. Without a qualifying numbered complexity anchor, I would present this as a promising but unanchored mirror, not as a case that satisfies the requested anchor test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed Baldwin problem has no eligible anchor. Theorem 3.15 gives an asymptotic manipulation-rate threshold under Perturbed Culture, \(\theta_c=(m-2)/(4m-5)\). It says nothing about worst-case complexity on arbitrary rational societies; the paper explicitly sets manipulation’s algorithmic complexity aside. The proposed decision problem is therefore a new computational question, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

There is also substantial overlap in the model: the paper defines continuous profiles and continuous coalitional manipulation, then studies manipulation on neighborhoods of the expected continuous profile. Its result covers a particular probabilistic family, not arbitrary supported distributions. Extending that analysis to arbitrary societies could be worthwhile, but it would fill a gap in the paper rather than mirror one of its complexity results.

The population model itself is hard to fault. Large elections with repeated rankings provide a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s own mass-based definition fits it. So the honest negative case is narrow: under the requested anchor test, this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. I cannot make a convincing universal case that the Baldwin problem, or a broader study of exact manipulation on continuous societies, is not worth doing.
