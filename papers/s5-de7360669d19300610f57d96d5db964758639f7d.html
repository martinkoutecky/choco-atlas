What the mirror covers

The proposed case centers on Theorem 1's individual menu-preference representation; the other named results discussed characterize preferences, choice behavior, or comparisons between preferences, rather than computational tasks.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive opening is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: it represents an individual’s value for a menu \(A\) as
\[ U(A)=\max_{x\in A}\bigl(u(x)+v(x)\bigr)-\max_{y\in A}v(y), \]
where \(u\) and \(v\) are the commitment and temptation rankings. One could imagine a large platform cohort in which millions of customers share a small number \(\tau\) of complete preference types \((u,v)\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of customers of each type. The theorem would then supply a per-type rule for evaluating a common menu, giving a natural ingredient for a population-level menu-design problem.

That would be a new problem, though. The paper does not introduce a planner, a population distribution over preference types, an intervention over menus, or a computational objective. Adding those would be doing substantial modeling work beyond the paper.

There are no qualifying computational anchors in the paper. Its numbered theorems characterize preferences, choice behavior, and comparative measures: for example, Theorems 1 and 3 give representations, Theorem 7 characterizes second-period choice, and Theorems 8 and 9 compare preference for commitment and self-control. The maximizations in these representations are parts of utility formulas, not computational results or algorithmic formulations the paper solves. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem or tractability prediction to give.

The weak point is decisive: the paper’s continuous objects are an individual’s menus and lotteries, while ChoCo’s target is a continuous population. A population menu-design model is a plausible extension, but the paper itself provides no computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s best anchor, and the cohort example makes a continuous population plausible: many customers could share each of \(\tau\) preference types \((u,v)\), with mass \(\mu_t\). But Theorem 1 supplies a representation of one person’s menu preferences. Its maxima are part of a utility formula; the theorem neither poses nor solves a computational problem over menus or populations.

The strongest proposed extension is for a platform to choose a common menu \(A\) to maximize \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t U_t(A)\). That could be a worthwhile project, but it adds a planner, feasible-menu constraints, and a social welfare objective. The last addition is substantive: each person’s \(U_t\) is defined only up to a positive affine transformation, and the paper supplies no interpersonal normalization for summing those utilities. Choosing one would shape the new problem. The authors’ policy discussion in Section 6 may motivate such a model, but it does not make it a computational question from this paper.

The same gap applies to Theorems 3 and 7, which characterize preferences and individual choice, and Theorems 8 and 9, which compare individual preferences. A population distribution can turn their properties into aggregate statistics, such as the share of people who benefit from commitment. Optimizing such a statistic would again require a newly specified collective objective. High multiplicity can be sensible here; it does not provide the missing computational task. The negative case is therefore strongest as a scope judgment: the paper offers rich inputs for future population-level work, but no computational result whose continuous mirror ChoCo could chart.
