The continuous mirror question

Let advertisers be sampled independently from a fixed finite set of complete types \(K\), with type \(k\) having valuation \(v_k(\theta)\), budget \(b_k\), and population mass \(\mu_k\). Scale item supply per capita, and let \(\hat\mu_N\) be the empirical type distribution of \(N\) advertisers. Under analogues of the paper's smoothness and strict-complementarity assumptions, does an adaptive bootstrap that resamples advertiser types and recomputes FPPE consistently estimate the limiting law of \(\sqrt{N}(\beta(\hat\mu_N)-\beta(\mu))\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete advertiser types. Each type has a shared valuation function and per-advertiser budget; its pacing multiplier is \(\beta_k\), and its per-member allocation is \(x_k(\theta)\). Prices are \(p(\theta)=\max_k \beta_k v_k(\theta)\), feasibility is weighted by type mass, and per-capita item supply scales with advertiser mass. The estimator resamples \(N\) type labels, forms bootstrap masses, and recomputes the type-level FPPE and adaptive bootstrap quadratic program.

The objection that survived

The paper samples items, while the proposed mirror needs a new sampling law for advertiser types and a per-capita supply normalization; the case does not specify either.

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Theorem 4's adaptive-bootstrap consistency result to advertiser-type masses. It leaves the paper's item-sampling results for LFM and FPPE, its other bootstrap procedures, and its confidence-region results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove or refute conditional bootstrap consistency under advertiser-type sampling, including the boundary cases for unpaced and degenerate types.
  • Determine the required supply normalization and the computational cost as a function of the number of supported types.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is to replace the paper’s finite set of advertisers with a large population drawn from a small set of complete advertiser types. A type \(k\) has the same valuation function \(v_k(\theta)\) and per-advertiser budget \(b_k\) for every member; its mass \(\mu_k\) is the fraction of advertisers of that type. This fits, for example, a platform with many small advertisers using a few standardized campaign templates and budget tiers, where the number of advertisers is much larger than the number of distinct templates.

For item context \(\theta\), let \(\beta_k\) be type \(k\)’s pacing multiplier and \(x_k(\theta)\) the allocation per member of that type. Prices are \(p(\theta)=\max_k\beta_kv_k(\theta)\); feasibility is \(\sum_k\mu_kx_k(\theta)\leq1\); and each type’s per-advertiser spend is \(\int x_k(\theta)p(\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)\leq b_k\). An allocation can go to type \(k\) only when its bid is highest, and a type that spends less than its budget is unpaced. The decision is the type-level pacing and allocation equilibrium. Fractional type-level allocation is an aggregate description; individual advertisers can still receive integral allocations.

The closest result in the paper is Theorem 4, proved there. For finite-buyer FPPE with no degenerate buyers, if the bootstrap and classification thresholds satisfy \(\delta_t=o(1)\), \(\sqrt{t}\delta_t\to\infty\), \(\sqrt{t}\delta_w\to c\in(0,\infty]\), and the estimated Hessian converges in probability to the population Hessian, then the adaptive bootstrap estimator satisfies \(\delta_t^{-1}(\beta^{\mathrm{boot}}-\beta^\gamma)\rightsquigarrow\mathcal J_{\mathrm{FPPE}}\) conditionally in probability. The proposed mirror question would ask whether the analogous type-aggregated adaptive bootstrap consistently estimates the sampling distribution of the continuum-advertiser FPPE pacing vector. With fixed finite type support, I would expect the equilibrium and bootstrap computations to remain tractable through finite-dimensional convex and quadratic programs; extending the theorem to that model would still need proof.

There is a strict limitation on the anchor, though: Theorem 4 is a statistical consistency result, not a named computational-complexity result. Nor does another numbered theorem in this paper claim that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a comparable class. Theorems 1–8 concern bootstrap validity or failure, asymptotics, and Hessian estimation. So this is a plausible continuous-population extension of one of the paper’s results, but it is not an eligible anchor under the requested standard.

That is also the weakest point in the positive case. The paper’s growing population is the set of sampled items; its advertisers are finite, and its own experiments use small advertiser counts. The mirror reverses which side of the market is continuous. Standardized advertiser cohorts make that reversal intelligible, but typical advertisers may differ in budgets or valuations enough to create many distinct types. The proposed mirror therefore covers an extension of Theorem 4 under a specific high-multiplicity advertiser regime; it does not establish a computational-complexity mirror for the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 fails ChoCo’s central gate: it proves statistical bootstrap consistency, not the complexity of a computational problem. Its sample size \(t\) counts sampled items. The theorem estimates the sampling distribution of the FPPE pacing vector as those item samples grow, with a fixed set of buyers. The other plausible anchors in the paper—such as Theorem 3’s bootstrap validity and Theorem 6’s confidence region—are also inferential results. None classifies an algorithmic problem by running time, hardness, or parameterized complexity.

The proposed advertiser-type model is coherent. Grouping advertisers with identical valuations and budgets is legitimate high multiplicity, and fractional type-level allocations can describe aggregate outcomes. But it changes the sampling question: to mirror Theorem 4, one would need to specify a new experiment in which advertiser-type masses vary or are sampled, alongside the paper’s item sampling. With fixed finite type support, the resulting equilibrium still has one pacing variable per type and an EG convex-program formulation. A bootstrap theorem for that model could be useful statistics, but it would not supply the missing complexity question. If the number of types grows enough to make computation difficult, the compact high-multiplicity representation that motivated the mirror has largely disappeared.

The paper already allows a continuous supply of items. That does not make its advertiser population continuous, and I would not count it as a prior-art collision. It does, however, sharpen the proposed extension: the mirror reverses which side of the market is growing and requires a new sampling regime. A further question about the complexity of that extension could be worth asking, but it would be a new computational project, not a complexity mirror of Theorem 4.

So the negative case is strong on ChoCo fit and weak on the literal claim that no scenario could be worthwhile. I can’t honestly dismiss the standardized-advertiser extension as meaningless; it may be a worthwhile statistical study. The case against is that this paper provides no named computational-complexity result for ChoCo to continuize.
