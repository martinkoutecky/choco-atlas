The case FOR (proponent)

The main limitation is formal: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Its own contribution is experimental. Table 1 summarizes complexity classifications from earlier work, but it is not a numbered result, so it cannot serve as a qualifying anchor under your rule.

The closest foothold is Table 1’s Bucklin constructive control by adding candidates entry, marked \(R\). The paper defines that problem as follows: given a Bucklin election \((C\cup D,V)\), registered candidates \(C\), spoiler candidates \(D\), a target \(c^\star\in C\), and a limit \(k\), decide whether some \(D'\subseteq D\) with \(|D'|\le k\) makes \(c^\star\) the unique Bucklin winner of \((C\cup D',V)\). Here \(R\) means resistant, or NP-hard. The paper presents this as known from the control-complexity literature it cites; it does not prove the hardness result.

A natural mirror of that problem is continuous Bucklin control by adding candidates. An instance consists of \(C,D,c^\star,k\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings over \(C\cup D\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Write \(\tau=|T|\), and let \(L\) be the input bit length. The question is whether there is a set \(D'\subseteq D\), \(|D'|\le k\), such that \(c^\star\) is the unique Bucklin winner under the society’s mass distribution, with each ranking restricted to \(C\cup D'\). A solution is the set \(D'\); the decision objective is exactly the paper’s yes-or-no control question.

The types are full ballots, and mass is the fraction of the electorate casting each ballot. A plausible regime is a large national, city-wide, or membership election with a modest candidate set and many repeated preference orders, so the number of voters greatly exceeds \(\tau\). The chair still makes the paper’s candidate-addition decision. This seems recognizably the same control problem to the authors: the population representation changes, while the rule, target, candidate choices, and success condition remain intact.

This mirror has a particularly clean hardness argument. Given a finite election with \(n_t\) voters of each ranking \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Every Bucklin level score then equals the corresponding discrete score divided by \(n\), and reaching a strict majority is equivalent to having mass greater than \(1/2\). Thus, for every possible added-candidate set \(D'\), the discrete and weighted elections have the same winners. The continuous decision instance has the same answer as the discrete one, so the Table 1 hardness transfers. The continuous problem is also in NP: a candidate subset is a certificate, and its weighted Bucklin winner can be computed with exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time. If this imported Table 1 result is accepted as the foothold, I would expect this mirror to be NP-complete and fall in Class B.

This case covers Bucklin constructive control by adding candidates, including the paper’s experiments on that scenario; it does not try to mirror the paper’s full control table. The paper’s IC and Two Mainstreams experiments generate finite elections and measure how its search algorithms perform. Their results about frequent practical success do not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for the continuous problem.

My weakest point is also decisive for the requested format: there is no qualifying numbered anchor here. The continuous question is precise and its connection to the table’s cited hardness result is strong, but it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror anchored on a numbered result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The one anchor is Bucklin constructive control by adding candidates, marked \(R\) in Table 1. But that table summarizes results established in earlier work; this paper does not prove them. Its own results are experimental measurements on sampled elections. Under the stated anchor rule, the proposed mirror is therefore not anchored on a named computational result of this paper.

The experimental findings do not supply a replacement anchor. The reported yes rates and runtimes concern particular random generators, finite electorates and candidate sets, and a search procedure that sometimes times out. Turning those findings into asymptotic probabilities would continuize the sampling or uncertainty model. Asking for exact control on a society of ballot masses would instead be a computational question imported from the background control literature, rather than a mirror of a complexity result this paper establishes.

That is the strongest case against treating this paper as a source for the programme. It does not, however, support the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed Bucklin mirror is well formed: ballot rankings are complete types, rational mass represents repeated ballots, and Bucklin scores and the majority threshold can be computed exactly after restricting rankings to the selected candidates. A large election with many repeated ballot types is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Moreover, a discrete election maps to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), preserving the winner for every candidate-addition choice. The resulting question may be Class B, but hardness transferring from the discrete case is not an objection under the programme’s rules.

So my negative case is strong only on eligibility: this paper has no qualifying named computational result of its own. If Table 1’s recap of prior work is allowed as an anchor, the proponent’s mirror survives, and I cannot honestly argue that it is worthless.
