The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow population-limit mirror of the paper’s strategic persuasion result.

The anchor is Theorem 5.5, proved in this paper. There is no named NP-hardness, FPT, or other complexity-class result here. Theorem 5.5 instead gives an exact solution to the sender’s optimization problem, which yields a polynomial-bit algorithm for the optimum and a succinct description of an optimal strategy. For \(k\le n-1\), among communication strategies under which sincere voting is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium, it is optimal to send recommendation \(x\) to everyone in state \(X\). In state \(Y\), send \(x\) to exactly \(k+1\) receivers with probability \(r^*_{k+1}\), and to nobody otherwise. The probability is \(1\) if \(\lambda_0(X)\ge (k+1)/(n+k+1)\); otherwise it is \(\frac{\lambda_0(X)}{\lambda_0(Y)}\frac{n}{k+1}\). The resulting maximum probability of implementing \(x\) is \(\min\{\frac{n+k+1}{k+1}\lambda_0(X),1\}\). This is a constructive optimization result, not a complexity classification stated by the authors.

A fitting regime is a very large referendum on a proposal where every receiver has the same prior and the same preferences: each wants the outcome to match the true state, while the sender always prefers \(x\). Think of \(n\) voters sharing those relevant characteristics, with a quota \(k=\lceil pn\rceil\) for fixed \(p\in(0,1)\). Here a type is the voter’s complete relevant description—prior and utilities—and there is one type with mass \(1\). The regime has \(n\) agents and \(\tau=1\) distinct types. Their private recommendations can still be correlated, as in the paper.

Call the mirror Atomless Quota Persuasion with Credible Sincere Recommendations. Its input is a rational prior \(\lambda=\Pr(X)\in(0,1)\) and a rational quota share \(p\in(0,1)\). The society is unit mass of identical receivers. A strategy sends recommendation \(x\) to everyone in state \(X\). In state \(Y\), it draws a random share \(Q\in[0,1]\), then privately recommends \(x\) to a uniformly chosen measurable subset of that mass and \(y\) to the rest. For example, a random rotation of an interval of length \(Q\) on the unit circle gives each receiver the same marginal chance of receiving \(x\).

The strategy must make sincere recommendations credible: after receiving \(x\), a receiver’s posterior probability of \(X\) must be at least \(1/2\). Since \(x\) is certain in state \(X\), while a randomly selected receiver gets it in state \(Y\) with probability \(\mathbb E[Q]\), this condition is \(\lambda\ge(1-\lambda)\mathbb E[Q]\). A \(y\) recommendation only occurs in state \(Y\), so it recommends the sincere action there. The sender wants to maximize the probability that at least share \(p\) receives \(x\). Thus the problem is to choose a probability law \(\rho\) for \(Q\), subject to \(\lambda\ge(1-\lambda)\mathbb E_\rho[Q]\), to maximize \(\lambda+(1-\lambda)\rho([p,1])\). A solution is such a law and its measurable implementation.

This problem has an exact, simple answer. Since \(Q\ge p\) whenever the vote passes, \(p\rho([p,1])\le\mathbb E[Q]\le\lambda/(1-\lambda)\). The bound is attained by choosing \(Q=p\) with probability \(\min\{1,\lambda/[p(1-\lambda)]\}\), and \(Q=0\) otherwise. The optimum is \(\min\{(1+p)\lambda/p,1\}\), and that two-point law is an optimal strategy. This is a tractable, one-moment optimization problem.

The connection to Theorem 5.5 is direct: with \(k=\lceil pn\rceil\), its optimal state-\(Y\) strategy recommends \(x\) to a coalition of \(k+1\) receivers, so the coalition’s share tends to \(p\). Its threshold and value converge to the continuous problem’s threshold \(\lambda=p/(1+p)\) and optimum \(\min\{(1+p)\lambda/p,1\}\). The paper itself notes this large-population limit for quotas that are a fixed fraction of the electorate. That makes this a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem, rather than a merely adjacent continuous model. The proposed question covers Theorem 5.5’s quota-fraction population regime; it does not claim to mirror every result in the paper.

The weakest point is also central: in a genuinely atomless voting game, one receiver cannot change the outcome, so ordinary Bayes–Nash incentives no longer capture the finite model’s swing-voter concern. The mirror therefore states credibility as a receiver-level sincere-recommendation condition, inherited from the finite model’s posterior and equilibrium restrictions. That is a deliberate population-limit co-modification, not a claim that the finite pivotality argument survives unchanged. The paper’s own limiting result supports the match, but a referee could reasonably ask whether this credibility condition, rather than literal atomless BNE, is the right continuous formulation.

The natural follow-up questions are whether the same limit and optimal two-point strategy persist with several voter types, how finite-\(n\) equilibrium constraints bound the continuous optimum’s error, and which equilibrium or refinement concept best captures strategic voting once individual pivotality vanishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 5.5. Its large electorate, fixed quota-share limit makes the proposed mirror unusually natural: an atomless society can be represented by the random share \(Q\) receiving recommendation \(x\), and the theorem’s \(k+1\) coalition converges to a share \(p\). I would not object that the continuous optimum agrees with the limit; that agreement could itself be a useful result.

The difficulty is that the theorem’s constraint is strategic. In its finite voting game, a receiver’s incentive to follow a sincere recommendation depends on the probability of being pivotal, conditional on the state and message. In an atomless electorate, one receiver has zero mass and cannot change whether the quota is met. Individual Bayes–Nash incentives therefore become vacuous: any vote is a best response, and sincere voting can be an equilibrium even when recommendations convey no reason to vote sincerely.

The proposed condition \(\lambda\ge(1-\lambda)\mathbb E[Q]\) restores bite by requiring an \(x\) recommendation to be sincerely credible. But that is a posterior condition on recommendations, not the finite model’s strategic-equilibrium condition. It resembles the paper’s sincere-voting analysis; it does not preserve the pivotality constraints that make Theorem 5.5 a result about strategic voting. The mirror is coherent, but it changes the central question.

The strongest repair would retain finite, diminishing voter masses, or replace individual deviations with a specified coalition or approximate-equilibrium concept. The first keeps the finite game and its pivotality rather than defining an atomless equilibrium. The latter choices add an equilibrium notion the paper does not study, and different choices could produce different persuasion problems. Adding several voter types does not fix the issue: each individual in an atomless type group still has zero influence. It could create a richer persuasion model, but it would no longer be a mirror of this theorem’s homogeneous strategic voters.

There is a further scope concern, though not a decisive one: the paper gives an exact optimization result, not a named complexity classification. A computational question about the continuous strategy is possible, but the proponent’s one-type version reduces to a law over a single mass share. The case against it is therefore not that the question has an uninteresting answer; it is that its proposed “credible recommendation” constraint sidesteps the strategic incentive the paper set out to analyze.

This is not an airtight negative. The paper’s own quota-share limit is strong evidence that a population limit is a sensible object, and an author might recognize the posterior-credibility version as a worthwhile companion question. The best case against is narrower: that version does not continue the paper’s distinctive BNE constraint, and preserving that constraint requires adding structure beyond an atomless population.
