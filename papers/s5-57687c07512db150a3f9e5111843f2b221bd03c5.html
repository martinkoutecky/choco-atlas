The continuous mirror question

Given two countries \(i\in\{1,2\}\), each with a finite support of user types \(T_i\) and rational type masses \(\mu_{it}\ge 0\) summing to one within each country, specify each type’s utility, endowment, and relevant location or infrastructure access, along with the river’s resource and production constraints, disagreement utilities, and discount factors. Each proposal assigns per-capita bundles to every type and specifies feasible production or flow plans; country \(i\)’s utility is \(U_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_{it}u_{it}\). Compute the stationary equilibrium proposals by optimizing the asymmetric Nash product subject to the two proposals’ feasibility and continuation-acceptance constraints.

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution over water-user types within each country, with type masses \(\mu_{it}\), per-capita proposal variables \(x_{it}\) and \(y_{it}\), mass-weighted utilities \(U_i\), and the paper’s resource balances and production constraints. The objective is the asymmetric Nash product of the proposer utilities above disagreement, with the paper’s acceptance constraints; the output is the pair of stationary equilibrium proposals and, where defined, supporting water prices.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is the paper’s computational treatment of bilateral alternating offers, extended to a population with a finite number of user types. The continuous object is the population distribution: many water users share a relatively small set of preference, endowment, and location types, each with a rational mass. Divisible water is part of the application, but it is not the continuization being proposed.

The lead anchor is Proposition 1. It states that \((s_1^*,s_2^*,x^*,y^*)\) solves program (3) if and only if \((x^*,y^*)\) is a pair of stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium allocations solving programs (1)–(2). In those programs, each country proposes a feasible allocation that maximizes its own utility while meeting the other country’s continuation payoff after rejection. Program (3) replaces that fixed-point pair with one convex optimization problem: it maximizes an asymmetric Nash product subject to the two proposals’ feasibility and the parties’ acceptance constraints. The paper credits this result to Houba (2005b); it is cited and applied here, rather than established as a new result. The paper reports no named result in a standard complexity class such as P or NP-hard. Proposition 1 is the relevant anchor because the paper computes with the formulation it provides.

I would mirror it as the finite-type continuous-population alternating-offers problem. An instance has two river-basin authorities, \(i\in\{1,2\}\), each representing a large population of users. Its supported types \(T_i\) have masses \(\mu_{it}\), with \(\sum_{i,t}\mu_{it}=1\). A type specifies everything relevant to the model: utility, local endowment, location, and access to production or water infrastructure. Thus many users can share a type, while the number of users \(N\) is much larger than the support size \(\tau=\sum_i |T_i|\). Each proposal assigns a per-capita water and money bundle to each type, along with any feasible production or flow plan. The authority’s welfare is \(U_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_{it}u_{it}\); disagreement utility \(d_i\) comes from the specified status quo. The input also gives discount factors \(\delta_i\) and the river’s resource balances and production constraints.

The question is to compute the stationary equilibrium proposals, not merely to find a feasible water allocation. Let \(x\) be country 1’s proposal and \(y\) country 2’s, each feasible for all type allocations and production plans. With \(\alpha=\ln(\delta_2)/(\ln(\delta_1)+\ln(\delta_2))\), the mirror asks for a global optimizer of the following problem, where \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) denote the bundles assigned to country \(i\)’s types:

\(\max (s_1-d_1)^\alpha(s_2-d_2)^{1-\alpha}\), subject to \(s_1\le U_1(x_1)\), \(s_2\le U_2(y_2)\), \((1-\delta_1)d_1+\delta_1s_1\le U_1(y_1)\), \((1-\delta_2)d_2+\delta_2s_2\le U_2(x_2)\), and \(x,y\) satisfying the model’s resource and production constraints.

The solution is an optimal pair \(x,y\) and associated \(s_1,s_2\); the outputs can also include supporting water prices from the resource-balance constraints. The time structure remains the paper’s alternating-offers game. It is the type distribution and the allocation across those types that are continuous-population features. The paper itself points toward this extension when it says a more detailed model could distinguish water users by location and use.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, for an explicit rational polyhedral version of the model: piecewise-linear concave type utilities and rational convex production constraints yield a convex optimization problem, with \(\tau\), the number of utility facets, commodities, and input bit length governing its size. That is an expected tractability claim, especially for approximate solutions under standard encoding and boundedness conditions; Proposition 1 does not establish a polynomial-time complexity bound. This mirror covers Proposition 1’s equilibrium computation and its bilateral river-basin application. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s time model or to provide a hardness result.

The mirror also raises questions about exact versus approximate bit complexity, which parameters control the cost as \(\tau\) and the number of utility facets grow, and how closely a large finite population approximates the continuous-type equilibrium.

My weakest point is that the paper’s computational gain comes from replacing a two-country bargaining fixed point with one convex program. It does not come from relaxing population multiplicity. The type-disaggregated population is a plausible, author-signposted extension, but Proposition 1 does not itself analyze that input regime or prove its complexity. The case rests on the extension preserving the paper’s actual equilibrium question and convex structure, not on a claim that the paper has already studied high-multiplicity populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only anchor, and it is a real computational result: it replaces a fixed point in a two-country alternating-offers game with one convex program. The strongest case against a mirror is that its strategic object is already exactly two agents. Each country has a utility function, an endowment and a role as proposer or responder. Water users do not make offers, accept them, or receive individually tracked outcomes in this result.

The best proposed mirror would give each country a finite-type population, with mass \(\mu_{it}\), type-specific utilities and per-capita allocations. If the country remains the bargaining agent, its utility becomes \(U_i=\sum_t\mu_{it}u_{it}\), and the resource constraints account for those allocations. That is a plausible economic extension, and the paper itself points to models with differentiated water users. But it places the population inside the country’s welfare and allocation primitives; the bargaining game and Proposition 1’s two-proposal structure remain unchanged. The new type data can be folded into the aggregate feasible set and utility function used by the same program. So this is a population-disaggregated application of the result, rather than a continuous-population version of the computational problem the proposition studies.

Making the users themselves the strategic agents would make population central, but then Proposition 1 no longer supplies the game. The paper’s bilateral proposer-responder protocol does not specify how a population proposes or consents, whether consent is unanimous or weighted by mass, or how disagreement utilities are aggregated. Those choices define a different bargaining problem; a distribution over user types alone cannot settle them.

That is the negative case’s limit. The paper’s invitation to distinguish users makes a finite-type water-allocation extension credible, so I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. The objection is narrower: the only version that directly preserves Proposition 1 treats population as a microfoundation for two country-level agents; a version where population drives strategic computation needs a new game and new results.
