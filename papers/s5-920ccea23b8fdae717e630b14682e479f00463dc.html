The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest case is a promising mirror for the paper’s allocation problem, but it is not anchored in a qualifying named result. I find no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. The closest claim is in Section 3.5: a batch allocation reduces to maximum weighted bipartite matching, which can be solved by the Hungarian algorithm. Tables 2–4 report simulation results, not complexity classifications. Under your anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors to restate.

As a forward-looking candidate, I would put Finite-Horizon Mass Heart Allocation forward as the lead. Consider a large allocation network in which \(N\) patients and donors greatly outnumber a moderate set of modeled types. A patient type records everything the allocation model uses—such as blood type, location class, clinical status, and predicted survival; a donor type records the corresponding donor and compatibility features. Two agents share a type only when they have the same modeled compatibility, reward, and transition behavior. Mass is their fraction of the catchment population. Each person still receives either one heart or none; fractional mass represents the aggregate assignment of many similar people.

An instance has a finite horizon represented by a scenario tree; rational initial patient masses and donor supplies at its nodes; rational patient entry and waiting-state transition data; a compatibility relation \(K\); and a reward \(g_{n,d,p}\), such as the paper’s estimated graft survival minus waitlist survival. To match the paper’s experiments in Sections 3.4 and 3.5, take offers to be accepted. At each history node \(n\), choose \(x_{n,d,p}\), the mass of donor type \(d\) assigned to patient type \(p\). Require donor and patient mass limits,

\[ \sum_p x_{n,d,p}\le \lambda_n(d),\qquad \sum_d x_{n,d,p}\le \mu_n(p),\qquad x_{n,d,p}=0\ \text{if }K(d,p)=0. \]

Waiting mass at a child node \(n'\) follows the supplied transition model, for example

\[ \mu_{n'}(p')=b_{n'}(p')+ \sum_p Q_n(p,p') \left(\mu_n(p)-\sum_d x_{n,d,p}\right). \]

The task is to return a feasible allocation at every history node maximizing expected cumulative life-years gained, \(\sum_n q_n\sum_{d,p}g_{n,d,p}x_{n,d,p}\), where \(q_n\) is the node’s probability. This preserves the paper’s dynamic composition of the waitlist and life-years objective while expressing assignments as flows over a continuous population. The paper’s authors could plausibly recognize it as a policy-optimization question adjacent to their own; the potentials become one way of approximating the future value captured by the full-horizon objective.

With an explicitly listed scenario tree and linear transitions, this version is an LP, so I would expect a Class A result, polynomial in the tree and type description. Follow-up questions include whether a compactly encoded stochastic simulator changes the complexity, and how well the flow solution approximates discrete allocations as population size grows.

The regime would need to be a large, repeated allocation system whose patients and donors fall into a manageable set of modeled clinical and logistical types. That is plausible as an extension, but the paper does not establish it: its prediction models use many covariates, and exact types under those models could approach one per person.

That is the soft spot. The paper studies online policy design with simulated trajectories and learned predictions; the proposed LP presumes an explicit finite type and transition model. It may be a useful continuous formulation of the allocation task, but it does not follow from a named computational result in this paper, and its type compression would need justification before it could count as a strong mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest paper-specific objection is that there is no qualifying named computational result to mirror. The paper reports learned predictors and simulation comparisons; it gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its closest algorithmic claim is that allocating a finite batch reduces to maximum-weight bipartite matching, solvable by the Hungarian algorithm. That is a useful modeling step, but it is not the kind of complexity result the programme was asked to mirror.

The proposed finite-horizon LP also goes beyond the paper’s results. It assumes an explicit scenario tree, rational type-level transitions and rewards, and a finite type system that captures everything relevant to matching and patient evolution. Polynomial time in the size of a fully listed tree says little about a compactly specified stochastic process, whose tree may be exponentially larger. For an exact representation of this paper’s predictors, a type would need to capture many donor and patient covariates, center behavior, and evolving clinical state. Those features can make the number of distinct types approach the number of individuals. Grouping patients into a manageable set of types is possible, but then the transition and reward model is an approximation that the paper itself does not supply.

That is the best negative case, but it does not defeat the stronger mirror. A repeated large allocation system with patients grouped by clinically meaningful, behaviorally complete types is plausible, and the paper’s own use of medical tiers and blood types gives some basis for it. With such a model, mass allocation could be a worthwhile problem in its own right. The proponent’s scenario-tree formulation is therefore not a mirror of a named result in this paper, but I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is strong on the absence of a paper-anchored complexity result and weak at the universal claim.
