The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are analytical equilibrium claims, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified.

The closest is Proposition 3, proved in the paper: under the selected voting strategies of Proposition 2, with a symmetric distribution of voter ideal points and odd quota \(s\), electoral equilibrium requires the middle party to locate at the median, \(p_M^*=x_\mu\), and the outer parties to be equally distant from it, with that distance in the stated interval \([\frac{8}{3}(x_{i^*}-x_\mu),\,4(x_{j^*}-x_\mu))\), where \(i^*=\mu+(s-1)/2\) and \(j^*=\mu+\lceil(n-1)/4\rceil\). Proposition 1, also proved here, characterizes legislative bargaining for fixed platforms and vote shares; Proposition 2 gives a selected voting equilibrium. None classifies the complexity of computing these outcomes.

A continuous-population version is conceivable: voter types could be ideal points \(x\), with mass \(\mu_x\), and one could ask for equilibrium platforms and the resulting legislative outcome. But the paper’s voting analysis relies on individual voters being pivotal. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot cannot change party shares, so that equilibrium argument does not carry over directly. Restoring pivotality by letting blocks of mass choose together would change the strategic model. That makes the mirror a possible new research question, but not a strong, anchored computational case based on this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to continuize. Propositions 1–3 characterize a legislative outcome, a selected voting equilibrium, and electoral equilibria. None defines a computational problem or classifies its complexity. That leaves the proposed mirror without a computational anchor in the sense ChoCo is meant to study.

Proposition 1 is the weakest candidate: it takes the parties’ vote shares as given, so the voter population has already dropped out. Its input is three weights, and the paper’s legislative calculation is not a population computation. Propositions 2 and 3 do involve voters, but their equilibrium construction depends on individual ballots changing the ranking of party vote shares. In an atomless society, one voter has measure zero and cannot change those shares. The paper’s selected criterion—that each voter is pivotal—has no direct counterpart there; under ordinary unilateral-deviation Nash equilibrium, a voter’s ballot has no effect on their payoff.

The strongest repair would be to give voter types positive-mass, coordinated deviations, perhaps with a fractional quota, and ask for equilibrium platforms or the least mass needed to change the legislative outcome. That could be a coherent computational project. But it gives a mass of like-minded voters the strategic role of a bloc, whereas this paper builds its result around individually strategic voters. Alternatively, retain individual ballots and high-multiplicity counts: that preserves the game, but it keeps discrete pivotal voters rather than making the population continuous. Neither repair follows from the paper’s results as a continuous mirror.

Proposition 3 can also be translated into a limit involving population quantiles. That would be a plausible analytic question about how its equilibrium conditions scale. It would not supply the missing computational question: the paper provides no computational task whose continuous version that limit would classify.

So the negative case is strong for a *paper-anchored ChoCo mirror*: every named proposition either omits the population or relies on individual pivotality that vanishes in the natural atomless model. It is not proof that no worthwhile project can be built from the paper. A mass-bloc or mass-control model is the weak point in this case; it may be worthwhile, but it would need to be established as the authors’ problem rather than assumed to be their mirror.
