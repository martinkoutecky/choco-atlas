What the mirror covers

The cited theorems concern necessity and sufficiency conditions for repeated implementability; none supplies a computational result for a mirror to cover.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is that the paper studies society-level outcomes for agents whose preferences change over repeated interactions. A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be many recurring task-assignment teams, grouped by complete utility type, with mass representing each type’s share. That gives a sensible population-continuum research direction.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a mirror to. Theorem 1 proves that repeated implementability implies dynamic monotonicity; Theorem 2 proves sufficiency with at least three agents and no-veto power; Theorem 3 gives a two-agent sufficiency result under additional assumptions. These are game-theoretic existence and characterization results, not complexity classifications or algorithmic results. The constructive mechanism in Theorem 2 does not establish an efficient procedure for deciding implementability.

There is also a model-fit problem: the paper’s results rely on named agents’ unilateral deviations. In an atomless population, one agent’s deviation may leave aggregate reports unchanged; making positive-mass coalitions the deviators would change the equilibrium concept. So a continuum model is imaginable, but specifying it would be a new extension rather than a continuous problem anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize when a social choice function is repeatedly implementable; they do not classify the complexity of deciding implementability or give algorithms for a computational problem. The constructive mechanism in Theorem 2 does not supply such a result. This is not a case where a computational anchor is hidden behind unfamiliar terminology: the paper’s contribution is an existence characterization.

The proposed setting of many recurring task assignment teams is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime, but it does not turn these results into computational ones. If each team is a copy of the paper’s two-agent interaction, the mechanism and equilibrium question can be posed team by team; the mass of copies adds no implementation problem for the paper to answer. If instead a single mechanism assigns a large population across tasks, with aggregate capacity or complementarities, the object being implemented and the strategic problem have changed.

The strongest attempt to build a mirror would be to study the computational complexity of repeated implementation when agents occur in a few complete utility types with rational population shares. But the paper’s conditions are stated in terms of each agent’s unilateral deviations and continuation payoffs. In an atomless model where mechanisms respond to aggregate reports, one agent’s deviation leaves those reports unchanged. Treating a positive mass of agents as the deviator would replace Nash implementation with a coalition or type-level deviation concept. Alternatively, if outcomes and incentives remain individual, the population measure does not by itself provide the finite, encoded input needed for a complexity classification. Either route needs a new formal problem before it can yield a continuous mirror of this paper.

So the positive case identifies a setting that could motivate new work, but not a computational question anchored in this paper’s results. My negative case is strongest on that boundary: an anonymous, continuum-agent theory of implementation might be worthwhile in its own right, but its central equilibrium concept and implementation conditions would have to be built anew. That possibility prevents a proof that no scenario could ever be fruitful; it does not give this paper a worthwhile continuous mirror under ChoCo’s computational brief.
