Why no mirror

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational result, and the proposed mass-allocation question is precise and plausible for a large, standardized swarm. But the theorem concerns decentralized convergence under finite agents’ unilateral moves; the mirror instead asks for a static nonatomic equilibrium, where an infinitesimal move does not change task loads. That changes the solution concept and leaves no continuous version of the anchored convergence result that the authors would recognize as the same problem.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mass model retains the task-allocation setting and stable-assignment goal, but leaves out Theorem 2’s finite-agent iteration guarantee, local communication process, and asynchronous convergence analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for any SPAO GRAPE instance, the agents can reach a Nash stable partition in at most \(|A|(|A|+1)/2\) iterations. The paper also says this remains true from a random initial partition, and analyzes its decentralized procedure as taking \(O(d_G n_a^2)\) time steps on a strongly connected communication network. This is a convergence result, not a hardness result; the paper offers no comparable named hardness result to anchor instead.

My lead mirror is the Continuous SPAO Task-Allocation Equilibrium problem. Its instance is a finite set of tasks, including a void task, and a society of total mass \(1\) divided among \(\tau\) types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a utility curve \(u_{tj}(q)\) for each task \(j\). The curve gives the utility to type \(t\) when the mass assigned to task \(j\) is \(q\); require it to be rational piecewise-linear and nonincreasing, the continuous version of SPAO. A type is a complete description of everything that affects its utility curves.

The decision variables are \(x_{tj}\geq0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to task \(j\), with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\). Let \(q_j=\sum_t x_{tj}\). The requested solution is an allocation \(x\) such that every type assigned positive mass to task \(j\) weakly prefers it to every other task at the resulting loads:
\[ x_{tj}>0 \implies u_{tj}(q_j)\geq u_{tk}(q_k)\quad\text{for every task }k. \]
This is a nonatomic Nash-stability condition: a vanishingly small agent’s move does not change a task’s mass. The computational question is whether an exact stable allocation can be found in time polynomial in the number of tasks, \(\tau\), the number of utility-curve segments, and the input bit length \(L\). Welfare maximization is not the objective of this mirror; the objective is to compute the stable task assignment the paper studies.

A credible regime is a very large, standardized robot swarm deployed in batches from a small number of staging sites to a fixed set of tasks. Robots in a batch share hardware, task costs, and utility curves; their individual utilities fall as more robots share a task because of interference or shared resources. The population might number hundreds of thousands while the number of distinct types is in the tens or hundreds. Mass then means the fraction of the swarm assigned to each task. This is a narrow but plausible regime: if exact location or cost differences make every robot distinct, the support is no longer small.

The bridge is the high-multiplicity limit. A finite swarm with \(n_t\) robots of type \(t\) has proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); as those counts grow with proportions fixed, the discrete coalition sizes become task masses. The mass split is over agents’ assignments, not over task outcomes. The paper’s own use of population-fraction models in its related-work discussion makes this a natural computational question, while its contribution remains the stable-allocation computation rather than a new analytic population model.

I would tentatively expect Class C, continuum-specifically hard, though with low confidence. The discrete convergence proof relies on single-agent moves and on adding agents one at a time; that argument does not automatically give a finite algorithm for reallocating divisible mass. In the continuous problem, type-specific utility curves couple each task’s load to the choices of all types, and there may be no common potential or LP. Finding structure that makes the equilibrium tractable is still a worthwhile outcome; if it is hard, the source of hardness would be the equilibrium conditions in the continuum model, not merely the number of named agents. The further questions are whether stable mass allocations always exist for these curves, whether exact solutions have polynomial bit complexity, and whether the paper’s asynchronous decentralized dynamics have a meaningful mass-based counterpart.

The weakest point is that Theorem 2’s finite-agent convergence guarantee may have no direct continuous analogue: when one agent has zero mass, a unilateral move does not change coalition mass. That is a serious gap in transferring the result, but not a reason to discard the mirror. The proposed problem keeps the paper’s tasks, type-dependent interests, social inhibition, and Nash-stability objective intact; it asks what computation looks like when the population itself is the continuous object. My case is for that question, not for inheriting the paper’s convergence bound.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor, so I would not reject the mirror on the ground that the paper has no computational result. My strongest objection is that the theorem bounds the iterations of a particular decentralized process: agents update one at a time, using locally known partitions, over a communication graph. The proposed mass model specifies utilities and task loads, but leaves out that graph and process. Its stable-allocation condition asks for a static nonatomic equilibrium: a type using a task must find it at least as good as every alternative at the resulting loads. That is a coherent question, but it does not preserve what Theorem 2 measures.

The mismatch remains in a more faithful version. A mass update would need a rule for how much mass moves, which types can communicate, and how updates are scheduled and propagated. Those choices define a new dynamic model; the theorem’s bound in individual-agent iterations has no canonical continuous unit. Encoding enough location and communication detail to recover the paper’s local process may also erase the small-support representation that makes continuization attractive.

The proponent’s static version is a multiclass Wardrop equilibrium on parallel resources. That is an established computational game model: the single-class case reduces to convex optimization, and affine multiclass cases have polynomial algorithms when the number of classes is fixed. Broader multiclass Wardrop equilibrium computation also has known complexity results. These results do not settle the proposed arbitrary piecewise-linear, variable-\(\tau\) task model, so they are evidence of a close neighboring literature rather than proof that the exact mirror is already done. [Meunier and Pradeau](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6496), [Klimm and Warode](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.08354).

I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity swarm exists; repeated utility profiles are credible. The negative case is strongest against calling this a mirror of Theorem 2: its most direct continuous formulation changes a decentralized convergence result into static equilibrium computation, while restoring the dynamics requires substantial new modeling choices. The case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weak. Variable-support Wardrop computation could still be a worthwhile project if the authors recognize it as their problem.
