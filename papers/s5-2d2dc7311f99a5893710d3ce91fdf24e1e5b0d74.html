What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 6; the paper's structural and set-theoretic results are left alone.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It claims that for voting procedures on a countably infinite society satisfying strong candidate stability, strong unanimity, and IIA, no finite program can decide whether the procedure has a dictator. Here a dictator is a particular voter whose top-ranked candidate is always chosen on every feasible ballot and profile. The paper proves the claim by a Cantor-style diagonal argument. This is the only result I would anchor: the paper’s other numbered results are structural or set-theoretic, rather than computational complexity results.

A plausible continuous analogue is Continuous Mass-Bloc Dictator Recognition. Consider a large electorate divided into \(q\ge 2\) enduring blocs. Each bloc contains many voters who share a weak order over a finite candidate set \(C\), with \(|C|\ge 3\). A type is a pair \((r,\succ_r)\): bloc \(r\) and its shared preference. A society is
\[ \mu_{w,\rho}=\sum_{r=1}^{q}w_r\delta_{(r,\succ_r)}, \]
where \(w_r\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), \(\sum_r w_r=1\), and \(\rho=(\succ_1,\ldots,\succ_q)\). The support has \(q\) types even when the blocs represent millions of people.

An instance is a code for a total voting rule \(F\) that takes such a distribution and a feasible candidate set \(B\) and returns one winner. Promise that, at every fixed mass vector \(w\), the induced rule over bloc rankings satisfies strong candidate stability, strong unanimity, and IIA. The question is whether there is one bloc \(r\) such that, for every rational mass vector \(w\), every ranking profile \(\rho\), and every feasible \(B\),
\[ F(\mu_{w,\rho},B)\in T(B,\succ_r). \]
A “yes” solution names the bloc; “no” means each bloc has some mass vector, profile, and ballot where its top-ranked set does not contain the winner. This mirrors the source’s search for one persistent preference-bearer whose ranking governs outcomes, with a whole homogeneous mass bloc taking the place of a single named voter.

I expect this question to be Class C, and this version appears undecidable by a direct reduction. Given a Turing machine \(M\), let \(b\) be the denominator of \(w_1\) in lowest terms. Define the rule to follow bloc 2 if \(w_1\ne 1/2\) and \(M\) halts within \(b\) steps; otherwise, follow bloc 1. The winner is the first candidate in a fixed tie-breaking order among the chosen bloc’s top-ranked candidates. At each fixed \(w\), this is an ordinary dictatorial rule, so it satisfies the promised axioms. If \(M\) never halts, bloc 1 is dictatorial for every society. If \(M\) halts, some rational mass vector makes bloc 2 dictate, while \(w_1=1/2\) makes bloc 1 dictate; with profiles where the blocs rank different candidates first, neither bloc is a dictator across all societies. Thus deciding this mirror would decide whether \(M\) halts. The undecidability here comes from quantifying over rational population shares and allowing a rule’s behavior to depend on their exact encoding; it is not a transfer of the paper’s diagonal proof.

The regime is plausible for large electorates organized into a few persistent constituencies with shared ballots: the number of people can be enormous while the number of bloc-types remains small. The mirror covers Theorem 6’s dictatorship-recognition question only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s full social-choice analysis.

My weakest point is the change from an individual dictator to a dictatorial bloc. Aggregating voters with the same ranking erases their individual identities, so a single-voter dictator is not directly represented by \(\mu\). A referee could reasonably say that the bloc question is adjacent to, but not the authors’ question. There is also a natural follow-up: does undecidability persist under meaningful restrictions on how \(F\) may depend on mass shares? The reduction uses deliberately denominator-sensitive behavior.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is the only anchor, and its most serious obstacle is the object it asks an algorithm to inspect. The paper’s voting procedure acts on profiles in \(6^\omega\): each voter has a separate coordinate, and dictatorship means that the *same named voter* governs outcomes across all profiles. The paper gives no finite encoding of a voting procedure. Its undecidability claim is therefore a diagonal argument over a set of functions, rather than a standard finite-input problem whose input is a society distribution. Replacing voters by a distribution does not carry that decision problem across.

The information loss is fundamental. Two profiles can have the same distribution over rankings while assigning those rankings to different voters. That distribution cannot say whether one particular voter is a dictator. Putting voter identity into the type would preserve the information, but then every voter has a distinct type and the countably infinite society has infinite support. A finite-support high-multiplicity input cannot encode the profiles the theorem quantifies over. In an atomless model, an individual has zero mass, so the distribution again fails to record the identity the property tracks.

The proponent’s persistent-bloc repair is the strongest alternative: a few large constituencies share rankings, and one asks whether the same bloc dictates across rankings and population shares. That is a plausible question about a family of aggregators. But the undecidability reduction makes the family’s behavior depend on the denominator of a rational share, so the halting information lives in the aggregator’s code and arithmetic tests on its inputs. The decision problem takes a rule description and quantifies over all mass vectors; it does not take a society as its computational instance. It could be worth studying as verification of programmable aggregation rules, but that would need its own motivation and a specified class of rule descriptions. Theorem 6 does not provide that bridge.

A model retaining identity through a continuum of labelled agents would preserve the dictator notion, but it would also preserve the identity-indexed profile rather than describe society by masses of finitely many types. So my case against is strong on fit with ChoCo’s high-multiplicity programme, though not airtight against every adjacent research question: a carefully motivated theory of bloc dictatorship could be worthwhile. I would classify that as a new question about aggregators over varying societies, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s undecidability result.
