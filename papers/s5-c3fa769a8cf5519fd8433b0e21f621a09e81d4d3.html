The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a continuous STV margin. But the paper has no qualifying numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. It does contain substantial computational work—Algorithms 1–4, a MINLP, and experimental comparisons—but those do not meet the anchor requirement. The closest complexity statement is the introduction’s citation of Xia (2012) for NP-hardness of exact IRV margins, from which the authors infer hardness for exact STV margins; it is not a numbered result of this paper.

The mirror itself is concrete. Let \(T\) be the valid ballot rankings, including partial rankings, and let the input be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Run Weighted Inclusive Gregory STV on this population, using quota \(1/(N+1)\), the large-population limit of the normalized Droop quota, and a fixed tie-breaking rule. Write \(W(\mu)\) for its winners. Choose nonnegative transfers \(x_{t,t'}\), with \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\), producing
\[ \mu'_t=\mu_t-\sum_{u\ne t}x_{t,u}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{u,t}. \]
The Continuous STV Margin asks for the minimum \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\) such that \(W(\mu')\ne W(\mu)\); a solution gives the transfers and the resulting winner set. Equivalently, its threshold version asks whether such a change is possible with at most a specified mass \(\delta\).

A plausible regime is a large proportional election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballot types, so \(n\gg\tau\). The question remains about the paper’s underlying problem—how much of the electorate must change its ballot to alter who wins. Normalizing by population size is a natural co-modification, and the paper’s type-based MINLP in Section 5 offers some modeling precedent, though its election instance remains finite. My tentative expectation is Class B: the algorithm still searches over combinatorial orders of candidate eliminations and elections, which population continuity does not remove. That is a prior, not a result; a discrete-to-continuous embedding or limit argument would be needed to establish hardness transfer. Further questions include whether margins on replicated profiles converge to this optimum and how its complexity depends on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length.

The weak point is the anchor: this is a credible mirror of the paper’s central margin question, but I cannot attach it to a qualifying named result. There is also real mathematical work in deciding whether fractional transfers and the limiting quota preserve the relevant discrete hardness; Gregory surplus transfers make the resulting optimization nonlinear.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper-specific anchor is that it is not a continuous mirror of a named result. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational complexity result. Its contributions are algorithmic heuristics, a MINLP, and empirical comparisons. The NP-hardness cited for exact IRV margins is prior work, not a result proved here. The proponent’s “Continuous STV Margin” is a plausible new question based on Definition 2.2, but their conjecture that it belongs to Class B is not a result of this paper, and the required discrete-to-continuous hardness transfer is still open.

That disqualifies the proposed anchor under the stated standard; it does not, by itself, show the mirror is unworthy. The stronger version of the mirror is quite natural: represent voters by complete ballot types, use rational masses and the limiting normalized quota, then ask for the least mass of ballots that must change type to change the winner set. Large proportional elections with many voters and relatively few distinct rankings are a credible high-multiplicity regime. The objective tracks only how many ballots change, not which named voters change, so identity loss is not a fundamental objection. Fractional mass and boundary cases call for careful definitions, but they do not defeat the question.

Nor do the paper’s lower-bound heuristics provide a stronger negative case. Their discrete integer outputs and audit motivation make the continuous version a different, less directly operational problem; yet the mass version still asks about the same winner-robustness question, and the transfer-path and displacement ideas could motivate continuous algorithms. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The defensible negative finding is narrower: this proponent has not anchored the mirror in a named computational result of the paper, and the paper itself establishes no complexity claim for the continuous problem.
