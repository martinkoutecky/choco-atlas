The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over rational locations on the line, a finite rational candidate set \(M\), a prediction \(\hat{o}\in M\), and rational \(\gamma\in[0,1)\), does there exist a deterministic anonymous mechanism \(F\) that is mass-group-strategy-proof and satisfies \(SC_\mu(F(\mu,M,\hat{o},\gamma))\leq \frac{3-\gamma}{1+\gamma}OPT_\mu\) whenever \(\hat{o}\) is optimal, and \(SC_\mu(F(\mu,M,\hat{o},\gamma))\leq \frac{3+\gamma}{1-\gamma}OPT_\mu\) for every prediction? Mass-group-strategy-proofness means no positive-mass submeasure of agents can replace its reports by another measure of equal mass and make almost all coalition members strictly better off. The candidate rule takes a weighted median of \(\mu+\gamma\delta_{\hat{o}}\) and returns a nearest site in \(M\), with fixed deterministic tie-breaking.

The model it lives in

The population is \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{x_j}\), with rational masses summing to one; the types are locations and the facility remains restricted to \(M\). Social cost is \(SC_\mu(y)=\sum_j\mu_j|x_j-y|\). The candidate mechanism computes a weighted median of \(\mu+\gamma\delta_{\hat{o}}\), then selects a nearest candidate site. Sorting the \(\tau\) locations and checking the \(|M|\) sites gives polynomial computation in \(\tau\), \(|M|\), and the input bit length; the mass-coalition incentive guarantee and approximation bounds require proof.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4 proves group strategy-proofness for finite-agent profiles, not for divisible-cohort deviations; the proponent acknowledges this gap, which remains the key proof obligation for the proposed mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s social-cost guarantee for Mechanism 2. It leaves Theorems 1–3 and 6 on ordinary facility location, and Theorems 7–9 on obnoxious facility location, outside its scope; the Theorem 5 frontier is a separate incentive-quality lower-bound question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the weighted-median rule remain mass-group-strategy-proof when a positive-mass coalition can report any alternative measure of equal mass?
  • Do its consistency and robustness bounds from Theorem 4 hold for every finite-support rational population and finite candidate set?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s social-cost facility-location problem. A type is a resident’s location \(x\in\mathbb{Q}\), which completely determines her cost \(|x-y|\) at facility site \(y\). The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{x_j}\), with rational masses summing to one; social cost per resident is
\[ SC_\mu(y)=\int |x-y|\,d\mu(x). \]
Take a finite set of rational candidate sites \(M\subset\mathbb{Q}\), and a public prediction \(\hat{o}\in M\) of an optimal site. The facility still has to be built at a candidate site: the continuous object is the population.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a city siting a school among a finite list of sites, with many households sharing an address or location type. The population could be very large while \(\tau\), the number of distinct location types, is comparatively small. The paper already treats location as the private information and predicted optimum as the signal, so this is a close translation of its problem rather than a new story for its mechanism.

For strategyproofness, the continuum needs a mass-based group notion: a coalition is a submeasure \(\eta\leq\mu\) of positive mass; it may replace its reports with any measure \(\rho\) of the same mass, changing the report distribution to \(\mu-\eta+\rho\), while \(\hat{o}\) stays fixed. A rule is mass-GSP if no such deviation makes almost every member of the coalition strictly better off at the resulting site. This keeps coalitional incentives meaningful when an individual has zero population mass.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that Mechanism 2, for the single facility-location game on the line, is deterministic, anonymous, and group strategy-proof, and achieves consistency \((3-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)\) and robustness \((3+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)\) for social cost, for a constant \(\gamma\in[0,1)\). In the finite model its virtual profile contains \(\gamma n\) agents at \(\hat{o}\), so \(\gamma n\) must be an integer.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is: given \((\mu,M,\hat{o},\gamma)\), with rational \(\gamma\in[0,1)\), find a deterministic anonymous mass-GSP facility rule whose approximation ratio is at most \((3-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)\) whenever \(\hat{o}\) is optimal, and at most \((3+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)\) for every prediction. A concrete candidate solution is to take a weighted median \(z\) of \(\mu+\gamma\delta_{\hat{o}}\), then return a site \(y\in M\) nearest to \(z\), with fixed deterministic tie-breaking. The virtual voters become prediction mass, exactly as the real voters become population mass. The expected answer is positive: sorting the \(\tau\) locations, finding the weighted median, and scanning or searching \(M\) gives a polynomial-time computation in \(\tau\), \(|M|\), and the input bit length. The paper’s theorem does not itself state that runtime or prove the mass-deviation version, so those belong in the mirror’s proof obligation.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here. It states that, under social cost, the robustness of any deterministic strategy-proof mechanism whose consistency is strictly below \((3-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)\) is at least \((3+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)-\delta\), for every \(\gamma\in[0,1)\) and \(\delta>0\). This is a mechanism-quality lower bound, not a computational hardness theorem.

Its continuous counterpart is the frontier question: among deterministic anonymous mass-GSP rules, can any rule with consistency strictly below \((3-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)\) have robustness below \((3+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)\)? A solution would establish the lower bound or exhibit a counterexample. I expect the bound to transfer. The paper’s construction can be written using only two location types: put mass \((1+\gamma)/2\) at \(1\) and \((1-\gamma)/2\) at \(2\), with candidate sites \(\{\varepsilon,2\}\) and prediction \(2\). For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\), consistency below the stated threshold forces the rule to choose \(2\). Mass-GSP then forces that choice to persist when the first cohort reports \(\varepsilon\), and when the second cohort at the resulting profile reports \(2\). The final distribution has those cohorts at \(\varepsilon\) and \(1+\varepsilon\); choosing \(2\) there gives a robustness ratio tending to \((3+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)\) as \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero. This lower bound therefore survives the continuum, even with support size two. It is a transferred approximation lower bound, not a claim of NP-hardness.

These two questions cover the paper’s social-cost objective for ordinary, non-obnoxious facility location. They do not continuize outcomes, time, or predictions. Nor do they cover the maximum-cost or obnoxious-facility results. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, W[1]-hard, or FPT; its anchors here are named mechanism and approximation results. The complexity trichotomy is consequently only a partial fit: the first mirror is tractable in support size, while the second asks whether an approximation lower bound transfers.

The weakest point is that the paper is about strategyproof mechanism design with predictions, not a complexity landscape. The upper-bound computation is simple, and a finite rational distribution can also be represented by duplicating agents in a sufficiently large discrete instance. Still, the paper studies named agents and individual reports, whereas the proposed questions treat a distribution as the input and allow mass coalitions; the computational parameter is \(\tau\), not the number of residents. Exact shared locations are also a narrower population regime than a general city. I think the mirror is plausible and the two-type lower bound makes it substantive, but it is a focused high-multiplicity mechanism-design mirror, not a central complexity case for the broader programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a boundary argument about what ChoCo is trying to study. The location distribution is a sensible high-multiplicity model: many residents can share a location type, and the objective becomes \(SC_\mu(y)=\int |x-y|\,d\mu(x)\). There is no honest objection based on the absence of repeated types.

Theorem 4 therefore has a coherent mirror. Given \((\mu,M,\hat{o},\gamma)\), ask for a deterministic mass-GSP rule with the stated consistency and robustness guarantees. The weighted-median rule in the proponent’s case is the natural candidate, and it can be computed by sorting the \(\tau\) support points and checking the candidate sites. Its central question is whether the incentive and approximation guarantees extend to divisible cohorts. Its computational question is modest: the rule reduces to weighted order statistics, with no difficult optimization or pricing problem over a large type space. That makes it a real mechanism-design question, but a peripheral fit for a programme centered on computational complexity and algorithms for problems such as bribery, control, and robustness.

Theorem 5 is weaker as a ChoCo anchor. Its continuous version asks whether the same approximation frontier holds under mass-GSP. The two-cohort construction already gives the route: positive masses at \(1\) and \(2\), followed by cohort deviations to \(\varepsilon\) and \(1+\varepsilon\). The resulting ratio approaches \((3+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)\). That is a substantive incentive lower bound, but it adds no computational problem for an algorithm to solve.

A stronger scenario—say, siting a school among a finite set of sites for a city with many residents at a small number of addresses—does not change this programme-fit argument. It makes the population interpretation convincing. It does not turn the paper’s approximation frontier into a complexity landscape. So the negative case is only that these mirrors should not be a priority for ChoCo’s stated agenda. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: if ChoCo includes computational mechanism design and incentive frontiers, Theorem 4 survives as a well-posed mirror.
