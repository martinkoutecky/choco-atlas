The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational complexity result to anchor one. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–3 are axiomatic characterizations of the \(\alpha\)-CEM value. Theorem 4 is a cited recursive formula for the Myerson value. Theorem 5 is the closest match, but it proves a game-theoretic implementation result: for a component-additive, zero-monotonic network game, every subgame-perfect equilibrium of the proposed bidding mechanism yields the \(\alpha\)-CEM payoff vector. It gives no complexity classification or algorithm for computing an underlying problem.

A possible large-population story—many interchangeable agents in recurring network roles—would still need a new model of how links and worth behave across types. More importantly, its natural questions would concern a continuum allocation rule or its axiomatic properties, not the computational complexity of the paper’s problem. Those would be inventions around the paper, rather than mirrors anchored in a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror is strongest on the programme’s basic gate: this paper does not pose a computational problem. Theorem 1–3 characterize an allocation rule by axioms; Theorem 5 proves an equilibrium implementation. Theorem 4 is a cited formula, not a new complexity result. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the difficulty of computing anything. The equilibrium theorem assumes the network and game are known, and its prescribed bids use Myerson values; it does not give an algorithm for finding an equilibrium.

One could propose computing the \(\alpha\)-CEM allocation. But the paper gives no input encoding for \(v\). With the direct encoding of a network game as a table over all subgraphs, the formula sums over only \(2^n\) coalitions, so evaluation is polynomial in the table’s already exponential size. A succinct encoding might make evaluation interesting, but its choice would define a new computational problem, rather than continue one this paper studies.

The proposed large-population story—many interchangeable agents in recurring network roles—also runs into a structural fork. If the population consists of repeated finite components, component additivity makes those copies independent: a distribution over them just aggregates allocations computed within each component. If instead the connected network grows with the population, a type must capture a player’s position across the induced subnetworks on which their Myerson marginal contributions depend. In generic instances, that can distinguish every player. A graphon or other continuum network could be a sensible new model, but the paper supplies no worth functional on such objects and no canonical continuum counterpart to its random-order marginal contributions.

The bidding mechanism does not supply the missing bridge. It selects a named proposer, removes that player, and recurses through a finite sequence of rounds. In a nonatomic population, no individual has positive probability of being selected, and an individual’s marginal contribution can vanish. Giving a positive mass a proposer role or replacing marginal contributions with derivatives may be defensible, but each requires a new bargaining model and allocation rule.

So I do not see a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results. A continuum network-allocation project could be worthwhile on its own; the paper’s axioms and mechanism do not anchor a computational question about a continuous society. That is a strong scope objection, though it is not a proof that no future formulation could be valuable.
