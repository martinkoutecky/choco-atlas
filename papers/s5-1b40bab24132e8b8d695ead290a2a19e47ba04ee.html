The continuous mirror question

Given finite goods \(G\), a finite set \(T\) of complete normalized monotone submodular valuation types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_g\), \(c>0\), and one private valuation input per positive-mass type, decide whether integral bundle templates \(B_t\subseteq G\) exist with \(\sum_{t:g\in B_t}\mu_t\le\sigma_g\) for every \(g\) and \(v_t(B_t)\ge c\,v_t(B_u)\) for all positive-mass \(t,u\); output the templates or certify infeasibility, with worst-case randomized communication measured between type representatives.

The model it lives in

A type-anonymous high-multiplicity fair-division model: valuation types carry masses \(\mu_t\), goods have per-capita capacities \(\sigma_g\), each type receives an integral bundle template, and the computational task is \(c\)-envy-free feasibility or certification under private type valuations.

The objection that survived

The uniform-bundle constraint may artificially couple interchangeable agents, so in the equal-mass construction the population size cancels and the mirror closely reproduces the finite role-allocation problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the multi-player submodular communication lower bounds in Theorems 7.1 and 7.2, while leaving the two-player bounds, randomized \(c\)-EF protocol, other valuation classes, and MMS results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(\Omega\!\left(\binom{2k}{k}\right)\) lower bound from Theorem 7.1 survive for typed-\(c\)-EF\(_\infty\) under rational \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and under what encoding?
  • Does allowing \(x_{t,B}\) fractions of a type to receive different integral bundles preserve ex-post envy-freeness without becoming a different fractional-fairness problem?
  • Which proportionality normalization is canonical for unequal masses, and does the lower bound of Theorem 7.2 transfer under that normalization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity fair-division problem in which goods remain indivisible and only the population is continuized. My lead mirror is \(\textsc{Typed-}c\textsc{-EF}_{\infty}\), anchored in Theorem 7.1.

Let \(G\) be a finite set of goods and \(T\) a finite set of complete valuation types. A type \(t\) consists of a normalized, monotone, submodular valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\). The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). Goods also have per-capita supplies \(\sigma_g\), because a continuum of agents cannot compete for one unique physical copy of an item while each receives an integral bundle.

In the most faithful, type-anonymous version, every unit of type-\(t\) mass receives the same integral bundle \(B_t\subseteq G\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_{t:g\in B_t}\mu_t\leq \sigma_g\) for every \(g\), with equality if all goods must be allocated. The question is whether there exists such a family \((B_t)_{t\in T}\) satisfying

\[ v_t(B_t)\geq c\,v_t(B_u) \]

for every pair of types \(t,u\) with positive mass. If so, output the bundles; otherwise certify that none exists. In the communication version, each valuation type is held privately by a representative of that cohort, and the objective is to minimize worst-case randomized communication.

This is a genuine population mirror. For a fixed \(q>2\), take \(q\) valuation cohorts of mass \(1/q\), \(N\) repeated agents per cohort, and \(N\) copies of every good. Normalizing by the \(qN\) agents gives \(\mu_t=\sigma_g=1/q\). Every individual still receives an integral bundle, while the number of agents tends to infinity and the number of types remains \(q\). Clearing denominators gives the exact dictionary back to finite repeated instances.

The regime is plausible whenever a large institution repeatedly allocates standardized packages among a small number of recurring valuation cohorts: for example, several large demographic or organizational groups competing for replicated inventories. The type includes every feature relevant to valuation and eligibility, so the construction is not pretending that idiosyncratic agents are identical.

The lead anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(n>2\) and every \(c>0\), determining whether a \(c\)-envy-free allocation exists for \(n\) players and \(2k+n-2\) indivisible goods requires randomized communication

\[ R_{\mathrm{submod}}(n,2k+n-2,\mathrm{EF},c) \in \Omega\!\left(\binom{2k}{k}\right), \]

even when every valuation is submodular. The theorem is proved here by a reduction from Disjointness; the paper’s Lemma 5.1 supplies the cited communication lower bound. The construction uses two privately held valuation functions, with the other players’ valuations public, and encodes the exponentially many balanced \(k\)-versus-\(k\) partitions of \(2k\) goods.

The continuous problem just defined contains the high-multiplicity version of this construction: take \(q=n\) cohorts, each of mass \(1/q\), and scale every good’s supply by the population. Repeating each cohort \(N\) times does not reveal anything new about the two private valuation types, so the same communication lower bound should survive. The combinatorics live in the goods and the exponentially many possible bundles, not in the number of named agents. I therefore expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers rather than dissolves.

A secondary anchor is Theorem 7.2, also proved in the paper. It states that for every \(n>2\) and \(c>0\),

\[ R_{\mathrm{submod}}(n,2k+n-2,\mathrm{Prop},c) \in \Omega\!\left(\binom{2k}{k}\right), \]

even for submodular valuations. Its continuous counterpart, \(\textsc{Typed-}c\textsc{-Prop}_{\infty}\), uses the same \(q\) equal-mass cohorts and integral bundles, and asks whether

\[ v_t(B_t)\geq \frac{c}{q}v_t(G) \]

for every type \(t\). Again, the question is to output the bundles or certify infeasibility. Theorem 7.2 predicts the same Class B communication-hardness boundary.

The authors should recognize these as continuizations of their questions: the goods, valuation classes, fairness inequalities, private-information model, and yes/no feasibility objective are unchanged. What changes is that each valuation role is occupied by a large mass of indistinguishable agents and the inventory scales per capita. This is not cake cutting, a lottery over outcomes, or a fractional valuation model.

The main weakness is real. The paper’s theorems concern communication complexity, not centralized NP-hardness, so they do not by themselves prove a time-complexity lower bound for a central planner. Also, a more permissive continuum model could let a type split its mass across several integral bundles. That mixing may convexify the problem and destroy the reduction. The pure type-anonymous formulation therefore builds in a substantive institutional assumption: identical cohort members receive one common bundle template.

That weakness does not eliminate the mirror. Cohort-uniform allocation is a defensible high-multiplicity regime, and it isolates an important research question: whether the paper’s communication hardness survives population scaling, and whether allowing mass to mix across integral bundles produces a genuinely tractable relaxation. The natural next problem is the mixed version with variables \(x_{t,B}\), where \(x_{t,B}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\), capacities are \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}\mu_t x_{t,B}\leq\sigma_g\), and envy-freeness is imposed support-wise. That is where a real continuum-specific tractability or hardness boundary should emerge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s construction is formally possible, but neither anchor yields a worthwhile continuization of this paper’s contribution. The problem is not that the continuous version might be easy; it is that the population disappears from the computational object.

Theorem 7.1 is a communication theorem about \(n\) privately informed role-holders. In the reduction, only players \(1\) and \(2\) possess private information; the remaining \(n-2\) players have public valuations and are explicitly described by the paper as essentially non-agents. Replacing each role-holder by a cohort of \(N\) identical agents therefore creates no new information problem. One representative already communicates the complete valuation of the entire cohort.

Indeed, in the proponent’s uniform model with \(q=n\), \(\mu_t=\sigma_g=1/q\), and one bundle \(B_t\) per type, the feasibility constraint becomes

\[ \sum_t \frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}[g\in B_t]\leq \frac{1}{q}, \]

or simply \(\sum_t\mathbf{1}[g\in B_t]\leq 1\). The mass cancels. Envy-freeness is exactly the original pairwise condition after replacing “player” by “type”. The parameter \(N\) never enters the instance, the protocol, or its communication cost. This is a continuum-degenerate model: the supposed population limit is only a relabelling of the original finite set of communication parties.

There is also an extra modelling restriction hidden in the construction. High multiplicity makes agents interchangeable; it does not require all identical agents to receive the same bundle. Requiring a whole cohort to receive one common bundle artificially couples otherwise interchangeable agents. It is precisely this coupling that reconstructs the original \(n\)-player instance. Once it is removed, the natural high-multiplicity problem is different.

That objection applies equally to Theorem 7.2. The proposed condition

\[ v_t(B_t)\geq \frac{c}{q}v_t(G) \]

is a reasonable equal-cohort convention, but it is not a canonical population version of proportionality. The paper’s threshold \(v_i(G)/n\) is based on \(n\) named, equal-claim players. With unequal type masses, one must decide whether proportionality is per individual, per cohort, or mass-weighted. Those choices produce different fairness notions. The theorem itself supplies no population semantics, and its equal-mass special case again reduces exactly to the original role allocation.

The proposed mixed model does not rescue the anchors cleanly. If \(x_{t,B}\) allows identical members of a type to receive different integral bundles, then one must choose between two interpretations. Under ex post envy-freeness, every bundle in the support must satisfy the relevant comparisons against every other supported bundle. The fractions \(x_{t,B}\) then affect only capacity; fairness depends on the support, not on the population mass. This is a new configuration-support problem, not the paper’s communication problem in which each private player chooses one bundle.

Under ex ante or average envy-freeness, the model becomes a fractional or randomized allocation problem: a type receives a lottery over bundles and is judged by expected value. That may be interesting, but its new tractability comes from relaxing individual fairness and fractionalizing assignments. It is outcome-space continuity in disguise, adjacent to the cake-cutting and randomized-allocation literature that the programme explicitly distinguishes from population continuization. Such mixing is not forced by having many agents; one can instead require almost-everywhere individual fairness.

Thus the two faithful choices are both unsatisfactory as mirrors. Cohort-uniform allocation preserves the paper’s communication instance but makes the population vacuous. Allowing cohort members to separate produces either a support-level assignment problem with no communication-theoretic population effect, or a different fractional-fairness problem. Neither is a continuous version of Theorems 7.1 or 7.2 in the sense needed here.

This negative case is not airtight: a researcher could reasonably study mass-weighted fair division with repeated valuation types and indivisible per-agent bundles. But that would be a new fair-division programme, not a population continuization motivated by this paper’s named results. The proponent has shown formal admissibility, not a compelling ChoCo mirror.
