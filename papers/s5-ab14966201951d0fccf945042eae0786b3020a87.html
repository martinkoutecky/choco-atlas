The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a possible population extension, but I can’t make it a qualifying positive case under your anchor rule. In the supplied paper, the results concern equilibrium existence and structure in multilateral bargaining games. I find no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result—such as hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or a parameterized algorithm—for a named problem. So there are no anchors to quote and restate, and I can’t honestly predict a complexity class for a mirror of one.

The most plausible scenario to explore would be bargaining in a large institution where many participants share the same preferences and institutional role. A type would include an agent’s utility over allocations, continuation preferences, and proposer or responder role; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of participants of type \(t\). A proposal could be a type-dependent allocation \(x(t)\), with total resources constrained by \(\int x(t)\,d\mu(t)\leq 1\); responses would be type-conditional strategies. One could ask whether a specified allocation is supported by a subgame-perfect equilibrium, or compute such an equilibrium. That is a recognizable population version of strategic bargaining, but it is only a prospective computational question, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s complexity results.

The weak point is central: the paper’s bargaining protocol gives individual players proposer and responder positions, and its voting rule can depend on the full sequence of individual votes. Passing to masses forces choices about how those positions and votes aggregate; in particular, a mass-based threshold can change the role of an individual veto. The continuous game could be worth formulating, but the paper provides no computational anchor or established bridge to justify calling it the continuous mirror of one of its named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no computational anchor to defend. The paper’s central result establishes pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibrium for a broad class of sequential bargaining games. Its other contributions concern equilibrium structure and continuity assumptions. It does not state a complexity result for a named computational problem. The strongest candidate for a mirror is therefore “does an equilibrium exist?” On the paper’s stated class, that question is already answered by its existence theorem. The more interesting task, computing an equilibrium or deciding whether one supports a target allocation, needs a finite input representation that the paper does not provide: utilities may be arbitrary continuous functions, protocols may vary at every date, and the game has an infinite horizon and histories of unbounded length. [The working paper](https://irihs.ihs.ac.at/id/eprint/4333/1/BargainingTreesDiscPaper.pdf), [published abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304406821001038).

The proposed large-institution story makes the mismatch concrete. In this game, a proposer offers a vector in \(\Delta\), individual players vote in sequence, and the acceptance rule can depend on the full vote profile. Players’ positions rotate over time. Turning participants into types and votes into masses would require defining what a type-level offer means for individual shares and how a mass vote determines acceptance. In an atomless electorate, one person’s deviation cannot change the aggregate vote, so the original individual pivotality disappears. A threshold over positive mass can restore a decision rule, but it defines a different bargaining protocol. That may be a sensible new model; it does not preserve the paper’s sequential, player-level result.

The best version of the mirror would restrict attention to anonymous preferences, a finite set of roles and types, and an aggregate quorum rule, then ask for equilibrium outcomes or equilibria with a specified property. Those choices could yield a meaningful research question. They also supply the payoff representation, voting protocol, and aggregate strategic structure that the paper deliberately leaves general. The result would be a new continuum bargaining model whose computational analysis needs its own motivation and input model; it would not turn one of this paper’s theorems into a ChoCo complexity question.

So the negative case is strong as a priority judgment for this programme: no computational result here gives ChoCo a result to mirror, and the natural population story requires changing the game’s central strategic structure before computation is even well-defined. It is weaker as a universal claim about research value. A continuum bargaining theory could be worthwhile on its own, and the paper gives no basis for ruling out every such model.
