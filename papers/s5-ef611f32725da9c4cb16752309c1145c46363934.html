The case FOR (proponent)

There is no valid computational anchor in this paper. Its Main Theorem, proved here, constructs virtual valuations and a rounding procedure with boundary-item guarantees. Corollary 1, also proved here, says that when the number of agents is a prime power, every instance with arbitrary valuations has an EF\(2^c_g\) allocation whose bundle sizes differ by at most two; for monotone valuations, the guarantee is EF2. These are existence results, not claims about polynomial time, hardness, or another complexity class. The cake-cutting results in Theorem 1 are cited existence results. So the paper offers zero named computational anchors of the kind the requested case must rest on.

The nearest positive lead is Corollary 1. A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large public allocation with many households, a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types, and population shares \(\mu_t\). Standardized indivisible goods would have per-capita supplies \(\beta_g\). A solution could assign mass \(x_{t,A}\) of type \(t\) to each integral bundle \(A\), with \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) and the supply constraints \(\sum_{t,A}x_{t,A}A_g=\beta_g\). The feasibility question would be whether such an assignment can use only bundles that are EF\(2^c_g\) against one another for every type, while their sizes differ by at most two. This keeps each agent’s bundle integral while making type-level allocation a mass assignment.

That is a coherent continuous-population question, but it is only a proposed analogue—not a result anchored by the paper. Corollary 1’s prime-power condition concerns a finite number of agents, and the paper gives no argument that its guarantee or proof extends to this mass-allocation model. The paper’s own continuity concerns divisible resources and fractional bundles; population continuity would be a new direction. That is the case’s weakest point, and it prevents an honest prediction of tractability or hardness from this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. The Main Theorem and both corollaries are existential guarantees; the paper gives no algorithm, running time, hardness result, or input model for finding the allocations. Its continuous objects are divisible resources and extensions of valuations, while its agents remain a finite set. That is a bridge from cake cutting to indivisible allocation, not a complexity result about a continuous population. This is the decisive problem for the proposed mirror.

Corollary 1 is the closest lead, but the proposed mass-assignment question has to be supplied from outside the paper. As stated, requiring every type to regard every pair of used bundles as EF\(2^c_g\) overstates the original guarantee: EF\(2^c_g\) compares an agent’s own bundle with another’s under that agent’s valuation. Repairing this to require fairness only between bundles actually assigned to types makes a coherent question, perhaps for a large population of households with repeated valuation types and scalable goods supplies. But it is then a new, support-constrained feasibility problem over masses and integral bundles. The paper gives no computational formulation or result that anchors it, and its prime-power existence theorem does not specify what should happen when the finite agent set becomes a distribution.

That leaves room for a useful project inspired by fair division generally, but not a worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity fair-division model exists; the household scenario is plausible. The negative case is strongest—and fairly decisive—on the narrower programme criterion: this paper supplies no named computational anchor for such a mirror.
