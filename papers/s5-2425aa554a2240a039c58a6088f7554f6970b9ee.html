The case FOR (proponent)

Its named theoretical results characterize equilibria in a three-player game. Propositions 1–3 are proved in Appendix A; Corollary 1 is stated as trivial.

The closest anchors are these equilibrium results. Proposition 1 says that with three players and a common reservation payoff \(v\), the symmetric equilibrium investment is \(e^*=(2-3v)/9\). The proposer randomly chooses one coalition member and offers \(v\); that member accepts, the proposer keeps \(1-v\), and each player’s expected payoff is \(1/3-e^*\). Proposition 2 says that with two low-reservation players at \(v_l\) and one high-reservation player, the equilibrium investments are \(e_h^*=(2-3v_l)/(9(1-v_l))\) and \(e_l^*=(2-3v_l)^2/(18(1-v_l))\). A high-type proposer randomly chooses a low-type coalition member; a low-type proposer chooses the other low-type player. The chosen member accepts an offer of \(v_l\), the proposer keeps \(1-v_l\), and each player’s expected payoff is \(1/3-e_i^*\). Proposition 3 gives the comparison \(e_l^*>e^*\) when \(\alpha\in(\alpha^*,v)\), with \(\alpha^*=(6v-4+\sqrt{(3v-8)(3v-2)})/9\). Corollary 1 says that knowing the other players’ equilibrium investments leaves a player’s best response unchanged. None of these results classifies the complexity of computing an equilibrium.

The most plausible population regime would be a large member organization in which people share a small number of reservation-payoff and contest-cost profiles. A candidate model could give each type mass \(\mu_t\), let each type choose per-capita contest effort, select a proposer in proportion to aggregate effort, and use a majority vote on the proposal. The question would be to compute equilibrium effort and proposal behavior, then report expected contest costs and allocations. That is a plausible extension of their bargaining contest. The paper’s results do not establish it as their continuous mirror, though: the authors analyze three players, and extending proposer selection and pivotal voting to an atomless population requires additional modeling. Equilibrium computation would also be an analytic question; the paper supplies no computational problem whose complexity this mirror could classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is the strongest point against a mirror: none of the named results classifies a computational problem. Propositions 1 and 2 give equilibrium behavior for a game with exactly three players; Proposition 3 compares two closed-form investment levels; Corollary 1 states an equilibrium best-response property. A question about computing equilibrium for a distribution of player types would be a new model, not a computational mirror of any of those results.

The proposed large-organization scenario does not preserve the anchors. In the experiment, participants are repeatedly rematched into separate groups of three. Adding more participants gives more observations of the same three-player game, not a larger society within one bargaining instance. Proposition 1’s symmetric case, Proposition 2’s one-high-type/two-low-type case, and Proposition 3’s comparison all depend on that fixed group. Corollary 1 concerns what a player does when the other two players choose their equilibrium investments; it does not raise a population-level computational question.

A larger assembly is the best alternative to consider, but it changes the mechanism in a consequential way. With an atomless population, each individual has zero mass: under the paper’s individual Tullock contest, an individual’s investment cannot affect their probability of becoming the single proposer. That removes the contest incentive the propositions analyze. Preserving it would require additional structure, such as proposers drawn from finite blocs or a population-dependent scaling of the prize and costs. Either could be a worthwhile model, but neither is supplied by the paper, and its results do not identify which computational problem to study.

So the negative case is strong against these anchors, though it cannot rule out every worthwhile future model of large-scale bargaining. The best proposed extension would ask for equilibrium behavior in a newly specified continuum bargaining game. That may be interesting economics, but it moves toward the analytic equilibrium questions the programme sets apart from its computational agenda. The paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and the larger-population version would need a new mechanism before there is a computational mirror to assess.
