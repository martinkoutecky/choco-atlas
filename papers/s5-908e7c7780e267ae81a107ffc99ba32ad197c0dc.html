The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of labels \(S\), pipeline types \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\), known accuracies \(x_t\), and response masses \(\nu_t(s)\) for one question, find a Bayes-optimal label under the paper's uniform-prior, shuffled-label, conditionally independent model. Is the answer always a maximizer over \(s\in S\) of \(\ell(s)=\sum_{t\in T}\left[\nu_t(s)\log x_t+(\mu_t-\nu_t(s))\log\frac{1-x_t}{K-1}\right]\)?

The model it lives in

A society of inference replicas has a finite set of pipeline types \(T\), each with mass \(\mu_t\) and accuracy \(x_t\). For one question, \(\nu_t(s)\) is the mass of type \(t\) answering label \(s\), with \(\sum_s\nu_t(s)=\mu_t\); the decision is the label maximizing the per-capita log-likelihood \(\ell(s)\). The answer space stays finite while the answering population is represented by type-level masses.

The objection that survived

With independent replicas, the answer masses converge to response probabilities; under the paper's symmetric-error assumptions, majority voting can then identify the truth exactly, so the continuous setting may remove the uncertainty that motivates the finite-agent comparison.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1's Bayesian-optimal one-round aggregator and can also express Theorem 4.1's expected ISP, MV, and SP advantage comparisons over type masses. It leaves aside finite-sample estimation of accuracies and second-order information, the empirical results, and multi-round or open-ended aggregation.

Open questions for a prover

  • How quickly does the finite-population posterior concentrate around the mass-likelihood maximizer as each type's replica count grows?
  • What guarantees remain when the accuracies are estimated from unlabeled questions rather than supplied?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is mass-based one-round answer aggregation. Consider a large annotation service that sends each unlabeled question to hundreds or thousands of independently sampled inference replicas drawn from a small menu of \(5\)–\(20\) fixed pipelines. A type \(t\) is a complete pipeline configuration—model checkpoint, prompt, decoding distribution and task stratum—so its replicas share a response law and accuracy \(x_t\). The society is their distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is type \(t\)’s share and \(\tau\ll n\). For one question, let \(\nu_t(s)\) be the mass of type \(t\) that answers label \(s\), with \(\sum_s\nu_t(s)=\mu_t\). The final decision is still one of the \(K\) labels; the continuous object is the answering population and its type-level answer masses.

That regime fits this paper better than a generic “LLM ensemble” story. The paper explicitly discusses repeated samples from one model as homogeneous agents, alongside heterogeneous agents. A small menu of replicated pipelines preserves the heterogeneity its results use while making \(n\gg\tau\). It also uses fractional answer masses only as the natural population accounting for those replicas; it does not make the answer space continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in the paper in Appendix E.3. Under the paper’s conditional-independence and random-shuffle assumptions, and when every agent’s accuracy satisfies \(x_i\ge 1/K\), ISP has at least as large an expected advantage for the true label as majority voting, which in turn has at least as large an expected advantage as surprising popularity. The theorem gives the exact gaps:
\[ \mathbb E[\mathrm{Adv}_{ISP}(s^\star)-\mathrm{Adv}_{MV}(s^\star)] = \frac{\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}(Kx_i-1)(Kx_j-1)^2} {(N-1)K(K-1)^3}, \]
and
\[ \mathbb E[\mathrm{Adv}_{MV}(s^\star)-\mathrm{Adv}_{SP}(s^\star)] = \frac{\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}(Kx_i-1)(Kx_j-1)^2} {(N-1)K(K-1)^2}. \]
These are expected *advantage* comparisons; they should not be inflated into a blanket theorem that ISP always has greater probability of choosing the correct answer.

Here is the continuous ISP problem I would hand to a prover. An instance gives \(K\) labels, a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, current answer masses \(\nu_t(s)\), and rational pairwise kernels \(p_{t,u}(s\mid a)\). The kernel is the paper’s second-order information: the probability that a type-\(t\) replica answers \(s\), conditional on a distinct type-\(u\) replica answering \(a\). The task is to return a label maximizing
\[ I(s)=\sum_t\nu_t(s) -\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{t,u}\mu_t \sum_{r\in S}\nu_u(r)\sum_{a\in S\setminus\{r\}}p_{t,u}(s\mid a). \]
The first term is the mass actually answering \(s\); the second is the continuous population version of ISP’s counterfactual correction. If \(x_t\ge 1/K\) for each positive-mass type and the paper’s shuffled, conditionally independent model holds, the theorem’s expected advantage gap carries over with the normalized pair sum replaced by
\[ \frac{1}{K(K-1)^3} \sum_{t,u}\mu_t\mu_u(Kx_t-1)(Kx_u-1)^2. \]
This is nonnegative. Given the kernels, computing all \(K\) scores takes polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(K\), and the input bit length. I would expect this mirror to be tractable. It generates follow-up questions about estimating the kernels from finitely many unlabeled questions, handling correlated replicas and heterogeneous question difficulty, and how performance changes as type masses or accuracy margins shrink.

A second, cleaner but less practical anchor is Theorem 3.1, also proved here, in Appendix D.1. Under the paper’s conditional-independence and shuffled-label assumptions, if the individual accuracies \(x_i\) are known, weighting each agent’s vote by \(\sigma_K^{-1}(x_i)\), where \(\sigma_K(z)=e^z/(K-1+e^z)\), gives a Bayesian-optimal aggregator among all aggregation rules for every admissible distribution \(P\). Its continuous problem takes \((\mu,\nu,x)\) and returns a label maximizing the per-capita log-likelihood rate
\[ \ell(s)=\sum_t\left[ \nu_t(s)\log x_t+ \bigl(\mu_t-\nu_t(s)\bigr) \log\frac{1-x_t}{K-1} \right]. \]
With a uniform prior induced by shuffling, this is the natural mass-valued likelihood objective; its label-dependent part is the weighted vote \(\sum_t\nu_t(s)\log\!\frac{(K-1)x_t}{1-x_t}\). I expect this mirror to be tractable as well, subject to the precision needed to compare scores. It covers the paper’s first-order aggregation result, but it needs known accuracies—the paper’s stated practical limitation—so ISP is the stronger lead.

The scope here is narrow: fixed finite-choice answers, one-round aggregation, and a large population of replicated agent types. It does not mirror multi-round debate, MoE expert selection, or open-ended text generation. Nor does it claim a hardness result has dissolved. The paper gives named theoretical results about aggregation quality and optimality, but no named NP-hardness, FPT, or other complexity-class theorem; the polynomial ISP score computation is a property of this continuous formulation.

The weakest point is the regime itself. The paper’s experiments use ensembles of four or eight distinct models, while thousands of replicas from a few pipelines may be costly and share question-specific errors. The paper’s idealized assumptions may therefore be least credible exactly where \(n\gg\tau\). Its extension to varying question difficulty offers some support, but does not remove the concern. Still, the high-multiplicity scenario is plausible for large-scale, unlabeled annotation with cheap inference replicas, and repeated sampling is already within the paper’s conception of an agent population. The mirror’s claim is modest: it is a population version of their one-round aggregation question, not a claim about every deployed ensemble.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed regime is plausible, but the two anchors do not open the kind of computational question ChoCo is looking for. For Theorem 4.1, the stronger mass-based ISP mirror is just a finite score calculation: given \(\mu_t\), answer masses and pairwise kernels, evaluate \(K\) scores in \(O(\tau^2 K^3)\) time and return the largest. The theorem’s expected-advantage comparison is a statistical guarantee, not a complexity result. Replacing finite sums by mass-weighted sums does not expose a search problem, a large implicit domain or a pricing problem. Estimating kernels from unlabeled questions could be interesting, but that is a sampling problem along the question axis; continuousizing the agents does not make it a new computational problem.

The proposed replica scenario also has a serious limit problem. If replicas of each pipeline answer independently as in the paper’s model, then with infinitely many replicas their answer masses converge to their response probabilities. Under the paper’s shuffled, symmetric-error assumptions, a type \(t\) of mass \(\mu_t\) and accuracy \(x_t\) then puts mass \(\mu_t x_t\) on the truth and \(\mu_t(1-x_t)/(K-1)\) on each wrong label. When the types are at least as accurate as random guessing and some are strictly better, the total truthful mass exceeds every wrong-label mass. The continuum limit therefore makes even majority voting identify the answer exactly; the finite-population uncertainty that motivates Theorem 4.1 disappears. If repeated copies instead share a deterministic response, they do not supply independent votes, and the theorem’s assumptions no longer justify the claimed advantage.

Theorem 3.1 has the same computational limitation. Its continuous likelihood rate is a sum over types, and its output is the maximum of \(K\) explicit scores. With known accuracies this is straightforward to compute; estimating unknown accuracies adds a calibration or statistical estimation task, not a difficult population-level optimization problem. Moving to an unrestricted continuous type space would require a new finite representation of the types and their parameters, with any difficulty coming from that representation rather than from the theorem’s aggregator.

So the negative case is strongest on fit: the paper gives named results about optimality and expected accuracy, but no complexity theorem, and the natural mass versions of both results remain explicit scoring rules. It is not airtight. The paper’s discussion of repeated sampling makes a high-multiplicity population of inference replicas a sensible object, and if ChoCo counts simple polynomial evaluators as worthwhile mirrors, Theorem 4.1 could still qualify. I would argue against prioritizing it, but I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror.
