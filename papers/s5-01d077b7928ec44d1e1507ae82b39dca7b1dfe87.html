The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of chore templates \(E\), complete cost types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_e\), and costs \(v_t(B)\) for each integral bundle \(B\subseteq E\), define \(M_t^\infty\) as the least \(b\) for which there is a distribution \(y_{t,B}\) over bundles with \(\sum_B y_{t,B}=1\), \(\sum_{B:e\in B}y_{t,B}=q_e\), and positive mass only on bundles with \(v_t(B)\le b\). Find the minimum \(\alpha\) and mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:e\in B}x_{t,B}=q_e\), and positive mass only where \(v_t(B)\le\alpha M_t^\infty\); each unit of mass receives an integral bundle.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete cost types. Chore templates have per-capita supplies \(q_e\), and decision variables \(x_{t,B}\) allocate type mass to integral bundles. With the type-specific benchmark distributions \(y_{t,B}\), setting \(x_{t,B}=\mu_t y_{t,B}\) gives an exact allocation with \(\alpha=1\).

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers subadditive MMS allocation, including Theorem 2’s multiplicative guarantee. It does not establish a computational hardness analogue for Theorem 1 or cover the ordinal, bin packing, job scheduling, PROP, or PROPX results.

Open questions for a prover

  • For succinct submodular cost representations, can the benchmark distributions be computed efficiently, or does their configuration pricing problem remain hard?
  • For finite populations and chore supplies scaled to the same proportions, what rounding guarantees preserve the continuous allocation’s fairness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(n\ge2\), there is an instance with \(n\) agents and \(m=n^n\) chores, each agent having submodular costs, in which every agent’s MMS is \(1\) but every allocation gives some agent cost \(n\). The construction labels chores by points \(x\in[n]^n\). Agent \(i\)’s cost for a bundle is the minimum number of planes \(x_i=\ell\) needed to cover its chores. A bundle of cost below \(n\) must omit at least one such plane; choosing one omitted plane per agent identifies a point that no agent receives. Since \(n\ge\log m/\log\log m\), this also gives the theorem’s stated lower bound against both approximation factors.

That is a strong fairness impossibility result, but it is not a conventional computational-hardness result: the proof shows that the required allocation does not exist, regardless of running time. The paper’s other named MMS and PROP results are likewise existence or nonexistence guarantees; its conclusion explicitly leaves polynomial-time algorithms open. So under the strict named-complexity-anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying anchor. If an approximation-guarantee impossibility is accepted as an anchor, Theorem 1 is the one I would choose.

Here is the continuous question I think best supports the positive case: Continuous-Type MMS Chore Allocation. Fix a finite set \(T\) of complete cost types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(E\) of chore templates, and rational per-capita supplies \(q_e\). A bundle is an integral set of chore copies. For each type \(t\), define its continuous MMS benchmark \(M_t^\infty\) as the least \(b\) for which there is a distribution \(y_{t,B}\) over integral bundles satisfying
\[ \sum_B y_{t,B}=1,\qquad \sum_{B:e\in B} y_{t,B}=q_e,\qquad y_{t,B}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ v_t(B)\le b. \]
The input also includes the type costs \(v_t\). The question is to find a mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) minimizing \(\alpha\), subject to
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B:e\in B}x_{t,B}=q_e,\qquad x_{t,B}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ v_t(B)\le\alpha M_t^\infty. \]
Each person represented by the mass assigned to \((t,B)\) receives the whole, indivisible bundle \(B\).

In the coordinate-plane construction, take \(\mu_i=1/n\), chore templates \(E=\{e_x:x\in[n]^n\}\), and supply \(q_{e_x}=1/n\) for every \(x\). For type \(i\), a uniform distribution over its \(n\) coordinate planes gives the required supply and has cost \(1\), so \(M_i^\infty=1\). Now assign mass \(1/n^2\) of type \(i\) to each plane \(C_{i\ell}=\{e_x:x_i=\ell\}\). Each type receives mass \(1/n\); each chore belongs to one plane for each of the \(n\) types, so its total assigned mass is \(1/n\). Every occupied bundle costs \(1\). Thus the continuous instance has an exact MMS allocation.

This is also realized by finite high-multiplicity instances. With \(K\) copies of each chore template and \(K\) agents of each type, where \(K\) is divisible by \(n\), split each type into \(n\) groups of \(K/n\) agents and give each group the chores in its corresponding plane. Each chore copy is allocated, and every agent’s bundle costs \(1\). The mirror therefore keeps each person’s bundle integral while using population mass to split a type across bundles. The per-capita chore supply is the necessary co-scaling that avoids a fixed finite chore set becoming negligible in a continuum population.

The predicted direction is Class A for the existence question: type-by-type MMS decompositions \(y_t\) immediately combine into a fair allocation by setting \(x_{t,B}=\mu_t y_{t,B}\). The computational search question is less settled. With an explicit table of bundle costs it is a finite configuration-LP problem; for succinct or value-oracle submodular costs, finding the decompositions may require a hard pricing problem. I would not claim a general polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\). Further questions are whether that pricing problem is tractable for natural submodular representations, and how closely finite instances with rational masses and supplies round to the continuous allocation.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s multiplicative MMS impossibility only; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s other results. Its weakest point is the change from a fixed finite instance to a per-capita, high-multiplicity regime whose allocation variables are masses over integral bundles. That fits the programme’s stated population-continuization model, but it is an extension of Theorem 1’s setting, and the source theorem itself does not establish the computational classification the programme seeks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is an allocation nonexistence result, not a computational complexity result. It proves that every allocation in a constructed instance gives some agent cost at least \(n\) times their MMS. It specifies no decision problem, cost representation for an algorithm, or running-time bound. Its wording about “no algorithm” follows from the fact that no qualifying allocation exists. That leaves no computational classification from Theorem 1 to carry into the continuous model.

The proposed mirror also has a structural degeneracy. For each type \(t\), take a distribution \(y_{t,B}\) attaining its benchmark \(M_t^\infty\), and set \(x_{t,B}=\mu_t y_{t,B}\). Then
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B:e\in B}x_{t,B} =\sum_t\mu_t q_e=q_e. \]
Every occupied bundle costs at most \(M_t^\infty\), so \(\alpha=1\) is feasible by construction. Conversely, any feasible allocation for a type of positive mass, divided by that mass, is a candidate distribution in the definition of \(M_t^\infty\). Thus the stated optimization has \(\alpha=1\) for every feasible instance. Population mass simply scales each type’s own benchmark decomposition.

Keeping the original discrete MMS benchmark does not fix this. For each agent type, choose an MMS partition of the chores and draw one of its \(n\) bundles uniformly. Each bundle remains integral, and each chore appears with probability \(1/n\). Weight these lotteries by type mass: every type receives its MMS cost bound, while each chore’s aggregate supply is respected. The coordinate-plane construction gives an explicit instance of this general convexification. The high-multiplicity interpretation is coherent; it also makes the proposed existence question immediate.

One could ask for an algorithm that computes the decompositions for succinct submodular cost representations. That requires specifying a representation or oracle model and analyzing its pricing problem. Theorem 1 supplies no such question or result, and its coordinate-plane decompositions are explicit. That would be a new computational project attached to this topic, rather than a mirror of this paper’s anchor. So the negative case is strong against the proposed mirror; the universal claim that no other worthwhile mirror could be formulated is harder to defend.
