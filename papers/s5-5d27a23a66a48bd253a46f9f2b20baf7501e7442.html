The strongest negative case is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Its numbered Results 1–4 report experimental differences in honesty and efficiency. They do not state the complexity of a defined computational problem. The Jackson–Sonnenschein mechanism is background from earlier work. So the proponent has no qualifying anchor.

The most promising repaired candidate is a large population of valuation types choosing partners, with a question about the existence or computation of a high-welfare equilibrium. But the paper’s central finding concerns how experimental subjects behave under different treatments. That behavior depends on beliefs, learning, coordination, perceived fairness, and partner histories. A distribution over preference types alone does not determine it. Adding strategies and histories to each type produces a dynamic matching game whose rules and equilibrium criterion would have to be designed from scratch; the paper provides no computational problem or result for that model to inherit.

One could instead scale up the linked-project mechanism and optimize partner assignments or budgets across a population. That could be a worthwhile high-multiplicity mechanism-design project. It would shift the question to welfare optimization in a new model, however, and leave the paper’s experimental contribution—whether social interaction changes behavior—unaddressed.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as written, though the universal claim is less secure: a new dynamic matching computation might be valuable in its own right. The paper simply gives no anchored computational result showing where continuous population analysis would do useful work.
