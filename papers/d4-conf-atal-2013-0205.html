What the mirror covers

No result is covered; Theorem 4 is the only candidate, while the paper’s equilibrium characterization and existence results lack a computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: large populations dividing a divisible resource, where many agents share the same valuation profile. A type would be a complete valuation density \(v_t\); \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the population with that density. Each agent’s action would remain a threshold strategy, and the individual objective would remain maximizing the value of the piece received. The hoped-for output is an equilibrium and its allocation.

That regime is plausible in settings such as allocating a long strip of land among many households whose location preferences fall into a small number of profiles: \(N\) is large while the number of types \(\tau\) is comparatively small. This would continuize the population; the cake itself is already continuous, which does not count as the programme’s move.

But I cannot give this case a qualifying anchor. The paper has numbered equilibrium results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest result is Theorem 4, proved here: for strictly positive value densities and every tie-breaking rule, the moving knife game has a pure \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium whose allocation uses the whole cake and is \(\varepsilon\)-envy-free. That is an existence result, not an algorithm or complexity classification. Under your anchor rule, it cannot support a per-anchor continuous problem.

A possible follow-on would take rational masses over finitely represented valuation densities and ask for an approximate equilibrium of a continuum-population moving knife game, with a type-conditional policy and an allocation as the solution. But the paper’s mechanism awards a whole interval to one named agent in each of \(n\) rounds. In an atomless population, the analogue of a named recipient, the tie-breaking rule, and an individual’s payoff all need new definitions. Until those are specified, this is not a prover-ready mirror the authors could be asked to solve.

That is the weak point, and it is substantial: this paper studies equilibrium and fairness, not computational complexity, while a continuum-agent version would naturally lead toward analytic mean-field game questions. The positive case is that a high-multiplicity population of shared valuation types is imaginable and preserves the strategic question. It does not yet meet the programme’s computational gate or its requirement for a precise anchored problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is at the anchor gate: this paper states no computational complexity result. Its numbered theorems establish equilibrium properties—Theorem 2 characterizes pure equilibria, Theorem 3 constructs one for a suitable tie-breaking rule, and Theorem 4 proves existence of an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium with an \(\varepsilon\)-envy-free allocation. None gives an algorithm, runtime, or complexity classification. Theorem 4 is the proponent’s closest candidate, but it is an existence theorem, not a computational result. Theorem 2 or 3 would have the same problem as anchors.

The most promising replacement is a large population with finitely many valuation-density types, perhaps households dividing land. One could ask for an approximate equilibrium of an atomless moving-knife game. That is a plausible new research question, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational problem this paper studies. To make the limit meaningful, the model needs a population-scale account of how intervals are assigned and how individuals’ incentives work: the paper’s game gives each named agent one piece over \(n\) rounds, while an aggregate mass distribution does not record which infinitesimal recipient gets which piece. A queue or rank coordinate, payoff normalization, and a corresponding strategy space could supply that account, but they would define a new game. The paper’s tie-independent approximate-existence result makes the best version less dependent on priority rules; it does not supply the missing computational question.

So the positive scenario is credible as motivation, and I would not claim no useful continuum model could ever be built from it. The negative case is that such a model would launch a separate computational mean-field game project, rather than continuize a named computational result of this paper. Under the programme’s stated anchor requirement, that is decisive.
