What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror restates Theorem 5’s probability-ratio guarantee, with Corollary 7 as a consequence; it leaves out Theorems 8 and 9 and the technical lemmas.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow: a continuous population can be attached to the paper’s geography without changing what a plan is. Take a large redistricting region in which many residents live in each census block and blocks remain indivisible for districting. A type is a block location, including its place in the adjacency graph; its mass is the fraction of residents living there. Residents of one type receive the same district assignment. With equal mass at every block, a plan of \(m\) connected districts, each with mass \(1/m\), is exactly the paper’s equal-size partition. The regime is many residents and fewer block types. It is a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation, though it does not reduce the number of geographic types.

The paper’s strongest possible anchor is Theorem 5, proved there. It says: for positive integers \(k_1,k_2\), \(\alpha\geq 1\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), define
\[ \lambda=\frac{\log(1/(2k_2))-\log(\alpha)}{\log(1-1/k_1)}+\varepsilon. \]
For any two \(m\)-partitions \(\mathcal P_1,\mathcal P_2\) of a \((k_1,k_2)\)-bounded graph, if \(\lvert\partial\mathcal P_2\rvert\geq\lambda\lvert\partial\mathcal P_1\rvert\) and \(\lvert\partial\mathcal P_1\rvert\geq(m-1)/\varepsilon\), then \(\Pr_{\dist}[\mathcal P_1]\geq\alpha\Pr_{\dist}[\mathcal P_2]\), where \(\dist\) is the spanning-tree distribution. This is a sampling-distribution guarantee, not a named result that places a problem in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous ReCom Compactness Comparison. An instance consists of a connected planar embedded graph \(G=(V,E)\) with the paper’s degree bounds, a fixed \(m\), uniform rational masses \(\mu_v=1/\lvert V\rvert\) on its block-location types, and two feasible plans \(\mathcal P_1,\mathcal P_2\), each partitioning the types into \(m\) connected districts of mass \(1/m\). Give a plan probability proportional to \(\prod_{D\in\mathcal P}\operatorname{trees}(G[D])\). The question is to compare their probabilities, or certify a lower bound on their ratio. Theorem 5 answers this mirror directly on the uniform-mass subclass: its perimeter conditions guarantee a ratio of at least \(\alpha\). For the exact comparison of two supplied plans, I expect tractability: the common normalization cancels, and the spanning-tree counts can be computed from Laplacian minors. That tractability observation is not a complexity result proved by this paper.

The scope is just that compactness-bias guarantee for equal-mass blocks and bounded-degree planar graphs. A natural further question is whether a comparable guarantee holds with unequal rational block masses and districts balanced by population; Theorem 5 does not establish it. Another is the probability of drawing *any* plan below a perimeter threshold, which the paper itself says needs bounds on how many plans of each compactness exist.

The weakest point is that the population continuum sits inside fixed geographic atoms: the paper’s distribution and theorem depend on the block graph, not on how many residents are aggregated at each block. So this is a credible continuous-population interpretation of the paper’s model, but a weak ChoCo complexity anchor. Under a strict reading that requires a named complexity result, the paper has no qualifying anchor; Theorem 5 is the closest honest candidate because it gives a named guarantee about an algorithmic sampling distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named complexity result here. Theorem 5 is a structural bound on a sampling distribution; Theorems 8 and 9 show where that bound fails, and the lemmas supply the spanning-tree analysis. The most plausible anchor is therefore Theorem 5, as the other analyst says, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem.

Their block-location model is a sensible high-multiplicity story: many residents can share a location and receive the same district assignment. But in the paper’s equal-block setting, changing the number of residents at each location changes nothing. Feasible plans, perimeters, and spanning-tree weights all remain functions of the finite graph. For two supplied plans, their probability ratio is just the ratio of their spanning-tree scores; those scores can be computed from Laplacian minors. So the proposed exact-comparison question is already tractable on the original graph, and the proposed guarantee is Theorem 5 restated with population labels.

Unequal rational block masses are the strongest improved mirror. Requiring each district to contain equal mass makes those masses matter to which plans are feasible. Yet the proof of Theorem 5 uses the graph, the number of districts, and the cut boundaries; it does not use district sizes. Its bound therefore carries over to any mass-balanced plans that are feasible on that graph. The masses filter the plans but add no new computation to the theorem. An optimization problem over mass-balanced maps might be interesting, but it would be a weighted graph-partitioning problem, not a complexity question created by treating the society as continuous.

A spatial continuum gives a more substantial change, but it pulls away from the paper’s result. Keeping a finite graph returns to the same fixed location types; replacing the graph with a continuous region requires a new input model and a new analogue of the spanning-tree distribution. That may be a worthwhile project in its own right, but it is not a clear mirror of this theorem.

So the negative case rests on a narrow gap: the paper’s named result is computationally motivated, but population mass does not drive its computation. I cannot rule out a useful weighted-redistricting problem, and the case is weakest there. What I do not see is a continuous-population complexity question that this paper itself anchors.
