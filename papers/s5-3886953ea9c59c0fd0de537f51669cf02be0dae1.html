The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets the required anchor test: the paper contains no numbered result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally hard or tractable.

Theorem 1 proves that every anchor-proof voting rule is constant; Theorems 3–5 and Propositions 7–8 characterize when manipulation is possible under specified information. These are substantive results, but they do not analyze the complexity of deciding or finding an outcome.

A continuous-population version of the manipulation problem might be worth posing—for example, with voter types consisting of a complete ranking and acceptability threshold, and mass recording each type’s population share. But that would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor, and I agree that none survives a close reading. The numbered results are not complexity results: Theorem 1 characterizes anchor-proof rules; Propositions 3–6 characterize profiles; and Theorems 3–5 and Propositions 7–8 establish when strategic manipulation is possible under different information assumptions. None defines an algorithmic problem or studies its complexity. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, that is the decisive objection.

The strongest candidate mirror would represent each voter type by a complete ranking and acceptability threshold, with a rational mass for each type. That is a plausible high-multiplicity model for a large participatory-budgeting platform. One could then ask whether AV is anchor-proof for a given distribution. But that translates a characterization in the paper; it does not mirror a computational result. A more ambitious question—whether a planner can choose a safe, beneficial presentation policy—would require specifying the allowed policies, information about the distribution, and the computational task. That may be a worthwhile new project, but the paper’s results do not supply the problem whose complexity the continuization programme would chart.

The negative case is strong on programme fit, but limited as a claim that no worthwhile continuous research could arise. A large population with repeated preference-threshold types is sensible, and the paper’s anonymous voting outcomes do not depend on individual identity. I would not argue that the population cannot be continuized. I would argue that any computational question built from this paper would need to be added, rather than mirrored.
