The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that Fang’s school choice model has a natural continuous-population analogue. But it has no named computational complexity result to anchor that case to. Theorems 1 and 2 are proved in the paper; neither gives an algorithm or classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

Theorem 1 says that if score partition \(Q\) refines \(P\), then \(GZ(Q)\) is more stable than \(GZ(P)\): whenever \(GZ(P)\) produces a stable matching, \(GZ(Q)\) does too, and there is a problem where \(GZ(Q)\) is stable but \(GZ(P)\) is not. Theorem 2 says that \(GZ(P)\) is more manipulable than \(GZ(Q)\): whenever \(GZ(Q)\) is manipulable, \(GZ(P)\) is too, and there is a problem where \(GZ(P)\) is manipulable but \(GZ(Q)\) is not. Both are mechanism-property results, not complexity results. Theorem 3 likewise says that \(GZ(P)\) may assign fewer students to their first choices than serial dictatorship. None qualifies as a computational anchor under your criterion.

There is nevertheless a plausible mirror. Take a large school-admission market with finitely many schools and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\). A type records a student’s complete school ranking and score priority \(u\in[0,1]\); the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types. An ordered partition of the score interval gives the rounds. In each round, the mechanism runs immediate acceptance on the mass in that score band, with each school taking the highest-priority applicant mass up to its remaining capacity. This preserves the paper’s central operation: changing which score groups enter together. It does not replace the mechanism with a divisible-outcome or lottery model.

That is plausible for a city admitting millions of students, where many students share ranking patterns and the score distribution is represented by a much smaller number of preference-and-score segments. The continuous score coordinate is needed to preserve the strict priority ordering. It also means this is an extension beyond the paper’s finite-agent model and beyond the programme’s usual finite type set.

If Theorem 1 were allowed as a substantive, though non-computational, anchor, the corresponding question could be stated as Continuous GZ Refinement-Stability. Its input is a finite school set, rational per-capita capacities, a rational piecewise-described distribution \(\mu\), and score partitions \(P,Q\) with \(Q\) refining \(P\). Its solution is the mass allocation under each partition and a stability verdict for each. Stability means individual rationality and no positive mass of students who prefer a school where they have priority over assigned mass or available capacity. The global question is whether stability under \(P\) always implies stability under \(Q\), and whether a strict witness exists.

The analogous question for Theorem 2 is Continuous GZ Refinement-Manipulation: on the same input, determine whether either mechanism admits a profitable unilateral report, and whether manipulation under \(Q\) implies manipulation under \(P\). A solution is an agent’s score and true ranking, together with a profitable reported ranking, or a certificate that none exists. Since one agent has zero mass, this uses pointwise assignment semantics: the deviator’s outcome is evaluated against the fixed aggregate distribution, without changing other agents’ assignments.

For explicitly represented piecewise-rational inputs, computing the allocation and checking stability look plausibly tractable. I would not assign a class to the manipulation problem from this paper: its report search and the continuum’s pointwise incentives need their own analysis. Nor do Theorems 1 and 2 establish that either continuous refinement claim survives. Their finite proofs use a highest-priority student among those whose assignments differ and, for manipulation, a finite chain of students; those arguments do not directly carry over to an atomless population.

That is the soft spot in the positive case. The authors would likely recognize the score-band population model as their mechanism at high multiplicity, but whether its stability and manipulation comparisons persist is open, and this paper gives no complexity result to mirror. So the mirror is credible as a new computational question prompted by their results, not as a continuization of a named tractability or hardness theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the paper’s actual contribution: none of Theorems 1–3 states a computational problem or classifies its complexity. Theorems 1 and 2 compare stability and manipulability across mechanisms; Theorem 3 gives a possible first-choice outcome. They are results about mechanism properties. A continuous version of those claims could be interesting, but that alone would not fill ChoCo’s gap in computational complexity.

The best version of the Theorem 1 mirror would compare stability under two refined score partitions for a school-admission population distribution. But there is a mismatch between the theorem and the proposed computational task. For a particular distribution, computing both allocations and checking stability is mechanism evaluation. The theorem’s claim is instead a universal comparison across problems. To turn that into a computational problem, one would need to define a new search or decision task—such as finding a counterexample within a bounded, encoded family of distributions. That might be worth studying, but it is not a complexity question supplied by this paper’s result.

Theorem 2 has a harder modeling mismatch. The paper asks whether *some student* can profit by changing their report. In an atomless society, an individual has zero mass and does not appear in the distribution as a positive-mass type. The proponent’s fixed-aggregate, pointwise-deviation semantics can give such a person an outcome, but it adds an individual at a particular score to the aggregate model. Alternatively, one could ask whether a positive mass of students can manipulate. That is a sensible population-level question, but it changes the theorem’s existential individual criterion. Either way, the continuous question is a new incentive concept, not a direct computational mirror of Theorem 2.

The score priority also complicates the high-multiplicity story. A student’s complete type must include their score rank because it affects school admission. With a continuous score coordinate, almost every exact score type has zero mass; compressing scores into preference types would lose priority information. A nonatomic matching model can represent that structure, but doing so moves away from the programme’s finite support of interchangeable types. This is not a proof that the model is invalid; it makes the proposed mirror less directly about the high-multiplicity relaxation ChoCo is designed to study.

Theorem 3 does not rescue the case. Its natural continuous counterpart compares the mass assigned to first choices under GZ and serial dictatorship. That is a coherent allocation comparison, but calculating the shares is still mechanism evaluation, not a complexity result from the paper.

So the defensible negative case is narrower than “no scenario could make a continuous school-admission model worthwhile.” A large admission market is plainly a plausible continuum setting, and high-multiplicity school choice supports that regime rather than undermining it. I cannot honestly rule out worthwhile continuous work here. What fails is the stronger claim that this paper offers a computational result to continuize: the proposed stability and manipulation questions require new computational targets and, for manipulation, new semantics. They may belong in a separate matching-theory project, but this paper is a weak anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme.
