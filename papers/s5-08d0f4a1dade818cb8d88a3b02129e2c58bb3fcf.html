What the mirror covers

Theorems 5 and 6 concern asymptotic success and sufficient finite sample sizes; Theorem 7 concerns failure of monotonicity. The proposed exact deployment-sizing task is a new finite-population optimization question, not a computational mirror of those results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case for a continuous-population *extension*, but this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor under your rule. Theorem 5, proved here, says that for fixed \(m\ge2\) and fixed reliability margin \(\Delta p>0\), approval voting’s worst-case success probability tends to \(1\) as the number of independent agents tends to infinity. Theorem 6, also proved here, gives a sufficient population size: with \(Q=2(m-1)/(1-P_{\min})\), success probability is at least \(P_{\min}\) whenever
\[ n\ge \min\!\left(\frac{2}{\Delta p^2}\ln Q,\;1+\left(\frac{1}{\Delta p^2}-1\right)Q\right). \]
Theorem 7, proved here, says that for any \(k\) and \(0<\Delta p<1\), there are larger and smaller complete two-choice electorates, both above \(k\), for which the larger one has lower success probability. These are probability and sample-size results, not results classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 1–4 are cited prior results and likewise are not computational-complexity results.

The strongest favorable regime is standardized sensor networks of the kind the paper itself discusses: many motes, but relatively few hardware or deployment profiles. Let a type \(t\) specify, for each possible true state \(\omega\), a rational distribution \(\pi_t^\omega\) over the approval subsets \(S\subseteq W\). This captures the mote’s full conditional voting behavior. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of motes of type \(t\), with rational masses summing to \(1\). A deployment could have millions of motes but only a small support \(\tau\) of common profiles. Conditional on the true state, agents’ votes are independent, as in the paper.

A concrete downstream question is Minimum Reliable Deployment: given \(W\), the finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), the laws \(\pi_t^\omega\), and a target \(P_{\min}\), find the least population scale \(q\) such that the deployment with \(q\mu_t\) motes of each type has probability at least \(P_{\min}\) of making the true state the strict approval winner, for every possible true state. Require \(q\mu_t\) to be integral. A solution is that least \(q\), or a report that none exists. The decision variable is deployment scale; the objective is to minimize it subject to the paper’s truth-tracking success criterion. Theorem 5 supports eventual attainment under the independence and positive group-reliability assumptions; Theorem 6 supplies a distribution-free sufficient scale from \(m,\Delta p,P_{\min}\).

This is a credible high-multiplicity scenario, and it keeps the population as the continuous object: alternatives and approval sets remain finite, and probabilities describe agents’ behavior. The paper’s practical question—how many motes are needed for a reliability guarantee—makes the extension recognizable to its authors. But exact computation of the least scale is a new computational question; the paper’s explicit sufficient bound does not classify that problem or show it is hard. Theorem 7 also warns against assuming success probability improves monotonically with population size when the agent mix varies, so “least scale meeting the target” should not be replaced by an unexamined monotone search.

That is the weak point, and it is decisive for the requested anchors: this candidate may be worth studying, but it is not anchored by a named computational result in the paper. I would not present Theorems 5–7 as if they were complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is an asymptotic probability result, Theorem 6 gives sufficient sample-size bounds, and Theorem 7 disproves monotonicity across certain electorates. Theorem 1–4 are also probability results, not complexity classifications. So there is no computational result in the paper to mirror.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is the exact deployment-sizing problem: given a fixed type mixture \(\mu\), each type’s voting laws, and a target \(P_{\min}\), find the smallest \(q\) for which every true state is the strict approval winner with the required probability. That is a coherent high-multiplicity planning problem, but its operative population is still \(q\mu_t\) individual random trials: the requirement that \(q\mu_t\) be integral makes \(q\) discrete, and the probability depends on finite-sample fluctuations. It is an adjacent computational question, not a computation on a continuous society.

If the population itself becomes nonatomic, aggregate approval mass is its expected approval rate. With a positive reliability margin, the true state then wins deterministically under the natural aggregate-mass interpretation; the finite-population tail probabilities and sample-size question disappear. Keeping those fluctuations requires keeping a finite population scale. Theorem 7 also does not support the fixed-mixture sizing problem: its comparison changes the voter distributions as well as the population size.

The sensor scenario is credible for high multiplicity, so I would not claim it makes the adjacent planning question worthless. The negative case is narrower: it does not supply a continuous-population computational mirror of a result in this paper, and the exact sizing proposal does not clear that bar.
