What the mirror covers

The proposed hiring-market question concerns equilibrium welfare; it leaves the appendix bid-threshold procedure, externality-auction equilibrium existence, and matroid \mathrm{VCG} implementation without a continuous-population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a plausible research direction; this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its closest results are about equilibrium existence and welfare: the unit-demand theorem in §3.2 says pure subgame-perfect equilibria of sequential first-price item auctions have price of anarchy at most \(2\), and mixed equilibria at most \(4\); the theorem in §3.3 says the price of anarchy is unbounded for submodular bidders. The matroid theorem says subgame-perfect equilibria in undominated strategies reproduce the VCG allocation and prices. None classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The appendix does give an algorithm for characterizing bids that survive iterated elimination, but it makes no complexity claim. I would not present any of these as the required anchor.

A plausible population regime would be a large hiring market with many interchangeable applicants, finitely many unit-demand valuation types over job categories, and job-slot supply that grows with the applicant population. One possible mirror would take the type masses, valuations, slot supplies, auction order, and a specified tie-rationing rule as input; strategies would be type-contingent bids depending on public history; and the question would be whether there is a subgame-perfect equilibrium whose welfare falls below a given fraction of the optimal matching welfare. This preserves the paper’s sequential-auction welfare question, and a large market with repeated valuation types is a credible high-multiplicity setting.

That candidate is not anchored to a named computational result in this paper, so I would not assign it a Class A/B/C prediction as though the paper had established one. Its main weakness is that atomless agents cannot change aggregate future outcomes by deviating; preserving the paper’s strategic externalities may therefore require a substantial change to the market model. The continuous welfare question may be worth studying, but it does not turn one of this paper’s results into the requested computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper supplies no named computational result to continuize. Its central theorems concern equilibrium existence, welfare loss, and VCG implementation: for example, the unit-demand \(\mathrm{PoA}\) bound, the unbounded submodular \(\mathrm{PoA}\), and the matroid-auction equivalence. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The appendix’s threshold theorem is the closest candidate: it characterizes bids surviving iterated elimination using an overbidding graph, but makes no complexity claim. Turning these results into “is there a low-welfare equilibrium?” or “what is the worst equilibrium welfare?” would pose new computational questions, not mirror a computational result of this paper.

The hiring-market example gives a sensible high-multiplicity story, but preserving the paper’s central mechanism is difficult. Its sequential externalities arise because an individual winner changes later bidders’ incentives and the continuation outcome. With per-capita slot supplies and atomless applicants, one person’s deviation changes no aggregate state. A better model can preserve fractional type-level allocations and specify tie rationing, but that produces a nonatomic market game with a different equilibrium concept. If instead a whole type cohort acts as one strategic bidder so its choices can change later outcomes, the cohort is a finite player, and the game is no longer the individual-agent continuum the proposed mirror claims to study. The mirror of the unit-demand welfare theorem thus either loses the pivotal deviations behind the result or changes who the players are.

The submodular lower bound has the same problem in a sharper form. Its construction uses a small set of named bidders and makes a later equilibrium depend on whether one of them won any early item. In an atomless version, a single applicant cannot trigger that branch. Making the trigger a positive-mass deviation requires a coordinated cohort action, again changing the strategic unit. A continuum analogue might still be worth studying, but its answer would concern a newly specified nonatomic or bloc game, not the paper’s submodular \(\mathrm{PoA}\) result.

The matroid theorem is an even poorer anchor: bidders correspond to distinct ground-set elements, and which elements can be exchanged depends on their positions in the matroid. Equal costs do not make two elements the same type. Aggregation would require strong structural symmetry, and the resulting question would still concern equilibrium implementation, not computational complexity. I would therefore reject all three as anchors and regard the proposed welfare-threshold problem as a separate research direction. The universal negative is not airtight: a well-defined complexity study of sequential-auction equilibria with type masses could be worthwhile. The honest case against is narrower: this paper does not establish a computational result for that programme, and the most natural continuous versions alter the strategic mechanism that its results analyze.
