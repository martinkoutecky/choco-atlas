The continuous mirror question

Given a finite result set \(Q\) represented by assignments on \(V\), and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over cuc-acyclic CP-theories \(\Gamma_t\) on \(V\), return for every pair \(\{\alpha,\beta\}\subseteq Q\) the exact masses of types for which \(\alpha\gg_{\Gamma_t}\beta\), for which \(\beta\gg_{\Gamma_t}\alpha\), and for which neither comparison holds. The three masses sum to \(1\); the output selects no collective ranking.

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete cuc-acyclic CP-theory over the shared query attributes, with rational mass \(\mu_t\). The decision for each outcome pair is its comparison status under that type’s \(\gg_{\Gamma_t}\) relation; the objective is to report the resulting mass partition, with no optimization.

The objection that survived

The paper ranks results for an individual and does not define a cohort-level summary; reporting pairwise comparison masses extends its task, though it does not impose a common ranking.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s pairwise outcome comparison over a distribution of preference types. It leaves the paper’s RDF encoding, SPARQL deployment, experiments, and preference elicitation outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a Mass-CP Majority Comparison for a large recommender service. Suppose millions of users receive the same query over the same catalogue. A user’s type is their complete CP-theory over the catalogue’s attributes; users with the same theory are indistinguishable for this task. If \(N\) users fall into \(\tau\) such types, the continuous instance replaces their counts by rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\). For example, a service might have millions of users but only hundreds of preference types. The catalogue and its outcomes remain discrete: it is the population that becomes continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, which the paper attributes to [44] rather than proving here. For a cuc-acyclic CP-theory \(\Gamma\), it defines a comparison \(\alpha \gg_\Gamma \beta\) between distinct outcomes using the variables in \(\Delta(\alpha,\beta)\) that are undominated in the transitive closure of \(J_\alpha(\Gamma)\). It compares \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) by their local preference order on every such variable. The theorem says this relation is a strict partial order extending \(>_\Gamma\), and that comparing any pair of outcomes takes polynomial time. This is the paper’s computational core for ordering results. Theorem 1 gives a related polynomial comparison for fully acyclic CP-theories, but Theorem 2 covers the broader cuc-acyclic case, so I would not add the narrower result as a second anchor. The paper has no comparable numbered hardness result to anchor; its hardness statements about consistency are unnumbered or cited from elsewhere.

Mass-CP Majority Comparison is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance consists of a finite attribute set \(V\), a finite catalogue \(Q\) of candidate outcomes (the results of a shared query, represented by their assignments on \(V\)), and \(\tau\) cuc-acyclic CP-theories \(\Gamma_1,\ldots,\Gamma_\tau\) over \(V\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t\). For each distinct pair \(\alpha,\beta\in Q\), compute
\[ w(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{t:\,\alpha\gg_{\Gamma_t}\beta}\mu_t. \]
Return these exact support weights and the directed majority graph, with an edge \(\alpha\to\beta\) exactly when \(w(\alpha,\beta)>\tfrac12\). The support weights are the solution; this anchor has no optimization objective. In a discrete cohort, \(n_t\) users of type \(t\) correspond to mass \(\mu_t=n_t/N\).

The authors could recognize this as a population-level extension of the comparison their paper uses to order query results: each user type is evaluated by the same CP-theory relation, and the output summarizes how much of the audience supports each comparison. Given the cuc-acyclicity promise, Theorem 2 lets us compute the weights with \(\tau |Q|(|Q|-1)\) polynomial-time comparisons, plus exact rational summation. I expect this mirror to be tractable. It is Class A through the theorem’s comparison algorithm, though it does not show that population continuization dissolves any hardness: the anchored task is already in P.

The mirror covers the paper’s outcome-comparison step, not its full SPARQL deployment, ontology, experiments, or preference elicitation. The weakest point is that the paper ranks results for an individual, whereas this problem aggregates users into a majority graph. Also, \(\gg_\Gamma\) is a sound partial-order extension of the stated preferences, not a claim that every comparison it settles is entailed by \(>_\Gamma\); incomparable pairs are not counted as indifference. Majority edges can cycle, so a single group top-\(k\) list would require an additional aggregation rule. Those are real gaps, but they leave a precise and computable continuous-population version of the paper’s core comparison problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and I would not dismiss it as unnamed or irrelevant: it gives a polynomial comparison for outcomes under a cuc-acyclic CP-theory. A cohort with repeated preference profiles, a shared query, and rational masses is also a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I would not argue that users can never share a type.

The difficulty is that the proposed majority graph changes the paper’s task. The paper ranks query results for an individual using that person’s preferences and hard constraints. If we preserve that task for a population, we can produce one ranking per preference type and attach each type’s mass. Asking what fraction of the population prefers one outcome to another, or whether a majority prefers it, adds a collective aggregation rule. Theorem 2 supplies individual comparisons; it gives no reason to choose majority over expected rank, top-\(k\) coverage, or another group objective. Partial comparisons and cycles make that choice consequential.

A stronger cohort recommendation mirror—say, finding the list that best serves a specified fraction of users—could make population mass central. It still needs a group objective the paper does not define. That is a legitimate new research direction, but it is not clearly a continuous mirror of the paper’s computational question. In the paper’s own service model, each user asks their own query and gets results ordered by their own preferences; cohort-level analytics requires a different system goal.

This negative case is weak at the universal level. A recommendation platform that deliberately serves a shared cohort could make the proposed aggregation worthwhile, and its authors might recognize it as a natural extension. The strongest objection is that the population-level objective must be supplied from outside the paper, not that the cohort or its computation is incoherent.
