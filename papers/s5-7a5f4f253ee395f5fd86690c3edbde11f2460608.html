The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(M\), supported types \(t=(i,S_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), common values \(v_k\), and a feasible initial mass assignment \(b_{t,k}\) to accessible schools \(k\in S_t\), set \(c_k=\sum_t b_{t,k}\). Find an assignment \(x_{t,k}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\), \(x_{t,k}=0\) for inaccessible schools, and \(\sum_t x_{t,k}=c_k\), such that no group \(i\) has a feasible exact-capacity witness assignment \(y\) with \(\sum_{t:g(t)=i} y_{t,k}\le\sum_{t:g(t)=j} x_{t,k}\) for every school \(k\) and \(U_i(y)>U_i(x)\), where \(U_i(z)=\sum_{t:g(t)=i}\sum_k v_k z_{t,k}\).

The model it lives in

A rational mass-allocation model over types \(t=(i,S_t)\), where each type records its group and accessible schools; the initial assignment \(b\) determines per-capita capacities \(c_k\), the decision variables are assignment masses \(x_{t,k}\), and the task is to construct an envy-free exact-capacity assignment.

The objection that survived

The one-seat tolerance vanishes after normalization, and the exact-capacity problem loses the theorem's discrete balancing step; the proponent acknowledges this but does not show that the simpler flow problem yields much new computational insight.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is Theorem 1, the paper’s main result, proved here: for every school-redistricting instance with two groups, a 1-relaxed envy-free allocation exists and can be constructed in polynomial time. The paper gives no comparable named hardness result for its problem, so this algorithmic theorem is the right anchor.

A natural high-multiplicity regime is a large school district with many students but relatively few distinct combinations of demographic group and reachable schools. Define a type \(t\) by a group \(g(t)\in\{1,2\}\) and an accessible-school set \(S_t\subseteq M\). The population is a rational distribution \(\mu_t\) over the supported types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type records everything this paper’s model uses to determine an agent’s feasible schools and group membership; the school values \(v_k\) are common to everyone, as in the paper. Think of many students per supported type, so the number of students is much larger than the support size \(\tau\).

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Two-Group Exact-Capacity School Redistricting. The input is \(M\), the supported types and their masses \(\mu_t\), common school values \(v_k\), and a feasible initial mass assignment \(b_{t,k}\), where \(b_{t,k}=0\) for \(k\notin S_t\) and \(\sum_k b_{t,k}=\mu_t\). This initial assignment sets the per-capita capacity \(c_k=\sum_t b_{t,k}\) of each school. Find a mass assignment \(x_{t,k}\geq 0\), with \(x_{t,k}=0\) for inaccessible schools, such that
\[ \sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,k}=c_k, \]
and no group envies the other. Define \(U_i(x)=\sum_{t:g(t)=i}\sum_k v_kx_{t,k}\). Group \(i\) envies group \(j\) at \(x\) if there is another feasible exact-capacity assignment \(y\) such that, for every school \(k\),
\[ \sum_{t:g(t)=i}y_{t,k}\leq \sum_{t:g(t)=j}x_{t,k}, \]
while \(U_i(y)>U_i(x)\). A solution is any feasible \(x\) for which no ordered pair of groups has such a witness. The masses, values, and initial assignment can be given as binary rationals; the relevant input measures are \(m,\tau\), and their encoding length \(L\).

This keeps the paper’s redistricting question intact: students move only to accessible schools, capacities come from a tentative initial assignment, and envy still requires a feasible alternative whose seats for one group fit within the other group’s school-by-school allocation. The continuous variable \(x_{t,k}\) means a fraction of a type is sent to a school; each infinitesimal student still attends one school. The co-modification is capacity: after normalizing a district of \(N\) students to mass one, the paper’s one-student-per-school allowance is \(1/N\). Exact per-capita capacities are its natural limit. And because an exact-capacity allocation has zero capacity deviation, the paper’s condition on an envy witness reduces to requiring that witness to preserve capacities exactly.

I expect this problem to be tractable. If the two groups have different masses, maximize the smaller group’s utility subject to the type supplies, accessibility constraints, and exact school capacities. This is a rational min-cost flow; the larger group cannot envy the smaller one because the school-by-school containment condition would imply its total mass is no greater. The smaller group cannot envy the chosen allocation because it already maximizes its utility among exact-capacity assignments. If the groups have equal mass, test whether a feasible assignment exists that swaps the two groups’ school-by-school masses in the initial assignment \(b\). If none exists, \(b\) is envy-free: any envy witness would have to fit within the other group’s mass at every school, and equality of total group masses forces equality at every school, giving precisely such a swap. If a swapped assignment exists, average it with \(b\). The resulting allocation gives the groups equal mass at every school and hence equal utility; any proposed envy witness must match those school-by-school masses exactly, so it cannot strictly improve utility. These checks and constructions are network-flow computations with rational capacities, polynomial in the explicit input size.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous analogue of their two-group result: it retains their accessibility graph, group-based envy, initial capacities, and construction objective. It covers Theorem 1’s setting, not the paper’s broader cited work or its \(g\geq3\) discussion. The main limitation is also the central difference: the discrete theorem handles indivisible student assignments and allows a one-seat deviation, while this mirror removes individual indivisibility and asks for exact capacities. Its tractability may therefore be a consequence of taking the limit, rather than a continuation of the paper’s difficult combinatorial argument.

Useful next questions would be whether one can round continuous envy-free assignments to finite assignments with the paper’s per-school one-seat guarantee; whether exact-capacity envy-free allocations still exist with group-specific school values; and what changes for three or more groups. The first is the closest bridge back to the paper’s theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 1, and it is a genuine computational result: a 1-relaxed envy-free allocation can be found in polynomial time. A large district with many students sharing a group and an accessible-school set is also a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Those features make it hard to dismiss the mirror on grounds of scope or modeling.

The strongest case against it is that the theorem’s distinctive allowance of one seat per school has no positive scale in a population limit. With \(N\) students, that allowance is \(1/N\) of the population, so it vanishes. The proposed exact-capacity mirror is natural, but its fairness question simplifies sharply. With unequal group masses, maximizing the smaller group’s utility over exact-capacity assignments gives the result. With equal masses, either the initial assignment has no feasible swapped group profile and is already envy-free, or averaging it with a feasible swap balances each group’s school masses and rules out strict envy. This is a rational flow computation; the theorem’s discrete one-seat adjustment has disappeared.

Keeping a fixed tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\) would preserve a nonzero capacity deviation, but it permits \(\varepsilon N\) seats and so does not represent the paper’s one-seat guarantee. The case against, then, is that the exact-limit question may add little computational content beyond a standard flow argument, while a fixed-error version lacks the theorem’s scale. That is a concern about the mirror’s research value, not a modeling defect.

I don’t think that concern defeats the anchor under ChoCo’s stated standard. The continuous question is well-posed, its population types are credible, and the programme treats tractability as a worthwhile outcome even when the algorithm is simple. The honest negative case is weak: the mirror may offer limited novelty, but I cannot justify claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
