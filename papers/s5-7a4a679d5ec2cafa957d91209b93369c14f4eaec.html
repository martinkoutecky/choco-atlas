The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(X\), a support \(\Pi\) of complete rankings with rational masses \(\mu_\pi>0\) summing to \(1\), no pairwise majority ties, and a designated triple \(R\subseteq X\), decide whether \(R\) is a minimal \(\tau\)-retentive set in the induced majority tournament \(T_\mu\), where \(x\to y\) exactly when \(\sum_{\pi:\,x\succ_\pi y}\mu_\pi>1/2\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass distribution \(\mu\) over supported complete ranking types induces \(T_\mu\) by pairwise majority; the decision is whether the designated triple \(R\) is minimal \(\tau\)-retentive.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 7’s triple-recognition characterization and its efficient algorithm; it leaves the tournament enumeration and the results on Schwartz’s Conjecture untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: recognize whether a specified triple is a minimal \(\tau\)-retentive set. I would not use this paper to claim that its broader structural results have a new continuous complexity landscape.

Take a large electorate whose agents share a moderate number \(q\) of complete rankings. Let \(X\) be the alternatives, and let each ranking type \(\pi\) have rational population mass \(\mu_\pi>0\), with \(\sum_\pi \mu_\pi=1\). For each pair \(x,y\), define \(x\to y\) when more than half the mass ranks \(x\) above \(y\); restrict the input to instances with no pairwise ties. This gives the majority tournament \(T_\mu\).

The continuous problem, Triple Retentiveness Recognition, takes \(X\), the supported ranking types and their masses, and a designated triple \(R\subseteq X\). It asks whether \(R\) is a minimal \(\tau\)-retentive set of \(T_\mu\); the solution is a yes/no answer. Lemma 7, proved in this paper, says exactly that this holds if and only if the triple induces a directed triangle and no alternative in \(T_\mu\) dominates two members of the triple. The paper notes that this characterization gives an efficient recognition algorithm. Constructing the pairwise majority relation and checking those conditions takes polynomial time in \(|X|\), \(q\), and the input bit length.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for instance, a large election may have many voters but only a moderate number of distinct ballot rankings. A type is a complete ranking, and mass records its share of the electorate. The authors introduce tournaments through majority preferences among agents, so they should recognize the resulting question as their triple-recognition problem with the electorate represented by type masses. I expect this mirror to be tractable.

The scope is just Lemma 7’s recognition result. It does not mirror the paper’s enumeration of retentive tournaments or its results on Schwartz’s Conjecture. It also suggests a follow-up: compute the full tournament equilibrium set from a weighted ranking population, and determine how that task depends on \(|X|\), \(q\), and the mass bit length.

The weak point is substantial: the majority-tournament projection discards the magnitudes of the masses once each pairwise majority is known. The recognition task is therefore the paper’s tournament problem preceded by aggregation, not a new optimization problem created by continuity. This makes it a defensible, modest mirror, but not evidence that continuization dissolves hardness or changes the complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 7 is the right anchor, and the strongest objection is about what its computation actually uses. Given ranking-type masses \(\mu_\pi\), compute each pairwise margin
\[ d_{xy}=\sum_{\pi:\,x\succ_\pi y}\mu_\pi-\sum_{\pi:\,y\succ_\pi x}\mu_\pi. \]
Once those signs define the majority tournament, Lemma 7 checks whether the designated triple is a directed triangle and whether any alternative dominates two of its members. The masses then disappear. The profile space is divided by the hyperplanes \(d_{xy}=0\) into regions on which the answer is constant. So this mirror adds profile aggregation to the paper’s tournament-level recognition algorithm, but asks no question about transfers of population mass, fractional intervention, or another feature of the continuous society.

The stronger version would compute the whole tournament equilibrium set from the weighted rankings. That still factors into “form the majority tournament, then solve the tournament problem.” A closer population-level question—such as finding the least mass transfer that makes a triple retentive—could be interesting, but it changes the task into a control problem that this paper does not study. It is not a stronger mirror of Lemma 7.

This is a narrow case against the mirror’s value, not against the plausibility of the regime. A large electorate with a moderate number of ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper motivates tournaments through majority preferences. The continuous input is also mathematically clean, with a running time expressible in the number of supported types and mass bit length. Those facts make the authors’ recognition of the mirror quite plausible. The negative case is therefore weak: the profile formulation may be a thin wrapper around the tournament result, but I cannot honestly claim that it fails ChoCo’s gate or defeats the anchor.
