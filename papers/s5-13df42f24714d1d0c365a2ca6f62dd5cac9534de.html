The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational nonnegative contact rates \(\lambda_{st}\), seed budget \(\kappa\), and accuracy \(\varepsilon\), choose seed masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\) to maximize \(F_\infty(x)=\sum_t\mu_t a_t^\infty\), where \(a_t^{-1}=0\), \(a_t^0=x_t/\mu_t\), and \(a_t^{r+1}=a_t^r+(1-a_t^r)\left(1-\exp\left(-\sum_s\lambda_{st}\mu_s(a_s^r-a_s^{r-1})\right)\right)\); determine the complexity of computing or approximating an optimizer within \(1-1/e-\varepsilon\), including whether exact optimization or that threshold remains hard for graph-shaped type kernels.

The model it lives in

A typed mean-field Independent-Cascade model: types \(t\in T\) carry population mass \(\mu_t\), newly active source mass contacts type \(t\) at rate \(\lambda_{st}\), decision variables are seed masses \(x_t\), and the objective is eventual active mass \(F_\infty(x)\). It is the limit of \(r\) exchangeable clones per original vertex with per-clone edge probability \(p_{uv}/r\), preserving arbitrary graph-shaped type kernels.

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Independent-Cascade seed-selection objective and its approximation-hardness question, but not the paper's other diffusion models, submodularity proofs, general marketing-action formulation, non-progressive reduction, or experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(F_\infty\) satisfy the required continuous diminishing-returns property, and can it and its marginals be evaluated to polynomial accuracy from rational input?
  • For graph-shaped kernels, is exact mass seeding NP-hard, and does a \(1-1/e\) inapproximability threshold survive fractional seeding?
  • What finite-\(N\) convergence bounds transfer approximation guarantees between the clone population and the mean-field objective, especially for the infinite horizon?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a single mirror anchored in Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper. The theorem says that, for the Independent Cascade model, selecting \(k\) initially active nodes to maximize expected eventual activation is NP-hard to approximate within any factor better than \(1-1/e\). The proof is an approximation-preserving reduction from Maximum Coverage: each set becomes a node, each universe element becomes \(n\) duplicate nodes, and selecting \(k\) set-nodes activates exactly \(k+n|T|\) nodes, where \(T\) is the union covered by the selected sets.

The continuous mirror is Typed Independent-Cascade Mass Seeding.

There is a finite type set \(T\), with \(|T|=\tau\), and a rational population distribution \(\mu=(\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\). A type is a complete contact profile: two users of the same type have the same influence rate toward every other type. The intended regime is a large platform, public-health campaign, or product-adoption population with \(N\mu_t\) exchangeable users of type \(t\), where \(N\gg\tau\). The users are not merely assigned the same degree; they share the entire type-level influence profile.

The instance also contains rational non-negative contact rates \(\lambda_{st}\). A newly active unit of mass of type \(s\) generates independent contacts toward type \(t\) at rate \(\lambda_{st}\), and a contact activates an otherwise inactive recipient. There is a campaign budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), measured as a fraction of the population.

The decision variable is a seed-mass vector \(x=(x_t)_{t\in T}\), with

\[ 0\le x_t\le \mu_t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_t\le \kappa. \]

Thus \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) initially activated. No named individual is selected.

Let \(a_t^0=x_t/\mu_t\) be the initially active fraction of type \(t\), let \(a_t^{-1}=0\), and write \(\Delta a_t^r=a_t^r-a_t^{r-1}\). The mean-field Independent Cascade process is

\[ a_t^{r+1} = a_t^r + (1-a_t^r) \left( 1-\exp\left( -\sum_{s\in T}\lambda_{st}\mu_s\Delta a_s^r \right) \right). \]

The first term is the already active population; the second is the fraction of the remaining population reached by the newly active mass in the previous round. Let \(a^\infty\) be the least fixed point reached from the seed vector, and define the influence of \(x\) by

\[ F_\infty(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t^\infty. \]

The continuous problem is:

\[ \text{maximize }F_\infty(x) \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\quad \sum_t x_t\le\kappa. \]

An approximate version asks for a feasible \(x\) with \(F_\infty(x)\ge (1-1/e-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}\), or asks for the exact complexity of deciding whether \(F_\infty(x)\ge Q\).

This is genuinely a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. For a finite population with \(N\mu_t\) users of type \(t\), give every newly active type-\(s\) user an independent edge to every type-\(t\) user with probability \(\lambda_{st}/N\). If a newly active source mass is \(\mu_s\Delta a_s^r\), the probability that a target avoids all such contacts converges to

\[ \left(1-\frac{\lambda_{st}}{N}\right)^{N\mu_s\Delta a_s^r} \longrightarrow \exp(-\lambda_{st}\mu_s\Delta a_s^r). \]

So the continuous process is the population limit of the paper’s progressive Independent Cascade process, not merely an analogy involving expected values.

I would expect this mirror to fall in Class A, at least for approximation. The discrete seed set has become a mass vector over exchangeable types, and the objective is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s monotone submodular influence function. The natural conjecture is that \(F_\infty\) is monotone and diminishing-returns submodular in the seed masses. If so, continuous-greedy or discretized marginal-gain methods should yield a polynomial-time \(1-1/e-\varepsilon\) approximation, with complexity measured in \(\tau\), the encoding length of \(\mu,\lambda,\kappa\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\). Exact optimization may still be difficult, and whether the \(1-1/e\) barrier survives for particular typed kernels is itself an interesting question.

The paper’s Maximum Coverage hardness does not automatically transfer: fractional seeding may dissolve the binary choice of selecting or not selecting a set-node. That is not a defect in the mirror; it is precisely the computational question continuization is meant to expose. In a one-wave bipartite kernel, for example, the mean-field objective can become a smooth fractional-coverage objective and may be substantially easier. In cyclic or multi-wave kernels, feedback could retain genuine combinatorial difficulty.

The paper’s authors should recognize the question. It preserves their central objects—initial seeding, progressive independent-cascade diffusion, and expected final spread—while changing the population from named nodes to a distribution over repeated network roles. The co-modification of the graph into a type-contact kernel is forced by high multiplicity: with many clones and fixed edge probabilities, every cascade saturates immediately; per-capita contact scaling is the natural non-degenerate limit.

The further questions are whether \(F_\infty\) is always DR-submodular, whether exact optimization is NP-hard, whether the \(1-1/e\) threshold survives for some kernels, how rapidly finite-\(N\) instances converge to the continuum, and which sparse or community-structured network limits admit analogous formulations.

The weakest point is that arbitrary individual topology is one of the paper’s main sources of richness, while the typed mirror replaces it by a block-structured contact matrix. A referee could argue that this loses the network problem rather than continuizing it. The answer is that the proposed scenario is deliberately a large exchangeable cohort system—such as users grouped by platform role, geography, or community—not a claim that every social network has a useful continuum limit. Within that regime, the decision is still campaign design under progressive stochastic diffusion, but the scarce resource and the population are both represented honestly by mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 2.2’s hardness is carried by an arbitrary incidence graph, not by population multiplicity. In the Maximum Coverage reduction, each set-node has a distinct neighborhood, and the overlap pattern among those neighborhoods is the entire source of difficulty. A genuinely small type space replaces this with a block contact matrix. It therefore discards precisely the individual network structure that makes the paper’s problem interesting.

The proposed mean-field model also introduces two substantial co-modifications: fractional seeding and contact probabilities scaled as \(1/N\). Without the scaling, the cascade becomes degenerate as \(N\) grows; with it, the model is no longer simply the paper’s Independent Cascade process on a large graph, but a new stochastic mean-field control problem. Its infinite-horizon objective may require solving a nonlinear fixed point involving exponentials, and its computational representation, approximation notion, and convergence guarantees are not specified. That makes the proposed mirror premature rather than an established computational continuation.

The better version of the mirror, however, defeats this objection. Take an arbitrary graph from the paper and replace each vertex \(u\) by \(r\) exchangeable clones. Give every clone of \(u\) an edge to every clone of \(v\) with probability \(p_{uv}/r\). If \(a_u^q\) is the active fraction of type \(u\), the limit process satisfies

\[ a_v^{q+1} = a_v^q+ (1-a_v^q) \left( 1-\exp\left(-\sum_u p_{uv}\bigl(a_u^q-a_u^{q-1}\bigr)\right) \right). \]

This preserves the original graph’s entire type-level incidence structure while making every type highly replicated. The objective counts active mass, so it does not depend on individual identity, and targeting a fraction of each clone class is a legitimate high-multiplicity analogue of choosing seed vertices.

Thus the negative objections do not survive the stronger construction. The small-type version may lose too much topology, and the exact infinite-horizon formulation needs care, but neither is a fundamental obstruction: finite horizons, rational approximations, or arbitrary graph-shaped type spaces repair them. The continuum may change or dissolve the Maximum Coverage hardness, but that is precisely the computational question the programme wants asked.

I therefore cannot honestly defeat this anchor. There is no “already continuous” collision, no identity-dependent objective, and no absence of a high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s Theorem 2.2 supplies a credible continuous mirror, even if the proponent’s particular tractability conjecture remains unproved.
