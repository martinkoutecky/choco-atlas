The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many institution types \(r\), with masses \(\mu_r\) and finite trees of rational entitlement streams \(v^{r,h}\in[0,1)^n\) whose coordinates sum to an integer, does there exist a causal mass-routing policy? At each node, the policy routes mass among integral subsets \(S\) of size \(H^{r,h}\), containing only categories with positive entitlement; every positive-mass institution history must preserve global quota, and the mass routed to category \(i\) must equal \(\mu_r v_i^{r,h}\) on each permitted path. Determine when such a policy exists, in particular whether the universal guarantee holds exactly for \(n\leq 3\).

The model it lives in

A continuous population of institution types with masses \(\mu_r\); each institution retains integral category assignments and an individual history, while decision variables route type mass across those histories and subsets \(S\). The constraints enforce per-institution global quota and type-level assignment mass \(\mu_r v_i^{r,h}\); the objective is feasibility of a causal policy.

The objection that survived

The type-specific mass constraints separate, so the cohort adds no coupling across institutions and reproduces the paper’s randomized policy by interpreting probabilities as population fractions.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 1’s existence boundary for global quota and ex-ante proportionality through recursive flow policies; leaves the deterministic deviation bound and the paper’s other rounding applications outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a finite entitlement tree, what is the complexity of deciding and constructing a causal mass-routing policy as a function of \(n\), the number of types, and tree size?
  • Which restricted entitlement trees with \(n\geq 4\) admit such a policy even though the universal guarantee fails?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a cohort version of the randomized result in Section 4. Consider a large network of public employers or universities that allocate indivisible positions among the same few beneficiary categories over successive recruitment rounds. Many institutions may share the same category definitions, slot rules, and entitlement process. Model those institutions as a population with \(\tau\) types and masses \(\mu_r\), where \(N\gg\tau\). An institution is a type’s agent; its assignment in each round remains an integral subset of categories.

For a finite-horizon version, each type has a finite tree of possible online entitlement streams. A node \(h\) reveals \(v^{r,h}\in[0,1)^n\cap\mathbb{Q}^n\), with integer sum \(H^{r,h}\); the policy sees the current node and the institution’s allocation history, but not the future branch. If \(q_{r,h,A}\) is the mass of type-\(r\) institutions at cumulative allocation history \(A\), the decision variable \(x_{r,h,A,S}\) is the mass routed this round to subset \(S\). These variables conserve mass, use only subsets of size \(H^{r,h}\) drawn from categories with positive entitlement, and send positive mass only along transitions that preserve global quota: every institution on the transition has cumulative allocation in \(\{\lfloor V_i^t\rfloor,\lceil V_i^t\rceil\}\). For each input path, type, round, and category, require
\[ \sum_{A,S:\,i\in S}x_{r,h,A,S}=\mu_r v_i^{r,h}. \]
Thus a fraction \(v_i^{r,h}\) of the type’s institutions receives category \(i\)’s position. At shared input prefixes the policy must make the same choices, so it remains online. A solution is a causal mass-routing policy satisfying these conditions on every permitted input path. There is no cost objective: the question is feasibility of a policy. Continuity lies in splitting the mass of institutions across histories and assignments; each institution still receives integral assignments.

My anchor is Theorem 1 in Section 4, proved in this paper. It states that an online apportionment method satisfying global quota and ex-ante proportionality on every \(n\)-dimensional instance exists if and only if \(n\leq3\). In full: every supported allocation history must keep each category’s cumulative seats at its cumulative entitlement’s floor or ceiling, while the probability that category \(i\) receives a seat in round \(t\) must equal \(v_i^t\). The authors construct the positive case using recursive flow networks and show the universal guarantee fails for \(n\geq4\). This is a named online-algorithm existence and impossibility result, not an NP-hardness or standard complexity-class theorem; I would not present it as one.

For the cohort problem, I expect the \(n\leq3\) case to be Class A: the paper’s flow construction supplies the mass transitions, and a finite input-tree version should admit a polynomial-size flow formulation when \(n\) is fixed. For \(n\geq4\), the universal guarantee should fail by the same obstruction: sampling a uniformly chosen institution from a mass policy would recover a randomized method for one institution, so the paper’s impossibility carries across. That is a transferred feasibility boundary, not a claim of computational hardness. The finite-tree formulation also raises a concrete complexity question in \(n\), the number of types \(\tau\), horizon \(T\), and tree size. A further question is whether exact marginal proportions can be retained for \(n\geq4\) if the per-institution global quota is relaxed to a bounded deviation.

This mirror covers the randomized online theorem, and potentially its dependent-rounding application when many similar institutions implement the same policy. It does not mirror the paper’s sharp deterministic deviation bound or continuize time. The weak point is that the population consists of many parallel institutions, not the voters or parties in a single parliament. The construction may be read as realizing randomization across a continuum of institutions; the authors would need to regard that system of institutions as their intended population-level setting for the mirror to be compelling.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the anchor to address. It is a named online existence and impossibility result, so dismissing it because it lacks an NP-hardness or running-time classification would be a weak argument.

The strongest negative case is that, in the proposed cohort model, mass tracks the randomized method’s probabilities. For a type of mass \(\mu_r>0\), dividing each transition mass \(x_{r,h,A,S}\) by \(\mu_r\) gives the probability of that transition for an institution of type \(r\). The per-institution quota condition becomes the paper’s global quota condition, and the mass marginal \(\mu_r v_i^{r,h}\) becomes ex-ante proportionality \(v_i^{r,h}\). Causality at shared prefixes is the same in both models. With one type, the population mass is therefore a realization of the lottery over methods. With several types, the stated constraints separate by type; the masses scale the policies without coupling them. The paper’s Proposition on the one-to-one flow characterization makes this correspondence especially close: its randomized methods already have the flow structure the proposed cohort would use.

The finite input tree offers a possible extension, but it does not make the cohort itself computationally consequential. For \(n\leq 3\), the paper’s universal method already handles every path, so it supplies a policy on any restricted tree. A tree-specific feasibility problem for \(n\geq4\) could be interesting when the tree excludes the paper’s obstruction, but then the new ingredient is the restricted scenario tree and its uncertainty model. A population-level coupling, such as a shared quota across institutions, could make the masses matter; that would also add constraints absent from Theorem 1.

This is a real case against the proposed mirror’s value to ChoCo: its continuous object appears to be the distribution over randomized outcomes, with institutions serving as parallel copies of the original rounding problem. The weakness is substantial, though. A large cohort of institutions using integral assignments is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the prompt permits a scenario different from the paper’s parliamentary one. I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would find that population interpretation worthwhile. The negative case is strongest on model identity and lack of new population coupling; it does not defeat the anchor universally.
