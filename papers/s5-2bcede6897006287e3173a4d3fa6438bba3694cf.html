The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a positive case that meets your anchor requirement: the paper has no named computational result. Theorem 1, proved here, says a blocking-neutral matching exists in every finite constrained roommate problem with strict preferences. It does not give an algorithm or a complexity bound. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are structural characterizations; Proposition 1 is a nonexistence result under weak preferences; and Proposition 2 proves existence for school choice. Corollary 1 is an incentive impossibility, not a computational-complexity result.

Theorem 1 could motivate a new question about blocking-neutral matchings for a large roommate population with finitely many preference types. But that would be a follow-on to an existence theorem, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The paper itself identifies algorithm design as future work. No continuous computational problem can therefore be stated per the required anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves existence by showing that blocking-neutral dominance is acyclic on a finite feasible set. Theorems 2 and 3 relate solution concepts; the remaining results concern existence, incentives, or preference domains. None asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity bound. That is a decisive mismatch for ChoCo’s computational brief.

The best candidate is the one the other analyst raises: take a large roommate population with finitely many preference types and ask for an algorithm to find a blocking-neutral matching. But getting there changes the object in ways the paper makes consequential. In its roommate model, agents rank named potential partners. As the population grows, a type that fully records those rankings generally grows with it. Grouping agents by preferences over partner *classes* instead makes members of a class interchangeable, introducing indifferences between individuals. The paper’s intended weak-preference version can lack blocking-neutral matchings; its strict-blocking alternative restores existence but changes the concept’s stated motivation. This does not make the altered model illegitimate. It means its solution and computational question would need to be specified afresh.

There is a further obstacle to treating type-level matching as a direct mirror. Blocking neutrality compares who improves or is harmed across two matchings, and whether those agents block. Aggregate masses of pairs do not by themselves record how the same individuals are assigned across both matchings. A model could add variables for that cross-matching alignment, but then it needs a new representation and a computational question the paper does not supply. Its feasibility set may be any nonempty subset of matchings, with no compact encoding or oracle specified.

So the obvious continuation—design an algorithm to compute the witness whose existence Theorem 1 proves—is a plausible research direction, especially after imposing structured constraints. But it is future algorithm work, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. I cannot make the broader claim airtight: a carefully specified high-multiplicity matching model could be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that this paper, as written, does not furnish ChoCo’s required computational anchor.
