What the mirror covers

The proposed population setting would concern Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollary 1; it leaves no computational result from the paper to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large population of citizens grouped by their complete welfare, preference, and judgment profiles across a finite set of social states. A type would encode those full performance profiles; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of society with type \(t\), with \(n\gg\tau\). That is a sensible continuous-population model.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror. The closest candidate, Theorem 2 (proved here), is an axiomatic impossibility result: under universal domain, independence of irrelevant alternatives, global unanimity, and cardinal information with inter-aspect non-comparability, some aspect must be dictatorial; adding continuity makes that aspect strongly dictatorial and implies the corresponding “ism.” Theorem 1 (proved here) is also axiomatic: universal domain, IIA, and \(A\)-indifference imply \(A\)-ism, and global unanimity then implies \(A\)-unanimity. Neither theorem gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a hard computational problem. Corollary 1 specializes the same axiomatic result to welfare, preference, and judgment.

Theorem 2 is the strongest *conceptual* lead: its obstruction and escape route suggest asking whether a continuum of citizens changes the conditions for trade-offs among aspects. But that would still be an axiomatic question, not the computational mirror this programme asks for. So there is no qualifying anchor, no per-anchor solver question, and no honest Class A/B/C prediction. The plausible population regime cannot make up for the missing computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest repaired candidate is Theorem 2: take a large society distributed over complete citizen types, where each type records its welfare, preference, and judgment profiles over social states, then ask whether the theorem’s dictatorial-aspect conclusion survives for that distribution. That is a coherent population model, but its question remains axiomatic. The theorem characterizes which aggregation rules satisfy a set of conditions; it gives no computational task with encoded instances, feasible actions, costs, or an algorithmic output. Passing from counts of citizens to type masses would change the domain of an axiomatic characterization, not expose a computational problem in the paper.

Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 have the same limitation. They derive informational restrictions from axioms; neither specifies a computation to perform. One could add a fixed aggregation rule and ask an algorithm to rank states, or add policy constraints and optimize over them. But those additions determine the computational problem. They are new models inspired by the paper, not continuous mirrors of its results.

The proposed citizen-type scenario is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime, so multiplicity itself is no objection. It still cannot supply the missing computational anchor: a distribution over citizens’ profiles does not tell us which computational question the authors’ results pose. A continuous-population treatment of Theorem 2 could be valuable to social-choice theory, but under ChoCo’s scope it would be an axiomatic continuization, not computational complexity work.

So the proponent’s conceptual lead does not survive as a ChoCo mirror, and none of the named results furnishes a computational anchor. The universal claim that no worthwhile computational project could ever be built from this paper is hard to prove; the narrower conclusion is strong: any such project would need substantial new modelling choices and would not be a continuization of a computational result in this paper.
