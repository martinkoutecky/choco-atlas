The continuous mirror question

Given depth \(n\), a unit-mass nonatomic electorate initially opposing a proposal, and a regular hierarchy with finite odd branching factors \(k_1,\ldots,k_n\), partition the mass equally among terminal constituencies. Convert opponent mass to supporter mass within constituencies at unit cost; each constituency and each internal node adopts the strict majority outcome. Choose the branching factors and constituency-level transfers to minimize total converted mass. What is the infimum, and is it attained?

The model it lives in

For each terminal constituency \(\ell\), types are \((\ell,\mathrm{support})\) and \((\ell,\mathrm{oppose})\); each constituency has mass \(1/\prod_i k_i\). Decision variables \(x_\ell\) transfer mass from opponent to supporter at unit cost. Choose finite odd branching factors and transfers minimizing \(\sum_\ell x_\ell\), subject to recursive strict mass-majority at every node.

The objection that survived

The proposed two-type version leaves constituency membership implicit even though it affects the root outcome; the model should index mass and transfers by constituency.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: in an \(n\)-level hierarchy with \(N_d\) voters, the fewest voters needed to support a winning outcome is minimized when every level has branching factor \(N_d^{1/n}\). The result is about an optimization over hierarchy shapes, but it is not a named computational-complexity result. More broadly, the paper gives no named result classifying a problem as tractable, NP-hard, or similar. Its theorems concern voting accuracy and hierarchy structure. So this is a plausible positive mirror, but not a qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s stated standard.

A concrete candidate is Continuous Pivotal-Mass Hierarchy Design. The society has unit mass and two complete voter types: supporters and opponents of a proposal. Mass is the fraction of eligible voters of each type; supporters and opponents are otherwise interchangeable. An instance specifies a finite-depth majority hierarchy and asks for the infimum mass of supporters, and an allocation of that mass among the terminal constituencies, that makes the root accept the proposal. Equivalently, start with all mass opposing and transfer mass to the supporter type at unit cost; minimize the transferred mass. The many-agent regime is a large referendum electorate with only two ballot types, so the number of voters can be enormous while the type support is \(2\).

The underlying question is one the authors could plausibly recognize: their discussion of Theorem 3 is explicitly about the fewest voters needed to sway an election, and phrasing that quantity as a share of eligible voters is natural. The continuous answer, however, exposes the mirror’s weakness. With divisible mass, majority thresholds lose the individual-voter rounding that contributes to the \(N_d^{1/n}\) balance. If the hierarchy may use arbitrarily many finite groups, the infimum approaches the threshold obtained by halving mass at each tier; there need not be a finite optimal branching pattern. That is a well-posed mass question, but it does not preserve Theorem 3’s square-root design conclusion.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable in a degenerate sense: the continuous threshold can be characterized directly, while the finite-population hierarchy-design optimum depends on granularity. Further questions include how the threshold changes when group counts are capped, when types have different costs of switching, or when supporter mass cannot be freely allocated across constituencies.

The paper’s focus on heterogeneous competence and abstention offers other tempting continuizations, but its results there are also analytic probability calculations, not computational anchors. The soft spot in this case is therefore substantial: Theorem 3 motivates a recognizable continuous mass problem, yet the result that makes it interesting may disappear in the continuum—and the paper supplies no named complexity result to anchor a ChoCo complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 1 compares reliability functions; Theorem 2 locates an asymptotic accuracy minimum; Theorem 3 characterizes the hierarchy shape that minimizes the number of voters needed to sway an outcome. That last result has an optimization flavor, but it is an analytic claim about a regular hierarchy in terms of \(n\) and \(N_d\), not a complexity result for an encoded computational problem. The paper gives no algorithmic formulation or complexity landscape. A continuous mass-control problem could be motivated by Theorem 3, but it would introduce the computational question rather than mirror one the paper studies.

The proposed two-type mirror is coherent and natural for a large referendum. Its difficulty is that the mass must be allocated among constituencies, and constituency membership affects the outcome. So either constituency is an additional problem-relevant type attribute, or allocation across constituencies is a new decision layer. A stronger mirror could add type-specific costs or competence and ask for the least mass transfer that changes the root result. That may be worthwhile, but the paper does not define or analyze those costed control problems. They would make a promising adjacent project, not supply the missing computational anchor.

This is a limited negative case. Theorem 3’s “fewest voters” quantity and the paper’s examples make mass a particularly natural language for its subject. If ChoCo accepts an analytic result as motivation for defining a new computational problem, the mirror could survive. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous scenario; I can argue that this paper, as written, is a weak target for a programme whose central contribution is the complexity of computational problems over continuous populations.
