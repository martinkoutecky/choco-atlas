Why no mirror

However, Theorem 2 is only a structural equality, so the paper contains no named computational result satisfying bit (a). Consequently, no anchor can yield a qualifying ChoCo mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the continuous analogue of Theorem 2 and the associated user-centric and pro-rata formulas, but leaves Theorems 1, 3, and 4 and the axiomatic analysis without computational counterparts.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: the paper has a natural high-multiplicity mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s strict rule. The authors explicitly say that they “do not consider computational aspects in this paper.” The named results are all axiomatic or structural: Theorem 1 characterizes weighted, pro-rata, and user-centric indices; Theorem 2 characterizes the core; Theorem 3 characterizes the user-centric index via core selection; and Theorem 4 gives claims-problem representations. None asserts membership in \(P\), hardness, parameterized tractability, or an algorithmic formulation used computationally. The NP-completeness and approximation statement about portfolio selection is only attributed to Alaei et al. (2022), and concerns a different problem.

If the formulation exception is interpreted broadly, my lead supporting anchor would be Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For a finite streaming problem, it defines \(v(S)=|\{j:L^j\subseteq S\}|\), the revenue generated by users listening only to artists in \(S\), and defines \(A(N,M,t)\) as the allocations \(x=\sum_{j\in M}x^j\) for which each \(x^j\) assigns one unit of user \(j\)’s payment only to artists in \(L^j\), with nonnegative shares summing to one. Theorem 2 states

\[ C(N,v)=A(N,M,t). \]

Thus the paper proves that a revenue vector is in the cooperative-game core exactly when every user’s subscription can be decomposed among that user’s listened-to artists.

The corresponding continuous problem would be:

Continuous Streaming-Core Allocation. An instance consists of a finite artist set \(A\), a finite set of complete user-profile types \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal T}\mu_\theta=1\), and an integral stream vector \(x^\theta\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{A}\) for every type \(\theta\). Let \(L_\theta=\{i:x_i^\theta>0\}\). The mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of subscribers having exactly that streaming profile; hence users with the same profile are one type. Given a target payment vector \(r\), decide whether there are variables \(z_{i\theta}\) satisfying

\[ z_{i\theta}\ge 0,\qquad z_{i\theta}=0\ \text{if }i\notin L_\theta,\qquad \sum_{i\in A}z_{i\theta}=\mu_\theta,\qquad r_i=\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal T}z_{i\theta}. \]

A solution is such a decomposition, or a certificate that none exists. Equivalently, the continuous core is

\[ C_\mu=\left\{r: \sum_{i\in A}r_i=1,\ \sum_{i\in S}r_i\ge \sum_{\theta:L_\theta\subseteq S}\mu_\theta \text{ for every }S\subseteq A\right\}. \]

This is a faithful mirror of the paper’s problem. The agents are many subscribers, mass is subscription share, and the decision variable is how each type’s subscription mass is divided among the artists it listens to. Money remains divisible exactly as in the paper; the object being continuized is the population, not the outcome space. A plausible regime is a platform with millions of subscribers assigned to a few thousand standardized listening templates, with each template recording the complete profile relevant to revenue allocation.

The user-centric rule is the canonical continuous solution:

\[ r_i^U=\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal T} \mu_\theta\frac{x_i^\theta}{\sum_{k\in A}x_k^\theta}, \]

witnessed by \(z_{i\theta}=\mu_\theta x_i^\theta/\sum_kx_k^\theta\). The pro-rata rule remains distinct:

\[ r_i^P= \frac{\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta x_i^\theta} {\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta\sum_kx_k^\theta}, \]

and can lie outside the continuous core for exactly the same cross-subsidization reason as in the finite model.

I would expect this continuous problem to be Class A: core membership and decomposition reduce to a bipartite flow or linear-feasibility problem over artist–type incidences, while the user-centric allocation has a closed form. Further questions include optimizing a fairness objective over all core allocations, adding type-dependent prices or budgets, and studying portfolio selection. Those would be genuine computational research questions, but they are extensions, not results established by this paper.

The weakest point is decisive: because every named theorem in the paper is noncomputational and the formulas are additive over users, grouping repeated users into masses may look like a convenient reparameterization rather than a new complexity problem. The positive case is therefore that the continuous population model is natural, exact, and author-recognizable—not that this particular paper supplies a qualifying ChoCo computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at ChoCo’s gate: this paper contains no computational anchor. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Theorem 3 are axiomatic or structural characterizations; Theorem 4 is a representation result. None defines a computational problem, states a complexity bound, or gives an algorithm. The NP-completeness result mentioned in the introduction belongs to Alaei et al. and concerns a different portfolio-selection problem. Thus the paper supplies no named computational result to continuize.

Theorem 2 is the strongest possible attempted exception, but it does not rescue the paper. Its statement

\(C(N,v)=A(N,M,t)\)

is an equality between two descriptions of a finite allocation set, not a discrete-to-continuous identity and not a computational theorem. One can manufacture a decision problem from it—“given \(r\), is \(r\) in the core?”—but that would be a new problem invented by the analyst. If every structural characterization could qualify merely because one can ask membership in the characterized set, the computational-anchor requirement would disappear.

The proposed continuous version is nevertheless mathematically legitimate. For a population measure \(\mu\) over user profiles, one can define

\(v_\mu(S)=\mu\{\theta:L_\theta\subseteq S\}\)

and allocations \(r_i=\sum_\theta z_{i\theta}\), where \(z_{i\theta}\) distributes type \(\theta\)’s mass among the artists it listens to. The analogue of Theorem 2 then holds. But this reveals why it is not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror: the theorem depends only on each user’s support \(L_\theta\), not on the streaming profile itself. Let

\(q_R=\mu\{\theta:L_\theta=R\}\).

Then the entire continuous core is determined by the finitely many masses \(q_R\), since \(v_\mu(S)=\sum_{R\subseteq S}q_R\). With explicitly listed types, membership or decomposition is just a capacitated bipartite-flow problem: source \(\to\) type nodes has capacities \(\mu_\theta\), type \(\to\) artist edges encode \(L_\theta\), and artist \(\to\) sink capacities encode \(r_i\). The finite-user version has exactly the same flow structure; passing to masses merely aggregates identical supply nodes.

This is not an objection that “continuity fails to help.” It is a more basic objection: the paper never poses a computational question whose continuous version could reveal anything about complexity, approximation, or high-multiplicity algorithms. The flow problem is an analyst-created membership query with an elementary formulation, not a computational mirror of Theorem 2. The user-centric and pro-rata formulas likewise become integrals or weighted sums with no optimization problem: \(U\) is a pointwise normalized contribution, while \(P\) is a ratio of aggregate streams.

The stronger variants do not repair this. Allowing an atomless or infinite type space still leaves the core dependent only on the pushforward distribution over listening sets. Making the artist population continuous changes the object more radically: artist coalitions become measurable sets, revenues become measures or densities, and the paper’s finite-agent core and axioms require a new model. Adding portfolio choice, endogenous artist participation, fairness optimization, prices, or strategic manipulation could produce genuine computational questions, but those are new problems—not mirrors of any result in this paper. Portfolio selection in particular is the cited result of another paper, not an anchor here.

The natural high-multiplicity story—millions of subscribers sharing standardized listening profiles—is sound. The failure is not multiplicity, nor divisibility, nor the plausibility of the application. It is that the paper is wholly noncomputational, and its best structural theorem yields only a routine aggregation of a per-user flow description. A future paper could build an interesting computational model around streaming platforms, but doing so would amount to writing the missing ChoCo paper rather than continuizing this one.
