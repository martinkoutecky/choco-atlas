The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 4.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4.1 — survived: The mass-based rates reproduce the theorem’s fees under replication and preserve the aggregate allocation; the opponent agrees this mirror likely survives.

The continuous mirror question

Given runway levels \(1,\ldots,H\), costs \(0=c_0\le c_1\le\cdots\le c_H\), and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over movement types \((r,S)\), where \(S\) is the nonempty set of operating airlines, compute the fee density attributed to each \(a\in S\) and the resulting type and airline aggregate fees. For \(M_{a,t}=\sum_{(r,S):\,r\ge t,\,a\in S}\mu_{r,S}\) and \(A_t=\{a:M_{a,t}>0\}\), the density is \(\lambda_{(r,S),a}=\sum_{t=1}^{r}(c_t-c_{t-1})/(|A_t|M_{a,t})\); the type density is \(\sum_{a\in S}\lambda_{(r,S),a}\), and aggregate fees are obtained by weighting densities by type mass.

The model it lives in

A distribution over movement types \((r,S)\), with mass \(\mu_{r,S}\), runway-cost increments \(c_t-c_{t-1}\), and exact operating-airline sets \(S\). Compute the type-level fee density and its airline-attributed components using Theorem 4.1’s formula; there are no optimization variables.

The objection that survived

The paper’s two-day sample does not establish that exact airline-set and runway-type combinations remain limited in a larger airport-period population; the annual regime is plausible but assumed.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the airport-game allocation formula in Theorem 4.1. It leaves aside the general axiomatic characterizations, the airline-merger result, and the numerical comparisons with other allocation rules.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do type-level aggregate charges behave as a supported type’s mass approaches zero, when its per-unit fee density may grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is a continuous version of the airport fee allocation problem in Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. The theorem gives an exact formula for the configuration value of an airport game: for a movement \(i\) of aircraft type \(\tau(i)\), operated by airlines in \(B_i\), its fee is
\[ \mathrm{CV}_i(N,c,B) = \sum_{a:B_a\in B_i} \sum_{t=1}^{\tau(i)} \frac{c_t-c_{t-1}}{|A_{\ge t}|\,|N^a_{\ge t}|}. \]
Here \(A_{\ge t}\) is the set of airlines operating movements of type \(t\) or larger, and \(N^a_{\ge t}\) is the set of those movements operated by airline \(a\). Thus the result computes each movement’s allocation by dividing each incremental runway cost among the relevant airlines, then among each airline’s qualifying movements. It is an exact computational result, though the paper does not state it as a formal complexity-class theorem.

For the mirror, fix an airport and accounting period. The population consists of its movements. A type is a pair \(k=(r,S)\): runway-cost type \(r\), and the exact nonempty set \(S\) of airlines operating that movement. The set \(S\) captures code-sharing, so a movement may belong to several airlines’ groups. Let \(\mu_{r,S}\) be the fraction of all movements of that type, with rational masses summing to one. The instance also gives the nondecreasing runway costs \(c_0,\ldots,c_H\). A unit of mass is a share of the period’s movement population, not a single named flight.

The Continuous Configuration-Value Airport Fee Problem asks for the fee rate for each supported type, and its airline-attributed components. Define
\[ M_{a,t}=\sum_{\substack{(r,S):\,r\ge t\\a\in S}}\mu_{r,S}, \qquad A_t=\{a:M_{a,t}>0\}. \]
For type \(k=(r,S)\), output the rate attributed to airline \(a\in S\),
\[ \lambda_{(r,S),a} = \sum_{t=1}^{r} \frac{c_t-c_{t-1}}{|A_t|\,M_{a,t}}, \]
and the movement type’s total rate \(\lambda_{(r,S)}=\sum_{a\in S}\lambda_{(r,S),a}\). The type’s aggregate fee is \(\mu_{r,S}\lambda_{(r,S)}\); the airline-attributed aggregate is \(\sum_{(r,S):a\in S}\mu_{r,S}\lambda_{(r,S),a}\). This is an allocation problem, not an optimization problem: the output is the fee vector specified by the rule.

The formula is not an arbitrary continuum guess. Replicate a society with masses \(\mu_{r,S}\) into \(n\mu_{r,S}\) movements per type, for a common denominator \(n\). Theorem 4.1 gives each movement a fee of \(\lambda_{(r,S)}/n\). Multiplying that fee by the number of movements recovers the type’s aggregate fee exactly. The mass allocation also distributes the cost correctly: at each runway tranche \(t\), every active airline’s qualifying mass contributes \((c_t-c_{t-1})/|A_t|\), so the tranche is allocated in full. Summing the rates weighted by mass gives the cost of the largest aircraft type present.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large airport over a season or year: millions of movements but comparatively few combinations of runway-cost type and code-sharing operator set. Movements share a type precisely when the paper’s model treats them alike: they require the same runway-cost level and are operated by the same airlines. The paper’s two-day data set of 131 movements helps make the type description concrete, but that sample itself is not a high-multiplicity regime. The mirror becomes persuasive where the actual movement count is much larger than the observed type support.

I expect this problem to be tractable: the rates can be computed by summing masses and applying the displayed formula, in time polynomial in the number of supported types, runway levels, airlines, and input bit length. A natural follow-up is whether the mass-weighted allocations remain stable as a small type mass approaches zero: the per-unit rate may grow while that type’s aggregate fee need not. Another is how the model changes if the airline set or the number of distinct operating patterns grows with the population.

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s airport-game allocation result. It does not claim that the paper’s axiomatic characterization results are computational results, and it does not turn its Santiago airport application into a complexity claim. My weakest point is that the finite problem is already efficiently computable by the paper’s formula, so this mirror may reveal little new complexity. Its case rests instead on a faithful change of population object: the same code-sharing fee rule is expressed over movement mass, with per-type allocation rates and aggregate fees as the outputs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is the one serious anchor, and it clears the basic test: it gives an exact formula for computing a named allocation. Calling it axiomatic rather than computational would be a mistake.

The strongest objection is about what survives the population limit. Let \(n\mu_{r,S}\) be the number of movements of type \((r,S)\). Then \(|N^a_{\ge t}|=nM_{a,t}\), so Theorem 4.1 assigns each such movement a fee of \(\lambda_{(r,S)}/n\). Its per-movement fee therefore tends to zero as the population grows. The proponent’s \(\lambda_{(r,S)}\) is a fee density—\(n\) times the individual fee—and the nonzero quantity that survives is the aggregate allocation \(\mu_{r,S}\lambda_{(r,S)}\).

That limits the mirror’s interpretation, but it does not defeat its best version. Aggregate charges by type and airline remain meaningful, and the mass-weighted formula preserves the allocation of the runway cost. The programme explicitly allows type-level averages and model co-modifications, so the density formulation is hard to rule out on scope grounds. Nor does the population premise fail automatically: across a season or year, many movements could share an aircraft cost tier and the same set of operating airlines. Those are precisely the features the paper’s formula uses.

The negative case is therefore weak. The paper’s two-day sample is not itself a high-multiplicity regime, and the continuous formulation shifts attention from individual movement fees to aggregate cost burdens. But a better mirror can make that shift explicit and still ask a sensible computational question. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this anchor; on the standards given, Theorem 4.1’s mirror likely survives.
