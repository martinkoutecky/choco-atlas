The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) candidates, nonnegative rational adjacent-swap weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_{n-1}\), and a society supported on \(\tau\) rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_\sigma\) summing to \(1\), return a ranking \(\pi\) such that \(\sum_\sigma \mu_\sigma d_w(\pi,\sigma) \le 2\min_{\rho\in S_n}\sum_\sigma \mu_\sigma d_w(\rho,\sigma)\), where \(d_w\) is the minimum total weight of adjacent swaps transforming one ranking into another.

The model it lives in

Types are rankings in \(S_n\), with mass \(\mu_\sigma\) on each supported type. The objective is expected weighted-Kendall distance from an output ranking; the algorithm minimizes expected generalized-footrule cost by a minimum-weight bipartite matching and returns a factor-2 approximation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the weighted-Kendall median's factor-2 approximation in Proposition 32. It leaves the paper's distance-computation results, weighted-transposition aggregation, exact-median complexity, and PageRank-style heuristic outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the factor-2 guarantee be improved for arbitrary nonnegative rational swap weights?
  • Which restrictions on support or swap weights permit exact weighted-Kendall aggregation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s weighted-Kendall aggregation problem itself, with its voters represented by a distribution over rankings. A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large, topic-specific search service: many query sessions draw from recurring contexts and produce the same ranking of a fixed set of pages. A type is that ranking, and its mass is the fraction of sessions producing it. The regime of interest has many sessions but comparatively few distinct rankings. This fits the paper’s own search-query motivation and its view of rankings as point masses whose aggregate is a kind of center of mass.

Here is a precise problem. Fix candidates \([n]\) and nonnegative rational swap weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_{n-1}\), where \(w_i\) is the cost of swapping adjacent positions \(i\) and \(i+1\). The instance is a finite support \(S\subseteq S_n\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_\sigma\) summing to \(1\). For rankings \(\pi,\sigma\), let \(d_w(\pi,\sigma)\) be the minimum total weight of adjacent swaps transforming \(\pi\) into \(\sigma\). The Continuous Weighted-Kendall Median, 2-Approximation problem asks for a ranking \(\pi\in S_n\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{\sigma\in S}\mu_\sigma d_w(\pi,\sigma) \le 2\min_{\rho\in S_n}\sum_{\sigma\in S}\mu_\sigma d_w(\rho,\sigma). \]
The output is an actual aggregate ranking. With a finite population of \(N\) sessions and \(n_\sigma\) sessions of each type, setting \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/N\) gives exactly the same objective up to the irrelevant factor \(1/N\).

My anchor is Proposition 32, proved in this paper. For a finite list of votes \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\), it says that if \(\pi^\star\) minimizes \(\sum_i d_w(\pi,\sigma_i)\), then a ranking \(\pi'\) minimizing \(\sum_i D_w(\pi,\sigma_i)\) is a 2-approximation to \(\pi^\star\) for weighted-Kendall aggregation. Here \(D_w\) is the generalized footrule surrogate, which sums the weighted positional distance for each candidate. This is a result about the underlying aggregation problem, not merely about an encoding. In the distributional version, minimizing \(\sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma D_w(\pi,\sigma)\) is still a minimum-weight bipartite matching: assign candidates to ranks, with each assignment cost averaged over the support. The paper’s factor-2 argument carries over because it uses inequalities between \(D_w\) and \(d_w\) and sums them over voters; replacing counts by rational masses preserves those inequalities. The matching has polynomial size, and its costs can be computed from the \(\tau=|S|\) supported rankings and their rational weights.

For this promised 2-approximation problem, I expect Class A: tractable. For the exact continuous median problem, I expect Class B: hardness transfers. With all \(w_i=1\), the objective is Kemeny aggregation; any explicitly listed discrete profile maps to rational masses \(n_\sigma/N\) with the same optimal rankings. Section V says that finding a Kemeny optimal solution is NP-hard, citing [8] and [23]—that claim is stated in the paper’s prose rather than as a numbered theorem. Further questions include whether the factor 2 can be improved, how the best approximation guarantee depends on the weights, and which restrictions permit exact aggregation.

The scope here is specifically weighted-Kendall rank aggregation and Proposition 32’s matching-based approximation. I would not extend this mirror to the paper’s weighted-transposition results or its PageRank-style heuristic. The weakest point is the scenario: the paper aggregates votes, but it does not establish that real search-query populations have a small stable support of recurring rankings. That needs evidence for a particular application. The mathematical mirror itself is direct: the paper’s sum of vote distances becomes expected distance under a population distribution, and the matching algorithm still applies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Proposition 32’s mirror is that it may be a representation change with almost no new mathematics. The paper’s objective depends only on the frequency of each ranking, not on voter identities. A rational distribution \(\mu\) can be converted into a finite profile by clearing denominators, and the proposition’s inequalities then apply to that profile. More directly, the matching algorithm can use edge costs weighted by \(\mu_\sigma\), so its input size depends on the support and the mass bit lengths rather than on the expanded electorate. The advertised continuous \(2\)-approximation is therefore an immediate extension of the paper’s existing argument.

That point does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s standard. The compressed support representation is exactly where the computational question lies, and an immediate Class A answer is still an answer. Nor does the search-session example fail: for this aggregation problem, a ranking completely describes a type, and repeated sessions can plausibly produce the same ranking. I cannot rule out that regime by appealing to personalization or query context, since those details matter only if the aggregation problem uses them.

The stronger exact-median mirror also survives the obvious negative arguments. With unit swap weights it is Kemeny aggregation over a distribution; finite profiles embed by setting \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/N\). That the exact problem is already hard, or that the rational distribution can be expanded into a discrete profile, is not a reason to reject it under the stated rules. The expansion may be enormous in the mass bit length, and studying the compressed input’s complexity is a legitimate computational question.

So my best case against is about likely research yield, not the model’s legitimacy: Proposition 32’s continuous version appears to follow directly from its proof and a weighted matching construction, while the exact version largely inherits the familiar Kemeny problem. The paper offers little sign of a new continuum-specific structure to uncover. But that is a weak objection to worth, and it does not defeat the proponent’s anchor. A negative verdict that claims no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario would go beyond what this paper supports.
