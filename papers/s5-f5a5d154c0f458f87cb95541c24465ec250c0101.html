The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), approval-ballot types \(B_1,\ldots,B_\tau\subseteq C\) with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(0\leq\beta<1\), decide whether rational deletion masses \(x_i\in[0,\mu_i]\) exist with \(\sum_i x_i\leq\beta\) such that \(\left|\arg\max_{c\in C}\sum_i(\mu_i-x_i)\mathbf{1}[c\in B_i]\right|<\left|\arg\max_{c\in C}\sum_i\mu_i\mathbf{1}[c\in B_i]\right|\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass electorate distributed over approval-ballot types; delete a budgeted mass from each type, paying the total mass removed, and ask whether the discrete candidate set's AV winner set becomes smaller.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 13's AV-DelVDec hardness claim; it leaves the other control results, winner-determination results, rules, and experiments untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Establish the proposed LP characterization rigorously, including ties and zero budget, and bound its running time in \(m,\tau,L\).
  • Characterize when solutions to the mass-deletion problem transfer to finite elections with integer voter deletions.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 13, proved in this paper: AV-DelVDec is NP-hard. In the paper’s definition, the input is an approval election \(E\) and a deletion budget \(b\); the question is whether deleting at most \(b\) voters can make the size of the AV winning committee smaller. Under AV, that committee consists of all candidates tied for highest approval score.

The paper proves this by reducing from Restricted X3C: there are \(3n\) elements and \(3n\) sets, each set contains three elements, and each element occurs in three sets; the question is whether \(n\) sets form an exact cover. The construction gives each element a pair of candidates, adds \(a,a'\) and \(p\), and uses one voter per set plus two groups of repeated ballots. Initially \(a,a'\) are the two AV winners. Deleting the \(n\) set voters for an exact cover makes \(p\) the unique winner. Conversely, a deletion that shrinks the winning committee within that budget must delete the set voters encoding an exact cover. The paper gives this reduction; it cites the NP-hardness of Restricted X3C.

A direct mirror is AV-Mass-DelVDec. Let the candidates be \(C\), and let the supported voter types be approval sets \(B_1,\ldots,B_\tau\subseteq C\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\). Given a rational budget \(\beta<1\), choose rational deletion masses \(x_i\) with \(0\le x_i\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_i\le\beta\). The remaining population has masses proportional to \(\mu_i-x_i\). Ask whether there is such a deletion that strictly reduces the number of AV winners. A solution is the deletion vector \(x\); its cost is the total mass removed, and the budgeted decision version matches the paper’s control question. Normalizing the remaining masses does not affect AV winners, so they can be compared using scores \(\hat s_x(c)=\sum_i(\mu_i-x_i)\mathbf 1[c\in B_i]\).

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for a large membership election or online consultation: many people vote on a small candidate slate, recurring approval ballots form a moderate support of \(\tau\) types, and an administrator or campaign can suppress a percentage of ballots within those cohorts. The type is the approval ballot because that is the complete information AV and this control action use. A finite election with \(n_i\) voters of each type maps to \(\mu_i=n_i/n\) and \(\beta=b/n\); requiring each \(x_i\) to be a multiple of \(1/n\) recovers the discrete deletion choices. The mirror relaxes that granularity while keeping the committee and the AV rule intact.

I expect this continuous problem to be in Class A, polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Group candidates by their approval signatures \(\sigma(c)=(\mathbf 1[c\in B_i])_{i=1}^{\tau}\). Candidates in one group always have identical scores after any permitted deletion. Let \(r\) be the initial number of AV winners. For each signature group \(G\) with \(|G|<r\), test by linear programming whether it can strictly outscore every other group within budget. One LP maximizes a margin \(\delta\) subject to the deletion bounds and \(\hat s_x(c)-\hat s_x(d)\ge\delta\) for \(c\in G\) and every \(d\notin G\). A positive optimum gives a deletion whose winning committee is exactly \(G\), hence smaller than the original one.

This check is also complete. If a feasible deletion produces fewer than \(r\) winners but several distinct signature groups tie at the top, their combined size is below \(r\). With positive budget, a small feasible perturbation can break those ties while preserving the gap to lower-scoring groups. It leaves one of those groups as the sole winning signature group, still of size below \(r\). Thus testing at most \(m\) groups suffices, with LPs of polynomial size.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: their rule, voter-deletion action, and objective of shrinking the winning committee are unchanged. The population and the amount deleted become continuous; the chosen committee remains discrete. The mirror covers Theorem 13’s AV-DelVDec result, not the paper’s other rules or control problems.

The weakest point is the operational meaning of fractional deletion. The paper’s reduction depends on choosing whole set-voters to encode an exact cover; the LP mirror allows partial deletion of each ballot type, which is a different action if an application can only remove named individuals. In that indivisible setting, the lattice restriction returns the discrete problem and this tractability argument no longer applies. The continuous question is strongest where the intervention itself is naturally measured and allocated as a share of each cohort.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this changes the intervention. Theorem 13’s reduction uses whole set-voters: each ballot encodes one set, and choosing exactly which of those voters to delete carries the exact-cover structure. Deleting a real amount \(x_t\) from each ballot type instead models cohort-level suppression or attrition. For an administrator who can act only on named voter records, that mass solution may not be implementable. The reduction therefore does not itself establish that its hardness result comes from a high-multiplicity regime.

That is a real caveat about the application and the discrete-to-continuous bridge, but it does not defeat the mirror. A large consultation or membership election can naturally be described by ballot shares, and an intervention can be budgeted as the share of each cohort whose ballots are removed. The types are complete for this problem: AV and voter deletion use only the approval ballot. Candidates remain discrete, and the winning committee remains the object being controlled. The population alone becomes continuous.

Nor does the loss of the exact-cover reduction count against the question. It may be the result the programme wants to discover: whether allowing fractional cohort deletion makes this control problem tractable. The supplied paper does not already answer that continuous computational question. I can make the mirror less persuasive for settings where deletion must be indivisible, but I cannot honestly rule it out across plausible scenarios. Theorem 13 survives as a worthwhile continuous mirror.
