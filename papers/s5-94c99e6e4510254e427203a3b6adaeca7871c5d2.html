The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational complexity result in this paper: it does not classify a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. Its results are analytical equilibrium characterizations. I won’t present one as a complexity theorem. The closest basis for a positive mirror is Proposition 2, proved in the paper, which explicitly characterizes the candidate equilibrium for every district preference distribution.

In full, Proposition 2 says the candidate equilibrium is unique for any \(f_d\). If the anti-reform share \(f_d(t_N)\) exceeds both ideological shares \(f_d(t_L)\) and \(1-f_d(t_L)=f_d(t_R)\), both candidates choose anti-reform. If the pro-reform share \(f_d(t_Y)=1-f_d(t_N)\) exceeds both ideological shares, both choose pro-reform. In the remaining cases, the finite-\(k\) equilibrium uses mixed strategies. As \(k\to\infty\), those strategies converge to degenerate probabilities: when \(f_d(t_L)>f_d(t_N)>0.5\), or \(0.5>f_d(t_N)>f_d(t_L)\), the left candidate chooses anti-reform and the right candidate pro-reform with probability tending to one; when \(f_d(t_L)>1-f_d(t_N)>0.5\), or \(0.5>1-f_d(t_N)>f_d(t_L)\), those choices reverse. Proposition 4, also proved here, further says the unique expected national policy takes the position preferred by the median district on each dimension, with the median district determined separately for left–right and reform preferences.

The concrete continuous problem I would put to a prover is Asymptotic Legislative Equilibrium from Type Masses. An instance has an odd number \(D\) of districts and, for each district \(d\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu_d\) over the paper’s eight voter types. A type records the voter’s preference ordering over the four policy bundles; district affiliation is part of the type for this problem. Each \(\mu_d\) sums to one. The two candidates in each district choose between the paper’s anti-reform and pro-reform platforms; voters elect a representative by plurality, and the legislature decides each policy dimension by majority vote.

The question is to compute, for every district, the limiting candidate platform probabilities in the unique responsive asymptotically strictly perfect equilibrium, and the resulting expected national policy. A solution is the exact list of those limiting probabilities and the policy bundle. To define voter incentives in a continuum, the model should retain the paper’s pivotality logic by defining the equilibrium as the limit of finite Poisson elections with mean \(k\) voters per district and type probabilities \(\mu_d\), as \(k\to\infty\). A nonatomic voter in a static continuum has zero pivotal probability, so this sequence-limit definition is essential to preserve the paper’s question.

The regime is a large parliamentary election: many voters per district, with at most eight preference types within each district. Those sharing a type have the same preferences for the four policy bundles and the same district affiliation. For a national legislature of hundreds of districts, the model has at most \(8D\) district-specific types, while the number of voters can be millions. The mass interpretation is natural: a district’s electorate is described by percentages of voter types rather than named voters.

I expect this problem to be tractable. This is an algorithmic consequence of the paper’s explicit characterization, not a complexity result it states. For each district, apply Proposition 2’s comparisons to its type masses; then use Proposition 4’s dimension-by-dimension median-district rule to compute policy. With binary rational masses of bit length \(L\), these operations take \(O(D\,\mathrm{poly}(L))\) time. The paper does not supply a hardness anchor, so this is not a case where discrete hardness is shown to dissolve in the continuum.

The authors would recognize the model: they already use \(f_d\) as a distribution over these voter types and study the \(k\to\infty\) limit. That is also the case’s weakest point. The proposed mirror is very close to computing the asymptotic object their propositions already characterize; it may be a continuous computational formulation, but it is not a strong new modeling contribution. The paper’s own results make the answer appear tractable before a separate algorithmic analysis begins. Useful follow-up questions would concern finite-\(k\) approximation guarantees, tie and boundary cases, or extensions to many policy dimensions—but those are not established by Proposition 2.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a novelty collision: the proposed mirror of Proposition 2 is already the paper’s own asymptotic model. Each district is described by \(f_d\), a distribution over the same eight voter types, and the analysis takes \(k\to\infty\). The proposed input \(\mu_d\) is just that distribution under another name; calling its entries rational and recording their bit length adds an encoding, not a new population model. Proposition 2 already gives the limiting platform choices from those masses, and Proposition 4 already gives the resulting expected policy. The claimed \(O(D\,\mathrm{poly}(L))\) computation is an implementation of those results, not an open computational question for a prover. This is not a case where an answer from outside the literature weakens a worthwhile mirror: the paper itself already formulates and answers it.

A static continuum does not rescue this anchor. A single voter has zero mass, so her ballot cannot change either legislative majority; the pivotality incentives that drive Proposition 2 disappear. The proponent preserves those incentives by defining the question as the limit of finite Poisson elections. That is exactly the limiting construction the paper already studies. So the proposed mirror faces a choice: keep the paper’s pivotality logic and restate its asymptotic result, or change the model enough that it asks a different question.

Proposition 4 adds no separate computational anchor. Its coordinate-by-coordinate median-district rule is already the paper’s answer for the national policy. Asking an algorithm to return that policy from rational type masses does not add an unsolved problem. Nor does Proposition 7 open a gap here: the paper already analyzes a class of distribution shifts—polarisation—and establishes their effect on outcomes.

A stronger possible proposal would be to ask how much voter mass must move between types to change the equilibrium policy, given per-capita costs for those changes. That sounds like campaigning or winner robustness, but the paper supplies neither such interventions nor their costs. Adding them could define a worthwhile new model; it would not be a continuization of one of these results. The article’s own model already has large electorates over a small set of types, and its equilibrium and policy rules are already characterized in that limit.

That makes the negative case strong against the submitted anchor, but not airtight as a universal claim. A mass-transfer robustness problem with well-motivated costs might fit the programme. I cannot honestly rule out its value; I would classify it as a new extension, not a surviving continuous mirror of the paper’s named results.
