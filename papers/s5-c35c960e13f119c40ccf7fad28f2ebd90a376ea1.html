What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: it concerns the one-house permutation game that the paper uses as the starting point for its two-good extension.

The anchor is Theorem 2.1, cited from Curiel and Tijs [2] and Quint [9], rather than proved by Wako and Quint. It says that for any permutation game with valuation matrix \(A=(a_{ij})\), a payoff vector \(x\) is in the core if and only if there are dual-optimal vectors \(u,v\) for the assignment LP’s dual,
\[ \min \sum_i (u_i+v_i) \quad\text{subject to}\quad u_i+v_j\ge a_{ij}\ \text{for all }i,j, \]
such that \(x_i=u_i+v_i\) for every player \(i\). Thus it characterizes the entire core as sums of optimal dual solutions. The paper does not state a named “in P” result, and it has no named hardness result. This is the closest defensible computational anchor: a polynomial-size LP characterization that the paper works with directly.

A natural high-multiplicity regime is a large exchange market for standardized housing units. Many households may have the same valuation for each housing class and the same class of unit they own. A type \(a\) is that complete combination of valuation vector and endowment class; its mass \(\mu_a\) is the fraction of households of that type. With hundreds of types and many thousands of households, the number of households can be much larger than the support. Each household still receives one indivisible unit; the aggregate assignment is represented by flows of mass between types and housing classes.

That gives the following prover-ready question.

Continuous Permutation-Game Core Membership *(lead).* The input is a finite set \(A\) of \(\tau\) types, rational masses \(\mu_a\ge0\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(H\) of housing classes, an endowment class \(e(a)\in H\) for each type, nonnegative rational values \(v_{ah}\), and a rational per-household payoff \(p_a\) for each type. Class \(h\) has supply \(\sigma_h=\sum_{a:e(a)=h}\mu_a\).

For any coalition mass vector \(q\) with \(0\le q_a\le\mu_a\), its endowment of class \(h\) is \(\sigma_h(q)=\sum_{a:e(a)=h}q_a\). Let \(W(q)\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{a,h}v_{ah}z_{ah}\) over nonnegative assignment flows with row sums \(q_a\) and column sums \(\sigma_h(q)\). The grand-coalition value is \(W(\mu)\). The question is whether \(p\) is a type-symmetric core payoff: does it satisfy
\[ \sum_a\mu_a p_a=W(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_a q_a p_a\ge W(q)\ \text{for every }q\in[0,\mu]? \]
A yes solution can be given by dual potentials \(\alpha_a,\beta_h\) with \(\alpha_a+\beta_h\ge v_{ah}\), \(\sum_a\mu_a\alpha_a+\sum_h\sigma_h\beta_h=W(\mu)\), and \(p_a=\alpha_a+\beta_{e(a)}\). A no answer has a failed efficiency equality or a blocking coalition and assignment.

I expect this question to be tractable: its type-level assignment and dual LPs have size polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|H|\), and the input bit length \(L\). This is the assignment-game structure of Theorem 2.1 carried over to rational masses. The type support, rather than the expanded household count, controls the computation. If the masses are proportions from a finite market, scaling the flow by the population size recovers the discrete assignment formulation.

The authors should recognize this as a continuous version of their own one-house baseline: Section 3 explicitly extends the permutation game, and Theorem 2.1 supplies the core/dual relationship for that baseline. This does not claim that the paper’s two-good examples have the same answer.

The paper’s Proposition 4.1 and Proposition 4.3 are named structural results, but they do not provide comparable computational anchors, so I would not use them to pad the case. They do suggest follow-up questions: in the continuum two-good model, when does an optimal dual-price representation capture the whole core, and what happens to the paper’s core/dual gap examples when masses replace individual counts?

My weakest point is substantial: the anchor is background, the mirror covers only the one-house case, and the paper’s central two-good contributions are examples rather than named complexity results. Also, the stated question concerns type-symmetric payoffs; it does not characterize every possibly asymmetric payoff vector in the expanded finite population. So this is a credible, tractable continuous mirror of the paper’s baseline, but not evidence that the paper’s main two-good phenomena themselves continuize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper supplies no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 2.1 is a core/dual characterization attributed to earlier work; the paper’s own results concern when the two-good game’s core is nonempty or when core payoffs can be represented by optimal dual variables. None states the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous core-membership algorithm would be a new computational question prompted by the paper’s background, rather than a mirror of a computational result in it.

That distinction matters for the proposed anchor. Its housing-class model replaces the paper’s individually identified houses and arbitrary valuation matrix with interchangeable classes and repeated valuation types. That can be a sensible high-multiplicity assignment market, but it is a different model. The resulting question—whether a proposed type-symmetric payoff lies in the core—is a plausible problem to study; it is not what Theorem 2.1 asks, and its algorithmic framing is added by the proponent.

The same issue limits a stronger two-good mirror. One could define a continuum of agents with types recording endowments and valuations, then ask about core membership or dual representations. But the paper gives structural claims and small counterexamples, not computational bounds for those tasks. Turning them into complexity questions requires choosing a new input, decision problem, and representation of coalitions and allocations. That is worthwhile only if the programme is willing to take up new cooperative-game computation, rather than continuize computational results this paper actually reports.

This is a weak case for the universal negative. The proposed continuum assignment problem is coherent, and the paper’s two-good setting can also be modeled with type-level mass flows. I cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror under the programme’s broader standard. The defensible negative conclusion is narrower: this paper itself offers no computational result whose continuous mirror it clearly motivates; the strongest candidate is an adjacent, newly formulated problem.
