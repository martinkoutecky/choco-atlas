What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the offline monopolistic price-of-anarchy bound; it leaves the online analysis, competition model, and experiments aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named result saying that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or another complexity class. Its closest named computationally relevant anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: a bound on the efficiency loss caused by the selfish matching objective. I would use that as a modest anchor, while being clear that it is a welfare guarantee, not a complexity classification.

The mirror I find credible is a platform with many users drawn from a relatively small set of exact profile types. A type must capture everything the model uses: a man’s compatibility with each woman type and his return-probability function, or a woman’s compatibility with each man type. For example, this could describe a large platform whose matching scores and engagement model use a finite set of standardized profiles. If there are millions of users and hundreds of such types, the population is large relative to the support. Users within a type are interchangeable under the model.

Here is the continuous problem. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite sets of men’s and women’s types, with per-side masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to one. Let \(w_{ab}\in[0,1]\) be the match quality for type pair \((a,b)\), and let \(q_a:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) be type \(a\)’s return-probability function. A feasible matching is a mass coupling \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\). Each type-\(a\) man’s expected utility is \(u_a=\sum_b w_{ab}x_{ab}/\mu_a\). The fair plan maximizes \(\sum_a\mu_a u_a\); the selfish plan maximizes \(\sum_a\mu_a\pi_a(u_a)\), where \(\pi_a(u)=q_a(u)/(1+q_a(u))\), as in the paper. The solution is the two optimal plans and the ratio of fair welfare under the selfish plan to optimal fair welfare. Each individual still receives at most one partner; \(x_{ab}\) records how much population mass is matched across a type pair.

The anchor is Theorem 1. For return functions satisfying the paper’s assumptions \(q_i(0)=q_i(1)=0\), twice differentiability, and strict concavity, define \(H(\mathbf q)=\max_i q_i'(0)>0\). Let \(c>0\) solve \(c=H(\mathbf q)L(\mathbf q,c)/2\), where \(L(\mathbf q,c)=\min_i\overline{u}_i(c)\), and \(\overline{u}_i(c)\) is the unique positive solution of \(c=\pi_{i,1}'(\overline{u}_i(c))=q_i'(\overline{u}_i(c))/(1+q_i(\overline{u}_i(c)))^2\). The theorem states that the worst-case price of anarchy satisfies \(\PoA(\mathbf q)\ge L(\mathbf q,c)/2\), a bound depending on the return functions but not on the number of men. This is proved here, with the proof in the supplementary text.

I expect the type-mass version of the matching problem to be Class A for computing the plans on a supplied instance: the fair objective is a linear program, and the selfish objective is concave maximization over the same transport polytope. With explicit response functions and standard evaluation access, approximate solutions should be accessible by continuous optimization. Theorem 1’s guarantee should also lift to the type-mass setting: changing unit capacities to type masses changes the right-hand sides of the flow constraints, while the dual and KKT argument has the same structure. That extension should be proved, not assumed. A further question is whether the *tight* worst-case ratio over all compatibility matrices can be computed efficiently; Theorem 1 supplies a lower bound, not an algorithm for finding that exact worst case. The mirror covers the paper’s offline monopolistic result only.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves their matching constraints, their two objectives, and their price-of-anarchy comparison. The continuity is in the society’s type masses and the resulting per-type capacities—not merely in the randomized assignment variables already present in the paper. It also creates natural follow-ups: how the tight ratio depends on the type distributions, how finite populations approximate the mass model, and whether an online arrival model has a useful continuum analogue.

My weakest point is the exactness of the types. If compatibility scores or return behavior vary idiosyncratically across users, grouping them would change the model; a large population alone does not justify that grouping. The finite-profile platform scenario is plausible only when the platform actually uses shared profile-level scores and response curves. Also, the paper already states that its finite offline problems can be solved by LP and convex programming, and its theorem already gives a population-size-independent bound. So the positive case is that the continuous population model is a faithful and useful type-level formulation—not that it unlocks a complexity gain the paper’s theorem currently lacks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named anchor is Theorem 1, and it bounds a welfare ratio; it does not classify a computational problem or establish an algorithmic running time. The paper says in prose that its offline objectives can be optimized by linear and convex programming, but the theorem itself is about the price of anarchy. That makes it a poor anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The strongest mirror is the finite-profile platform the other analyst describes. It is a coherent population model: each type has shared match scores and a shared return function, and \(x_{ab}\) records mass matched between types. It is also genuinely a population continuization; the paper’s individual assignment probabilities alone do not make its society continuous. But on this model, Theorem 1’s proof carries over through the same matching constraints and KKT argument. That could merit a welfare-theorem note. It does not expose a computational question the paper leaves unresolved: the paper already treats its offline optimization as polynomial-time solvable, and the mirrored problem is an explicit type-level convex program.

A stronger proposal would ask for the exact worst-case price of anarchy over compatibility matrices and type distributions. That may be interesting, but it adds an outer optimization problem and requires finite input encodings for the response functions. The paper supplies neither as a computational problem. Once those choices are made, the result would be a new robust-welfare project prompted by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The real-world fit is also narrow. In dating, match scores depend on pairs of people’s features, and return behaviour can vary by individual; exact shared profiles and response curves may therefore leave few people per type. Finite profile classes are plausible in a standardized platform, but the paper does not motivate them, and approximate buckets would require a separate error analysis.

This is not an airtight case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The finite-type scenario is sensible, and I would not reject it on the grounds that it merely changes fractional assignments into masses. My case against is narrower: the paper offers a welfare guarantee rather than the kind of computational result ChoCo is meant to map, while its best mirror appears to be a direct extension of that guarantee. If ChoCo also wants to study continuous welfare bounds, this paper has a viable mirror; on the programme’s stated computational remit, the negative case is stronger.
