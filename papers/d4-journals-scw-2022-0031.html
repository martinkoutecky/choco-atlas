The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) resident types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and \(K\) room categories with rational supplies \(q_a>0\), \(\sum_a q_a=1\), let each unit of type \(t\) have utility \(u_t(r_a,a)=v_{ta}-r_a-\rho_t\max\{0,r_a-b_t\}\), where \(v_{ta},b_t,\rho_t\) are rational and \(\rho_t\) comes from a fixed finite set of size \(k\). Room categories consist of identical room copies. Given rational per-capita total rent \(m\), choose rents \(r_a\) and assignment masses \(x_{ta}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{ta}=q_a\), and \(\sum_a q_a r_a=m\), such that \(x_{ta}>0\) implies \(u_t(r_a,a)\ge u_t(r_b,b)\) for every category \(b\). Compute an envy-free assignment and rents maximizing \(\min_t \max_a u_t(r_a,a)\); determine whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(\tau,K,L\) for fixed \(k\), where \(L\) is the binary input length.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population split among \(\tau\) preference and soft-budget types is assigned whole rooms from \(K\) categories with per-capita supplies; choose category rents and type-to-category assignment masses to maximize the minimum type utility subject to envy-freeness and total rent \(m\).

The objection that survived

The theorem's polynomial bound is in the explicitly listed number of agents; expanding binary-encoded masses may be exponentially larger than \((\tau,K,L)\), and the paper's perturbation proof does not establish a support-polynomial bound.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For a rent division economy \(e=(N,A,u,m)\) with \(u\in B^N\), and lists of positive affine transformations \(f_i\) for \(i\in S\subseteq N\) and \(g_a\) for \(a\in C\subseteq A\), it gives polynomial algorithms that compute an envy-free allocation maximizing the minimum transformed utility over \(S\), minimizing the maximum transformed utility over \(S\), maximizing the minimum transformed rent over \(C\), or minimizing the maximum transformed rent over \(C\). Here \(B\) is the paper’s budget-constrained piecewise-linear preference domain, with marginal disutility parameters drawn from a fixed finite set. The lead case for a mirror is the first one with \(S=N\) and every \(f_i\) the identity: compute a maxmin-utility envy-free allocation.

A plausible large-scale setting is a university or housing provider assigning many residents to a recurring menu of room categories. Residents’ types are their complete preference and budget records: a value \(v_{ta}\) for each room category \(a\), a soft budget \(b_t\), and a marginal-disutility parameter \(\rho_t\). People of one type share all these parameters. A standardized intake form could produce a large population but a much smaller support of types. That scenario is more convincing than treating a small roommate group as a limit object.

Here is the corresponding problem to hand to a prover. Call it Soft-Budget Maxmin Envy-Free Rent Division\(_\infty\). An instance gives a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau\) supported types, rational room-category supplies \(q_a\) summing to one, a per-capita aggregate rent \(m\), and rational type parameters \(v_{ta},b_t,\rho_t\), with \(\rho_t\) drawn from the fixed finite slope set. Type \(t\)’s utility for category \(a\) at rent \(r_a\) is
\[ u_t(r_a,a)=v_{ta}-r_a-\rho_t\max\{0,r_a-b_t\}. \]
Choose rents \(r_a\) and assignment masses \(x_{ta}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{ta}=q_a\), and \(\sum_a q_a r_a=m\). Each unit of mass gets one whole room and pays its rent; only the aggregate assignment is fractional. Require envy-freeness: whenever \(x_{ta}>0\), type \(t\) weakly prefers \((r_a,a)\) to every offered bundle \((r_b,b)\). Return an envy-free \((x,r)\) that maximizes the minimum utility of any supported type, together with that optimum value. The objective is well-defined because envy-freeness makes all room categories assigned to a given type equally good for that type.

This is a population continuization: \(\mu_t\) is the share of residents of type \(t\), rather than a count of named roommates. Room-category supplies scale with the population, while each resident still receives an integral room. That supply change is part of making the same rent-division question meaningful at continuum scale. The finite input is described by its support, not by listing every resident. A useful complexity target is a bit-polynomial algorithm in \(\tau\), the number \(K\) of room categories, and the total rational input length \(L\), for fixed slope-set size \(k\).

I expect this mirror to be Class A, though that is a conjecture for the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The paper’s maxmin algorithms rely on perturbing rents and finding a maximum-weight matching. With repeated types and room categories, the analogous assignment step is a capacitated transportation problem, which is polynomially solvable; the finite set of budget slopes also suggests a finite progress argument may survive. The resulting question is whether the rest of the paper’s structural argument can be made to run on binary-encoded masses and supplies. Expanding those masses into individual agents and applying the paper’s \(n\)-agent algorithm would not answer it: \(n\) can be exponentially larger than the compressed input length.

The mirror covers the maxmin-utility selection, not every selection in Theorem 1. It also generates a high-multiplicity bridge question: when a discrete market has type counts \(n_t\) and room counts \(n_a\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), \(q_a=n_a/n\), and scaling total rent to per-capita rent gives the natural comparison instance. How close is the continuous optimum to the discrete optimum, and can an envy-free continuous solution be rounded with a useful additive guarantee? The paper contains no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution claim.

The weakest point is that the paper’s theorem is about individually listed agents and rooms, and its proof uses perfect matchings. Replacing those with masses and capacities preserves the economic question plausibly, but it does not automatically preserve the rent-monotonicity and perturbation arguments—or establish bit-polynomial complexity. That is the main thing the opposing case can press. The authors should nevertheless recognize the proposed problem: it asks for the same envy-free rent division and the same maxmin selection, in a large cohort where preferences and budget parameters repeat.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it gives polynomial algorithms for selections of the envy-free set, including maxmin utility. The strongest case against mirroring it is that the paper’s allocation problem is built around a bijection between \(n\) named agents and \(n\) distinct rooms. Its input gives each agent a value for each room, and its output can choose a separate rent for each room. In the paper’s roommate setting, that is a small, heterogeneous market, not obviously a high-multiplicity one.

The proponent’s larger housing-market model addresses that concern by scaling room supply and grouping rooms into categories. But grouping copies under one category rent restricts the rent vectors available in the paper’s theorem. Keeping rooms separately priced preserves more of the original model, but can leave the number of room types growing with the population. That makes the proposed compressed regime less automatic than the argument suggests.

This is not a decisive objection, though. A provider with many residents, repeated room categories, and category-level rents is a plausible setting. Fractional assignment mass can describe groups of residents each receiving a whole room, and envy-freeness and maxmin utility are meaningful at the type level. The resulting problem asks for computational results on a continuous population; it is not merely an outcome-space or axiomatic continuization. A transportation problem for the assignment step may even make the algorithmic question promising.

So the negative case is weak. The category model changes the paper’s feasible rent choices, but the programme allows co-modifications that make a population-scale problem meaningful. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror of Theorem 1 across plausible scenarios.
