The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the welfare-constrained equilibrium question in Theorem 9. The paper proves here that deciding whether a synchronisation game has a Nash equilibrium with social welfare at least \(L\) is NP-complete; its proof is sketched as a reduction from hypergraph \(K\)-colouring. This is a better lead than a mere existence result: it asks which equilibrium outcomes a society can support, not just whether some equilibrium exists.

I would model a large population of interchangeable units choosing projects, standards, or coalition labels. A type \(t\) completely specifies a unit’s available colours \(C_t\), its exposure to each kind of interaction, and the payoff weights it faces. Let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the population. There are many units but relatively few types: for instance, many suppliers or relief organisations in a few roles repeatedly form teams according to a modest list of interaction templates. A template \(h\) specifies the types in a group, its per-unit population rate \(\rho_h\), and the reward \(w_h(c)\) if the group all chooses colour \(c\). Groups are formed by matching units from the specified type pools. This preserves the paper’s local, hyperedge-based coordination rule while allowing repeated, exchangeable interactions.

Let \(q_{t,c}\) be the fraction of type \(t\) choosing \(c\), with \(\sum_{c\in C_t}q_{t,c}=1\). These fractions describe a population split among pure choices; no individual receives a fractional colour. If template \(h\) has type \(t_{h,j}\) in slot \(j\), a type-\(t\) unit’s expected payoff from choosing \(c\) is

\[ u_t(c;q)=\sum_h\sum_{j:t_{h,j}=t}\frac{\rho_h}{\mu_t}\,w_h(c)\prod_{\ell\ne j}q_{t_{h,\ell},c}. \]

A population Nash equilibrium is a \(q\) such that \(q_{t,c}>0\) only when \(c\) maximises \(u_t(c;q)\) over \(C_t\). Its per-capita welfare is \(W(q)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_c q_{t,c}u_t(c;q)\).

Lead problem — Mass-Template Synchronisation Welfare-Threshold Equilibrium. Given rational type masses, colour sets, template rates and weights, and a rational threshold \(\ell\), decide whether there is a population Nash equilibrium \(q\) with \(W(q)\ge\ell\). A yes-instance has an exact equilibrium allocation \(q\) meeting the threshold. The paper’s total-welfare cutoff becomes a per-capita cutoff here; for a finite population of size \(n\), the corresponding threshold is \(\ell=L/n\).

My initial expectation for unrestricted templates is Class C: continuum-specific hardness, plausibly of the existential-real kind. The equilibrium conditions and welfare bound form a system of polynomial equalities and inequalities in the \(q_{t,c}\); the all-members-agree payoff produces products of type-level masses. That makes the continuous question well-posed, but does not turn it into the LP that appears in the programme’s scoring-rule examples. The paper’s hypergraph-colouring reduction is not itself a hardness proof for this mirror: population splitting changes the equilibrium question. Whether hardness survives when each type has large mass, or dissolves under useful structural restrictions, is precisely worth asking. Natural follow-ups include whether bounded template arity or acyclic template structure yields tractability, and whether a discrete-to-continuous welfare identity holds in any restricted regime.

A second anchor is Theorem 8, also proved in this paper, with a proof sketch reducing from Local Max-Cut: finding a Nash equilibrium in a synchronisation game with only two colours is PLS-complete. Its continuous question is Two-Colour Mass-Template Synchronisation Equilibrium Search: given the same typed-population model with \(|M|=2\), return an exact population Nash equilibrium \(q\). Algebraic coordinates can be represented exactly. My prior is again Class C for unrestricted templates, because the equilibrium conditions are nonlinear complementarity conditions; the discrete PLS-completeness result alone does not settle this. In particular, a continuum algorithm may find a fractional population equilibrium even when finding a discrete equilibrium is PLS-hard. Further questions are whether such fractional equilibria can be found efficiently, when one can always find an equilibrium with each type using a single colour, and which template restrictions make exact search tractable.

The scenario is plausible as a scale-up of the paper’s distributed team and humanitarian-coordination settings: the types preserve feasible actions and payoff roles, while template matching represents repeated interactions among large pools of comparable units. The paper’s exact hyperedges, synchronization rewards, and unilateral best-response notion remain recognisable. These mirrors cover its Nash-equilibrium computation and welfare-threshold results; they do not claim to continuize the strong-equilibrium results or the whole paper.

The weak point is that the paper studies a fixed hypergraph of named players, while this mirror uses exchangeable template interactions and expected synchronization payoffs. That changes how incidence is represented, and the authors might regard it as a different game family. I think it is a defensible co-modification for a large, repeatedly matched population, rather than continuity of the colour or the outcome. If fixed, named-neighbour structure is essential to the authors’ problem, the mirror loses force; the type-template version is strongest where repeated role-based coalition formation is already a natural instance regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to both anchors is that the paper’s input is a particular hypergraph, and its computational questions depend on that specific incidence structure. A player’s payoff depends on whether the *particular* hyperedges containing them are unicoloured. Aggregate counts of players by action, or even counts by local role, do not determine who shares edges with whom or how those edges overlap. For arbitrary instances, including those used in the reductions behind Theorems 8 and 9, preserving that information can require a separate type for nearly every vertex.

The proposed template model supplies the missing information by changing it: groups are repeatedly and independently matched from type pools. Its products of type-level action fractions describe expected coordination payoffs under that random matching. The result is a coherent population game, but its equilibrium conditions concern an expected-payoff allocation among exchangeable roles. The paper’s results concern pure strategy profiles on a fixed network. In particular, Theorem 8’s local-search landscape and Theorem 9’s coloring constraints depend on the fixed edges; neither follows from the template rates. That makes this a substantial change to the game family, not a high-multiplicity representation of the paper’s instances.

The obvious alternatives expose the tradeoff. If one repeats disconnected copies of a fixed finite hypergraph, the copies’ equilibria separate, and a welfare threshold can be decided by choosing the best equilibrium in each copy. If one repeats a connected structure with overlapping groups, type masses alone lose the correlations among neighboring choices; retaining them requires a richer object, such as a distribution over local configurations. That may support interesting work, but it no longer gives the paper’s finite-type population model.

This case is weakest against the proponent’s specific scenario. Large pools of role-equivalent agents who are repeatedly rematched into teams are plausible, and the resulting equilibrium and welfare-threshold questions are well-defined computational problems. I can argue that they are new questions about random-matching population games rather than close mirrors of Theorems 8 and 9; I cannot honestly argue that they are not worth studying if the authors recognize that setting as part of their problem. The negative case therefore rules out the most faithful mirror of the paper, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
