What the mirror covers

The proposed restatement addresses Theorems 1 and 2; Theorem 4 varies the state space while retaining finitely many agents, and the remaining extensions are not mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: a social choice function is implementable in mixed-strategy Nash equilibria by a finite mechanism if and only if it satisfies Maskin monotonicity. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the corresponding characterization for social choice correspondences when there are at least three agents. These are substantial implementation results, but neither asserts a computational complexity classification. The paper contains no named result establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a comparable class. So it has no qualifying computational anchor for the requested case.

The obvious large-population setting would have many agents whose preferences fall into a small number of types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of agents of type \(t\). But turning the paper’s theorems into questions about implementing a social choice rule over \(\mu\) would require specifying a new strategic model. In an atomless population, one person’s deviation does not change aggregate reports; allowing a positive mass to deviate changes the equilibrium concept. The paper’s mechanism instead relies on deviations and cross-checks by a fixed set of individually consequential agents. I cannot give a continuous problem for either theorem that preserves that question and is ready to hand to a prover.

Theorem 4 does not supply the missing anchor: it extends implementation to a Polish, or in a further case compact and continuous, *state space* while retaining the finite set of agents. That is continuity of the state, not the population, and it remains an implementation existence result rather than a complexity result.

Thus the honest positive case is only that large societies with few shared preference types offer a conceivable setting in which implementation theory might be recast. This paper does not establish that mirror. Its theorems are meaningful anchors for implementation research, but they do not support the requested computational continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that Theorems 1 and 2 provide no computational result to continuize. They characterize when a social choice function or correspondence is implementable in a finite-agent game. The paper gives no encoded decision or optimization problem, input-size measure, or complexity bound. Turning the characterization into “compute an implementing mechanism” or “minimize its messages or transfers” would define a new computational project, rather than mirror a result in this paper.

The best population version would give agents a finite set of preference types \(T\), represent society by rational masses \(\mu\), and let the social choice rule depend on \(\mu\). That makes multiplicity plausible, but it changes the strategic hinge of both theorems. Their mechanisms depend on individual deviations, cross-checks between named agents’ reports, and transfers targeted to those agents. In an anonymous atomless model, one person’s report leaves the aggregate unchanged, so the whistle-blower argument no longer works as stated. Treating a whole type group as a strategic player, or allowing coordinated positive-mass deviations, would supply different agents or a different equilibrium concept. Those could motivate new research, but they do not preserve the paper’s implementation problem.

Theorem 2 inherits the same obstacle: requiring each selected outcome to arise at a pure-strategy equilibrium does not provide a computational task or resolve how a continuum of individuals can make consequential deviations. Theorem 4 is also no population mirror. It makes the state space continuous while keeping a finite set of agents; the paper itself distinguishes that extension from its finite-agent results.

I cannot rule out every worthwhile model that combines a continuous population with implementation. The negative case is narrower: this paper supplies no computational anchor for ChoCo, and its most natural mass-based restatement loses the individual incentives on which its main results rely. A computational study of implementation in a newly specified population game might be worthwhile, but it would be a new problem to propose, not a continuous mirror of these results.
