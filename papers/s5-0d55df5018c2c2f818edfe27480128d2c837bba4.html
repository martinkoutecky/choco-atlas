The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(X,Y\), positive rational masses \(a_x,b_y\) summing to \(1\), complete strict rankings of \(Y\) for each \(x\in X\), and a rational target flow \(z\geq 0\) with row sums \(a_x\) and column sums \(b_y\), can one choose a complete strict reported ranking \(\rho_y\) of \(X\) for each \(y\in Y\) such that mass-proposing deferred acceptance produces exactly \(z\)? In mass deferred acceptance, unmatched mass proposes to its best type not yet rejected; each receiving type keeps the highest-ranked incoming mass up to its capacity and rejects the remainder. Output such reports if they exist, or determine that none exist.

The model it lives in

Represent each side as a finite set of preference types carrying rational mass. The proposing types have fixed rankings over receiving types; the decision variables are one reported ranking per receiving type; the objective is feasibility of the target type-to-type matching flow under mass deferred acceptance.

The objection that survived

Each responding cohort must share one report, whereas the theorem allows women to report independently; the target flow also forgets which named agents are paired.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the main report-realizability theorem and its construction over type cohorts; it does not cover the paper’s cited incentive results or application discussion.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is there a weighted component or flow characterization of attainable target flows, and can it be computed efficiently in the number of types and mass bit length?
  • Does the continuous algorithm have an outcome independent of proposal order?
  • How does attainability change if members of one cohort may use different reports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a type-cohort version of the paper’s target-realizability problem. Picture a large two-sided clearinghouse, such as graduate hiring, with perhaps hundreds of thousands of participants but only a few dozen or hundred recurring preference classes. A type is a cohort with the same ranking of the other side’s types. Its mass is its fraction of the population. This is a co-modification of the paper’s model: agents within a type are interchangeable, so rankings are over cohorts and a matching may split a cohort’s mass.

Here is a prover-ready problem. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be finite sets of proposing-side and responding-side types, with positive rational masses \(a_x\) and \(b_y\) summing to \(1\). Each \(x\in X\) has a complete strict ranking of \(Y\). The target is a rational matrix \(z\geq 0\) with row sums \(a_x\) and column sums \(b_y\). Continuous Type-Cohort DA Attainability asks: can we choose one complete strict reported ranking \(\rho_y\) of \(X\) for each \(y\in Y\), so that mass-proposing deferred acceptance produces exactly \(z\)? In the mass version of DA, unmatched mass proposes to its best cohort that has not rejected it; each receiving cohort keeps its highest-ranked incoming mass up to its capacity and rejects the remainder. A solution is the list of reports \((\rho_y)_{y\in Y}\), or “none exists.” The cohort-uniform report is a deliberate group-manipulation regime: everyone in a type submits the same list.

My anchor is the paper’s main “Theorem :”, printed without a number in §3 and proved there. For individual men’s complete lists \(L_m\) and a target marriage \(\mu\), it builds \(G(L_m,\mu)\) on the men and women: each woman \(w\) has an arc to \(\mu(w)\), and each man \(m\) has an arc to every woman he prefers to \(\mu(m)\), as well as to \(\mu(m)\). The theorem says that women’s complete lists making men-proposing deferred acceptance output \(\mu\) exist if and only if every minimal strong component of this graph consists of exactly one matched pair. The paper then gives an \(O(n^2)\) construction algorithm from this condition, using strong-component decomposition. This is the paper’s computational anchor; it contains no comparable hardness result.

I expect the type-cohort problem to be tractable (Class A), perhaps through a weighted component or flow characterization whose running time depends on \(|X|,|Y|\) and the mass bit length \(L\), rather than on the population size. That is a forecast, not a transfer of the theorem: partial matches within a type and fractional capacities may change its condition. The useful open questions are whether an efficient characterization exists, whether the mass-DA outcome is independent of proposal order, and whether allowing a cohort to split among different reports changes the complexity.

The mirror covers only the paper’s question of realizing a specified outcome by choosing the nonproposing side’s reports. It does not cover the paper’s cited results about incentives or the application discussion. Its weakest point is the co-modification itself: the paper ranks individuals and targets a bijection; this mirror ranks types and targets a mass assignment. In addition, cohort-uniform reports restrict the paper’s freedom to choose a different list for each woman. Those are substantial changes, so the paper’s graph criterion cannot be claimed to solve the mirror. The positive case is that the underlying question—whether reports on one side can make deferred acceptance produce a specified matching—remains recognizable, and the cohort regime gives it a credible high-multiplicity setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s §3 theorem is a genuine computational anchor: it characterizes when women’s reports can make deferred acceptance produce a specified marriage, and yields an \(O(n^2)\) construction. The strongest objection is that its object is unusually identity-sensitive. The input gives each man a complete ranking of named women, the target \(\mu\) specifies named pairs, and the construction chooses a separate list for every woman. Aggregating agents into cohorts and targeting a mass matrix \(z\) can merge distinct target marriages and restrict the reporting choices. It therefore does not preserve the paper’s exact problem.

That objection also applies to the proponent’s uniform-report model: asking each cohort to use one list turns individual report construction into a group-report problem. Allowing a cohort to split its mass across reports is the stronger repair. But it still targets a type-level flow, not the named marriage in the theorem. Retaining named partners and exact target pairs, meanwhile, makes identity part of the relevant type; as the population grows, rankings over those named partners do not give a fixed finite type space.

This is the best case against the anchor, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The programme explicitly allows matching types and fractional type-level outcomes, and the inverse question—whether reports by one side can realize a prescribed aggregate matching—remains recognizably close to the theorem. I can defend a fidelity objection to the exact mirror; I cannot honestly turn it into a convincing universal case against every continuous mirror.
