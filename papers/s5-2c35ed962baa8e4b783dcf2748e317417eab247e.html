The continuous mirror question

Given finite buyer-type support \(\{(\theta_a,\mu_a)\}_{a=1}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\), item-feature vectors \(\psi_\ell\), rates \(\kappa_\ell\) items per buyer, and supply \(s\) per physical item, set \(v_{a\ell}=\theta_a^\top\psi_\ell\). Compute the mass-level optimizer \(z\ge0\) of \(\max \sum_a\mu_a\log(\sum_\ell\kappa_\ell v_{a\ell}z_{a\ell})\), subject to \(\sum_a\mu_a z_{a\ell}\le s\). Interpret \(z_{a\ell}/N\) as each type member’s allocation of each physical \(\ell\)-item, and characterize when the resulting prices support CEEI and how the finite-\(N\) AMO-EG markets converge to this mass solution.

The model it lives in

A finite distribution \(\mu\) over latent buyer vectors \(\theta_a\), with item-feature classes \(\psi_\ell\) appearing at rates \(\kappa_\ell\) and supply \(s\) per physical item. The variable \(z_{a\ell}\) is the scaled expected allocation to a member of type \(a\); maximize \(\sum_a\mu_a\log(\sum_\ell\kappa_\ell(\theta_a^\top\psi_\ell)z_{a\ell})\) subject to \(\sum_a\mu_a z_{a\ell}\le s\). A dual value \(q_\ell\) corresponds to per-item price \(p_\ell=q_\ell/\kappa_\ell\); finite-market per-item allocations are \(z_{a\ell}/N\), so their AMO caps eventually become slack.

The objection that survived

With \(\kappa_\ell N\) physical items and fixed supply per item, \(z_{a\ell}\le N\) becomes slack for bounded allocations, so the limit loses the AMO-specific multipliers; the paper does not establish its CEEI guarantee under the opponent’s fixed-item, per-capita-supply repair.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a low-rank continuous-population version of AMO-MNW. The paper’s agents remain finite in each instance, but its theory already describes preferences through latent types and studies what happens as the market grows. That gives a credible route from individual buyers to a society represented by type masses.

The paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a similar complexity class. The closest named computational anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(\epsilon,\delta>0\), there is a threshold \(\bar N\) such that, when \(N>\bar N\) in the paper’s low-rank market model, the probability that the AMO-EG allocation and its supply multipliers form a \(\delta\)-approximate CEEI exceeds \(1-\delta\). In the paper’s definition, this means each buyer’s allocated utility is within \(\delta\) of their best utility at the induced prices. The theorem is proved here using the approximate-twin argument and concentration; it is not a cited result. The paper presents AMO-EG as a convex program that can be solved efficiently, but Theorem 2 itself is an equilibrium guarantee, not a formal running-time theorem.

Call the mirror Low-Rank Mass AMO-MNW/CEEI. An instance has buyer types \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_\tau\in\mathbb R^d_+\), rational masses \(\mu_a>0\) summing to \(1\), item-feature types \(\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_\sigma\in\mathbb R^d_+\), and rational rates \(\kappa_\ell>0\) of items per buyer. Type \(a\) has valuation \(v_{a\ell}=\theta_a\cdot\psi_\ell\) for each item feature and the same budget, AMO constraint, and other relevant preferences as every other member of that type. Each item has supply \(s\).

The decision variable is \(z_{a\ell}\ge 0\): the scaled expected allocation of feature-\(\ell\) items to a type-\(a\) buyer. In a finite realization with \(N\) buyers and about \(\kappa_\ell N\) items of feature \(\ell\), each such buyer receives an expected fraction \(z_{a\ell}/N\) of each item in that class. The per-item supply constraints are \(\sum_a\mu_a z_{a\ell}\le s\), and type \(a\)’s utility is \(U_a(z)=\sum_\ell\kappa_\ell v_{a\ell}z_{a\ell}\). The problem is to maximize the mass-weighted Nash welfare \(\sum_a\mu_a\log U_a(z)\) and find supporting prices \(p_\ell\). A solution should give an optimal, or \(\eta\)-optimal, feasible \(z\), with each supported item class cleared, and prices for which every type’s utility is within \(\delta\) of its best utility subject to the unit budget \(\sum_\ell\kappa_\ell p_\ell z_\ell\le 1\).

This scaling preserves the paper’s AMO interpretation: each actual person receives at most one of each individual item, while type-level averages can be fractional. The feature-class allocation \(z_{a\ell}/N\) respects the per-person cap once \(N\) is large enough. Mass \(\mu_a\) is a population share, not a count of named buyers. Thus the population is the continuous object; the fractional lotteries in the paper are only part of how its allocations are represented.

A plausible regime is a large recommender or advertising market with a fixed low latent dimension \(d\), many more users than preference clusters, and a catalog whose size grows proportionally with the user population. Users in a cluster share the same complete preference and budget description; bins of near-identical latent vectors provide the approximate-twin regime used by the paper. The paper’s small MovieLens instance suggests the application, while its HBS course market of 936 students and 93 courses is not itself the compelling large-market regime.

For explicit finite supports, I expect this mirror to be Class A: it is a concave maximization problem over \(\tau\sigma\) variables with linear supply constraints, and its prices come from the corresponding dual variables. An approximate solution should be accessible by convex optimization; a formal bit-complexity bound would need the usual positivity, boundedness, and accuracy assumptions. The mirror covers Theorem 2 and the AMO-EG allocation computation. It does not cover the paper’s experimental search for profitable misreports or establish complexity results about that search.

The natural follow-up is whether the same guarantee and algorithm extend from rational finite supports to arbitrary latent-type distributions given by an oracle or density representation, with complexity expressed in \(d\), support description, and accuracy. A second question is to give explicit finite-\(N\) convergence bounds linking the mass solution’s prices and regret to the sampled AMO-EG market.

My weakest point is that AMO-EG is already a convex program in the finite model, and Theorem 2 already studies its large-market behavior. This mirror therefore does not show discrete hardness disappearing; the paper supplies no named hardness result to test. Its positive case is narrower: the authors’ low-rank types, twin condition, and growing market make the distribution-input allocation problem a recognizable continuation of their own model, even if the underlying optimization is already tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the best anchor, but it is an asymptotic equilibrium guarantee, not a complexity result. The theorem says that a randomly generated *finite* market’s AMO-EG allocation is likely to be an approximate CEEI once the market is large. The paper gives no named bound on the running time or bit complexity of computing that allocation. Its other named results, including Theorem 3, concern asymptotic equilibrium or incentives. So the proposed mirror has to supply a computational question of its own; Theorem 2 does not supply one.

For the proposed low-rank mirror, that question loses the feature that makes the paper’s model AMO. With \(\kappa_\ell N\) copies of item feature \(\ell\), fixed supply \(s\) per item, and a type of positive mass \(\mu_a\), the symmetric allocation to one type member per item is \(z_{a\ell}/N\). The AMO constraint becomes \(z_{a\ell}\leq N\), so it vanishes for bounded per-capita allocations. The remaining finite-support problem is a weighted EG program over \(\tau\sigma\) variables. That is a coherent continuous-population problem, but its tractability comes from the ordinary EG structure; it does not expose a new pricing problem or a computational boundary for AMO. This is the strongest objection to the mirror as stated: it carries over the large-market equilibrium conclusion by dropping the cap whose multipliers motivated the paper.

There is a stronger AMO-preserving version: hold the item set fixed, give item \(j\) supply \(\rho_j\) per capita, and optimize over type allocations \(x_{aj}\in[0,1]\) subject to \(\sum_a\mu_a x_{aj}\leq\rho_j\). A growing course cohort with seats scaling to enrollment is a plausible setting, and the resulting program retains both mass and the per-person cap. But the paper’s theorems do not analyze that supply scaling, and with finite type support the new optimization is still a polynomial-size concave program. That makes it a weak candidate for the programme’s complexity landscape, though “easy” by itself does not make a well-posed mirror unworthy.

Theorem 3 is a poor alternative anchor: in an atomless population, one person’s misreport has zero effect on aggregate prices and allocation, so the unilateral incentive question becomes vacuous. Studying finite-market influence or deviations by positive-mass coalitions would require a different question.

The negative case is therefore strong against the proposed item-rate mirror as a distinctive AMO computation, but it does not establish the requested universal claim. The paper explicitly studies large markets with many twins, and a per-capita-supply course market gives a sensible high-multiplicity regime that preserves AMO. Under the ChoCo standard, that is meaningful evidence for a mirror. I cannot honestly argue that every worthwhile continuous-population version fails.
