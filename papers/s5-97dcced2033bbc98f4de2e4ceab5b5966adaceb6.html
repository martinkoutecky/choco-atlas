The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible named computational anchors in this paper. Its numbered propositions establish mathematical properties and closed forms for the indices, but none asserts a complexity result such as polynomial-time solvability or NP-hardness, and the paper gives no computational encoding that it then uses to solve an underlying problem. The nearest foothold is Proposition 6.3, proved in Appendix A: it gives a closed-form Shapley-Owen coopetition index for symmetric majority games. That is useful evidence that the authors compute specific indices, but it is not a named complexity result under your criteria. So I can’t honestly supply an anchor-based mirror or a predicted complexity class.

A plausible *unanchored* mirror would be Mass-Threshold Coopetition Evaluation. Imagine a large union whose members have identical voting rights, with a proposal passing at quota \(\theta\). A target caucus has mass \(x\), and the whole union has mass \(1\). The regime is many interchangeable members and one relevant type. To make the index precise, include in the instance rational, piecewise-constant probability densities \(p_x\) over split fractions \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and \(q_x\) over external cooperating mass \(y\in[0,1-x]\). Let \(v(z)=1\) if \(z\ge\theta\), and \(0\) otherwise. Ask for the exact value

\[ C_{p,q}(x)= \int_0^{1-x}\int_0^1 q_x(y)p_x(\alpha) \bigl[v(y+x)-v(y+\alpha x)-v(y+(1-\alpha)x)+v(y)\bigr] \,d\alpha\,dy . \]

The solution is the resulting rational index; when its expected group value is nonzero, one could also return the normalized index. This is a direct integral analogue of the paper’s generalized coopetition definition: coalition mass is split into two parts, and the block interaction is averaged over outside mass and internal splits.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous extension of their general \(p,q\) framework. But it is not yet a mirror of their Banzhaf or Shapley-Owen indices: the paper does not derive these mass distributions as limits of the finite random-coalition models. In a one-type threshold game, the integral reduces to a low-dimensional exact calculation. Letting the number of types grow creates a new evaluation problem, whose complexity this paper does not address. The natural further questions are how the finite Banzhaf and Shapley-Owen distributions behave in the high-multiplicity limit, and how exact or approximate evaluation scales with the number of types.

The weakest point is therefore substantial: the candidate is a plausible continuous version of the *index definition*, but the paper supplies no named computational result for it to mirror, and no canonical continuum sampling rule that preserves its two principal indices. The scope of this foothold is limited to evaluating coopetition indices for threshold games; it does not cover the paper’s axiomatic questions or establish that any discrete hardness dissolves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no eligible computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its named propositions establish properties of the indices and formulas for particular games. Proposition 6.3, the strongest candidate, gives a closed form for the Shapley-Owen coopetition index in symmetric majority games; it does not formulate a computational problem, specify an input encoding, or analyze an algorithm’s complexity. A continuous version of that formula might be interesting, but it would not mirror a complexity result from this paper.

The proposed mass-threshold index is a coherent extension of the definition. Large interchangeable voting bodies are a plausible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not reject it on population grounds. But the arbitrary densities \(p_x\) and \(q_x\) choose a new sampling model. Requiring a limit of the paper’s finite indices narrows the choices: Banzhaf sampling concentrates on equal internal splits and halfway external mass, while Shapley-Owen sampling induces specific limiting distributions. Those limits could support a continuous index or a limit calculation. They still supply no computational question that the paper asks or answers.

One could build a stronger algorithmic project around exact or approximate evaluation for succinctly represented, weighted threshold games with rational type masses. That could be worthwhile in its own right. Its complexity would depend on choices the paper leaves open: how the game is represented, how the target coalition is specified, and how coalition splits and outside coalitions are sampled. The resulting work would create a new computational agenda around coopetition indices; it would not continue a computational result in this paper. The Spanish party application is an especially poor population mirror, since its coalition structure concerns a small set of distinct parties. The large-union example repairs that modelling concern, but not the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong against presenting this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror: even its best candidate continues the index definitions, not a complexity result. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise. The mass-threshold extension is plausible; what it fails to establish is that this paper supplies the computational problem for such a project.
