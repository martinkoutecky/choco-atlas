The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_b\), initial opinion masses \(x_{b,o}(0)\) with \(\sum_o x_{b,o}(0)=\mu_b\), a rational \(\beta>0\), type-specific belief costs, a nondecreasing piecewise-constant pressure schedule \(\lambda(t)\), a tolerance \(0<\epsilon<1/2\), a horizon \(H\), and accuracy \(\delta\), compute a \(\delta\)-accurate first time \(t_\epsilon\) at which \(\max(q_0(t),q_1(t))\ge 1-\epsilon\), or report that it exceeds \(H\), under a margin promise at \(H\). Here \(q_o(t)=\sum_b x_{b,o}(t)\), \(p_b(o\mid q,t)\) is the logit probability for opinion \(o\) under per-capita cost \(f_b(\operatorname{dist}(o,b))+\lambda(t)g(1)(1-q_o(t))\), and &#92;dot{x}_{b,o}(t)=\mu_b p_b(o\mid q,t)-x_{b,o}(t)&#92;dot.

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents grouped into types \(b\), with mass \(\mu_b\) and fractional masses \(x_{b,0},x_{b,1}\) holding each opinion. Each cohort follows the paper’s logit response to per-capita belief and disagreement costs; the decision variable is the evolving mass distribution, and the requested output is its first \(\epsilon\)-consensus time to accuracy \(\delta\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow and algorithmic. This paper has no numbered theorem asserting a standard complexity classification such as NP-hardness or membership in P. Its explicit NP-completeness discussion is in prose and cites Auletta et al. (2018); I would not present that as a result proved here. The best anchor for a population mirror is Theorem 4, proved in this paper.

Theorem 4 says: if the social network is a clique, \(S=B=\{0,1\}\), and
\[ \rho^* > M \quad\text{where}\quad M=\max\left\{\frac{f(1)}{g(1)},\frac{3}{\beta g(1)n}, \frac{2\log n}{\beta g(1)n}\right\}, \]
then, from every initial opinion profile \(x\), the logit dynamics reaches consensus in expected time at most \(n^3+k^*\), where \(k^*=\min\{k:\rho(k)>M\}\). Here consensus means that every individual holds the same opinion, and the time counts individual update steps. The theorem is proved here using a comparison to an anonymous process and a birth-and-death chain.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large group deciding the privacy setting for shared online content. Many people participate, but their modeled differences are few: each type is a belief and cost profile, and \(\mu_b\) is the fraction of participants of type \(b\). Participants in a type use the same logit response rule; their current opinions can differ. A clique is plausible when the decision concerns one shared item and all affected people can respond to the same group opinion. Here \(n\) is large while the number \(\tau\) of belief types is much smaller.

The corresponding continuous question, Fluid Logit \(\epsilon\)-Consensus Time, keeps the paper’s binary opinions, beliefs, logit rule, and clique, but tracks mass rather than individuals. Its instance gives rational type masses \(\mu_b\), initial masses \(x_{b,o}(0)\) for \(o\in\{0,1\}\), a rational \(\beta>0\), the belief-cost table \(f(\operatorname{dist}(o,b))\), a nondecreasing piecewise-constant pressure schedule \(\lambda(t)\), a tolerance \(0<\epsilon<1/2\), a horizon \(H\), and an accuracy \(\delta\). Let \(q_o(t)=\sum_b x_{b,o}(t)\). Under the per-capita cost
\[ f(\operatorname{dist}(o,b))+\lambda(t)g(1)(1-q_o(t)), \]
define \(p_b(o\mid q,t)\) by the logit rule over these two costs, and let each infinitesimal cohort update at rate one:
\[ \dot{x}_{b,o}(t)=\mu_b p_b(o\mid q,t)-x_{b,o}(t). \]
The requested solution is a \(\delta\)-accurate first time \(t_\epsilon\) at which \(\max(q_0(t),q_1(t))\ge 1-\epsilon\), or a report that it exceeds \(H\). A margin promise around \(H\) makes the decision version well-defined. This is a genuine population mirror of their convergence question, with the per-capita pressure and \(\epsilon\)-consensus endpoint as natural mass-level choices.

I expect this approximate trajectory problem to be Class A under standard numerical conditioning bounds: the aggregate \(q_1\) obeys a scalar ODE,
\[ \dot q_1=\sum_b \mu_b p_b(1\mid q,t)-q_1, \]
so the solver need not track every agent or even the full type-state vector to determine the consensus threshold. This does not establish a uniform polynomial-time bound for unrestricted parameter magnitudes. The mirror covers Theorem 4’s clique, binary-opinion, logit result; it does not cover the paper’s arbitrary-network results.

The weakest point is that the fluid system does not preserve Theorem 4’s exact target. At finite \(\beta\), both logit choices have positive probability, so a deterministic mass trajectory does not hit exact consensus; in symmetric cases it can remain at a balanced state. The paper’s \(n^3\) hitting bound uses finite-population stochastic fluctuations, which the fluid limit removes. So the authors may accept this as their high-multiplicity *approximate-consensus* question, but not as a direct transfer of their exact-consensus theorem. A finite-population correction that recovers the exact hitting-time behavior would be a valuable next question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the paper’s strongest anchor: it gives an \(n^3\) expected hitting-time bound, so I would not dismiss it as having no named result. And a large group deciding a shared privacy setting could plausibly have many participants but few belief and cost types. The high-multiplicity setting itself is sensible.

The mismatch is in what the theorem measures. Its target is exact unanimity in a finite population, reached by a random sequence of individual updates. The proposed ODE instead describes the typical mass trajectory. For finite \(\beta\) and finite pressure, logit assigns positive probability to either opinion. Thus at \(q_1=0\), \(\dot q_1>0\), and at \(q_1=1\), \(\dot q_1<0\): the continuum trajectory is pushed away from both unanimous states and cannot hit them. The ODE’s \(\epsilon\)-consensus question is well-posed, but it replaces the theorem’s exact finite-population hitting event with an approximate trajectory threshold.

That gap is especially awkward for the clique. Each agent’s social cost sums disagreement over \(n-1\) neighbors. Keeping the paper’s \(\rho\) fixed makes the interaction grow with \(n\); normalizing it to obtain a finite per-capita pressure changes the scale of the logit incentives. Either way, the ODE does not directly preserve the theorem’s regime. One can build a more careful limit, but exact consensus depends on the final mass of size \(1/n\) and on the stochastic updates that remove it. A deterministic population limit discards both. A diffusion or large-deviation correction could retain some finite-size behavior, but then \(n\) remains an essential parameter, and the question becomes stochastic asymptotic analysis rather than computation over a continuous society.

The proponent’s \(\epsilon\)-hitting-time problem is the best repair, but on a clique with binary opinions its aggregate dynamics is a scalar ODE. Computing a threshold-crossing time is then numerical analysis of that ODE, not a complexity landscape with a pricing problem, optimization structure, or meaningful algorithmic parameterization. That places it on the analytic mean-field side of the programme’s stated boundary. The negative case is vulnerable here: an author could still find the approximate hitting-time question useful. But its usefulness would come from studying a new mean-field behavior, not from continuizing the computational result Theorem 4 establishes.
