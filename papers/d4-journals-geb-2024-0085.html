The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchor-based positive case under your rules: this paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity.

The closest results are Proposition 1, which summarizes the PBE predictions attributed to Guarino et al. (2011), and Proposition 2, proved here. Proposition 1 says that good-signal agents invest for every observed investment count; bad-signal agents invest only at \(T_i=2\) when \(n=3\), at \(T_i\ge4\) when \(n=10\), and at \(T_i\ge7\) when \(n=19\). Proposition 2 says that when \(T_i=0\), following one’s signal is a dominant strategy. These are equilibrium and strategic claims, not computational-complexity results. Results 1–7 report experimental and QRE findings. So none qualifies as an anchor, and there is no honest per-anchor prover question to submit.

The strongest nearby research lead is a continuum version of the paper’s equilibrium problem. Imagine a unit mass of doctors considering a treatment in sequence. Each has the same payoff and signal accuracy \(q\), but a private signal \(s\in\{0,1\}\); each observes only the mass \(x\) of earlier doctors who adopted, and does not know their own position. Conditional on the state, the signal-type masses are \(q\) and \(1-q\), reversed across states. A strategy is a pair of rules \(a_s(x)\); each doctor maximizes the probability of choosing the action that matches the state. The question would be: given a rational prior and \(q\), characterize all measurable symmetric pure-strategy equilibria and determine whether they contain a positive-length down-cascade interval or an up-cascade cutoff. The solution is the strategy rules and consistent posterior beliefs.

That is a plausible population mirror: the types are doctors with each signal, mass means their population share, and the observed investment count becomes a fraction. I would tentatively expect a tractable structural answer for threshold strategies, with follow-up questions about uniqueness and convergence of the finite-\(n\) thresholds. But this is an analytic equilibrium question, not the computational question ChoCo targets, and it is not anchored in a named complexity result of this paper.

The soft spot is the continuum limit itself: assuming signal types are evenly spread across every prefix removes finite-sample fluctuations that may matter to social learning. A prover would need to justify that limit and show that the resulting equilibrium question is one the authors would accept as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the anchor level: the proponent names no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is an equilibrium characterization, Proposition 2 is a dominant-strategy claim, and Results 1–7 report experimental and QRE findings. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no paper-specific complexity question to hand a prover; any such question would have to be added.

The proposed continuum equilibrium is plausible as social-learning research, and a population of doctors is a credible high-multiplicity setting. But the proposed task—characterizing measurable equilibria and cascade regions—is analytic. Asking for an algorithm to compute its cutoff does not by itself make it a ChoCo complexity problem: in the paper’s homogeneous binary model, there are only two signal types and a small set of actions, and the manuscript supplies no harder computational task. A stronger version might optimize a health agency’s disclosure policy for a heterogeneous continuum of doctors. That is a reasonable follow-on, but adding many signal types, payoff classes, or disclosure choices to make the algorithmic problem substantial would make it a new model, not a mirror of a named result here.

There is also a real modelling cost in the continuum proposal. The paper’s inference depends on random order, unknown position, and the realized sequence of private signals and actions. Replacing finite prefixes with deterministic masses may remove the fluctuations that drive that inference. One can repair this by retaining a law over ordered histories or random type distributions, but then the object to compute is a stochastic sequential equilibrium, not the paper’s finite-support society distribution. That may be worthwhile work; its computational question and input representation would need to be supplied independently.

So the proponent’s lead does not survive as a computational mirror, and there are no other anchors to rescue. The honest limit of this negative case is that it cannot rule out a worthwhile computational disclosure or equilibrium project inspired by the paper.
