The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) piecewise-uniform preference types over \([0,1]\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supply \(\rho\), and evaluation and cut oracles for each type, determine the minimum worst-case number of type-oracle queries needed to decide whether a finite-menu envy-free assignment exists and output one when it does. The assignment gives fractions \(\pi_t(B)\) of each type mass bundles \(B\), with \(\sum_B\pi_t(B)=1\); every assigned bundle must be weakly preferred by its recipients to every bundle assigned to anyone else, and the supply constraint must hold with equality, \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_B\pi_t(B)\mathbf{1}_{x\in B}=\rho\) almost everywhere.

The model it lives in

A continuum population of total mass \(1\) over \(\tau\) shared preference types, with rational mass \(\mu_t\) per type and per-capita cake supply \(\rho\). The decision variables are fractions \(\pi_t(B)\) of each type assigned finite-union-of-intervals bundles; the output is an envy-free assignment that allocates all available supply, measured by queries to the type evaluation and cut oracles.

The objection that survived

The \(\Omega(n^2)\) bound counts queries to distinct individual oracles, so it gives no evidence that a nontrivial bound remains with only \(\tau\) shared type oracles; the proponent acknowledges the theorem does not establish the new bound but does not show a type-level analogue.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Only the reported query-complexity lower bound for envy-free Robertson–Webb protocols; the mirror leaves the thesis’s strategic mechanisms, equilibrium results, and other cake-cutting results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which type distributions does an envy-free finite-menu assignment that uses all per-capita supply exist?
  • What is the optimal type-oracle query complexity as a function of \(\tau\) and the mass and supply bit lengths?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is narrow: Proposition 9 is a good anchor for a high-multiplicity question about the query cost of finding envy-free allocations.

Proposition 9, citing Procaccia [Pro09], states that any envy-free Robertson–Webb protocol has query complexity at least \(\Omega(n^2)\). The thesis reports this result; it does not prove it. The result concerns procedures that query individual agents’ evaluation and cut oracles. It is a query-complexity lower bound, not an NP-hardness result.

The lead mirror I would propose is Type-Oracle Envy-Free Allocation with Per-Capita Supply. Its input is a population of total mass \(1\), represented by a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) piecewise-uniform preference types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a normalized utility function \(u_t\) with rational breakpoints. The input also gives each type’s evaluation and cut oracle. There are many individual agents in each cohort, but only \(\tau\) distinct preference descriptions.

A continuous society needs a resource convention that keeps individual allocations nonzero as its population grows. I would give it a per-capita cake supply: for simplicity, each cake location has availability \(\rho\), where \(0<\rho<1\) is rational. This is a permitted co-modification of the model: for a finite population of size \(N\), it corresponds to supply that grows with \(N\).

A solution is a finite allocation policy. For each type \(t\), it gives a finite menu of bundles \(B\) and fractions \(\pi_t(B)\) of that type’s mass who receive each bundle, with \(\sum_B\pi_t(B)=1\). These fractions describe actual subcohorts receiving bundles, not lotteries for individual agents. The policy must satisfy the supply constraint
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_B\pi_t(B)\mathbf{1}_{x\in B}\le \rho \quad\text{for almost every }x, \]
and envy-freeness: every type \(t\) must value every bundle assigned to its members at least as highly as every bundle assigned to anyone else. The computational question is to find such a policy using as few type-oracle queries as possible, with query complexity measured in \(\tau\), the number of preference intervals, and the input bit length—not in the total number of people.

A plausible regime is a large allocation of land, space, or another divisible resource to recurring cohorts with the same preference patterns. There, \(N\) could be enormous while \(\tau\ll N\). The paper’s query model and fairness criterion carry over directly, and treating identical preferences as one type is exactly the high-multiplicity move. My conjecture is that the query complexity could be polynomial in \(\tau\) and the preference description size, perhaps using finite type compression and continuous optimization. Proposition 9 does not establish that: at most, it motivates asking whether the relevant query cost follows the number of distinct types rather than the population size. The question also raises whether envy-free policies of this finite-menu form always exist and what lower bounds remain when \(\tau\) grows.

The mirror covers only the query-complexity side of envy-free cake division. It does not continuize the thesis’s strategic-mechanism results or its other cake-cutting results. The cake is already continuous in the paper; the proposed change is to make the population a mass distribution over types.

The soft spot is that Proposition 9 concerns a finite roster dividing a single cake, while this mirror scales supply per capita and lets a cohort split among bundles. Those choices make the high-multiplicity regime meaningful, but they change the resource-allocation model. A referee might accept the oracle-complexity question as a natural extension of the paper’s envy-free problem, yet reject it as too far from the single-cake protocol setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 9 is a real computational anchor: it gives an \(\Omega(n^2)\) query lower bound for envy-free Robertson–Webb protocols. But the parameter \(n\) counts individually addressable utility oracles. If many agents share a complete preference type, querying one member answers the same query for the whole cohort. The lower bound therefore does not carry over by replacing population size with mass. At most, a type-level version would count queries to \(\tau\) distinct oracles; multiplying a cohort’s mass creates no new oracle information.

The unmodified cake model has a separate limit problem. With a fixed unit cake, the resource available per person vanishes as the population grows. A mass allocation cannot give positive-mass cohorts positive-measure bundles while preserving that supply. The proposed repair—scaling supply with population and allowing cohorts to split among bundles—can make a coherent problem, but it changes the output from a partition of one cake to a capacity-constrained assignment of bundle mass. That is a reasonable adjacent question; it is not the query problem Proposition 9 bounds.

The proposed formulation also mixes two input models. If piecewise-uniform preferences are given by rational breakpoints, the utility functions are explicitly represented, so Robertson–Webb oracle-query complexity is no longer the natural measure. If they are instead black-box oracles, the stated breakpoint count and bit length do not describe the input. Keeping type oracles fixes that mismatch, but then the question is about learning and allocating among \(\tau\) distinct preferences. The mass-splitting and scaled-capacity rules may make that a worthwhile new fair-division problem, but Proposition 9 supplies neither its lower bound nor its central computational obstacle.

So the best negative case is that the paper’s strongest anchor measures the cost of interrogating distinct individuals, while continuization merges repeated individuals into shared types. Preserving the cake’s fixed supply makes the limit degenerate; repairing it produces a different allocation model. That weakens this mirror substantially. It does not establish that no useful continuous cake-division problem exists: the type-level, capacity-scaled version is coherent, and a thoughtful reader could still judge it worth studying. The case against a worthwhile mirror is therefore plausible but not airtight.
