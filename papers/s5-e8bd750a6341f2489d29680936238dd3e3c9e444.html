The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a pure positional scoring rule with vector \(s_m\), at most \(\tau\) partial-chain types \(P\) with rational masses \(\mu_P\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a target \(c\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(x_{P,\pi}\), for each full ranking \(\pi\) extending \(P\), such that \(\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{Ext}(P)}x_{P,\pi}=\mu_P\) for each type and \(\sum_{P,\pi}x_{P,\pi}s_m(\pi(c))\geq\sum_{P,\pi}x_{P,\pi}s_m(\pi(d))\) for every \(d\in C\).

The model it lives in

Types are partial-chain reports \(P\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_P\); the decision variables \(x_{P,\pi}\) allocate that mass among full rankings extending the report. The objective is feasibility: the target's aggregate score must be at least every other candidate's.

The objection that survived

The paper's illustrative settings do not establish that partial-chain reports have small support relative to the population, so the proposed high-multiplicity regime remains plausible rather than demonstrated for those examples.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's Possible Winner classification on partial chains, including the tractable plurality and veto cases and the NP-complete cases for other pure positional scoring rules. It leaves the paper's results on partitioned, top-truncated, bottom-truncated, and doubly-truncated profiles outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact feasibility be solved in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length for every pure positional scoring rule?
  • Does the continuous problem remain tractable for scoring rules where the discrete problem is NP-complete?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high multiplicity version of Possible Winner on Partial Chains. A type is a reported partial chain \(P\): a complete order on the candidates that voter ranked, with its set \(\operatorname{Ext}(P)\) of allowed full rankings. Mass \(\mu_P\) is the fraction of the population with that same report. For each type and each allowed completion \(\pi\), let \(x_{P,\pi}\) be the mass assigned to that completion.

The instance gives \(C\), a pure positional scoring rule \(r\), at most \(\tau\) distinct partial-chain types with nonnegative rational masses summing to \(1\), and a target \(c\). Ask whether there are nonnegative \(x_{P,\pi}\) such that
\[ \sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{Ext}(P)}x_{P,\pi}=\mu_P \]
for every type \(P\), and \(c\)'s aggregate score is at least every other candidate’s:
\[ \sum_{P,\pi}x_{P,\pi}s_m(\pi(c)) \ \geq\ \sum_{P,\pi}x_{P,\pi}s_m(\pi(d)) \qquad\text{for every }d\in C. \]
A solution is such a mass allocation among full rankings. The reports stay fixed; the question is whether some completion of the population’s partial information makes \(c\) a winner.

The regime I find plausible is a large platform or audience choosing among movies, restaurants, or other options people have sampled unevenly. Millions of participants may produce far fewer distinct ordered-subset reports, so \(N\gg\tau\). The type must include the whole partial chain: people with the same report have the same allowed completions for this problem. The mass allocation represents how an atomless population with that report can be divided among those completions.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper through reductions from 3-Dimensional Matching. It states that for every pure positional scoring rule other than plurality and veto, Possible Winner restricted to partial-chain profiles is NP-complete. Thus the mirror preserves the paper’s central question and its scoring rules, while replacing indivisible choices of one completion per voter with divisible mass over completions.

I expect this continuous problem to be tractable (Class A). Its direct formulation is an LP with a variable for every completion, potentially exponentially many. But its pricing step appears polynomial. For a given partial chain, the ranked candidates must keep their chain order; the unranked candidates have no constraints among themselves. In a pricing objective, each candidate has a weight, and the positional scores are nonincreasing. An optimal completion can therefore put the unranked candidates in order of their weights and interleave that sequence with the fixed chain. A dynamic program over the interleavings takes polynomial time. That gives the separation or pricing routine needed for exact LP methods, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) and the scoring rule’s input size.

The high multiplicity bridge is concrete: the rational masses in this instance describe some finite election after a common-denominator scaling, and any finite completion of that replicated election gives a feasible mass allocation. Conversely, a feasible rational LP solution can be realized by replicating the finite profile enough times. So the continuous question asks whether the target can win after some replication, not necessarily in the original electorate. The paper’s NP-hardness may dissolve because the completion choices can be split fractionally; that is precisely the phenomenon this mirror would test.

My weakest point is the type interpretation. The paper’s type is an incomplete report, not a known full ranking. That fits Possible Winner’s input and makes identical reports indistinguishable for the problem, but it extends the programme’s default ranking-as-type model. The other limitation is scope: this mirrors Theorem 2’s partial-chain result, not the paper’s broader results on truncated or partitioned profiles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 2: Possible Winner on partial chains is NP-complete for every pure positional scoring rule except plurality and veto. It is a named computational result, so the negative case cannot dismiss it for lack of an algorithmic target.

The strongest objection is a possible mismatch in what counts as a type. In the paper, a partial chain is a constraint on one voter’s unknown ranking, and a witness assigns that voter one full ranking. The proposed LP groups voters by their report and splits each group’s mass among completions. In an application where identical reports conceal a fixed, correlated distribution of actual preferences, that split could describe populations that do not exist.

But that objection does not defeat the mirror the proponent defined. Possible Winner leaves each voter’s completion unconstrained beyond the reported partial order. In an atomless population, a measurable partition of one report type across its allowed completions is a coherent completion of that population. The rational LP also has a finite replication interpretation. Treating the partial report as the type is therefore a reasonable model co-modification, and the paper’s own settings can plausibly supply many people per report type.

I see no stronger objection to the LP itself: its pricing problem appears manageable by ordering unranked candidates by their dual weights and using dynamic programming to interleave them with the fixed chain. The programme allows a tractable answer to count as worthwhile, so that possibility is no rebuttal. The honest case against is weak: the paper does not establish that its illustrative populations have small support over partial-chain reports, and the type interpretation needs care. Those are limits on the motivating story, not grounds for rejecting the mirror in every scenario. Theorem 2 survives.
