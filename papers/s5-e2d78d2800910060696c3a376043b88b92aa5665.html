The continuous mirror question

Given rational mass distributions \(\mu\) over item types and \(\nu\) over worker types, with fixed supports, item-type qualities \(q_j\), and worker-type rating laws with means \(a_{rj}\), suppose at least an \(\alpha\) fraction of worker mass is reliable, its ratings have independent errors, and its means satisfy the paper’s \( (L,\epsilon_0) \)-monotonicity condition relative to \(q\). With access to the paper’s sparse worker-item rating and curator-judgment process at the type level, can a randomized algorithm select masses \(x_j\in[0,\mu_j]\), \(\sum_j x_j=\beta\), such that \(\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_jx_jq_j\ge Q_\beta^* - ((L+1)\epsilon+\epsilon_0)\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), using query bounds and computation polynomial in the support sizes and input encoding length, independent of the replication scale?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity curation model with rational item-type and worker-type masses; the decision variable \(x_j\) selects item mass, the objective is its average quality, reliable worker types obey the monotonicity condition, and unreliable types may use arbitrary strategies within the stated finite-support regime.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s approximate top-\(\beta\)-quantile curation guarantee in a fixed-support population; it leaves aside recovery of individual ratings and incentive design for peer prediction.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s sampling guarantee be preserved when observations are aggregated by item and worker type?
  • What support-dependent query and running-time bounds suffice when unreliable types use coordinated strategies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case for a mirror is the paper’s quantile-curation problem: use noisy ratings, including ratings from an adversarial workforce, to select almost all of the best \(\beta\)-fraction of a large collection. I would anchor the case on Theorem 2, proved here (its proof is in Appendix A.9). The paper has no named NP-hardness result or conventional “in P” classification; Theorem 2 is an algorithmic recovery guarantee with query bounds.

Theorem 2 assumes \(m\ge n\), independent errors from reliable raters and from the curator’s own ratings, and reliable raters that are \((L,\epsilon_0)\)-monotonic with respect to the curator’s true item qualities. The other raters may behave arbitrarily. It says that if Algorithm 3 collects ratings using
\[ k\ge \Omega\!\left(\frac{\log^3(1/\delta)}{\beta\alpha^3\epsilon^4}\frac{m}{n}\right) \quad\text{and}\quad k_0\ge \Omega\!\left(\frac{\log(1/(\alpha\beta\epsilon\delta))}{\beta\epsilon^2}\right), \]
then Algorithms 1 and 2 recover a set \(T\) which, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), satisfies
\[ \frac{1}{\beta m} \left(\sum_{j\in T^*} r_j^*-\sum_{j\in T} r_j^*\right) \le (L+1)\epsilon+\epsilon_0, \]
where \(T^*\) is the curator’s top-\(\beta\) quantile. The result uses only a fraction \(\alpha\) of reliable raters, and its guarantee tolerates arbitrary reports from the rest. The algorithm computes with a nuclear-norm optimization and randomized rounding; the theorem states the sampling guarantee, not a running-time classification.

Here is a continuous version I would put to a prover: Mass Quantile Curation under Adversarial Peer Ratings. There are item types \(j\in J\), with rational masses \(\mu_j\) summing to \(1\), and worker types \(r\in R\), with rational masses \(\nu_r\) summing to \(1\). The support sizes \(\tau_I=|J|\) and \(\tau_R=|R|\) are small compared with the populations being curated and doing the rating. An item type specifies its quality \(q_j\in[0,1]\); a worker type specifies its rating behavior, including its response strategy. Types are complete descriptions of the relevant behavior, and workers of a type are interchangeable apart from independent rating noise. At least an \(\alpha\) fraction of worker mass is reliable: for each reliable type \(r\), its mean rating \(a_{rj}\) obeys
\[ q_j\ge q_{j'}\ \Longrightarrow\ q_j-q_{j'}\le L(a_{rj}-a_{rj'})+\epsilon_0. \]
The unreliable mass may coordinate and report adversarially. As in the paper, the curator sees noisy ratings rather than the true \(q_j\), and has only a limited budget of independent quality judgments.

The decision is a mass-selection vector \(x\), where \(0\le x_j\le\mu_j\) is the amount of type \(j\) selected and \(\sum_jx_j\le\beta\). The benchmark is
\[ Q_\beta^*=\frac{1}{\beta} \max\left\{\sum_jx_jq_j: 0\le x_j\le\mu_j,\ \sum_jx_j=\beta\right\}. \]
The task is to give a randomized algorithm, using sparse ratings and curator judgments, that returns \(x\) with
\[ \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_jx_jq_j \ge Q_\beta^*-\bigl((L+1)\epsilon+\epsilon_0\bigr) \]
with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The natural resource target is the query dependence in Theorem 2, expressed for samples from the type masses, together with computation polynomial in the type-support and encoding sizes. This asks for the same curation decision under a continuous item and worker population; \(x\) selects mass, rather than making the outcome space continuous.

A plausible regime is a large corpus built from recurring content templates, curated by a large workforce drawn from a few recurring evaluation cohorts. For example, each template may generate many items of the same quality class, while raters come from a small number of rubric-training cohorts and coordinated spam playbooks. As the number of items and workers grows, \(\tau_I+\tau_R\) can stay small. The setting does require repeated, genuinely interchangeable types: if each rater uses a unique adaptive strategy, or every item has an idiosyncratic quality, the proposed high-multiplicity mirror is a poor fit.

I expect the fixed-support version to be Class A: mass selection is a weighted quantile problem, and the paper’s convex matrix-recovery approach suggests a type-level convex algorithm. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 2. The mirror covers the paper’s robust top-quantile curation result; it does not claim to cover incentive design for peer prediction or accurate recovery of every individual rating. It raises questions about whether the discrete-to-continuous query guarantees can be made exact under type aggregation, how much support compression arbitrary collusion permits, and which query bounds remain sharp.

The weakest point is that Theorem 2 is a statistical query guarantee, not a worst-case complexity theorem, and its stated algorithms work on a finite rating matrix. A type-compressed algorithm independent of the replication scale still needs to be established. The other concern is that unrestricted per-person adversarial strategies can destroy the small-support regime. Those are real limits, but they leave a recognizable mirror: robust selection of the best mass from a population using imperfect, potentially collusive peer ratings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only strong anchor, and I would attack what kind of result it is. Its guarantee counts ratings queried from workers and the curator. It gives no running-time bound or complexity classification. Algorithms 1 and 2 optimize over a finite, sparse \(n\times m\) rating matrix, but the theorem’s central claim is statistical: how many observations suffice for accurate curation. A continuous version could ask for sample complexity over type distributions, but that would be a new statistical query problem, not the computational-complexity question ChoCo is set up to chart.

The proposed mass-selection model also compresses structure the theorem leaves unrestricted. The paper permits arbitrary item qualities \(r_j^*\) and worker-specific expected ratings \(A^*_{ij}\); reliable workers need only satisfy the monotonicity condition. For the theorem’s sparse-matrix guarantee to become a small-support model, many items must share quality and rating behavior, and many workers must share complete response behavior. If those profiles are unique, the type support grows with the populations and the proposed compression disappears.

The sharper problem is the adversary. The theorem allows unreliable workers to collude and choose arbitrary reports with full knowledge of the reliable workers. If workers of one type must be indistinguishable, their strategy cannot use individual identities to produce different report patterns. Allow that identity-dependent behavior, and the identities become part of the complete type description, potentially making the support as large as the workforce. The type-level model therefore either limits the paper’s defining adversarial power or gives up the hoped-for small support. Its sparse, identity-indexed observations are also where the paper’s matrix-recovery method operates; a table of type-pair averages poses a different observation problem.

The best repair is to keep the items discrete and continuize only the workforce, so curation still selects actual items. But then a small number of worker types must have the same response distributions across those items, including for adversarial workers. With exact type-level means, the recovery problem has already been replaced by a finite table; with sampled ratings, the main question is how to estimate that table. Either way, the result to prove is a type-based query-complexity theorem, not a continuous counterpart of a computational complexity result in this paper.

This is a case against the mirror, not a proof that no worthwhile version exists. Repeated task templates, stable worker cohorts, and recurring spam strategies are plausible, and their mass-cura­tion problem is recognizable. That leaves my negative case weakest precisely where the proposed example is strongest: if ChoCo includes statistical query complexity and accepts the symmetric adversary model, a narrower mirror could survive. My argument is that it would change the paper’s central guarantee and miss the programme’s stated computational gap.
