The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper gives a useful *source of questions* for a later computational project, but it has no qualifying computational anchor for a continuous mirror.

The paper’s two named results are proved here, but neither asserts a complexity result. Proposition 1 says that when the status quo evolves independently of players’ actions, every MPE avoids commitments below \(1\), and the players’ total discounted average payoff is at most \(1\), for any \(c>0\). Theorem 1 says that for any \(\alpha_0\in(0,1)\) and strictly concave \(u\), there is a class of asymptotically renegotiation-proof MPEs achieving asymptotic efficiency. Those are equilibrium and welfare results, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

There is also no population to continuize in the model as written: it has two strategic players. The continuous quantities are the good’s allocation, the status quo, and the players’ possible demands. Those are outcome and state spaces, not a continuous society. One could imagine many repeated bargaining pairs sharing complete types—utility, status quo, and parameters—and ask for an aggregate equilibrium calculation. But that changes the object into a population of bargaining games; it does not mirror the paper’s bilateral equilibrium problem. Treating the two bargaining sides as large blocs keeps two strategic players and still does not make society the continuous object.

So there are zero eligible anchors, and therefore no per-anchor continuous problem I can hand to a prover without inventing a different paper. The weakness is decisive for this assignment: the central theorem may inspire computational work, but the paper itself does not supply a computational result whose discrete hardness or tractability could dissolve under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper supplies no computational problem to continuize. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 are equilibrium and welfare results: one characterizes what equilibria do under exogenous status quo evolution; the other constructs asymptotically efficient, renegotiation-proof equilibria. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem or identifies a discrete complexity barrier that a continuous population might remove. Turning either theorem into “compute an equilibrium” would be a new question. As stated, even its computational input and output need definitions: \(u\) is an arbitrary strictly concave function, and an equilibrium strategy is a function over a continuum of status quo states.

The two results also resist the best population reinterpretation. One could take a large population of identical bargaining pairs, or distribute pairs across types \( (u,\alpha_0,c,\delta) \). That is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, but the pairs bargain independently. Proposition 1 or Theorem 1 applies pair by pair; the population’s aggregate payoff is then an average of those outcomes. The mass representation does not create a coupled computational task. Adding shared constraints or strategic effects between pairs could make the aggregate problem interesting, but those additions would supply the substance of a new model, not a mirror of either result.

Treating each bargaining side as a large bloc is a closer fit to legislative bargaining, but it keeps two strategic players. If each bloc’s members are identical, their population can be absorbed into the bloc’s utility function \(u\); if members differ and their individual outcomes matter, the model needs new within-bloc allocation and incentive constraints. Either way, the paper’s equilibrium results do not become a problem over voter-type masses. The paper’s divisible good, continuous demand \(s\), and status quo \(\alpha\) are already continuous, but they are outcomes and states—the wrong axis for ChoCo’s population continuization.

I cannot rule out a worthwhile computational project built around a many-party extension of this bargaining model. That is where the negative case is weakest. But such a project would need new strategic structure and its own computational question; this paper does not provide an anchor for it. For both named results, the best population scenarios either leave the original two-player analysis separable or change the bargaining problem enough that they no longer count as mirrors.
