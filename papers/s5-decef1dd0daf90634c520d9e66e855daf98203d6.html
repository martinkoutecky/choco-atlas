The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper’s central object already has a natural population interpretation. In a large recommendation platform, let a type be a user’s complete ranking of the \(n\) items, and let \(\mu_\sigma\) be the fraction of users with ranking \(\sigma\). A platform with many users but relatively few observed ranking types is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Contamination then has an honest mass-transfer meaning: an adversary changes an \(\varepsilon\) fraction of the population’s rankings. The mass is over agents’ preferences, not over the outcome space.

A precise candidate problem is Sparse-Population Breakdown for Bucket Consensus. The input is \(n\), a distribution \(\mu\) given by \(\tau\) rankings and binary-rational masses, a threshold \(\delta\), and a fixed statistic \(T\), such as Kemeny followed by Downward Merge with a specified tie-breaking rule and merge threshold. Find
\[ \varepsilon^\star =\inf_{\nu:\ H^{(1/2)}_{d_\tau}(T(\mu),T(\nu))\ge\delta} \operatorname{TV}(\mu,\nu), \]
and return a distribution \(\nu\) attaining the infimum, if one exists. Here \(\nu\) is the attacked population and the solution specifies how much ranking mass must move, and where, to change the summary by at least \(\delta\).

The paper’s closest numbered computational anchor is Proposition D.1, proved here: for any two bucket rankings, their non-symmetric Hausdorff Kendall distance and their \(1/2\)-symmetric Hausdorff Kendall distance can each be computed in \(O(n^2)\) time. This is a useful subroutine for the candidate problem’s objective, but it does not establish the complexity of finding the cheapest attack. Theorems 3.1 and 3.2, also proved here, give bounds on breakdown functions; they are relevant support, but they do not classify a problem as polynomial-time, hard, or parameterized-tractable. The paper gives no numbered complexity classification for the exact breakdown-optimization problem. Its statement that Kemeny consensus is NP-hard is cited from elsewhere and is not a numbered result of this paper.

My provisional prediction for the exact sparse-population breakdown problem is continuum-specific hardness: the optimization ranges over distributions on \(S_n\), while the consensus statistic changes combinatorially with the distribution. That is a conjectural direction, not a result of this paper; a reduction from a discrete high-multiplicity version could instead place it in Class B. Questions include exact versus additive approximation, parameterization by \(\tau\), and whether the answer changes between Kemeny and Downward Merge.

The main weakness is also the clearest limit on calling this a *mirror*: the paper already defines its breakdown function over probability distributions \(p,q\) on rankings, with total variation as the contamination budget. The candidate problem above is essentially that formulation with a sparse rational representation made explicit. So the high-multiplicity regime is compelling and the authors would recognize the question, but the model is already distributional; the strongest positive case is for studying its still-unclassified computational complexity, not for claiming a new continuous reformulation. The mirror covers the Kemeny and bucket-consensus robustness results, not the paper’s broader experimental claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a collision: the proposed “mirror” is already the paper’s mathematical problem. The paper defines a ranking distribution \(p\), an attacked distribution \(q\), a statistic \(T\), and the minimum total-variation budget needed to move the statistic by at least \(\delta\). For a platform with users grouped by ranking, set \(p=\mu\) and \(q=\nu\): total variation is exactly the least population mass that must change type. Restricting the input to \(\tau\) supported rankings with rational masses makes this a sparse high-multiplicity encoding; it does not create a new continuous model.

That also answers the stronger versions of the proponent’s candidate. The exact sparse-population breakdown problem is already the paper’s breakdown function, with its existing choices of Kemeny or bucket-valued \(T\) and Kendall or Hausdorff distance. The paper even formulates the optimization over \(q\) in Equation (3), then develops a smoothed numerical method in Equations (4)–(6). Choosing a real user population as the story makes the interpretation concrete, but leaves the mathematical question unchanged.

The named results do not supply a separate computational problem to mirror. Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 bound breakdown functions; Proposition D.1 gives an \(O(n^2)\) method for computing a distance between two bucket rankings. None classifies the complexity of finding the cheapest attack. The paper does offer a numerical optimization method, so computation is present, but the proponent’s proposed exact-complexity question would be further work on the paper’s existing distributional formulation—not a discrete-to-continuous reformulation.

I can’t make a credible case that this robustness problem has no research value: its exact complexity may be worth studying. The negative case is narrower but strong for this programme’s purpose. Here the population is already represented by a distribution over types, and contamination is already mass transfer. Calling that same problem a continuous mirror would restate the paper rather than identify a new continuization.
