The continuous mirror question

Given bounded rooted buyer-community profiles \(g\), each specifying a fixed network pattern and member valuations, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over profiles, and rational per-community seller supply \(\nu_s\) at each cost \(s\), consider admissible markets with \(N\mu_g\) copies of each profile and \(N\nu_s\) sellers of each cost. Let \(B_g\) be the highest valuation in profile \(g\). Determine the limit of INA's normalized allocation \(x_{g,s}\) and its welfare gap to the efficient mass matching, where each community and each seller can be matched at most once and welfare is measured by gains from trade \(\sum_{g,s}x_{g,s}(B_g-s)\). Split ties fractionally and give an algorithm for the finite-support instance.

The model it lives in

Use types \(g\) for complete bounded rooted buyer-community profiles and seller types \(s\) for costs. The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu_g\), with per-community seller supply \(\nu_s\). Variables \(x_{g,s}\ge 0\) represent matched mass, subject to \(\sum_s x_{g,s}\le\mu_g\) and \(\sum_g x_{g,s}\le\nu_s\); compare the limiting INA matching with the matching maximizing \(\sum_{g,s}x_{g,s}(B_g-s)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's INA welfare-loss guarantee and limiting allocation. It leaves Theorems 1–2 on individual rationality and incentive compatibility, Theorems 4–6 on DNA, Theorem 7's DNA efficiency bound, and the simulations outside scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove convergence of normalized INA allocations and welfare gaps for rational \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), including ties at the marginal bid-cost crossing.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is narrow: the paper has a named welfare guarantee for its Information Network Auction, but no numbered theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or parameterized-hard. Its polynomial-time implementation claim appears in the prose. So the anchor is an approximation-style result, not a complexity classification.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that INA’s efficiency loss is at most \(1/q\), where \(q\) is the valid transaction quantity: groups are ranked by their highest bid, sellers by their lowest cost, and INA trades \(q-1\) pairs. In the paper’s notation, the loss is \(b_{G_q}-s_q\), and its share of the efficient social welfare is at most \(1/q\).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a platform serving many independent local communities, each with a fixed, bounded referral-network pattern. That fits the paper’s own local-area-network example and its assumption that buyer groups do not overlap. A complete community type records its rooted network pattern and the valuations of the buyers in it; copies of that type are indistinguishable for the allocation question. Mass is the fraction of community copies of each type. Sellers have cost types, with supply scaling alongside the number of communities. The number of copies can be large while the number of distinct community and seller types, \(\tau\), stays small.

I would give a prover this problem:

Mass-INA Welfare Limit. The input is a finite family of rooted buyer-network patterns and a finite set of rational-valued community profiles \(g\), each specifying the pattern and every buyer’s valuation. It includes a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these profiles, with \(\sum_g\mu_g=1\), and a rational supply measure \(\nu\) over seller costs. One unit of community mass represents one copy of a buyer group, which can receive at most one item. Let \(B_g\) be the highest valuation in community type \(g\).

For each pair \((g,s)\), choose \(x_{g,s}\ge 0\), the mass of type-\(g\) communities matched to sellers of cost \(s\). Require
\[ \sum_s x_{g,s}\le \mu_g,\qquad \sum_g x_{g,s}\le \nu_s. \]
The objective is to compute the mass-INA allocation and its welfare loss against the efficient allocation, measured by gains from trade
\[ \sum_{g,s}x_{g,s}(B_g-s). \]
Mass-INA ranks community types by \(B_g\), ranks sellers by \(s\), matches positive-surplus mass, and splits ties fractionally. Within a traded community, the item goes to a highest-valued buyer. A solution gives the allocation measure and the exact welfare gap.

This is a continuous population version of the allocation question behind Theorem 3: the network still determines which buyers compete for a community’s one item, while mass records how many copies of each complete profile participate. It also has a direct discrete dictionary. Rational masses are empirical frequencies in a sufficiently large replicated market. If there is positive trade mass, the finite crossing index \(q\) grows with the number of copies; Theorem 3’s relative-loss bound \(1/q\) therefore tends to zero. The continuous mass allocation is efficient, with ties handled by fractional averages across copies. I expect this problem to be tractable by sorting or a transportation LP, so it is a Class A style mirror. The paper’s theorem does not prove that continuous algorithmic result; it motivates the question.

The mirror covers INA’s allocation efficiency guarantee, not the paper’s full mechanism-design contribution. Natural follow-up questions are whether the limiting payment rule remains well-defined, whether incentive compatibility survives for individual buyers, and what changes when referral groups overlap.

The weak point is that my types describe whole bounded communities, not just individual buyers. That aggregation preserves the one-item-per-group allocation rule and is plausible in the repeated-community regime, but it makes the mirror less direct than an ordinary distribution over buyer types. In particular, the argument that the boundary trade has vanishing mass says nothing by itself about whether an individual buyer still has a meaningful incentive to invite neighbors. The positive case is strongest for the named welfare result, and does not extend to those strategic claims without further work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection targets Theorem 3. Its \(1/q\) bound comes from discarding the marginal trade: INA’s allocation ranks buyer groups by their highest bid and sellers by cost, then trades \(q-1\) pairs. The network affects which buyers belong to a group, but its topology does not otherwise enter this welfare guarantee. With finitely many repeated community types, the proposed mass problem is ordinary sorting or a transportation LP. If a positive fraction of groups trade, the omitted boundary trade has vanishing mass as the market grows, so the relative welfare loss tends to zero. The paper’s theorem therefore points toward a simple asymptotic limit, rather than a new complexity landscape.

A stronger version of the mirror can use the paper’s own independent-community scenario: each type is a complete, bounded referral-network pattern with fixed valuations, and many indistinguishable copies compete alongside seller cost types. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime. Treating a whole community as the unit is a substantial modeling choice, but the one-item-per-community constraint gives it a natural basis. So neither the aggregation nor the existence of a straightforward solution defeats the mirror.

The negative case is consequently weak. The mirror leaves the paper’s incentive and information-diffusion claims untouched, and Theorem 3 is a welfare guarantee rather than a complexity classification. Those points limit its reach within ChoCo, but they do not establish that the continuous question is unworthy: the mass allocation and its limiting welfare gap are well-posed, and a routine tractable answer can still be useful. I cannot honestly sustain the claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
