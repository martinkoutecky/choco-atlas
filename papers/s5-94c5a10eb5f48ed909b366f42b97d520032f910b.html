The continuous mirror question

Given positive rational type masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), a rational row-stochastic contact matrix \(P\) reversible with respect to \(\mu\), and initial blue fractions \(x_i^0\in[0,1]\), suppose the nonconstant spectral norm of \(P\) in \(L^2(\mu)\) is at most a fixed \(r_0<1/4\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i^0\le 1/2-2r_0\). At each round let \(q_i^t=\sum_j P_{ij}x_j^t\), set \(x_i^{t+1}=1\) when \(q_i^t>1/2\), set it to \(0\) when \(q_i^t<1/2\), and retain \(x_i^t\) when \(q_i^t=1/2\). For rational \(\varepsilon>0\), compute the least \(t\) such that \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i^t\le\varepsilon\), together with \(x^t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population has finitely many complete contact-profile types with masses \(\mu_i\); \(P_{ij}\) gives type \(i\)’s exposure to type \(j\), and \(x_i^t\) is the blue fraction within type \(i\). Synchronous majority updates act on the aggregate exposure \(q_i^t=\sum_jP_{ij}x_j^t\), with ties preserving the current type-level fraction. The task is to compute the first round at which total blue mass is at most \(\varepsilon\).

The objection that survived

The aggregate contact kernel does not preserve the arbitrary within-type placements and neighborhood correlations handled by the expander theorem, so the connection to that theorem rests on a separate mass-mixing argument.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that on a \(\Delta\)-regular graph \(G\), if the initial number of blue nodes satisfies
\[ |B(0)|\le \left(\frac12-\frac{2\lambda}{\Delta}\right)n, \]
where \(\lambda\) is the second-largest absolute adjacency eigenvalue, then majority dynamics reaches the all-red configuration in \(O(\log_{\Delta^2/\lambda^2} n)\) rounds. The authors prove this from their two lemmas on expansion and contraction. This is a round-complexity result, rather than a standard classification such as P or NP-hardness. So, if “computational anchor” is restricted to those classifications, the paper has no qualifying anchor. Theorem 2 is the nearest defensible anchor for a positive case.

A plausible regime is a very large online community divided into a modest number \(\tau\) of cohorts with stable contact patterns. There may be millions of people but only dozens or hundreds of distinct interaction profiles. A type records a cohort’s contact profile and update rule; \(\mu_i\) is its share of the population. Members of a type are exchangeable in their contacts, while their opinions may differ. This makes the society continuous while retaining the paper’s majority process.

Here is a precise mirror of Theorem 2. Call it Finite-Type Mass Majority \(\varepsilon\)-Consensus. An instance gives binary-rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a binary-rational contact matrix \(P\), an initial blue fraction \(x_i^0\in[0,1]\) for each type, and a tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\). The matrix is stochastic and reversible with respect to \(\mu\): \(\mu_iP_{ij}=\mu_jP_{ji}\). Its nonconstant spectral norm is at most a fixed \(r_0<1/4\). Each type-\(i\) agent sees the same population proportions in their contacts, as specified by row \(i\) of \(P\). Given blue fractions \(x^t\), let \(q_i^t=\sum_jP_{ij}x_j^t\). Set \(x_i^{t+1}=1\) if \(q_i^t>1/2\), set it to \(0\) if \(q_i^t<1/2\), and leave it at \(x_i^t\) on a tie. The problem is to return the least \(t\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i^t\le\varepsilon\), together with \(x^t\). The input is promised to satisfy \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i^0\le 1/2-2r_0\).

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). The expander-mixing argument has a mass version: the first update brings blue mass below \(1/4\), and thereafter it contracts by a factor at most \(16r_0^2\) per round. Thus the requested tolerance is reached in \(O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\) rounds for fixed \(r_0\), with \(O(\tau^2)\) arithmetic operations per update. This preserves the theorem’s density threshold, majority rule, synchronous updates, and arbitrary initial distribution of opinions within types. The tolerance is the natural co-modification for an atomless population: there is no smallest person whose disappearance marks exact extinction. Taking \(\varepsilon=1/N\) recovers the scale of one person in a population of size \(N\).

This case covers only the expander density-classification result, Theorem 2. It does not mirror the paper’s random-graph phase transition, dynamo lower bound, or Ramanujan immunity result. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper proves a theorem about individual graph neighborhoods, while this mirror assumes every member of a type sees the same aggregate contact proportions. That mean-field model is sensible for large, well-mixed cohorts, but it suppresses neighborhood correlations that can matter in an expander. And the source result gives a convergence-time bound, not a computational-complexity classification. The mirror is therefore a credible round-complexity question, but a modest fit to a programme centered on complexity landscapes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it establishes a bound on the number of synchronous updates of a given graph. It poses no computational task such as choosing an intervention, finding an optimum, or deciding whether a target is reachable. A continuous version can prove a round bound too, but that would be a continuum dynamics result—not the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed finite-type model is coherent, but its contact matrix replaces local neighborhoods with average exposure. The theorem’s expander argument matters because it controls how *every* pair of node sets can align across the graph. In a sparse expander, two blue sets with the same mass can affect the next round differently depending on where they sit. A vector of type masses cannot represent that placement. Making the vector sufficient requires agents of a type to respond to the same aggregate majority, which removes the local variation the theorem handles. That is a reasonable dense, well-mixed model; under the stated spectral promise, though, its trajectory is computed by directly iterating a short mass update. The contraction bound is useful analysis, but it does not expose an algorithmic complexity question.

The minimum-dynamo result is the best alternative to consider: minimum seed mass would be a genuine intervention problem. But a type-mass version again needs the mean-field assumption that seed placement within a type is irrelevant. Keeping the paper’s guarantee against arbitrary placements requires retaining enough neighborhood structure that the finite-type compression may disappear. A continuous dynamo problem on a chosen interaction kernel could still be worthwhile, but it would be a new optimization problem rather than a computational result already anchored by this paper.

So the strongest negative case is that every clean finite-type mirror moves the paper toward tractable mean-field analysis, while preserving the paper’s distinctive worst-case network geometry requires more individual structure. That makes the proposed mirror a modest fit for ChoCo. The universal claim is weaker: a large, exchangeable community or a continuum dynamo problem is a sensible model, and there is no basis here for ruling out every worthwhile version. The case against is about the value of this paper as a source of ChoCo complexity questions, not about whether a continuous population model can be written down.
