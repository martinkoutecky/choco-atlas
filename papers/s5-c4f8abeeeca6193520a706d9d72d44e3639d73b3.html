The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant anchor-based case for this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness or tractability. Its findings are empirical estimates and comparisons; equations (1), (2), and (A1) specify regression models and a Condorcet Jury Theorem benchmark, but they are not named computational results.

A continuous population of legislators could be a sensible extension: types might record party affiliation and voting behavior, and mass would represent each type’s share of a delegation. But the paper does not study a computational problem over such a population. Its central question is empirical—how district size relates to congruence—and the CJT formula already gives an analytic probability benchmark. Turning that into an optimization or complexity question would introduce a new problem rather than mirror a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its numbered equations are regression specifications and a CJT probability benchmark; its hypotheses concern empirical relationships. It proves no complexity bound and presents no algorithmic problem. A continuous model of the data might be possible, but it would not turn one of the paper’s results into the kind of computational question ChoCo studies.

The best candidate extension still runs into a mismatch. Treat legislators as types and their votes as mass, and a delegation’s majority becomes a deterministic mass comparison. The paper’s central object—the probability that a finite delegation’s majority agrees with constituents, as a function of delegation size—depends on finite aggregation. In the continuum, if the mass share agreeing is \(p>1/2\), the majority agrees exactly; the finite-\(n\) CJT curve that the paper tests has disappeared. Keeping that curve means retaining finite random delegations, or putting the uncertainty in a distribution over outcomes. Neither makes the society itself the continuous computational object.

One could instead pose a new optimization problem about choosing party shares or defection rates to achieve a target level of congruence. That may be worth studying, but the paper does not ask it or establish a computational result that it mirrors. The legislators in its Swiss setting are also a small, individually counted population: districts have at most 35 seats, and effective size explicitly counts parties and individual defections. A larger, repeated-delegation setting could make mass more natural, but it would still need a new computational question.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s paper-mirroring task, though it cannot rule out every useful problem one might build from the paper’s subject. The paper offers empirical and analytic findings, not a computational result whose continuous counterpart it motivates.
