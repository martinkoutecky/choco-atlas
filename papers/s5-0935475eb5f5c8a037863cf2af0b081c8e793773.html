The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational result. The closest is Proposition 6, proved here using the median-property result of Nehring and Puppe: with a common consistent TBox \(T\), absolute majority returns a consistent ontology for every profile exactly when every set \(X\) of ABox formulas inconsistent with \(T\) contains an inconsistent subset of size at most two. That is a characterization of when a rule is safe, not a result classifying a problem as tractable or hard. Proposition 4 and Corollary 5 are axiomatic impossibility and characterization results; Definitions 17–20 introduce procedures without giving complexity bounds.

The strongest candidate mirror is Continuous Majority Safety. Fix a finite agenda \(\Phi\) and a consistent shared TBox \(T\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) consistent ontology types \(T\cup A_j\), where each \(A_j\) is an ABox. Form the collective ontology
\[ M_\mu=T\cup\left\{\varphi\in\Phi_A:\sum_{j:\varphi\in A_j}\mu_j>\tfrac12\right\}. \]
The question is whether \(M_\mu\) is consistent; for the universal safety version, ask whether it is consistent for every such society. This covers Proposition 6 alone. The paper’s result answers that universal question: safety holds exactly when the agenda has the \(T\)-median property. Since rational masses can be represented by finite counts after scaling, the continuous and finite versions have the same safety boundary.

A plausible regime is a large population of medical ontology providers or institutional knowledge sources choosing among a relatively small set of standardized ontology templates. A type is the provider’s complete ontology, and its mass is the fraction of providers using it. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity model of the paper’s agents. Any remaining complexity question—such as the cost of checking consistency for a given society or recognizing the \(T\)-median property—would likely be driven by the agenda and its logical structure, so I would tentatively expect hardness to transfer rather than arise from the continuum. The paper does not establish that expectation.

The weak point is substantial: the authors study axioms and aggregation procedures, not computational complexity, and rational continuous populations here are exactly representable as scaled finite profiles. So this is a credible continuous formulation of one result, but a weak ChoCo case: it identifies a computational question to ask, while the paper supplies neither a qualifying complexity anchor nor evidence that continuization changes the complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper reports no computational complexity result to continuize. Proposition 6 characterizes when majority aggregation is safe on an agenda with a common TBox. Proposition 4 and Corollary 5 characterize aggregators under axioms. The procedures in Section 4 are introduced without complexity bounds. The proponent’s “Continuous Majority Safety” is therefore a new computational question built around Proposition 6, rather than a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed source-template scenario is plausible: many providers could use a small set of ontology templates, with each type recording the ontology they supply. But the paper’s safety question quantifies over profiles; it does not take a particular society as input. In its continuous version, the same universal property is characterized by the \(T\)-median condition on the agenda. A per-society consistency check would be a different task: sum the rational masses supporting each formula, apply the majority threshold, then check consistency. That is a legitimate decision problem, but its computational content comes from the chosen logical representation and consistency check. The paper gives no complexity result for it.

There is also no continuous optimization in this mirror. A rational distribution over finitely many ontology types can be scaled to integer counts, and the majority rule uses those counts only through formula-support thresholds. Changing the story to institutional providers, adding reliability to types, or choosing a richer type space does not supply the missing algorithmic result.

A stronger-looking extension would optimize a distance-based consistent merge, or find the minimum mass of providers whose ontologies must change to restore consistency. Those might be worthwhile problems. They add an objective or intervention that the paper does not analyze, so they cannot rescue the proposed anchor.

The negative case is strongest against this paper as a *computational-results* anchor. It cannot establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem can be built from the paper’s setting: the distance-based procedure and other aggregation rules could motivate such work. But those possibilities are new research directions, not continuous mirrors of results this paper proves.
