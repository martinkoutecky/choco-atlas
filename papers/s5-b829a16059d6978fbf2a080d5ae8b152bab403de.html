The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: computationally bounding the mass of a particular incomplete-preference type from aggregate choice shares.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper using Artstein’s lemma. Under the paper’s assumptions that the population is nonatomic and each observed choice is a selection from the decision maker’s set of non-dominated alternatives, it characterizes the *sharp* identification region for \(\theta\), where \(\theta_A\) is the probability that this set is exactly \(A\). The region consists of all nonnegative \(\theta\) with \(\sum_A\theta_A=1\) such that, for every \(A\subseteq\mathcal A\), \(\sum_{A'\subseteq A}\theta_{A'}\leq\Pr(y\in A)\). The theorem says these conditions are both necessary and sufficient. It is not a complexity-class result; its computational content is the finite inequality description that the paper uses to calculate identification regions.

Here is a continuous problem based on that result. Let the alternatives be \(\mathcal A\), and let \(\mathcal S\) be the allowed family of nonempty sets of non-dominated alternatives, with \(\tau=|\mathcal S|\). The instance gives rational aggregate choice shares \(p_a\) summing to \(1\), and a designated type \(S^\dagger\in\mathcal S\). Ask for the sharp minimum and maximum population mass \(\theta_{S^\dagger}\) among societies consistent with those shares. A solution gives the two endpoint values and a mass allocation witnessing each. For example, use variables \(x_{S,a}\geq0\) for the mass of type \(S\) that chooses \(a\), where \(a\in S\); require \(\sum_{S:a\in S}x_{S,a}=p_a\), and set \(\theta_S=\sum_{a\in S}x_{S,a}\). Minimize and maximize \(\theta_{S^\dagger}\). This is a linear program equivalent to the selection-feasibility conditions in Theorem 1. The full type universe can be all nonempty subsets of \(\mathcal A\); a scenario with a known restricted family \(\mathcal S\) gives the support-sensitive version.

A plausible regime is a large market with a modest catalogue of plans, or an election with many voters and a small ballot. Many people may share the same set of alternatives they cannot rule out as dominated because they face similar information or uncertainty. Mass is the fraction of the population in each such type; \(x_{S,a}\) records how that type’s mass is allocated across its admissible choices. The useful high-multiplicity regime has \(n\) very large relative to the number \(\tau\) of relevant types. Agents with the same \(S\) are indistinguishable for the parameter being bounded: their particular utility values do not affect either the event \(M=S\) or the allowed choices. The paper’s Ohio voting application makes this setting especially recognizable to its authors.

The expected classification is Class A when the allowed type family is explicit: the LP has size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and returns exact rational bounds and witnesses. With all nonempty subsets allowed, \(\tau=2^m-1\), so that claim is not polynomial in \(m\) alone. A natural follow-up is whether the full-support problem admits a more compact algorithm for selected coordinates \(\theta_{S^\dagger}\), without listing every possible type.

This covers Theorem 1’s distribution of non-dominated sets. It does not attempt to mirror the paper’s instrumental-variable or policy results. The main weakness is substantial: the paper already models a nonatomic population and a probability distribution over preference types. So this is not a clean population continuization of a discrete paper model; it is a computational question extracted from a model that is already continuous. The authors would likely recognize the identification problem, but the mirror’s novelty depends on treating its support-sensitive optimization as a distinct computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is already a continuous-population result. The paper assumes a nonatomic population, treats choices as population probabilities, and defines \(\theta_A\) as the mass of people whose non-dominated set is exactly \(A\). That matches the proposed mirror’s central object directly. The Ohio example reinforces this: its inputs are vote shares and abstention rates, and its conclusions concern population fractions. Recasting those fractions as masses does not continuize the population; the paper has already done so.

The proposed LP also follows directly from Theorem 1’s sharp identification region. The variables \(x_{S,a}\) are a coupling that assigns each set type’s mass to a choice it permits. For the full family of nonempty subsets, Theorem 1 already gives the exact feasible region, and the proposed bounds on one coordinate reduce to \(0\leq\theta_{S^\dagger}\leq\sum_{a\in S^\dagger}p_a\). With an explicitly listed restricted type family, the optimization is an ordinary finite LP. Either way, this is computation within the paper’s existing continuous model, rather than a new continuous mirror.

The strongest extension would ask for a compact algorithm when the full subset family or objectives are represented succinctly. That could be a legitimate adjacent optimization question, but it changes the task into one about compressing or searching the alternative-subset structure. The paper neither poses that problem nor supplies a discrete computational result whose continuous counterpart it would be. Its named theorems establish identification, not complexity classifications or algorithms for a discrete problem.

So this anchor does not support a worthwhile *continuization* of the paper. The negative case is strongest for the proposed identification task: it is already continuous and essentially solved by the paper’s characterization. It is weaker as a universal claim, since a genuinely new downstream computational problem could still be posed on this model; that would be further work on an already continuous framework, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
