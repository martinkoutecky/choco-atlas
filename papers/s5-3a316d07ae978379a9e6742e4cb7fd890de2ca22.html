The continuous mirror question

Given candidates, seats, rational masses μ on supported ballot types, an idealized STV count, a winner w, a specified round i, and a rational safety margin ε>0, find the minimum mass m of ballots with first preference w to retarget among the other continuing candidates. Require that the prior elimination path remains as in the paper and that t_i(w)-m+ε≤t_i(c)+δ_c for every continuing c≠w, with ∑_{c≠w}δ_c=m; each source type's transferred mass is bounded by its μ-mass.

The model it lives in

Types are complete ballot rankings, with unranked candidates placed last, and μ gives each supported type's share of the society. Decision variables transfer mass from supported w-first types to ballot types with other continuing candidates first; each unit has cost 1, and the objective is total transferred mass subject to the round-local tally constraints under idealized STV.

What the mirror covers

Covers Lemma 1's round-local first-preference-changing witness in idealized STV; leaves the global minimum mass needed to change the winner set, full Senate counting rules, and the audit methods unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Lemma 1's sufficiency proof extend to rational masses under an explicit tie rule and the stated positive safety margin?
  • Can the minimum transfer and its redistribution among continuing candidates be computed exactly from rational round tallies and source-type mass bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Lemma 1, proved in this paper. It is not a complexity classification: the paper has no numbered result saying that an audit or margin problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Lemma 1 gives a narrower computational guarantee. In the paper’s idealized STV setting, let \(w\) be a winner who remains in the count, and let \(i\) be a round at which another candidate is eliminated. Define \(m_i(w)\) as the least amount of \(w\)’s tally to remove and redistribute among the other continuing candidates so that
\[ t_i(w)-m_i(w)<t_i(c)+\delta_c \quad\text{for every continuing }c\ne w, \qquad \sum_{c\ne w}\delta_c=m_i(w). \]
Lemma 1 says that if \(w\) has \(m_i(w)\) first-preference votes available, this reassignment gives a valid way to change the outcome: \(w\) is eliminated by round \(i\). The lemma is proved here. It guarantees a successful witness, not a globally minimum election margin.

My lead mirror is the Continuous STV Elimination-Witness Problem. A type is a complete ballot preference order, with any unranked candidates placed last. An instance gives the candidate set, number of seats, rational masses \(\mu_r\) on the supported ballot types, and the paper’s idealized STV count with a fixed tie rule. The masses sum to \(1\); \(t_i(c)\) is candidate \(c\)’s tally at the specified round, and \(w\) is a winner who would be left standing at the end. Given a rational safety margin \(\varepsilon>0\), find the least mass \(m\) of ballots with first preference \(w\) to retarget among the other continuing candidates, so that
\[ t_i(w)-m+\varepsilon\le t_i(c)+\delta_c \quad\text{for every continuing }c\ne w, \qquad \sum_{c\ne w}\delta_c=m. \]
A solution is a mass-transfer plan from supported \(w\)-first ballot types to new ballot types, together with its objective value; an optimality certificate establishes that no smaller transfer meets these constraints. The positive buffer handles the strict inequality in Lemma 1. This is the continuous version of the paper’s round-local construction, not a claim that the lemma finds the exact margin over all possible ballot changes.

This model has a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large Senate district with many repeated preference patterns, where the number of cast ballots is far greater than the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballot types. Mass is the share of ballots of each type, and the intervention changes a share of the reported ballot records. For this question, ballots with the same preference order are interchangeable and each edited unit has cost \(1\). A paper ballot can be recoded as another preference order, just as the paper’s hypothetical manipulations alter ballots. The paper itself reports millions of ballots in some states, but does not show that their support size is small; the regime is plausible for districts with recurring ballot patterns, rather than guaranteed for every Senate election.

The authors should recognize this mirror: it preserves the ballot-change objective, STV winner-elimination condition, and round-local construction in Lemma 1, while replacing ballot counts with ballot mass. The stated problem is a linear optimization problem with \(O(m)\) redistribution variables once the round tallies are known, so I expect this restricted question to be Class A, tractable. That conclusion applies to the lemma’s witness construction. It does not establish tractability for finding the true minimum mass needed to change the final STV winner set across all possible interventions and count trajectories.

The paper’s scope this mirror covers is its upper-bound search for winner-changing ballot alterations, especially the first-preference-changing variant in the idealized preliminaries. It does not continuize the Bayesian audits, sampling process, error-rate estimates, or conditional risk-limiting audit: those use probability or sampling over ballots, while the population itself remains a finite ballot file. The paper cites Xia (2012) for NP-hardness of computing STV margins, but that is not a numbered result proved here, so it is not my anchor.

My weakest point is that Lemma 1 is a local witness guarantee, not a complexity result for the paper’s overall margin-search problem. Also, a Senate profile may have many distinct rankings, and real Australian counting rules include transfer values, tie handling, and rounding that the idealized lemma avoids. Those limitations leave the full continuous STV Mass Margin question open: minimize the total mass transferred between ballot types so that a recomputed count changes the winner set, under an explicit fractional version of the chosen STV rules. Whether that broader question is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is hard for continuum-specific reasons needs separate study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 1 is the only plausible anchor, and it is a poor fit for a complexity mirror. It gives a conditional witness: once the idealized count, round \(i\), and tallies \(t_i(c)\) are fixed, a specified redistribution is enough to eliminate \(w\). The paper does not give a complexity result for computing a minimum margin. The proposed continuous version mainly replaces ballot counts in that local construction with rational masses. Its objective is already determined by the round tallies through the same small redistribution problem; it does not create a type-space pricing problem or a new algorithmic obstacle. That makes it hard to justify as a research target for mapping the continuous complexity landscape.

The stronger version would minimize the mass of ballot changes across complete rankings, then rerun STV and require a different winner set. That is a real computational question, but it is no longer the guarantee in Lemma 1: it asks for a global margin that the paper explicitly leaves unresolved. I cannot rule it out as a worthwhile mirror. Large elections with repeated party-ticket ballots give a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and aggregate ballot types retain the information needed to compute STV outcomes. The audit’s paper-to-record sampling depends on individual ballot correspondence, but that does not dispose of this separate margin problem.

So the negative case against Lemma 1 is strong: its continuous mirror appears to be a rescaling of a local calculation, rather than a new complexity question. The broader margin mirror survives that objection. I do not see an honest basis in this paper for the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
