The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compact convex action sets \(A_t\), and circuit-described payoffs \(U_t(a;q)\) that are continuous in \(q\) and concave in \(a\), let type \(t\) have mean action \(x_t\) and aggregate action mass \(q_t=\mu_t x_t\). Find \(x_t\in A_t\) such that \(x_t\in\arg\max_{a\in A_t}U_t(a;q)\) for every \(t\), holding \(q\) fixed during an individual deviation.

The model it lives in

A finite-support nonatomic concave game: a continuum of agents is grouped into finitely many complete types, each type shares its action set and payoff rule, and agents best-respond to fixed aggregate action masses. The task is to find equilibrium type means and their induced aggregate masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1; it leaves the paper’s cake-cutting, stochastic-game, market, \(\varepsilon\)-proper-equilibrium, and other results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does FIXP-hardness persist when the number of types \(|T|\) is fixed, or is the problem tractable in that regime?
  • Under what assumptions do equilibria of finite anonymous populations converge to CTCE equilibria as the population grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a population version of the paper’s concave-game equilibrium result. The relevant named anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: computing an equilibrium of a concave \(n\)-player game is FIXP-complete. In the game model, each player has a compact convex strategy set, and their continuous payoff is concave in their own strategy when the other players’ strategies are fixed. An equilibrium is a profile in which every player’s strategy maximizes their payoff given the others. The paper proves FIXP membership using its OPT-gate; the hardness follows from FIXP-hardness for normal-form games by Etessami and Yannakakis (2010). The paper’s computational representation supplies circuits for the relevant functions and the needed subgradient or supergradient access.

Here is a concrete mirror: Continuum-Type Concave Equilibrium (CTCE). An instance has a finite set of types \(T\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type is a complete strategic role: its agents share an action set \(A_t\) and payoff data. Let \(A_t\) be compact and convex, given in the paper’s convex-optimization representation. The society is a continuum of agents, with mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\). If \(x_t\) is the average action of that type, its aggregate action mass is \(q_t=\mu_t x_t\). A type-\(t\) agent choosing action \(a\in A_t\) gets payoff \(U_t(a;q)\), continuous in the population aggregates and concave in \(a\). The functions and constraints have the circuit and subgradient representations needed for FIXP computation.

The question is to find type averages \(x=(x_t)_{t\in T}\), with \(q_t=\mu_t x_t\), such that for every type \(t\),
\[ x_t\in\arg\max_{a\in A_t} U_t(a;q). \]
A solution is this type-average profile; the objective is to find any equilibrium, not to optimize a social welfare function. The averages faithfully describe continuum equilibria here: agents of a type face the same payoff and feasible set, so their equilibrium actions are all best responses. Concavity makes their average a best response too. Conversely, if the displayed conditions hold, every agent of type \(t\) can choose \(x_t\), realizing those aggregates in equilibrium.

The regime is a large population divided among finitely many strategic roles—for example, repeated cohorts of workers, firms, or market participants with the same feasible actions and payoff response. In a finite approximation, each role has many agents; the computational input records the \(\tau=|T|\) types and their masses, not named individuals. Type-level aggregates affect payoffs, and a single agent has no material effect on them. This continuizes the population; the real-valued actions and equilibrium calculation remain part of the problem.

I expect CTCE to be FIXP-complete, with hardness transferring from Theorem 4.1. Given a concave \(n\)-player game, make one type for each player, give each type mass \(1/n\), and define its payoff from the other types’ mean actions using that player’s original payoff function. Then a profile of type averages is an equilibrium exactly when it is an equilibrium of the original game. The construction adds only rational mass data and a polynomial-size rescaling of the payoff inputs. For membership, the type-average profile is a finite-dimensional concave game on \(T\): the paper’s best-response OPT-gates give a FIXP circuit whose fixed points satisfy the CTCE conditions. Thus the mirror has the same exact-equilibrium hardness as its anchor. In the programme’s terms, this is a hardness-transfer case, with the relevant combinatorics in the number of strategic types.

The paper’s other results are outside this mirror’s scope: this case covers Theorem 4.1, not the cake-cutting, stochastic-game, or market results. I think the authors would recognize CTCE as a population analogue of their concave games: the equilibrium condition is the same best-response condition, applied to type averages in an atomless society.

The weakest point is that the reduction gives each source-game player a distinct type. Hardness therefore grows with \(\tau\), and comes from the strategic roles rather than from population multiplicity. The mirror is most convincing in settings with many agents per role; it does not show that continuum populations make the corresponding finite game easier. Natural follow-up questions are whether FIXP-hardness persists when \(\tau\) is fixed, and how finite high-multiplicity games relate to CTCE equilibria as the number of agents per type grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.1 is about strategic roles, not population size. A player’s type must include the player’s full payoff role: whom they interact with and how their payoff depends on the whole profile. In a general concave game, those roles are usually distinct. The proposed reduction therefore uses \(\tau=n\) types, one for each source player. Its FIXP-hardness shows that the continuum model can encode the original game; it does not show that the result captures the computational effect of many interchangeable agents. Nor does it establish what happens when the number of types stays small as populations grow.

That objection weakens the reduction as evidence for a high-multiplicity phenomenon, but it does not defeat the best mirror. A continuum of agents in a small number of roles, with payoffs determined by aggregate actions, is a coherent anonymous game. Under concavity, averaging agents’ best responses within a type preserves best-response status, so type averages give a finite-dimensional equilibrium question. The paper’s OPT-gate provides a plausible route to FIXP membership. The mean-field literature cited in the programme does not settle this exact computational question.

So the case against is narrow: the paper’s hardness result alone does not justify the claim that population multiplicity is doing useful work in the mirror. But anonymity and negligible individual impact are plausible modeling choices, and the resulting exact complexity question remains open to ask. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror here; under the programme’s criteria, Theorem 4.1 survives.
