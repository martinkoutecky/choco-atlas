The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(C\) with rational per-capita capacities, and a finite set of student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict school rankings, and priority tiers, compute a feasible type-mass assignment that weakly improves on the fractional deferred-acceptance assignment and is Pareto efficient. Weak improvement is defined by a feasible reassignment of each type's baseline mass to schools it ranks at least as highly; Pareto efficiency means no feasible reassignment weakly benefits all mass and strictly benefits positive mass.

The model it lives in

Use rational type masses over preference rankings and school-priority tiers, with school capacities measured per capita and an outside option for unassigned students. Let the variables describe mass transferred from each type's baseline school to each weakly preferred school, subject to type-mass conservation and school-capacity constraints; maximize total ordinal rank score to select a Pareto-efficient weak improvement.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered complexity classification here: the paper proves no result of the form “in P” or “NP-hard.” Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 17, proved in this paper (Appendix C): for every School Choice problem \(P\), the Student Optimal Compensating Exchange outcome \(\mu^{ce}(P)\) is a \(\tau\)-fair allocation. The theorem gives a constructive route—run the Student Optimal Stable mechanism, then apply Top Trading Cycles—but does not state a complexity bound.

The supporting bridge is Theorem 11, also proved here (Appendix A): an allocation is \(\tau\)-fair if and only if it is efficient and every student weakly prefers her assignment to the one she receives under the Student Optimal Stable matching. I would use this characterization to define the continuous target, while keeping Theorem 17 as the single anchor. This is an algorithmic mirror, not a case about continuous relaxation dissolving discrete hardness.

My lead problem is Continuous \(\tau\)-Fair School Placement. An instance has a finite school set \(C\), rational school capacities \(q_j\) measured as fractions of the population, and a finite set of student types \(T\). Each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a strict ranking of the schools (with being unassigned last), and a priority tier at each school. The input gives the positive masses as binary rationals. A type is complete for this model: its members share both preferences and priority tiers.

First compute the type-mass Student Optimal Stable assignment \(\sigma\) by deferred acceptance: types apply by preference, and schools keep the highest-priority applicant mass up to capacity, splitting mass at a cutoff tier. The requested solution is a feasible mass assignment \(x_{tj}\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) sent to school \(j\). Each unit of mass receives one whole school; only the aggregate type totals can be fractional. The assignment must be Pareto efficient and weakly improve on \(\sigma\): there must be a way to reassign the mass from its schools under \(\sigma\) to its schools under \(x\) so that no mass moves to a less-preferred school and some mass moves to a strictly preferred one. This is the type-mass counterpart of every student weakly preferring her final assignment to her Student Optimal Stable assignment, combined with efficiency.

A plausible regime is a large city or regional intake, with many incoming students but a much smaller number of recurring combinations of preference ranking and school-priority tier. For example, preferences may follow a limited set of school or commute archetypes, while priorities depend on a limited set of catchment and sibling categories. The paper itself describes categorical priorities and lotteries; splitting mass at a cutoff is a natural way to represent the lottery within a priority tier. This keeps the population as the continuous object: school options remain discrete, and each person still receives one school.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. After computing \(\sigma\), a mass-transfer formulation has variables for moving each type’s baseline mass from school \(j\) to school \(k\), permits only weakly preferred moves, and enforces school capacities. Maximizing a strictly increasing ordinal rank score over those feasible improvements selects an efficient assignment; a strict Pareto improvement would increase that score. With finitely many types and schools, this suggests a polynomial-size LP, with running time to be established in \(m,\tau,L\). The LP is a proposed way to compute the allocation, not the problem being mirrored.

The weakest point is the priority model. The paper formally gives each student a place in a strict school priority order. If those individual tie-break positions count as part of type, the number of types can approach the number of students, removing the high-multiplicity regime. My mirror instead groups students at shared priority tiers and treats cutoff ties atomlessly. That matches the paper’s described categorical priorities and lotteries, but it is a real modeling extension that the authors and a referee would need to accept.

This covers the paper’s \(\tau\)-fair allocation result and its constructive SOCE mechanism, not its restricted-domain strategy-proofness result: individual manipulation by a zero-mass agent is not meaningfully tested by this model. The case is therefore positive but modest. It gives a concrete high-multiplicity allocation problem and a likely continuous-optimization route; it does not claim a discrete hardness result that vanishes in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 17 is a theorem about a mechanism’s outcome, not a computational-complexity result. It proves that the explicitly defined SOCE procedure returns a \(\tau\)-fair allocation; the paper does not classify the complexity of computing that allocation or pose an open algorithmic problem. The finite procedure is deferred acceptance followed by Top Trading Cycles. For a complexity-focused continuization programme, there is little computational landscape in this result to chart.

The better continuous mirror is the one built from Theorem 11: start with a mass version of the Student Optimal Stable assignment, then find a feasible allocation that weakly improves on it for everyone and is Pareto efficient. That is a sensible allocation problem. But it is not a continuous version of SOCE as specified in the paper. It replaces the paper’s fairness test and its TTC exchange mechanism with a mass-level feasibility and optimization problem. The proposed rank-score LP may compute such an allocation, but its success would establish an algorithm for that newly defined problem; it would not show that the paper’s SOCE result has a continuous computational counterpart.

There is a substantive modelling obstacle behind that shift. Types in this paper must include students’ positions in strict school priority orders. Those positions distinguish individuals, so an exact type generally cannot have positive multiplicity. A large intake with repeated preference and priority categories is plausible, but representing it requires replacing strict priorities with tiers and splitting mass at a cutoff. That co-modification is allowed by the programme, yet it changes how objections and exchanges work: a single student has zero mass, and the paper’s TTC step trades individually held places, with its tie-breaking among students assigned to the same school. A tier-based model needs new definitions of mass-level objections and exchanges before Theorem 11’s characterization can be carried over.

This is not a decisive case against studying continuous school placement. Large cohorts and categorical priorities make the setting plausible, and the mass-allocation problem could be worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is narrower: the strongest anchor is a constructive social-choice result, not a computational classification, and its natural continuous reformulation requires redefining the fairness and exchange structure that gives the result its meaning. The case against a worthwhile mirror is therefore real but weak; the proponent’s tier-based model survives as a potentially valuable new problem.
