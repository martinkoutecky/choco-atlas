What the mirror covers

The cases discuss Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollary 1; they leave the paper’s broader computational discussion and applications without a stated population-mass mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered result classifying the complexity of a computational problem.

The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here: every finite cooperative strategic game with perfect information has a CES consisting of pure strategies, and the proof gives a constructive algorithm; and Theorem 2, proved here: every finite cooperative extensive form game has a CES, possibly in mixed strategies. Corollary 1, also proved here from those theorems, applies existence to logrolling games. None gives a running-time bound or a hardness result. Section 3.5 only makes general observations about the difficulty of computing a CES.

So I don’t have a valid anchor on which to base a continuous computational problem. Treating CES existence as an algorithmic complexity result would overstate what the paper proves; proposing a continuum-player CES question would instead mirror its equilibrium-existence result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 1 do not supply computational anchors. They establish CES existence; Theorem 1’s proof is constructive, but gives no running-time bound or complexity classification. Section 3.5 informally says that computation grows exponentially and is at least as challenging as computing an SPNE, but states no formal problem, input-size measure, or algorithmic result. A continuous existence question would extend an equilibrium-existence result. Asking for the complexity of computing a CES would add a new computational question.

Corollary 1’s logrolling application is the strongest candidate for a population model. A large electorate with finitely many complete voter types—preferences, vote endowments, and other relevant parameters—has a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation. But the paper’s CES depends on coalitions, partitions, and recursively credible deviations. Its general framework permits arbitrary coalition utilities that depend on the partition. A type histogram alone does not encode those utilities for every possible coalition and partition. One could impose anonymity and a finite representation of how coalitions form and share payoffs, but those choices define a new model. With a continuum of voters, forming a coalition also does not reduce the population to a smaller finite game, as the existence proof’s induction requires.

Theorem 2 has the same gap in the imperfect-information setting, and Theorem 1 has it even in the more tractable perfect-information setting. Their continuous counterparts could be interesting equilibrium questions, but they are not complexity results from this paper. On the evidence provided, there is no worthwhile *ChoCo mirror of a computational result* to pursue here.

The negative case is weakest if the programme is willing to originate a computational problem from an existence paper. Continuous logrolling with finitely many voter types could motivate such a project. That possibility prevents a universal claim that no continuous model could be worthwhile; it does not rescue any of the three anchors as stated.
