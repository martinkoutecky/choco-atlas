The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its results are empirical comparisons of allocation and voting mechanisms on synthetic and Microlending data (Sections 6–7). The paper’s description of allocation as an online matching problem is not itself a proved complexity result. So there are no qualifying anchors to restate, and no per-anchor continuous problems whose tractability or hardness I can honestly classify.

There is a plausible *unanchored* population model behind the paper. In a large recommender service, users could be grouped into types that share their relevant preference ranking, compatibility with each fairness agent, and baseline recommendation behavior. A type’s mass would be its fraction of arrivals in each time segment. The action would be the mass allocated to each set of fairness agents; the resulting recommendations would be computed by the paper’s chosen aggregation rule. One could ask how to allocate that mass to maximize average recommendation accuracy subject to the agents’ rolling-window exposure targets.

That scenario makes population continuization plausible, and it stays focused on users rather than fractional recommendation outcomes. But preserving the paper’s central feature—the allocation mechanisms’ response to changing arrivals and recent fairness history—requires a time-ordered, stateful model. Replacing it with a static batch allocation would lose part of the problem the authors study. More fundamentally, the optimization question above is a proposed research problem, not a named result of this paper. Calling it a mirror of one of the paper’s computational results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to mirror. It reports empirical comparisons of allocation and voting mechanisms on two datasets; it proves no theorem about their complexity or algorithms. Its description of allocation as online matching is not a complexity result. So there are no qualifying anchors here, and the proposed mass-allocation optimization is a new research question, not a continuous version of a named result.

The strongest objection to that proposed question is that users are not merely a large anonymous electorate in this architecture. Each arrival brings its own profile, candidate recommendation list, and compatibility with each fairness concern; the mechanism then changes that user’s list. The fairness scores also depend on recent outcomes. An exact type must preserve everything that affects those choices and their accuracy. In realistic personalized systems, histories and recommendation contexts may make most arrivals distinct, so the support grows with the number of users. Grouping users into coarser classes could keep the support small, but would discard information the paper’s accuracy and allocation decisions use. This makes the proposed mirror a poor fit for the paper’s actual setting.

A better mirror could preserve time by representing arrivals as flows over a finite set of fully specified user contexts, with state for the rolling fairness window. That would be a legitimate computational model if those contexts recur at high multiplicity. But it would require choosing a new objective, state representation, and algorithmic question: the paper supplies no optimization problem whose complexity such a model could classify. A static batch allocation would be easier to formulate, but it would leave out the arrival order and adaptation that motivate the paper.

That is a strong case against a *paper-anchored* continuous mirror. The claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is weaker: a large service with recurring user contexts could make the finite-type, time-varying model sensible. I can’t rule that regime out honestly. The negative case is therefore decisive on the absence of an anchor, but not universal across every plausible recommender-system regime.
