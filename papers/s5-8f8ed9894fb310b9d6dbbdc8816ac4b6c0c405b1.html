The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered complexity-theoretic result: none of its theorems or propositions classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise. The closest operational anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that when \(\gamma=1\), a coalition \(S\) of \(s\) agents will not deviate if it believes the \(n-s\) outsiders will form more than
\[ j^*(n,s)=2\left(\sqrt{\frac ns}-1\right) \]
coalitions. The proof gives the comparison behind that threshold: the grand coalition pays each agent \((a-c)^2/(4n)\), while \(S\)’s equal-split payoff is \((a-c)^2/[s(j+2)^2]\), independent of how the outsiders divide their members among those \(j\) coalitions. This is a stability result, not a complexity classification; if a complexity-class anchor is mandatory, the paper supplies none.

A plausible regime is a large population of identical small producers, such as microgenerators with the same cost and market parameters, who coordinate production in consortia and split each consortium’s profit equally. There may be many producers but only one intrinsic agent type, \(\theta=(a,c,\gamma)\), so \(\tau=1\). A prospective breakaway group has mass \(\alpha\), and the outsiders divide into a fixed number \(j\) of consortia. The paper already treats members of a coalition symmetrically and lets coalitions choose output jointly, so this scenario preserves its strategic structure.

Here is a precise lead mirror, Mass-Belief Stability at \(\gamma=1\). The society has mass one and a single type. A challenger has mass \(\alpha\in(0,1)\); the outsiders form \(j\) positive-mass blocs of sizes \(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_j\), with \(\sum_k\beta_k=1-\alpha\). Each bloc jointly chooses a common output for its members. In the large-population limit, scale individual output as \(q_i=x_k/n\) and per-agent payoff by \(n\), so payoffs remain finite. Writing \(A=a-c\), a bloc of mass \(\mu_k\) choosing scaled output \(x_k\) maximizes
\[ \Pi_k=\mu_k x_k\left(A-\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\mu_\ell x_\ell\right), \]
where \(\mu_0=\alpha\) is the challenger’s mass and \(\mu_k=\beta_k\) for the outsiders. Ask whether the grand coalition is stable against this mass-\(\alpha\) deviation under the assumed outsider count \(j\): stability means the challenger’s equal-split per-agent payoff at the coalition-bloc equilibrium is no greater than its grand-coalition payoff. A solution returns yes or no and the equilibrium outputs and payoff comparison; in a no instance, any positive outsider partition is a destabilizing witness.

At equilibrium, every bloc satisfies \(\mu_k x_k=A-Q\), where \(Q=\sum_k\mu_kx_k\). Thus \(x_k=A/[\mu_k(j+2)]\), and the challenger’s per-agent payoff is \(A^2/[\alpha(j+2)^2]\). The grand coalition’s per-agent payoff is \(A^2/4\). So the continuous decision reduces exactly to
\[ \alpha(j+2)^2\ge 4. \]
The outsiders’ mass split drops out, just as it does in Proposition 2. This problem is in Class A: with rational input, the comparison is an exact polynomial-time arithmetic test. Its threshold is the mass-scaled form of the proposition’s, \(j\ge 2(\alpha^{-1/2}-1)\), with equality corresponding to indifference. This is a genuine population-limit question: the finite result concerns \(n\) agents and the mirror concerns a unit-mass society; the matching threshold is something the limit establishes, not a reason to dismiss the mirror.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Proposition 2’s \(\gamma=1\) stability claim, not the paper’s general-\(\gamma\) results or every aspect of its network model. It also generates useful next questions: for \(0<\gamma<1\), does the exact worst-case outsider partition still admit a closed-form threshold? What happens when stability must hold for every positive challenger mass, including masses tending to zero?

The weak point is that this is a tractable stability calculation, not a hardness result whose discrete complexity might dissolve in the continuum. The limit also retains coalitions as coordinated decision-makers: if agents instead act independently as infinitesimal firms, the deviation and its market power change. Still, coordinated coalitions are central to the paper’s model, and replacing their cardinalities with masses is a credible continuous analogue of the specific question Proposition 2 answers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the strongest anchor, and the proponent’s version is a sensible population model. A large population of identical producers can be represented by a single type, with a challenger occupying mass \(\alpha\). But this paper contains no numbered result about computational complexity. Proposition 2 is a payoff comparison; Theorem 2’s phrase “computational efficient” introduces no algorithmic problem or complexity analysis.

For the proposed mirror, the comparison is just the paper’s formula with \(s/n\) replaced by \(\alpha\). Writing \(A=a-c\), the grand-coalition payoff per agent is \(A^2/(4n)\), while the challenger’s is \(A^2/[s(j+2)^2]\). After the proponent’s scaling, stability is exactly \(\alpha(j+2)^2\ge 4\). Given rational \(\alpha\) and integer \(j\), that is an exact arithmetic test. The mirror is well-posed, but its algorithmic content is exhausted by evaluating one inequality; it does not open a complexity question of the kind this programme is meant to chart.

A stronger mirror could ask about arbitrary outsider mass partitions and general \(\gamma\). Yet the relevant partition dependence in Theorem 2 is already a sum of separable terms of the form
\[ g(\beta_k)=\frac{\gamma\beta_k}{\gamma\beta_k+2(1-\gamma)}. \]
For \(0<\gamma<1\), \(g\) is concave, so extrema under a fixed total mass follow from equal partitions or boundary limits, as in Proposition 1. At \(\gamma=1\), the partition dependence vanishes; for \(\gamma<0\), Theorem 3 already gives stability across the stated partitions. Replacing integer sizes with real masses may sharpen the comparative statics, but it supplies no search or pricing problem.

There is a limit issue for a broader “stability against every coalition” mirror: for fixed finite \(j\), the condition fails for sufficiently small positive \(\alpha\), while \(\alpha=0\) is no coalition at all. A minimum challenger mass could remove that discontinuity, but it would add a scale parameter and still leave the fixed-mass arithmetic test.

The negative case is strongest for the results this paper actually proves. A heterogeneous Cournot model with a distribution of costs or other agent parameters might create a worthwhile computational problem, but this paper’s symmetric formulas do not establish that extension or anchor its complexity. The case against a mirror is therefore not that a continuous population is implausible; it is that the paper’s credible mirrors remain payoff calculations rather than computational results.
