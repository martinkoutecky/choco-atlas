What the mirror covers

The proposed extension represents Theorem 3's majority-equilibrium existence question; Theorems 1 and 2 are not mirrored, and the extension does not make any of these results computational results of the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition places a problem in P, proves it hard, or gives an algorithmic formulation that the authors compute with. Theorem 1 characterizes rules; Theorem 2 proves there is no majority winner for the family of general rules; and Theorem 3 proves that a majority winner exists for every bounded family of general compromise rules. These are results about characterization and equilibrium existence, not computational complexity. They are proved in this paper, but none qualifies as an anchor under your criterion.

The strongest possible positive lead, if that criterion were relaxed, is a continuous version of Theorem 3. Let the voters be clubs with types \(t\) drawn from a finite support with rational masses \(\mu_t\). A type specifies a club’s audience against each type: \(a(t,s)\) is the audience when type \(t\) hosts type \(s\). Define its normalized claim as \(q(t)=\sum_s\mu_s[a(t,s)+a(s,t)]\), and let \(\bar q=\sum_t\mu_tq(t)\). The continuum limit of the paper’s general compromise rule assigns type \(t\) the normalized amount \(u_\lambda(t)=\bar q/2+\lambda(q(t)-\bar q)\), for \(\lambda\) in a bounded interval.

The resulting computational question would be: given the rational masses, audience kernel, and interval of allowed \(\lambda\), return a \(\lambda^\star\) such that no other allowed \(\lambda\) gives strictly more than half the club mass a larger allocation. This is a plausible population mirror because the agents who vote, their audience-based claims, and their rule-dependent allocations all come from the paper’s model. In a regime with many clubs but relatively few shared audience profiles, the masses represent fractions of clubs, not numbers of individually targeted clubs. The expected answer follows the paper’s Proposition 2: if more than half the mass has claims below the mean, choose the lower endpoint; if more than half has claims above the mean, choose the upper endpoint; otherwise every allowed rule is a majority winner. That would be easy to compute from the type support.

The regime is possible but not especially strong: a large association pooling broadcasting revenue across many clubs could have many clubs and a moderate number of audience-profile types, while a typical professional league has relatively few clubs and potentially distinctive audience relationships. More decisively, the proposed problem would be our computational reformulation of an equilibrium result. It is not a computational result named in this paper, so it cannot serve as the required anchor. The paper therefore offers an interesting population-continuation seed, but no valid anchor for a continuous computational mirror under the stated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes rules; Theorem 2 gives a nonexistence result for majority winners; Theorem 3 gives an existence result on a restricted family. None studies the complexity of a computational problem or presents an algorithmic problem for the reader to solve. The proposed mirror of Theorem 3 therefore turns an equilibrium theorem into an algorithmic task after the fact. That may be a sensible exercise, but it does not fill the programme’s stated gap in computational social choice.

The proposed continuum model is coherent: with a distribution over club types and an audience kernel \(a(t,s)\), each type has a claim \(q(t)\), and the compromise-rule allocation depends on that claim and the mean. But this preserves the theorem’s subject—whether majority voting selects a rule. Computing the proposed \(\lambda^\star\) is not a computational result of the paper. Recasting Proposition 2’s median/mean cases as instructions for an algorithm does not change that. The paper does not ask for an algorithm, establish a complexity boundary, or identify a computational bottleneck.

A stronger attempt might continuize the full family of general rules and ask whether a majority winner exists or can be found for a distribution of clubs. That is still a new computational layer over Theorem 2’s equilibrium question. Its ingredients are the two type-level quantities \(h(t)\) and \(w(t)\), and each rule gives each type an affine payoff in the two rule parameters. This makes the proposed task a study of majority equilibrium over a low-dimensional rule space, not a missing computational problem from the paper. Enlarging the question does not supply the absent computational anchor.

I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity regime exists. A large federation with repeated audience profiles could make the population model meaningful, though the audience kernel would need to capture the pairwise relationship between club types. That is the weak point in the negative case: a recognized continuous analogue of the equilibrium results is possible. But on the programme’s stated standard, this paper offers no worthwhile *computational* mirror to pursue.
