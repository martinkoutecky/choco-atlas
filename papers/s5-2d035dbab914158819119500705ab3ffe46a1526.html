The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(G\), finite buyer and seller type sets, and rational masses \(\mu\) over those types, with each type specifying per-agent valuations or reservation prices and integral capacities, compute a common nonnegative price vector \(p\) and a nonnegative type-level trade flow \(x_{ajk}\). Require each type’s average bundle to maximize its individual utility at \(p\), subject to its per-agent capacity, and require the flow to respect type masses and clear each good’s market. Equivalently, find a welfare-maximizing feasible flow supported by such equilibrium prices.

The model it lives in

A continuum of buyers and sellers grouped into complete types with masses \(\mu\). The decision variables are common prices \(p_j\) and type-level trade masses \(x_{ajk}\); feasibility scales each type’s integral per-agent capacities by its mass, and the objective is to maximize total gains from trade \(\sum_{a,j,k}(v_{aj}-r_{jk})x_{ajk}\). Each flow can be interpreted as a mixture of integral individual trades within a type.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no running-time bound for its inequality system, and the polynomial flow-LP route is supplied by the proposed mirror rather than proved in the paper.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the computation of −1-competitive equilibria characterized in Proposition 6. It leaves the 0-equilibrium, the group-stability results, and the full nesting theorem aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the entire set of equilibrium price vectors and type payoffs be represented and computed efficiently, rather than finding one equilibrium?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the high multiplicity version of the paper’s \(-1\)-competitive-equilibrium price problem.

The anchor is Proposition 6, proved in the paper. For \(t\in\{-1,0\}\), it says six conditions on a market \(M\) and a nonnegative price vector \(p\) are equivalent: \(p\) is a \(t\)-equilibrium price; the associated transferable-utility game has a nonempty Core; the grand coalition’s worth equals the sum of singleton worths; there is an optimal assignment \(A\) for which each singleton coalition’s worth equals its gain at \((A,p)\); that equality holds for every optimal assignment; and \(p\) satisfies system (23). That system requires, for every singleton coalition \(C\) and every \(t\)-compatible pair of buyer and seller plans \((A^B,A^S)\), that the coalition’s gain under those plans is no greater than its gain under \((A,A)\). I use the \(t=-1\) case: the price-taking equilibrium where agents choose without accounting for aggregate market capacities. This is a computational characterization, not a named complexity classification: the paper does not prove a result such as polynomial time or NP-hardness.

Here is the mirror I believe the authors would recognize: High-Multiplicity Competitive Equilibrium for a Generalized Assignment Market. There are goods \(G\), buyer types \(T_B\), and seller types \(T_S\), with rational type masses \(\mu\) over \(T_B\sqcup T_S\) summing to one. A buyer type \(a\) has per-agent valuations \(v_{aj}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) and an integral per-agent demand capacity \(d_a\). A seller type \(k\) has reservation prices \(r_{jk}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) and integral per-agent supplies \(q_{jk}\). Each type is complete: its members share all these attributes. The support size is \(\tau=|T_B|+|T_S|\), while the population size \(N\) is large and grows with the type proportions held fixed.

A solution is a common price vector \(p\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{G}\) and nonnegative type-level trade quantities \(x_{ajk}\), measured per unit of total population mass. They must satisfy \(\sum_{j,k}x_{ajk}\le \mu_a d_a\) for each buyer type and \(\sum_a x_{ajk}\le \mu_k q_{jk}\) for each seller type and good. For each buyer type, the average bundle \(y_{aj}=(\sum_k x_{ajk})/\mu_a\) must maximize \(\sum_j(v_{aj}-p_j)y_j\) subject to \(y_j\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j y_j\le d_a\). For each seller type, \(z_{jk}=(\sum_a x_{ajk})/\mu_k\) must maximize \(\sum_j(p_j-r_{jk})z_j\) subject to \(0\le z_j\le q_{jk}\). The flow \(x\) specifies who trades with whom and clears each good’s market. The objective is to find such an equilibrium; equivalently, equilibrium trades maximize total gains \(\sum_{a,j,k}(v_{aj}-r_{jk})x_{ajk}\). Type-level averages may be fractional because mass can be split among agents receiving integral bundles.

This regime fits a large procurement market for standardized materials: many buyers share a valuation and storage-capacity vector, and many sellers share a reservation-price and inventory vector. There can be millions of agents but comparatively few distinct complete types. Taking the population to a continuum scales each type’s capacity and supply with its mass, while preserving the paper’s price-taking question. The mirror covers the \(-1\)-competitive-equilibrium computation in Proposition 6; it does not extend the claim to the paper’s group-stability notions or to its full nesting theorem.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable by linear programming. A flow network with buyer-type, good, and seller-type-good nodes represents welfare maximization; its size is polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational input length \(L\). Dual prices at the good nodes provide equilibrium prices. This gives a credible route to compute an equilibrium, and potentially a compact representation of its price set.

The weakest point is that the paper’s central results concern the structure of market solutions, and its individual market model already permits a tractable welfare formulation. Proposition 6’s displayed system ranges over compatible plans and does not itself give a polynomial-time bound. The tractability claim for the continuous version comes from the flow-LP formulation of this mirror, rather than a complexity theorem proved in the paper. I would still defend it as a valid positive case: the population is genuinely the continuous object, the complete-type regime is plausible, and the equilibrium problem keeps the paper’s agents, prices, and market-clearing question intact.

Two natural follow-ups are whether one can compute the full set of high-multiplicity equilibrium prices and type payoffs, and how the mirror changes for the paper’s \(0\)-equilibrium when agents account for aggregate capacities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 6 is the strongest ground against this anchor: it characterizes \(-1\)-equilibrium prices through equivalent conditions, including system (23), but gives no complexity bound, algorithm, hardness result, or tractable separation procedure. The system ranges over compatible buyer and seller plans; the proposition does not show how to check it efficiently. So the proposed continuous computation is a new algorithmic question adjacent to the paper’s result, rather than a continuization of a computational result the paper establishes.

The best version of the mirror is still convincing as a model. A large procurement market can plausibly have many buyers and sellers in a small number of complete types. Fractional type-level trades can represent averages of integral bundles, and the equilibrium question retains the paper’s prices, capacities, and market clearing. I see no sound objection based on multiplicity, identity, or the continuum limit.

That leaves the negative case weak. The mirror may be worthwhile under ChoCo’s broader aim of charting computational questions for continuous populations, but Proposition 6 alone does not establish that case. I cannot honestly defeat the stronger mirror; if a well-posed continuous computational question suffices, this anchor survives.
