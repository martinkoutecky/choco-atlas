The continuous mirror question

Given a finite house set \(H\), acceptable ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(c_h\), a rational bound \(b\), and an increase-only or signed-change mode, decide whether there are rational changes \(\delta_h\) and an allocation \(x_{t,h}\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\), zero allocation to unacceptable houses, and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le c_h+\delta_h\), such that the allocation is perfect and popular. Require \(\delta_h\ge 0\) in increase-only mode, \(c_h+\delta_h\ge 0\), and \(|\delta_h|\le b\). Popularity means that every feasible alternative reallocation of the same type mass, possibly leaving some unmatched, has aggregate incumbent vote at least zero, with each unit of mass voting according to its type's ranking and unmatched ranked last.

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings, and \(\mu_t\) is the share of applicants with type \(t\). The decisions are per-capita capacity changes \(\delta_h\) and a type-mass allocation \(x_{t,h}\); popularity compares that allocation with every feasible reallocation under the same changed capacities, summing the type-level votes.

The objection that survived

Theorem 8's bounds of one or two seats become \(1/n\) or \(2/n\) under the stated normalization, so the reduction does not establish complexity for a fixed positive per-capita bound.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s capacity-adjustment problem for a perfect popular matching. Picture a large school or internship cohort: many applicants rank the same finite set of placements in the same order, and planners ask how much to change each placement’s capacity so everyone can be assigned and no alternative assignment wins a majority of applicants.

A type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking of the houses the applicant finds acceptable, with being unmatched below every acceptable house. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with total mass \(1\). For example, \(N=100{,}000\) applicants might fall into \(\tau=500\) recurring preference types. Each individual still receives one whole house; \(x_{t,h}\) records what fraction of type \(t\)’s mass receives house \(h\). House capacities \(c_h\) and their changes are measured as shares of the cohort.

My anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It establishes that the paper’s discrete \(\maxpop\) decision problem is NP-complete in two cases: when only capacity increases are allowed and the maximum permitted change is \(2\); and when both increases and decreases are allowed and the maximum absolute change is \(1\). The proof is by reductions from a restricted three-dimensional matching problem: the elements are partitioned into three equal sets, there are \(3\hat n\) triples, each triple contains one element from each part, and each element occurs in exactly three triples; the question is whether there is an exact cover. The paper also establishes membership in NP. The result concerns changing house capacities enough to obtain an applicant-perfect popular matching, not the complexity of an encoding.

Here is the corresponding continuous decision problem, Continuous Max-Popular Capacity Adjustment. Its input is a finite house set \(H\), a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), baseline rational capacities \(c_h\), a rational bound \(b\), and a mode specifying increase-only changes or signed changes. It asks whether there are capacity changes \(\delta_h\) and an allocation \(x_{t,h}\) such that \(c_h+\delta_h\ge 0\), \(\max_h|\delta_h|\le b\), and \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le c_h+\delta_h\). In increase-only mode also require \(\delta_h\ge0\). The allocation must be perfect: all mass is assigned to acceptable houses.

Define popularity by comparing the incumbent allocation with every feasible alternative. For each type \(t\), let \(z_{t,h,g}\) be mass currently at \(h\) that the alternative assigns to \(g\), where \(g\) may also be unmatched. Require \(\sum_g z_{t,h,g}=x_{t,h}\); the alternative’s total assignment to each house must respect its changed capacity. The incumbent is popular if every such alternative has nonnegative total incumbent vote: each unit of mass counts \(+1\) when its current house is preferred to the alternative, \(-1\) when the alternative is preferred, and \(0\) when they are equal. This captures the paper’s majority comparison at population scale. A discrete instance with \(n\) applicants maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), \(c_h=q_h/n\), and \(b=K/n\), where \(K\) is the discrete change bound. The continuous question allows type mass to split across houses, while still assigning each infinitesimal applicant an ordinary house.

I expect this mirror’s answer to lean toward Class A, though that is a conjecture, not a result. The paper’s reduction uses an exact-cover choice among sets; when choices can be fractional, the regular three-dimensional matching instance has the fractional solution that selects every triple to extent \(1/3\). That suggests the reduction’s integrality barrier may dissolve. A tractable answer might come from a type-level allocation LP together with a dual characterization of popularity, but the universal comparison against alternatives means that tractability still needs to be established.

The main weakness is that Theorem 8’s reductions do not themselves exhibit a high-multiplicity regime: the number of distinct preference types may grow with the instance. Also, allowing a type to split its mass is a real relaxation of discrete matching, even though each person still gets one house. I think authors would recognize the proposed problem as their \(\maxpop\) question for a cohort measured in shares, but they could reasonably insist on studying how its solutions relate to integer seat counts.

This mirror covers Theorem 8 only. It generates concrete follow-up questions: is either mode polynomial-time solvable in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length; can the paper’s popularity characterization yield an LP or separation algorithm for the continuous problem; and how much discrete optimality or feasibility can be recovered from a continuous solution when the cohort is large?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 8 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and the population version is credible: a large applicant cohort can have recurring preference rankings, while capacities and majority votes are measured as shares. The proposed flow \(z_{t,h,g}\) gives a concrete way to compare assignments, so I do not see a basic modeling flaw or an identity objection.

The best negative point is the scale mismatch. Theorem 8 proves hardness for maximum changes of one or two seats, with small gadget capacities. Dividing by cohort size \(n\) sends those bounds to \(1/n\) or \(2/n\), which vanish. Keeping a positive per-capita bound instead asks a rescaled capacity-planning question. In that version the exact-cover gadget loses its integral choice: because each element occurs in three sets, assigning mass \(1/3\) to each incident set covers it fractionally. This weakens the connection to the theorem’s hardness and gives no basis for the proponent’s conjecture that the continuous problem is tractable.

There is also substantial adjacent algorithmic work: Kavitha, Mestre, and Nasre give efficient algorithms for popular mixed matchings with capacitated houses. [Popular mixed matchings](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcs.2010.03.028) This makes the proposed popularity machinery look less novel, but it does not solve the population-level capacity-adjustment problem. It is evidence of relevant fractional-matching technology, not a continuous-population collision.

So I can challenge the claim that Theorem 8’s reduction predicts an especially valuable or tractable mirror. I cannot make the stronger case that no worthwhile mirror exists: per-capita capacity planning for a large cohort remains a sensible computational question the authors could recognize. The negative case is weak at that point.
