The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational complexity result. Proposition 1 says that the proposed mechanism implements the top-\(k\) allocation in iteratively undominated strategies, after one round of weak-dominance elimination and at most two rounds of strict-dominance elimination. Proposition 2 establishes a pairwise-deviation vulnerability for Olszewski’s mechanism under its stated subsidy condition; Proposition 3 establishes pairwise stability for the paper’s mechanism under positive valuations and nonnegative bids. These are mechanism-design results, not results that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. I would not present any of them as a qualifying anchor.

There is a plausible *exploratory* mirror of Proposition 1. Consider a population of permit applicants with finitely many rational valuation types. Let \(\mu\) be a distribution over types \((v,h)\), where \(h=1\) marks the top-valued group, that group has mass \(\kappa\), and every \(h=1\) valuation exceeds every \(h=0\) valuation by more than a known fee \(\delta>0\). The regime could be a large allocation of identical permits, with supply scaling as a fixed fraction \(\kappa=k/n\) of the applicant population and many applicants sharing valuation and top-group status.

Define a continuous-\(\kappa\) Solomon implementation problem: each agent chooses whether to enter and, if an auction occurs, submits a bid. If entering mass is at most \(\kappa\), entrants receive a unit for free; otherwise, a uniform-price auction allocates mass \(\kappa\) to the highest bids, with boundary ties allocated by selecting the requisite mass of tied agents. The question is whether the top-\(\kappa\)-mass allocation, free of charge to applicants and planner, is the outcome of every iteratively undominated strategy profile. This is recognizably a population-scaled version of the paper’s implementation question. If whole type cohorts are the strategic actors, I would expect the dominance argument to extend and the problem to be tractable. That is a proposed research question, however—not a result established by this paper or a valid computational anchor.

The weak point is substantial: in an atomless population, a single agent has zero mass and cannot push participation from \(\kappa\) to above \(\kappa\). The paper’s argument depends on individual agents being pivotal around the threshold of \(k+1\) entrants. Treating a whole type as the deviating actor may restore that logic, but changes the strategic unit. Whether the authors would accept that as their continuous analogue is therefore unresolved.

My positive case would cover only Proposition 1; Propositions 2 and 3 concern pairwise collusion and stability, not computational complexity, and I would not stretch the mirror to include them. The strongest honest conclusion is that the paper suggests a natural population-scaled implementation question, but supplies no named computational result to anchor the continuous computational-social-choice case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts before the proposed permit example: this paper has no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Proposition 1 proves implementation in iteratively undominated strategies; Propositions 2 and 3 concern pairwise deviations and stability. None gives a complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem with an encoded input. In particular, the paper’s \(Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\) is an arbitrary set of valuation profiles, not an input representation for a complexity question. A computational follow-up could be worthwhile, but it would introduce a new problem rather than continuize a result in this paper.

The permit scenario is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many applicants can share valuation types, and the supply can scale as a fraction \(\kappa=k/n\). That is not an objection to the mirror. The harder issue is what happens to the strategic argument. In the finite mechanism, one person’s entry can change the number of participants from \(k\) to \(k+1\), triggering an auction and its fee. With atomless agents, a single person has zero mass and cannot make that change. If whole valuation types choose together, the model has type cohorts as strategic players; if individuals choose, the threshold response and the paper’s pointwise dominance argument need a new nonatomic-game formulation. Either may be a legitimate implementation question, but neither supplies the missing computational anchor.

One could add an algorithmic task—for example, deciding implementation from a rational finite-support distribution and an explicit representation of the information structure. That might merit study, but its substance would be the computational complexity of iterated-dominance analysis in a newly specified game. The paper does not formulate or motivate that task as a computational result. The same gap applies to turning its pairwise-bribery propositions into a mass-coalition problem: scaling a pair of agents into positive-mass coalitions changes the question.

So the proponent’s strongest mirror passes the population-regime test, but not ChoCo’s computational one. This is a strong case against treating the paper as a continuous-computation anchor; it is not a claim that continuous implementation theory has no worthwhile questions.
