What the mirror covers

No named computational result is covered. The proposed calibration reuses the paper's incentive and participation conditions for typed populations, while leaving its finite-player equilibrium analysis and pivotal effects behind.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result of the required kind in this paper. Proposition 1 derives the direction and threshold of a base-tier IC bound; Proposition 2 derives the IR bound. Theorem 1 says that, if the cost-adjusted deviation gap is nonnegative, the all-conforming profile is a Nash equilibrium within the two-strategy set (with the stated agent-by-agent condition for Tier 3). Corollary 1 says that, when IC gives an upper bound and the IR lower bound exceeds it, no ratio satisfies both sufficient conditions for this construction. These are proved analytical statements, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. None is a qualifying anchor, so a compliant anchor-by-anchor case cannot be made.

The strongest positive case I can make is a candidate research question, motivated by Theorem 1 and the IC/IR bounds but not anchored to a computational result in the paper. Call it *Mean-Field 3TTM Calibration*. Consider a very large binary crowdsourcing or forecasting task: many workers respond to the same item, with relatively few exact classes of signal accuracy and effort costs. A type is \(\theta=(\epsilon_\theta,c_\theta^C,c_\theta^{NC})\); \(\mu_\theta\) is that type’s fraction of the population. Workers share the prior \(p\), and conditional on the latent label \(G\), their signals are independent and correct with probability \(1-\epsilon_\theta\), where \(0<\epsilon_\theta<1/2\). Each chooses the paper’s conforming strategy, reporting the signal, or its non-conforming strategy, reporting the prior-favored label \(U\).

In the all-conforming population, let \(\bar\epsilon=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\epsilon_\theta\). The continuum has mass \(1-\bar\epsilon\) reporting \(G\) and mass \(\bar\epsilon\) reporting the other label, so majority aggregation selects \(G\). Let \(b>0\) be a fixed penalty-pool intensity per agent and \(r=b\rho\) the reward-pool intensity. This corresponds to pools of size \(Nb\) and \(Nr\) in an \(N\)-agent population, so equal splitting leaves finite per-agent payoffs as \(N\) grows. Write \(q^+=\Pr(U=G)\), determined by \(p\) and the paper’s prior-favored reporting rule. A type-\(\theta\) worker’s limiting expected payoffs are

\[ u_\theta^C(\rho) =b\left(\rho\frac{1-\epsilon_\theta}{1-\bar\epsilon} -\frac{\epsilon_\theta}{\bar\epsilon}\right)-c_\theta^C, \qquad u_\theta^{NC}(\rho) =b\left(q^+\frac{\rho}{1-\bar\epsilon} -\frac{1-q^+}{\bar\epsilon}\right)-c_\theta^{NC}. \]

The problem is: given the rational type masses, signal accuracies, costs, prior, and \(b\), compute the set
\[ \mathcal R=\{\rho>0:\ u_\theta^C(\rho)\ge 0 \ \text{and}\ u_\theta^C(\rho)\ge u_\theta^{NC}(\rho) \text{ for every }\theta\}. \]
A solution returns the feasible interval, or certifies that it is empty; it can also report the interval’s infimum and whether that endpoint is feasible. This asks for an all-conforming equilibrium with individually rational participation in the paper’s restricted strategy set. Each constraint is linear in \(\rho\), so for finite support the expected answer is a tractable, exact feasibility calculation in the number of types and input bit length—Class A. Type-specific upper and lower bounds can still conflict, echoing the construction-specific empty intervals in Corollary 1.

The regime is a large cohort assigned to one binary item, with many workers sharing each exact accuracy-and-cost type: population size \(N\) is large relative to support size \(\tau\). The authors could plausibly recognize this as the large-population limit of their majority-based incentive and participation question. It retains their latent label, private signals, deterministic prior-informed alternative, majority outcome, pool allocation, costs, and IC/IR tests; the per-capita pool scaling is the co-modification needed to keep incentives meaningful.

The weak point is substantial: a massless worker cannot change the outcome, so this mirror removes finite-population pivot effects that may matter to the paper’s deviation calculation. It also yields a simple one-variable feasibility problem rather than a new complexity landscape, and the paper itself does not claim computational difficulty. I would treat it as a plausible continuous extension of one part of the paper, not as evidence that the paper contains a computational result to continuize. The natural follow-ups are finite-\(N\) approximation bounds, robust calibration when the type masses are uncertain, and whether the Tier 2 and stake-weighted Tier 3 extensions preserve similarly tractable feasibility.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives no computational result to continuize. Propositions 1 and 2 rearrange payoff inequalities into bounds on one ratio; Corollary 1 intersects those bounds. Theorem 1 is the two-strategy Nash condition written as a deviation inequality. These are analytical statements about the proposed mechanism, not complexity results or algorithmic problems.

The proposed Mean-Field 3TTM calibration is the strongest repair, and its formulas look sound under its assumptions: pools scale with population size, types share exact signal and cost parameters, and each individual has zero mass. But that constructs a new asymptotic calibration question. For finite type support, it intersects finitely many affine constraints on \(\rho\); the coefficients are type-weighted averages. The paper already derives the relevant ratio bounds, so the continuous version adds heterogeneous types and a limit, without opening a computational landscape. Adding stake types leaves the same basic calculation. Optimizing over a few pool parameters also remains a small feasibility problem.

A more ambitious version could keep positive-mass deviations so that a group’s report can change the majority. That changes the paper’s unilateral-deviation equilibrium question into coalition or mass-deviation stability. It may be a worthwhile research direction, but it would need a new stability concept and analysis before it could count as a mirror of Theorem 1. Retaining finite-population pivotal effects instead keeps the original game, but gives up the continuum limit that makes the proposed formulas simple.

So the negative case is strongest on scope and contribution: there is no named computational problem in the paper, and the most faithful continuous extension is principally a new mean-field mechanism-calibration exercise. It is weaker as a universal claim. The paper’s subject is incentive calibration, and authors could reasonably recognize large crowds with repeated signal-and-cost types as their setting. If that recognition is enough, the proponent has a plausible mirror; I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario.
