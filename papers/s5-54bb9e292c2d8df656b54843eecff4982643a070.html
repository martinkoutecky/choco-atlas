The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Equations (1)–(4) define the voting game and its power indices; the tables report computed results. Neither is a named complexity result, so there is no anchor to restate or a continuous problem to pair with one.

The best positive lead I can offer is therefore prospective, not anchored: a continuum limit of the paper’s dual-quota voting-power model. Imagine a large federation with many voting jurisdictions. A type \(t\) is a jurisdiction profile, including its population contribution; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of jurisdictions of that type. For \(n\) jurisdictions, type \(t\) contributes \(n\mu_t\) jurisdictions, each with population weight \(a_t/n\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=1\). A coalition wins if it meets both the member-count threshold \(\alpha\) and the population threshold \(\beta\). The question would be: for each type, do its aggregate normalized Banzhaf power and aggregate Shapley–Shubik power converge as \(n\) grows, and if so, can those limits be characterized and approximated from \((\mu_t,a_t,\alpha,\beta)\)?

This has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a federation with many jurisdictions, relatively few repeated jurisdiction profiles, and binary votes under the same kind of two-part quota rule. It keeps the paper’s underlying concern—how pivotal power is distributed under a dual threshold—and asks what happens to that distribution in a large-system regime. The likely direction is open from the paper: the limit might admit a tractable characterization, or threshold interactions might make it continuum-specifically hard. Questions about convergence rates and sensitivity to changes in type masses follow naturally.

The weakness is substantial: this is not one of the paper’s results, and it replaces the EU’s 27 distinct member states with many jurisdictions. In the paper, citizens’ populations change the weights of country-players; citizens are not themselves the voting agents. Its calculations are about a finite weighted voting game, not the computational complexity of a high-multiplicity problem. So there is a conceivable continuous extension, but no honest anchor-based positive case for this paper under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. The paper states no theorem or proposition about complexity, exact algorithms, approximation, or parameterized algorithms. Equations (1)–(4) define the dual-quota game and its Banzhaf and Shapley–Shubik indices; the results are computed index values for the EU’s 27 member states. The proponent agrees that its federation proposal is prospective, not an anchor.

The strongest version of that proposal is coherent: take a federation with many repeated jurisdiction types, give each type a population contribution and mass, retain the membership and population quotas, and ask for exact or approximate algorithms for type-aggregate power—or for the limit of those values as the number of jurisdictions grows. But it changes the object of study. In the paper, people are not the voting players: sovereign states are, and citizens’ population affects each state’s weight. Treating citizens as players would lose the state-count quota; treating jurisdictions as a continuum preserves the rule but replaces the EU question with one about power in a large federation.

There is also a substantive gap between that proposal and the programme’s usual continuous models. Banzhaf and Shapley–Shubik power are defined through discrete pivotal events: a player changes a coalition’s result, or is pivotal in an ordering. A nonatomic limit has no individual pivotal player. Aggregating power by type can address that, but it must be defined through a sequence of finite games, with quota rounding and the scale of the pivotal events specified. Studying that limit could be worthwhile; it would be a new asymptotic theory of power indices, not a continuous version of a computational result established by this paper.

The paper’s fixed set of 27 uniquely constituted states also supplies no high-multiplicity regime of its own. A different federation with many similar jurisdictions might supply one, so I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario. The negative case is strong for the paper as written: it offers no anchored computational mirror, and the proposed lead requires a substantial change in the research question. It is weaker as the universal claim that no plausible continuous mirror could ever be worth studying.
