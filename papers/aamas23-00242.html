The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-unit weights \(w_t\), rational prizes \(p_1\ge\cdots\ge p_k>0\), a type-symmetric tie rule, and a feasible anonymous current payoff rate \(r_t\), decide whether there exist \(i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and a mass coalition \(y\in[0,\mu]\) such that \(R(y)=\sum_t r_ty_t<p_i\) and, under every measurable partition of the residual mass \(\mu-y\), \(y\), with weight \(W(y)=\sum_t w_ty_t\), ranks among the top \(i\) coalitions under that tie rule.

The model it lives in

An atomless, type-symmetric \(k\)-WVG with type masses \(\mu_t\), additive coalition weight \(W(y)\), prize vector \(p\), a finite tie-boundary rule, and current payoff \(R(y)\); the decision variables are \(y\) and \(i\), maximizing \(p_i-R(y)\) subject to universal residual-partition attainability.

The objection that survived

The paper permits arbitrary identity-sensitive tie-breaking and heterogeneous outcome payoffs, whereas the proposed finite-type model restricts both to type-symmetric data and therefore does not mirror every ∃-SCD instance.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.5 and the associated ∃-SCD beneficial-deviation problem, while leaving Theorems 6.2 and 6.6, the stability and core results, the efficiency propositions, and the other deviation concepts untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can universal residual-partition attainability, including tie boundaries, be characterized and solved in polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the encoding length?
  • Does the continuous SCD-core admit an LP-duality or threshold characterization?
  • What approximation or rounding guarantees follow when a rational mass society is expanded into finitely many clones?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a single anchor: Theorem 6.5, proved in this paper, which shows that \(\exists\)-SCD is NP-hard. I would mirror the paper’s beneficial-deviation problem, not its whole stability theory.

Consider a large federation of interchangeable delegates. Each delegate has one of \(\tau\) constitutional weight classes, and delegates of the same class have the same weight and the same tie-breaking status. The population contains fractions \(\mu_t\) of the classes, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\tau\ll N\). This could represent many constituency delegates, shareholder representatives, or local political organisations competing for \(k\) cabinet positions or institutional prizes. The paper’s political-party example has too few players; this is the more convincing high-multiplicity regime.

The continuous problem, which I would call Anonymous-SCD\(_\infty\), is as follows. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-agent weights \(w_t\), \(k\) rational prizes \(p_1\ge\cdots\ge p_k>0\), a type-symmetric tie-breaking rule, and an anonymous current payoff \(r_t\) for each unit of type \(t\). The payoff field is assumed to describe a feasible anonymous outcome. A proposed deviating coalition is a mass vector \(y\in\mathbb{R}^{T}\) with \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\). Its aggregate weight and current payoff are \(W(y)=\sum_t w_ty_t\) and \(R(y)=\sum_t r_ty_t\).

The residual mass \(\mu-y\) may form any measurable partition. We say that \(y\) is \(i\)-attaining if, under every such residual partition and the prescribed tie-breaking rule, the deviating coalition ranks among the top \(i\) coalitions and therefore receives a prize of at least \(p_i\). The decision question is whether there exist \(i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and \(y\) such that \(y\) is \(i\)-attaining and \(R(y)<p_i\). Equivalently, the optimization version maximizes the deviation gain \(p_i-R(y)\) over all \(i\) and all \(i\)-attaining mass coalitions.

This is recognisably the paper’s problem. The action is still coalition formation, the coalition strength is still the sum of player weights, the deviators still face the paper’s maximally pessimistic residual response, and the improvement test is still current payoff versus the prize guaranteed in every residual partition. Only the population representation changes from integer multiplicities to rational mass.

In the atomless high-multiplicity regime, the residual partition has a simple characterization. Away from tie boundaries, a residual population can form \(i\) coalitions each strictly heavier than \(y\) exactly when \(W(\mu-y)>iW(y)\). Hence \(y\) is \(i\)-attaining precisely when \((i+1)W(y)>W(\mu)\), with a finite boundary case for the chosen tie rule. For each \(i\), Anonymous-SCD\(_\infty\) therefore reduces to linear feasibility, or to the LP

\(\max\{p_i-R(y):0\le y\le\mu,\ (i+1)W(y)>W(\mu)\}\).

Thus I expect Anonymous-SCD\(_\infty\) to be in Class A: polynomial-time solvable in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the encoding length of the rational data. The strict inequalities and equality cases can be handled by standard rational LP procedures and the finite type-level tie rule.

This prediction illustrates exactly why the mirror is interesting. Theorem 6.5 reduces from Partition by asking whether a coalition contains a subset of item players with total weight exactly \(S\). In the continuous regime, a mass of a weight type can be divided, so the subset-sum combinatorics becomes a linear filling problem. The hardness is driven by population multiplicity and indivisibility, while the weight-ranking and prize structure remain intact. This is the kind of Class-A collapse that continuization is supposed to expose.

The high-multiplicity bridge is direct for rational data. Clearing denominators turns \(\mu_t\) into repeated clones of type \(t\); conversely, integer coalition counts normalize to mass vectors. A continuous solution with a strict improvement margin can be rounded to sufficiently many clones while preserving the deviation. The resulting algorithm would therefore give a useful preprocessing or additive approximation method for large finite federations, even though the original finite problem remains NP-hard.

I would not claim that Theorem 4.9 or Corollary 4.12 transfers literally. Their “iterative singleton winner” structure is tied to indivisible players: in an atomless population, a singleton has zero mass and cannot win a prize by itself. That is a real limitation, not something to hide. The proposed mirror covers Theorem 6.5 and the paper’s \(\exists\)-SCD question only; it does not cover the paper’s full core-existence theory, MCD/wMCD variants, or arbitrary identity-based tie-breaking.

The weakest point is therefore clear: the paper’s coalitions are sets of indivisible players, whereas Anonymous-SCD\(_\infty\) permits fractional participation by a type. If the authors regard indivisibility as essential rather than as the population-multiplicity source of hardness, they may call this an extension rather than a direct mirror. The case remains persuasive because the paper’s central SCD quantifier and weighted-prize semantics survive exactly, and because large populations of exchangeable delegates provide a credible setting in which mass coalition formation is the natural resolution.

The immediate follow-up questions are whether the continuous SCD-core can be characterized by LP duality, whether MCD and wMCD also collapse to polynomial-time mass optimization, and how much discrete hardness remains when clone counts are integral but the number of weight types \(\tau\) is small.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 6.5, but the proposed mirror does not preserve the problem that theorem makes hard.

The reduction from Partition depends on two discrete features. A coalition must contain an indivisible subset of named item players, and the successful coalition wins precisely on an equality \(w(C)=2S\), with ties resolved in favour of the coalition containing the distinguished player \(1\). Thus both subset indivisibility and identity-sensitive tie-breaking are load-bearing.

The proposed mass model removes both. Once \(y_t\) may be any real number in \([0,\mu_t]\), the item population can be filled continuously to any aggregate weight between zero and its total weight. The condition \(w(C)=S\) no longer asks for a subset of items summing to \(S\); it is simply a linear filling condition. Moreover, under atomless semantics, the residual population can be partitioned into \(i\) coalitions beating a deviating mass exactly according to its total weight. Away from ties, attainability depends only on

\[ (i+1)W(y)>W(\mu), \]

not on the composition of the residual coalitions. The paper’s partition-function externality has therefore disappeared. What remains is a threshold selection problem over two additive quantities, not the paper’s SCD problem with indivisible coalition members.

That this new problem may be tractable is not itself an objection: a discrete hardness collapse is precisely something continuization can reveal. The objection is semantic. The proposed LP is tractable because it has replaced “choose a coalition of players” by “choose a divisible mass of types.” It is an atomless coalition-formation model inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 6.5.

The more faithful alternatives do not rescue the mirror. Keeping integer clone counts gives a high-multiplicity \(k\)-WVG, but not a continuous society. Allowing real counts returns to the proposed fractional extension. Requiring an entire type mass to move together preserves Partition-like choices, but makes each type an indivisible super-player; the population distribution is then only a list of blocs, not a continuum. Retaining the paper’s tie-breaking rule requires named identities. Encoding each identity as a separate type makes \(\tau\) grow with the number of players and destroys the intended compression; assigning the distinguished player vanishing mass makes its tie-breaking role disappear. A hybrid with finitely many persistent atoms could retain the theorem, but then the hardness resides in those atoms rather than in the continuous population.

There is also a problem with the proposed payoff field \(r_t\). In the paper, payoffs are part of an outcome and can differ between otherwise identical players because they may belong to different coalitions or receive different shares. To make \(r_t\) a type parameter, one must refine types by current payoff and often by coalition role. For the Partition construction, the item payoffs are generally all distinct, so the alleged few-type federation is not the instance regime supporting the theorem. Refining types makes the mass formulation a fractionalized encoding of individually specified players rather than a genuine high-multiplicity object.

The delegate story does not resolve this choice. If delegates are independent, they remain indivisible coalition members and the mass model changes their action space. If they coordinate as a party or bloc, the blocs are indivisible and cannot be fractionally selected. There is no obvious author-recognizable interpretation that preserves both the paper’s SCD semantics and the proposed atomless variables.

This is not a source-gate objection: Theorem 6.5 is a genuine named computational result. Nor is it an objection that the continuous problem lands in polynomial time. The negative case is that every faithful version retains indivisible or identity-bearing agents and is therefore not continuous, while every genuinely atomless version changes the deviation problem into a different threshold model. If ChoCo accepts such author-recognizable extensions as worthwhile, the proponent’s federation example remains a plausible Class-A project. Under a stricter mirror standard, however, this paper has no surviving anchor.
