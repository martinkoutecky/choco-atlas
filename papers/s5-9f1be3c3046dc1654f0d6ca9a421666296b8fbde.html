The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an honest computational anchor for a continuous mirror here. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result.

Its central result is an impossibility theorem: no aggregation procedure can guarantee complete and consistent collective judgments while satisfying universal domain, anonymity, and systematicity. The paper attributes that result to List and Pettit’s earlier work, “Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result” (2002); it is not a numbered result in this paper, and it is an axiomatic impossibility, not a result about computational complexity.

There is a conceivable continuous population model in which voter types are complete, consistent judgment sets and their masses are population fractions. But asking whether a procedure can aggregate those masses into complete, consistent collective judgments would recast the paper’s axiomatic question, not mirror a named computational result of this paper. I therefore have no qualifying anchor—and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor, and I think that is the right verdict. This paper contains no complexity result to continuize. Its central impossibility theorem concerns whether *any* aggregation procedure can satisfy universal domain, anonymity and systematicity while guaranteeing complete, consistent group judgments. The paper presents that as an axiomatic result, citing List and Pettit’s earlier work; its own contribution is to widen the integrity challenge and discuss its implications for public groups. There is no computational problem here whose complexity the paper establishes.

The strongest candidate for a mirror would represent complete individual judgment sets as types, give those types rational masses, and ask whether the resulting group judgments are consistent. But that changes the question: testing the output of a specified rule on one mass profile is not the theorem’s question about the existence of a rule satisfying axioms across all profiles. Searching for a rule, or minimizing the mass of judgment changes needed to restore consistency, would add a computational objective the paper does not study. Those could be worthwhile questions in their own right, but they are not continuous mirrors of a named result here. The negative case is strongest on that boundary; it does not show that no related computational problem could be designed.
