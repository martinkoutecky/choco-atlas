The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. It reports experiments and draws an empirical conjecture. The NP-hardness of single-voter STV manipulation is attributed to Bartholdi and Orlin [2]; this paper cites it but does not prove it as a numbered result. The figures’ finding that the tested instances were usually easy to solve is not a worst-case complexity classification. So there are zero anchors, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand a prover under your rules.

The closest positive lead, explicitly not a qualifying anchor, is continuous single-agent STV manipulation. Give a fixed tie-breaking rule, a target candidate \(c^\star\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, including a distinguished manipulator of mass \(\delta=1/N\) whose true ranking is \(t_0\). Ask whether some reported ranking \(r\) makes \(c^\star\) win under \(\mu-\delta e_{t_0}+\delta e_r\); a solution is such an \(r\), or a proof that none exists. A plausible regime is a large election with many voters sharing a relatively small repertoire of rankings, so the support size \(\tau\) is much smaller than \(N\). Since discrete profiles embed as rational-mass instances, I would expect hardness to transfer from the cited discrete problem; a separate question is whether bounded \(\tau\) makes the problem tractable.

Its weakest point is central: preserving one manipulator means retaining an atom of mass \(1/N\), which shrinks as the population grows. Replacing that atom with a positive-mass bloc gives a cleaner continuum model but changes the problem to coalition manipulation. The paper treats that as an open empirical question, so this is only a plausible follow-on, not a result-based mirror of the paper’s findings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s zero-anchor point is the strongest part of the negative case: this paper establishes no named worst-case complexity result to continuize. It reports experiments and improves a search procedure, while citing the NP-hardness result from earlier work. Its findings about how often finite elections were easy to solve are empirical observations, not a complexity classification.

The proposed single-manipulator mirror preserves those finite experiments only by keeping the manipulator’s mass at \(1/N\). Then the decisive input is still a finite profile with one marked voter, encoded as rational masses; it does not relax the one-voter manipulation question. If instead \(N\) grows and that mass tends to zero, a typical limiting society will not retain whether one ballot could change an elimination. Any surviving cases depend on vanishingly small score margins and tie-breaking details. The paper’s IC, urn, and resampled-election experiments do not supply a law over limiting societies that would preserve their search-cost findings.

A positive-mass coalition avoids the vanishing-manipulator problem, and a continuous coalition question could be sensible. But it would study a different problem, one the paper explicitly leaves open, rather than mirror a result the paper established. I can’t honestly rule out that such a follow-on would be worthwhile; the negative case is strongest against calling it a continuous mirror of this paper’s findings.
