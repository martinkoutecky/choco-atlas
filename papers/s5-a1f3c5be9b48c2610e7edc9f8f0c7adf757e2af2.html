What the mirror covers

The only proposed anchor is Theorem 1; the TTC description and cited characterizations provide no additional named computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a natural high-multiplicity setting for assignment: a large housing or course exchange where many people share a ranking over a small set of interchangeable object classes and own an object in one of those classes. Let a type be \(t=(P_t,e_t)\), where \(P_t\) ranks the classes and \(e_t\) is the endowed class, and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the population. A mass allocation would be \(x_{tj}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}=q_j\), where \(q_j=\sum_{t:e_t=j}\mu_t\). Then \(x_{tj}/\mu_t\) is the type’s average allocation. This makes the population continuous while keeping the type support small.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper apart from its “if” direction, which follows from Ma (1994). It states that, for a deterministic initial endowment, a probabilistic assignment rule is SD-Pareto-efficient, SD-individually rational, and SD-top-strategy-proof if and only if it is TTC. That gives a structural target for a mass version of TTC. But Theorem 1 is a characterization of rules, not a computational result: it gives no complexity classification, and the paper computes no LP, MILP, or other formulation whose result could qualify as an anchor. So there are no qualifying anchors here, and I cannot honestly give a continuous computational problem “per anchor” or predict its place in the A/B/C trichotomy.

The positive lead is also a substantial model extension. The paper’s agents own distinct objects and rank those objects strictly; in the proposed regime, objects within a class must become interchangeable. And its fractional assignments are lottery outcomes for a finite population, not a continuous population. Theorem 1 could motivate a follow-on about a continuum TTC rule, but defining its incentive conditions and mass-level TTC would be new work, not a mirror established by this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only serious anchor, and it is a characterization of mechanisms, not a computational result. It says which rule is uniquely determined by SD-efficiency, individual rationality, and top-strategy-proofness; it gives no complexity classification for a problem. The paper describes TTC algorithmically, but does not study its computational complexity. Its probabilistic assignments also do not make the *population* continuous: they are lottery outcomes for a finite set of agents. Under ChoCo’s computational remit, there is no result here to classify.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is still a different kind of question. A large housing or course exchange could plausibly have types \(t=(P_t,e_t)\), where agents share preferences over object classes and an endowed class, with type masses \(\mu_t\). One could ask for a mass version of TTC or characterize mass assignment rules satisfying adapted axioms. That is a sensible population model; grouping interchangeable endowments is a legitimate co-modification, not a fatal objection. But characterizing such rules remains an axiomatic question. Asking for an algorithm to compute the mass-TTC outcome would add a computational problem the paper does not pose or classify. It may be worth studying on its own, but it is not a computational mirror of Theorem 1.

There is also a real modelling choice hidden in the incentive condition. For an anonymous rule that depends only on the aggregate distribution \(\mu\), one agent’s report changes no mass, so unilateral top-strategy-proofness can become vacuous. Preserving a meaningful condition requires adding a pointwise mechanism for individual reports or instead considering positive-mass group deviations; the latter changes the property the theorem characterizes. Neither choice rules out a useful follow-on, but both reinforce that it would require a new problem specification.

So the negative case is strong within ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper supplies no named computational result for a continuous-population mirror. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile computational question is weaker; one could build a research agenda around mass TTC. What the paper itself offers is no computational anchor for that agenda.
