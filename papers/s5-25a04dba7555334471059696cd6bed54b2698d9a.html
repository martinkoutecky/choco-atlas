The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(m\), let the voter types be complete rankings \(t\in S_m\). A distribution \(\mu\) is admissible when no forbidden configuration in the paper’s enriched group-separable characterization can be realized using distinct voter instances drawn from \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\). Find a distribution maximizing \(H(\mu)=-\sum_{t\in S_m}\mu_t\ln\mu_t\), and its optimal value.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(m\) candidates. The paper’s forbidden-configuration constraints apply to the positive-mass types, with arbitrarily many instances of each type available; the objective is Shannon entropy.

The objection that survived

Admissibility changes discontinuously when a type’s mass reaches zero, so the optimization reduces to finding a largest admissible support and introduces entropy as an objective absent from the paper; the asymptotic profile-count connection makes that extension plausible.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror captures the asymptotic growth rate of the enriched group-separable election count in Proposition 3.3, using Theorem 3.1’s recursive characterization for a maximizing-support witness. It leaves the paper’s other enumeration results and its strong-order results in Section 4 aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is the paper’s enumeration of enriched group-separable elections, viewed through its high-multiplicity limit. There are no named results here classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The strongest anchor is an exact counting result; the mirror below turns its growth rate into a continuous optimization question.

Lead mirror: Maximum-Entropy Enriched Society. Fix \(m\) candidates and let each voter type be a complete ranking \(t\in S_m\). A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings, with \(\mu_t\) the population share of type \(t\). Call \(\mu\) admissible when its positive-mass types, replicated in proportions approaching \(\mu\), satisfy the paper’s enriched group-separability constraints. Equivalently, no forbidden configuration from the paper can be realized using voter types in \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), with distinct voter instances available wherever the configuration requires them.

The decision variable is \(\mu\). The task is to find an admissible society maximizing
\[ H(\mu)=-\sum_{t\in S_m}\mu_t\ln\mu_t. \]
A solution is an optimal value and a distribution attaining it. This asks for the most diverse continuous population consistent with the paper’s domain restriction.

The anchor is Proposition 3.3, proved in this paper. For \(f(m,n)\), the number of enriched group-separable elections with \(m\) candidates and \(n\) electors, and \(f_r(m,n)\), the number when one preference is fixed, it proves
\[ f_r(m,n)=2^n f_r(m-1,n)-2^{n-1}f_r(m-2,n), \qquad f_r(0,n)=f_r(1,n)=1, \]
and \(f(m,n)=m!\,f_r(m,n)\). For fixed \(m\ge2\), this gives
\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln f(m,n)=(m-1)\ln 2. \]
Indeed, the recurrence gives \(f(m,n)^{1/n}\to 2^{m-1}\). Since there are only \(m!\) possible types, the exponential growth rate of valid \(n\)-voter profiles is the logarithm of the largest admissible type support. Thus the continuous optimum is
\[ H_m^\star=(m-1)\ln 2, \]
attained by a uniform society on an admissible support of size \(2^{m-1}\). Theorem 3.1’s recursive characterization also supplies such a support: recursively take rankings that put the new candidate at either end of rankings from the smaller candidate set. It yields \(2^{m-1}\) types, with mass \(2^{-(m-1)}\) each. This is a direct count-to-continuum bridge: finite profile counts determine the entropy of the limiting society.

The regime is fixed, relatively small \(m\) and large \(n\): for example, a very large electorate ranking a fixed shortlist of candidates, with many voters sharing each complete ranking. Then \(n\) can greatly exceed \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\), and the paper itself treats voters with identical preferences as repeated types. The interpretation is credible for large survey or election populations grouped by ballot. It requires the ranking to capture everything relevant about a voter, which is exactly the high-multiplicity assumption.

I expect the optimum-value problem to be tractable: the recurrence gives the value, and the recursive support gives a witness in time polynomial in its output size \(\tau\) (with masses of \(O(m)\) bits). The mirror covers Proposition 3.3’s enumeration result, not the paper’s full agenda. In particular, Section 4’s strong order on pairs of permutations fixes three voters and varies rankings over candidates; that is not population continuization.

The weak point is that the authors count finite elections; they do not optimize over continuous societies or make a complexity claim. Entropy captures the leading exponential rate and loses lower-order counting information. So this is an asymptotic computational mirror of their enumeration, not a continuous version of every result in the paper. It still seems plausible they would recognize it: their object is the number of profiles satisfying a voter-domain restriction, and the mirror asks which type distribution dominates that count when the population grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Proposition 3.3. It is a genuine exact enumeration result, but the paper’s theorems and propositions are about counting and characterizing restricted profiles, not the complexity of a computational problem. The entropy mirror therefore needs to supply a new task of its own.

Its count-to-entropy connection is mathematically plausible. In the large-population limit, profile counts are dominated by the largest admissible set of ranking types, and the corresponding entropy maximum is attained by a uniform distribution on such a set. But that exposes the weakness of the mirror: enriched group-separability is determined by which types have positive mass. Once a type has positive mass, its size does not affect whether the forbidden configurations occur. The continuous coordinates disappear from feasibility; entropy then selects uniform weights, leaving a discrete maximum-support problem. Changing a type’s mass from tiny positive to zero can change feasibility abruptly.

A more faithful version could ask for the full entropy rate at each empirical distribution. That still adds an entropy objective absent from the paper, and the domain condition remains support-based. A version with prescribed margins, mass-sensitive violations, or a cost for moving population mass could make the weights matter, but those additions would pose new problems rather than mirror a result here. Likewise, testing whether a given distribution’s support satisfies the restriction simply restates the forbidden-configuration characterization.

So the strongest case against this anchor is that it produces an asymptotic statistical summary of a combinatorial enumeration, not a continuous computational-social-choice problem of the kind the programme is charting. The honest weak point in that case is substantial: the paper counts repeated voter rankings, so a high-multiplicity population is natural, and the entropy interpretation is not contrived. If ChoCo includes continuous limits of enumeration as well as algorithmic problems, this anchor may survive; on the programme’s stated computational scope, it looks thin.
