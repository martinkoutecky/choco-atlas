The continuous mirror question

Given a finite metric support \(T=\{p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\}\), rational distances, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and \(2\le k\le\tau\), find rational assignments \(x_{ti}\ge0\) with \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\) and \(q_i=\sum_t x_{ti}>0\). Define \(D_i(t)=\sum_u x_{ui}d(p_t,p_u)/q_i\). Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in \((\tau,L)\) that returns an assignment and support centers \(z_i\in T\), with universal constants \(A,B\), such that \(D_i(t)\le A D_h(t)\) for every \(i\) with \(x_{ti}>0\) and every \(h\ne i\), while \(d(p_t,z_i)\le B\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\) whenever \(x_{ti}>0\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}_k\) is the support’s optimal \(k\)-center radius?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population distributed over a finite metric support, with type mass assignable across clusters. Each individual compares mass-weighted average distances to its own cluster and to every other cluster; the output is a constant-factor stable assignment with a constant-factor \(k\)-center guarantee.

The objection that survived

When a type is split across clusters, its mass can create a zero average distance in another cluster, so the ball-carving argument and a polynomial bound in \((\tau,L)\) need a new justification.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s average-distance IP-stability guarantee and its \(k\)-center guarantee; it leaves the Min-IP and Max-IP results, experiments, and other discussion untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can every rational mass distribution on a finite metric support be partitioned into \(k\) clusters with a universal constant IP-stability factor, and can such a partition be found in time polynomial in \((\tau,L)\)?
  • Can the same algorithm guarantee a constant-factor approximation to the support’s optimal \(k\)-center radius when type mass may be split?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. For any \(n\)-point metric space and \(k\le n\), Algorithm 2 runs in \(O(n^2T+n^2\log n)\) time, where \(T\) is the time to compute a point-to-point distance, and returns a \(k\)-clustering that is \(O(1)\)-IP stable and a constant-factor approximation to \(k\)-center. Theorem 1.3 is its informal summary, so I would count Theorem 3.1 as the single anchor. The paper mentions that deciding whether a 1-IP-stable clustering exists is NP-hard, even for \(k=2\), but attributes that result to Ahmadi et al. [AAK+22]; it is not a numbered hardness result of this paper, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large health system assigning patients to \(k\) care pathways. Patients with the same standardized clinical profile have the same distance-based preferences over who is grouped with them. There may be millions of patients but only \(\tau\) recurring profiles, with \(\tau\ll n\). A type is such a complete profile, including everything that affects those preferences; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of patients with that profile. Each patient still goes to one pathway. At the population level, however, a profile’s mass can be divided among pathways: \(x_{ti}\) is the amount of type \(t\)’s population mass assigned to pathway \(i\). That is a population continuization, while \(k\) and the pathways themselves remain discrete.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite metric support \(T=\{p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\}\), rational distances \(d(p_t,p_u)\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and \(2\le k\le\tau\). Find nonnegative rational assignments \(x_{ti}\) and centers \(z_i\in T\), with \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\) and \(q_i=\sum_t x_{ti}>0\) for every cluster. For a type assigned positive mass to cluster \(i\), define its average distance to that cluster as \(D_i(t)=\sum_u x_{ui}d(p_t,p_u)/q_i\). Require universal constants \(A,B\) such that \(D_i(t)\le A D_h(t)\) for every other cluster \(h\), and \(d(p_t,z_i)\le B\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\) for every type assigned to \(i\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}_k\) is the optimal \(k\)-center radius on the support. In the continuum, an individual has zero mass, so excluding that individual from their own cluster does not change its average. The displayed bounds in the paper’s proof suggest \(A=240\) and \(B=4\) after the weighted translation; the paper itself states the guarantees as \(O(1)\).

I expect this problem to be tractable, in the sense of Class A. The proof’s ball-carving argument compares numbers of points in balls and annuli; its key step is a majority-within-a-radius argument. Replacing counts by masses appears to preserve that reasoning, and a nonatomic cohort can be sliced to supply exactly the mass needed from an annulus. The greedy \(k\)-center step depends on the metric support rather than on population counts. That suggests an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the binary input length \(L\), instead of in the number of individuals \(n\).

The scope is deliberately the paper’s main average-distance result, Theorem 3.1, including its \(k\)-center guarantee. I would not stretch this case to cover the paper’s Min-IP and Max-IP results: the minimum-distance version risks becoming degenerate when a positive-mass cohort occupies one location, while the maximum-distance version is insensitive to masses except through cluster support. Nor would I claim that the cited discrete NP-hardness transfers to this fractional population problem. Further questions include how to round an assignment to multiples of \(1/n\) while preserving stability, whether exact 1-IP feasibility is tractable for mass assignments, and whether the best achievable stability factor can be approximated in terms of the instance’s optimum.

The soft spot is real: Theorem 3.1 is proved for individually listed points, with cardinalities and an \(n\)-based running time. This paper does not prove a rationally weighted version compressed to \(\tau\) types. The proposed mirror depends on showing that the ball-carving proof still works with fractional mass, including very small rational masses and exact mass slicing. But the required change tracks the paper’s central individual-preference question closely, and the proof’s majority argument gives a concrete reason to think the weighted extension will hold.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.1: this paper proves a polynomial-time algorithm for a named computational problem, so the “no computational result to mirror” objection does not apply. Nor is there a convincing regime objection. The paper explicitly allows multiple copies of the same point, and a large system with many patients sharing a finite set of clinical profiles is plausible.

The best case against is that the proposed weighted proof does not follow just by replacing counts with masses. Its exact-mass slicing can put the same type in two clusters. But an individual of that type then sees positive mass at distance zero in the other cluster. If that other cluster consists only of that type, its average distance is zero, while the individual’s own-cluster average may be positive; no finite multiplicative stability factor can satisfy the condition. More generally, splitting a type can destroy the proof’s lower bound on distances to other clusters. So the claim that the ball-carving argument transfers directly is unestablished.

That exposes a real modeling choice. Keep each type intact, and the mirror becomes a weighted clustering problem over a finite set of types. Allow types to split, and one needs to handle the stability constraints created by assigning otherwise identical agents to different clusters; the paper’s geometric invariant does not do that for free. Either version may take new work.

But this does not defeat the best mirror. The intact-type version is still a sensible high-multiplicity problem, and the split-type version is a valid population model if its assignment and stability conditions are stated carefully. Either could be worth investigating. The honest negative case is therefore weak: the proponent has overstated how directly the proof carries over, but I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
