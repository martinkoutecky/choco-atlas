The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly provide a qualifying computational-complexity anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The closest positive lead is Theorem 4.14, proved in the paper: under the authors’ \(\gamma\)-conjecture, their EV-aggregator game is balanced and therefore has a non-empty core. The implication from balancedness to core existence is Theorem 4.13, cited from [28]. This is a structural existence result, not a complexity result. The least-core LP in Equation (8) and the reported runtime comparisons are computational material, but they are not numbered results of the required kind.

If Theorem 4.14 is accepted as a structural anchor, my strongest proposed mirror is Continuous EV-Aggregator Least-Core. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of aggregator types and rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type completely specifies an aggregator’s hourly minimum and maximum energy requirements, charging capacity, and other parameters used by the market and bidding model. A coalition is a submass \(z\) with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\). Its value \(v_\mu(z)\) is the negative of its members’ electricity cost under joint bidding, with outsiders bidding individually as in the paper’s \(\gamma\)-conjecture. The hourly price-impact curves and the cost-allocation rule are part of the instance; market quantities must be scaled consistently with population size.

The question is to find a payoff rate \(x_t\) for each type and the smallest worst-case coalition excess \(\epsilon\), subject to efficiency, individual rationality, and stability:

\[ \begin{aligned} \min\quad &\epsilon\\ \text{s.t.}\quad &\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=v_\mu(\mu),\\ &x_t\ge \rho_t \quad (t\in T),\\ &v_\mu(z)-\sum_{t\in T}z_t x_t\le \epsilon \quad\text{for every }z\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t],\\ &\epsilon\ge 0. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\rho_t\) is the type’s per-unit stand-alone value. A solution is the rates \(x\) and optimal \(\epsilon\); \(\epsilon=0\) gives a stable grand-coalition allocation. This is the continuum analogue of the paper’s least-core computation, not a claim that the paper already gives this formulation.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a market with many independently operated charging sites—such as parking facilities or municipal depots—whose fleet and market parameters fall into a relatively small number of standard profiles. The sites, rather than the EVs inside each site, are the game’s agents. Mass records what fraction of sites have each complete profile. I would expect a Class A result for a restricted model with convex, explicitly represented price-impact curves if the worst-coalition pricing step can be solved by convex optimization. That separation step is the main open algorithmic question; Theorem 4.14 alone does not settle it. This mirror covers core stability and least-core allocation, not the paper’s Shapley-value approximation or its EV scheduling model in isolation.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper studies a small number of large aggregators, while the mirror needs many independently acting sites and allows type mass to split fractionally between coalitions. That regime is plausible, but it changes the granularity of coalition formation. So this is a defensible positive direction around the paper’s central stability result, not a complexity-theoretic mirror anchored by a qualifying named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. The proponent identifies Theorem 4.14, but it proves a structural fact: under the paper’s \(\gamma\)-conjecture, the finite game is balanced and its core is non-empty. The least-core LP in Equation (8) and the runtime plots do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. They leave open the most important algorithmic question the proposed mirror would ask.

The proposed mass-based least-core problem is more compelling than a literal continuum version of Theorem 4.14. It asks for payoff rates stable against every submass coalition, and finding the worst coalition could be a serious optimization problem. But that computational question is newly formulated: it is not a result established or classified by this paper. A more plausible setting—many charging depots drawn from a small set of standard profiles—helps make high multiplicity credible. It does not turn the paper’s core-existence theorem into a computational result. The authors might recognize the resulting question as relevant, but that cannot supply the missing anchor.

I would not argue that aggregator types are inherently unsuitable, or that a continuous game here could not be worthwhile. The negative case is weaker on that ground: a mass model of many similar depots seems plausible. Its force is narrower. This paper may inspire an adjacent continuous cooperative-game project, but it does not offer the named computational result the programme asked analysts to continuize.
