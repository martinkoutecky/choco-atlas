What the mirror covers

The proposed bridge covers neither proposition computationally: both are equilibrium claims for finite committees, and the paper presents no other numbered computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population regime behind this paper: a very large panel reviewing a binary claim, where many reviewers share the same signal record and precision. A type could be \(t=(k,s)\), with \(k\) the number of signals and \(s\in\{-1,1\}^{2k+1}\) their realized values; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the panel. Let \(x_t\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of type \(t\) voting \(1\). This is a natural high-multiplicity description of the jury model.

The closest precise continuous question would be: given finite support over signal types and their rational conditional masses \(\mu_t^\omega\) in each state \(\omega\), characterize the Bayesian Nash equilibria under majority rule, and decide whether sincere voting is one. If \(M_\omega(x)=\sum_t\mu_t^\omega x_t\), the group chooses \(1\) when \(M_\omega(x)>1/2\), with a fixed tie rule. But in a nonatomic society, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the outcome. Every strategy is therefore an equilibrium, including sincere voting. That makes the question mathematically precise but empties the paper’s strategic claim of content; in particular, it cannot reproduce the paper’s result that sincere voting may fail to be an equilibrium.

The paper’s only numbered results that might look like anchors are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in the paper. Proposition 1 says that with symmetric states, state-independent signal precision, and simple majority rule, sincere voting is an equilibrium—even when voters receive different odd numbers of independent private signals and vote according to the majority of their own signals. Proposition 2 says that with symmetric priors and payoffs, simple majority voting, and common signal precision that depends on the state, sincere voting may fail to be an equilibrium; the paper gives a three-voter example with \(q_1=0.9\) and \(q_2=0.6\). The quota \(1.13\) used in that example comes from the optimal-rule result cited as reference [17].

Neither proposition asserts a complexity classification, algorithmic result, or computational problem of the kind required for an anchor here. The paper contains no named computational result in that sense, so I cannot honestly provide a qualifying mirror problem per anchor or assign one of the programme’s complexity classes. Its nearest continuous analogue is a trivial equilibrium question, not a ChoCo computational mirror. The weakest point is the same mechanism that drives the paper: individual pivotality. Preserving it would require keeping voters finite and consequential, or adding a pivotality rescaling that changes the model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. Proposition 1 establishes an equilibrium claim for a finite committee with symmetric signals; Proposition 2 gives a finite example where sincere voting may fail. Neither poses an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. The qualified-majority quota used in the example is cited from Ben-Yashar and Nitzan [17]. These results concern incentives, and their operative object is whether one voter can be pivotal.

The natural mass model makes that object disappear. Let a type be \(t=(k,s)\), recording a voter’s number and realization of signals, and let \(\mu_t^\omega\) be its mass in state \(\omega\). If \(x_t\) is the fraction of that type voting for \(1\), the vote share is \(M_\omega(x)=\sum_t\mu_t^\omega x_t\). Under majority rule, a single voter’s deviation changes \(M_\omega\) by zero. A fixed tie rule does not change that. Consequently, every strategy profile is an equilibrium: sincerity holds vacuously in Proposition 1’s setting, and Proposition 2’s failure of sincere equilibrium has no counterpart. This is a consequence of individual voters having zero mass, not a poor choice of signal types.

Treating each positive-mass type as one player would restore consequential deviations, but it would make all members of a type a coordinated bloc. Allowing a positive mass to deviate likewise turns the question into coalition manipulation or control. Either route replaces the individual best-response claim that the propositions establish.

The best rescue is to retain finite-population pivotality while taking a large-population limit. For both propositions, the relevant object is the ratio of probabilities that the other voters split evenly across states. That ratio can remain informative even when each tie probability becomes tiny. But computing it requires the finite count and the effect of inserting one voter—the \(1/n\)-scale perturbation that the mass model discards. Conditioning on pivotal events, rescaling their payoffs, or adding Poisson turnout could produce a substantive strategic-voting question. Each adds count fluctuations or a finite-size correction as part of the model; the resulting target is a pivotality or asymptotic-computation problem, rather than a complexity question already posed by this paper.

The negative case is strongest for the direct continuous-population mirror. I cannot rule out a worthwhile computational study of typed finite committees or their pivotality asymptotics. That would be a new problem built around the paper’s mechanism, though, and its usefulness would not establish that the paper itself supplies a continuous computational mirror.
