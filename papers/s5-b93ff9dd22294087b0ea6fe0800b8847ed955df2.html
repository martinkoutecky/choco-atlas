The continuous mirror question

Given plus and minus classes \(k\) with rational population masses \(\rho_k\), fixed impacts \(a_k\), and explicitly encoded regular distributions \(H_k\) over private costs or values, treat \((k,z)\) as the realized agent type with population measure \(\rho_k\,dH_k(z)\). Choose direct allocation and payment rules \(x_k(\hat z)\) and \(t_k(\hat z)\), DSIC and IR for each realized type, to maximize per-capita utility \(\sum_k \rho_k a_k \int x_k(z)\,dH_k(z)\), subject to aggregate minus-agent payments covering plus-agent payments, \(\sum_{k\in T_-}\rho_k\int t_k(z)\,dH_k(z)\ge\sum_{k\in T_+}\rho_k\int t_k(z)\,dH_k(z)\). What is the optimal utility and can an optimal mechanism be computed from the encoded instance?

A continuum of realized participant types, each carrying population mass; direct allocation and payment rules depend on reported private cost or value, and per-capita utility and budget balance are integrals over the type measure.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is for the paper’s additive-utility result, Theorem 4. It is proved in this paper. The theorem says that, under regular distributions, Algorithm 1 gives a DSIC, IR, ex-ante budget-balanced mechanism that maximizes the designer’s utility. It chooses the smallest shadow price \(\lambda^*\ge 0\) that satisfies expected budget balance, selects plus agent \(i\) when \(p_i-\lambda^*\psi_i(c_i)\ge 0\), and selects minus agent \(j\) when \(n_j+\lambda^*\phi_j(v_j)\ge 0\). Payments are the truthful payments characterized by equations (4.1) and (4.2). This is a concrete mechanism-design result, not just an encoding.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring curation market: many content providers and advertisers serving a platform, drawn from a small number of standardized segments. For instance, providers in a segment share the same positive impact and cost distribution; advertisers in a segment share the same negative impact and value distribution. The segment is the ex-ante class used by the mechanism. Individual costs or values are still private and drawn independently, as in the paper. There are \(N\) agents and \(\tau\) such classes, with \(\tau\) fixed or small as \(N\) grows.

I would name the mirror Continuous Additive Self-Financing Curation. An instance gives finite sets \(T_+\) and \(T_-\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, impacts \(p_t\ge 0\) and \(n_s\le 0\), and regular cost and value distributions \(G_t\) and \(F_s\). Class \(t\) contains \(N\mu_t\) agents in the \(N\)-agent replication, for values of \(N\) where those counts are integral. Each agent receives an indivisible allocation; the accepted fraction of a class can nevertheless be fractional. The objective, divided by \(N\), is

\[ \sum_{t\in T_+}\mu_t p_t\,\mathbb E[x_t] \;+\; \sum_{s\in T_-}\mu_s n_s\,\mathbb E[x_s]. \]

The question is to compute the limiting optimal per-capita expected utility and a DSIC, IR mechanism attaining it, subject to expected payments from minus agents covering expected payments to plus agents. A solution specifies each class’s allocation and payment rule. For the rule in Theorem 4, with
\[ \psi_t(c)=c+\frac{G_t(c)}{g_t(c)} \quad\text{and}\quad \phi_s(v)=v-\frac{1-F_s(v)}{f_s(v)}, \]
the allocation decisions are \(x_t(c)=\mathbf 1[p_t-\lambda^*\psi_t(c)\ge0]\) and \(x_s(v)=\mathbf 1[n_s+\lambda^*\phi_s(v)\ge0]\). The shadow price uses the class masses when balancing expected payments. Normalizing both utility and budget by \(N\) leaves that balance intact, and the same class-level rule applies throughout the replication. Each person is still selected individually; the continuum describes the aggregate population.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A) for explicit, efficiently evaluable distributions: the relevant allocation rule is governed by one shadow price and the \(\tau\) class descriptions, rather than a decision variable for every agent. A computational follow-up would be to establish bit-complexity bounds for finding \(\lambda^*\) from succinctly represented distributions. The mirror covers Theorem 4’s additive, regular-distribution setting; it does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s separable-submodular result. The paper’s NP-hardness discussion in Section 5 is not a numbered result, so I do not use it as an anchor.

The weak point is that the paper studies finite Bayesian markets, not an asymptotic population. This mirror groups agents by their role, impact, and private-value distribution, and asks about the limit of replicated finite markets. It is therefore a faithful high-multiplicity regime, but the theorem’s rule already solves every finite replication too. The continuous result may turn out to be an exact limit identity rather than a new algorithmic advantage. That still makes it a legitimate mirror of the authors’ mechanism-design problem; it limits the novelty of the answer, not the plausibility of the question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed replication groups agents by their impact and prior distribution, but those are not the agents’ complete types in the problem. The mechanism uses each agent’s private cost or value: two members of the same proposed class can face different allocations and payments because they report different \(c_i\) or \(v_j\). So \(N\mu_t\) counts agents with the same prior, not agents with the same realized type. The displayed per-capita objective averages over private draws; it is not, as stated, an objective on a society whose type masses are given.

The strongest repair is to put mass on the full type: side, impact, and realized cost or value. That is a coherent continuous-population question, but it changes the input and the budget constraint. With a continuum of private types, the mechanism needs pointwise incentive constraints and aggregate budget balance over the type distribution. Theorem 4 instead treats a finite vector of agents with independent private draws and ex-ante budget balance. Theorem 7’s payment transformation does not by itself establish that the continuum model has the same feasible mechanisms or optimum. And if the cost or value domain is continuous, the paper and the programme’s finite, rational-support model do not specify how that input is encoded for a complexity analysis.

That objection limits the proponent’s particular mirror; it does not defeat the repaired one. One can use a finite set of realized cost and value types with rational masses, or explicitly encode continuous distributions. In either case, the result asks a recognizable computational question about optimal curation over a large population. Theorem 4’s shadow-price structure suggests the answer may be simple, but simplicity or an exact limit identity is not a reason to reject a well-posed mirror under the stated standard.

So the best case against is that the proposed class-level formulation conflates repeated priors with repeated complete types, and the paper does not supply the continuum model’s input encoding. I cannot honestly turn that into the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the full-type mass formulation remains plausible, and Theorem 4 gives it a natural starting point.
