The continuous mirror question

Given \(r\ge1\), compute the Lebesgue volume \(V_r\) of the parameter region \(\mathcal P_r\) consisting of \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) and \(r\) frontier points \(v_i=(a_i,b_i)\in[0,\lambda]\times[0,1-\lambda]\), pairwise incomparable under \(\preceq\) and in decreasing lexicographic order, with at least one \(i\) satisfying \(a_i>0\) and \(b_i<1-\lambda\). Here \((a,b)\preceq(c,d)\) iff \(a\le c\) and \(a+b\le c+d\), and the frontier defines \(W=\{q:\exists i,\ v_i\preceq q\}\).

The model it lives in

A family of two-type nonatomic complete simple games, with type masses \(\lambda\) and \(1-\lambda\), coalitions represented by mass vectors \(q\in[0,\lambda]\times[0,1-\lambda]\), and each rule represented by its \(r\) shift-minimal winning mass vectors. The decision object is the canonical frontier; the requested statistic is the Lebesgue volume over tier shares and frontier coordinates.

The objection that survived

The proposed volume ranges over all tier shares \(\lambda\), rather than taking one specified distribution \(\mu\) as input; this departs from the standard fixed-society formulation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 2’s fixed-\(r\), \(t=2\) enumeration through its leading volume coefficient; it leaves Theorem 4’s all-\(r\) total and the paper’s \(t\ge3\) enumeration results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a prescribed tier share \(\lambda\), what is the volume of the corresponding fixed-\(\lambda\) frontier region?
  • Can analogous continuous volumes be computed for \(t\ge3\), or when \(r\) grows with \(n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the paper’s fixed-\(r\), two-type enumeration. Its named anchor is Lemma 2, proved in this paper. Writing \(cs(n,2,r)\) for the number of complete simple games on \(n\) voters with two desirability classes and \(r\) shift-minimal winning coalitions, the lemma gives, for \(r\ge 2\),
\[ cs(n,2,r)=\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor (n-3r+3)/2\right\rfloor} \binom{i+r-2}{r-2}\binom{n-2r-2i+5}{r+2}, \]
and for \(r=1\),
\[ cs(n,2,1)=\frac{n^3-n}{6}. \]
It also gives the ordinary generating functions \(x^{3r-3}/((1-x)^{r+3}(1-x^2)^{r-1})\) for \(r\ge2\), and \(x^2/(1-x)^4\) for \(r=1\). The paper does not give a named P/NP-hardness classification; this exact counting result is the honest computational anchor.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization with two chartered voting tiers. Members within a tier have identical voting roles, while the tiers differ in desirability. The society is the distribution \(\mu=(\lambda,1-\lambda)\), where \(\lambda\) is the fraction in the first tier. A coalition is represented by its support masses \(q=(q_1,q_2)\), with \(0\le q_1\le\lambda\) and \(0\le q_2\le1-\lambda\). For mass vectors define \(u\preceq v\) when \(u_1\le v_1\) and \(u_1+u_2\le v_1+v_2\), the paper’s two-type shift order extended to real masses.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Two-Tier Complete-Rule Volume. Its input is \(r\ge1\). Output the exact Lebesgue volume \(V_r\) of the following canonical parameter region \(\mathcal P_r\). A point in the region consists of \((\lambda,a_1,b_1,\ldots,a_r,b_r)\), where \(0<\lambda<1\) and each frontier point \(v_i=(a_i,b_i)\) lies in \([0,\lambda]\times[0,1-\lambda]\). The points are pairwise incomparable under \(\preceq\), ordered by decreasing lexicographic order, and satisfy \(a_i>0,\ b_i<1-\lambda\) for at least one \(i\). They describe the rule
\[ W=\{q:\text{ for some }i,\ v_i\preceq q\}. \]
Thus the decision object is the winning region, represented by its canonical frontier; the requested solution is the volume of the parameter region in these normalized population-mass coordinates. The incomparability conditions split the region into finitely many rational polyhedral pieces, much like the paper’s own subcase construction.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime: for fixed \(r\), let \(n\) grow while the two tiers remain macroscopic. The finite game’s coordinates \(n_1/n\) and \(m_{i,j}/n\) become the continuous coordinates \(\lambda\) and \((a_i,b_i)\). The paper’s canonical game representation makes the integer points in dilations of \(\mathcal P_r\) correspond to its two-type games. Consequently the leading coefficient of \(cs(n,2,r)\) is the continuous volume. Lemma 2 yields
\[ V_r=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{cs(n,2,r)}{n^{2r+1}} =\frac{1}{2^{r-1}(2r+1)!}. \]
For \(r=1\), this follows directly from the cubic formula; for \(r\ge2\), it follows from the lemma’s sum. The expected answer is therefore tractable, with an explicit rational formula. This mirrors the paper’s fixed-frontier enumeration result, rather than claiming to cover its general multi-type formulas or Theorem 4’s sum over all frontier sizes.

The weak point is that the paper counts discrete rules, whereas this mirror measures a space of continuous rules and mass splits. Volume is a new statistic whose interpretation depends on the chosen canonical coordinates. Also, in an atomless society, the original individual-level notion of desirability needs the aggregate shift-order interpretation used here. A referee could reasonably ask whether that is the authors’ question. The case survives because both the coordinates and the ordering come directly from the paper’s population and coalition vectors, and its enumeration method already turns those vectors into lattice points in polyhedral regions. The natural next questions are whether similar volumes are tractable for \(t\ge3\), and what happens when \(r\) grows with population size.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 2 is the only anchor actually raised, and it is a real enumerative computation. The strongest objection is about what it computes: \(cs(n,2,r)\) counts isomorphism classes of rules across every split \(n_1+n_2=n\). The proposed \(V_r\) likewise ranges over \(\lambda\) and over frontiers. It therefore measures a space of possible constitutional rules and population compositions; it does not compute something for a society supplied as \(\mu\). The population appears as coordinates in the rule-space enumeration, rather than as the input to a computational social-choice task.

The charitable repair is to fix \(\lambda\) and ask for the volume of the feasible \(r\)-frontier rules for that two-tier society. That is a coherent continuous model, and the mass-based shift order is a reasonable extension of the paper’s order. But it remains a measure of an axiomatically defined design space. The discrete count gives every isomorphism class one unit; its continuous counterpart needs a measure over rule parameters. Lebesgue measure in the proposed coordinates is a defensible choice, but the paper’s result does not select it or give it an operational interpretation. Restricting to finitely many frontier points also selects a subfamily: a continuous mass game can have an entire boundary of shift-minimal coalitions. So this repair yields a new geometric enumeration question, not a direct continuation of the paper’s computational task.

Theorem 4’s count over all two-type games is a tempting stronger anchor, but its continuous counterpart faces the same problem more sharply: it ranges over varying frontier sizes. A single volume across those strata would require an additional weighting convention. The lattice-point formulation in Theorem 5 does not remove the mismatch; it encodes the same rule-enumeration problem.

A mass-robustness or mass-control problem for a fixed complete game could be a genuine computational question. But it would be a new question, not a mirror of the result the proponent anchored on.

This is a limited case against, not a convincing universal one. A large organization with two membership tiers is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the leading coefficient of Lemma 2 really does match the proposed normalized volume. That is unusually strong evidence for a continuous mathematical analogue. The negative case works only if ChoCo’s scope requires computation *on a given society*, rather than computation about the space of rules compatible with a society. If exact enumeration and rule-space geometry count as in-scope computational work, this anchor survives.
