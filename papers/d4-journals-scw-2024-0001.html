The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive point is that the paper already treats a large electorate through a distribution over voters’ evaluation vectors. For a fixed candidate set \(C\), take a voter’s type to be their complete evaluation vector \(x\in[0,1]^{|C|}\) (or \(x\in\{0,\ldots,K\}^{|C|}\)); mass is the fraction of the electorate with that vector. A large survey electorate with fixed \(C\) is a plausible regime, especially for discrete grades, where repeated types are possible. The paper’s IID, copula, Dirichlet and spatial models give concrete ways to describe such populations. That makes a continuous-population viewpoint natural, and the paper’s existing distributional modeling is evidence that the regime makes sense.

But this is only support for a possible mirror, not an anchor for one. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its numbered statements are Definitions 1–8; its main contributions are generative models and statistical fits. Those are not qualifying anchors under the requested standard.

I therefore can’t honestly give the required precise continuous problem *per anchor*, or claim that the authors’ computational question has a continuous version: the paper does not formulate a computational problem of the kind the programme studies. One could propose new questions about computing winner probabilities or fitting a population distribution, but they would be extensions of this paper’s probabilistic modeling, not mirrors anchored in its named complexity results. The strongest case is thus that a continuous population is natural in the paper’s setting; the central weakness is that there is no computational result here for continuization to explain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t find a computational anchor in the proponent’s case. The paper’s numbered statements are Definitions 1–8 of evaluation models. Its results concern fitting and simulating distributions; it gives no theorem about the complexity of a computational social choice problem. So there is no named complexity result here with a continuous version to assess.

The proposed type space—complete evaluation vectors \(x\in[0,1]^m\), or \(x\in\{0,\ldots,K\}^m\)—does make a population distribution natural. But that is a modeling observation, not an anchor. The paper treats evaluations as finite samples from a law and studies how to fit that law and generate new samples. With continuous grades, exact evaluation vectors are distinct almost surely, so representing the observed electorate by masses on exact types generally leaves support size \(n\). Discrete grades permit repeated types, but the paper does not pose a high-multiplicity computational problem over them.

The strongest proposed mirror would be to take a mass distribution \(\mu\) over evaluation vectors and ask for a winner, a property’s limiting frequency, or the minimum mass change needed to make a candidate win. Those are coherent questions, but the first two recast the paper’s distributional analysis, while the last adds an intervention objective and costs the paper never studies. None mirrors a computational result in this paper.

That is a strong negative case against using this paper as a ChoCo anchor, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any scenario. A new computational question could be built on its models; it would be a new research direction rather than a mirror of a result the authors proved. The proponent’s point that a population distribution is natural stands as evidence for such a direction, but it does not supply an anchor that survives the requested test.
