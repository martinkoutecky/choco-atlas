What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only selecting the candidate with minimum ElecMeter on ranking-type masses; it does not cover the simulation, clustering, or empirical claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no eligible named computational anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. It defines ElecMeter in Eqs. (1)–(11), runs simulations, and reports an illustrative winner, but those are not the kind of named result your anchor requirement specifies. I therefore can’t honestly provide an anchor-based case.

The strongest plausible mirror, if an unanchored question is still useful, is Continuous ElecMeter Winner. An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of strict rankings \(t\), each with binary rational mass \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), together with a fixed \(p\), say \(p=3\). For each candidate \(c\), let \(q_j(c)=\sum_{t:\,c\text{ is ranked at position }j}\mu_t\). Compute that candidate’s normalized average dissatisfaction and the divergence ratios from the paper’s ideal, maximum, neutral, and polarized benchmarks; combine them using Eq. (11). Return every candidate minimizing the resulting ElecMeter value.

The regime is a large electorate in which voters with the same complete ranking form a type, with many voters but relatively few supported rankings. This is a credible population-continuous interpretation of the paper’s setting: its index already uses normalized ranking frequencies, so replacing finite counts with masses preserves the object being measured. The natural expectation is efficient approximation, since the candidate distributions can be computed from the support and the index uses finitely many divergence calculations. But that is a proposed computational question, not a result established by the paper, and it may be too close to directly extending the paper’s existing formula to count as a substantive complexity contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no eligible computational result, and that is the strongest point against a mirror: the paper states no complexity theorem to continuize.

Their proposed Continuous ElecMeter Winner also has little left to ask. The paper already computes ElecMeter from normalized dissatisfaction frequencies and says to evaluate each candidate and select one with minimum score. Given ranking-type masses \(\mu_t\), one can form \(q_j(c)=\sum_{t:\,c\text{ is at position }j}\mu_t\) and apply the paper’s formulas directly. The electorate size \(N\) drops out. This is the paper’s existing candidate-selection procedure on distributional input, rather than a new computational question exposed by replacing voters with mass.

A stronger proposal might ask for the minimum mass of voters whose rankings must change, at some cost, to make a target candidate the ElecMeter winner. That could be a worthwhile intervention problem. The paper supplies neither that intervention objective nor its cost model, though; it would be a new question built around the index, not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s computational results.

The negative case is strongest on that narrow ground: no named computational result, and the suggested winner computation is already specified by the paper’s distribution-based formula. The universal claim is weaker. Large electorates with repeated rankings are a credible continuous-population regime, and this paper’s normalization makes such an extension natural. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous research question could grow from it.
