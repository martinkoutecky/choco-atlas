What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper’s weighted-committee equivalence classes for antiplurality, Borda, Copeland, and plurality; it does not supply a computational theorem for the enumeration tables or the empirical sensitivity discussion.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(E^r_{\alpha,m}\) be represented and membership-tested in time polynomial in \(m,n\) and the input bit-length, without enumerating \((m!)^n\) profiles?
  • For Borda, Copeland, and plurality, does allowing real population masses change computational complexity beyond rational rescaling of integer weights?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified yes: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it does not contain the kind of named complexity theorem that would make the case especially strong.

In particular, it has no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, W[1]-hardness, or FPT. Its numbered results are structural propositions, proved in the paper, while the computational core is an unnumbered ILP in Section 4.1 and the branch-and-cut procedure in Table 3. I therefore use the structural propositions as anchors, without pretending that they are complexity classifications.

The natural regime is a large population of shareholders, party members, or institutional delegates divided into a modest number \(n\) of homogeneous voting blocs. A type is a complete pair \((i,P)\), where \(i\) records the bloc or voting-rights class and \(P\) is a complete ranking of the \(m\) alternatives. The type is complete because members of the same class have the same voting rights and the same preference ranking. The population may contain millions of agents but only \(n\) occupied types in a given profile, with \(N\gg n\).

For a normalized mass vector \(\alpha\in\Delta_n\), where

\[ \Delta_n=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^n:\sum_i\alpha_i=1\right\}, \]

and a bloc-ranking profile \(P=(P_1,\ldots,P_n)\), define the society

\[ \mu^{\alpha,P}_{(i,Q)} = \begin{cases} \alpha_i & \text{if }Q=P_i,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Thus \(\alpha_i\) is the fraction of voting mass held by bloc \(i\). For a scoring rule \(r\),

\[ \operatorname{score}_{\alpha}(a;P) = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i s(P_i,a), \]

with the paper’s lexicographic tie-breaking rule. If \(\alpha\) is rational, clearing denominators gives exactly the paper’s integer model: \(\alpha_i=w_i/\sum_jw_j\). This is therefore a genuine high-multiplicity relaxation, not merely an analogy.

The lead anchor is Proposition 4, proved in Appendix A. It states:

For Borda rule \(r_B\) and \(m\ge 3\), every vector
\[ > \tilde w_j=(j,1,0,\ldots,0), > \qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}, > \]
identifies a different equivalence class \(E^{r_B}_{\tilde w_j,m}\). Consequently, for fixed \(n\ge3\), the number of structurally distinct Borda committee games grows without bound as \(m\) increases.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Borda Weight-Robustness.

An instance consists of \(m\) alternatives, \(n\) bloc types, a rational baseline mass vector \(\alpha\in\Delta_n\), and a rational movement budget \(\delta\). The action is to choose a new mass vector \(\beta\in\Delta_n\), with movement cost

\[ d(\alpha,\beta)=\frac12\|\alpha-\beta\|_1. \]

The question is whether there exists \(\beta\) with \(d(\alpha,\beta)\le\delta\) and a preference profile \(P\in\mathcal{R}(A)^n\) such that

\[ r_B(\alpha,P)\neq r_B(\beta,P). \]

A yes-solution consists of \(\beta\) and a separating profile \(P\); the optimization version minimizes \(d(\alpha,\beta)\). If one wants the paper’s structural equivalence rather than labelled equivalence, allow bloc and alternative relabellings exactly as in Definition (3), and ask for the minimum movement to leave the corresponding equivalence class.

This is a direct continuous version of the paper’s question about how small changes in voting weights can change collective choices. Proposition 4 shows that the Borda parameter space really contains distinct decision regions: the normalized vectors

\[ \alpha^{(j)}=\frac{1}{j+1}(j,1,0,\ldots,0) \]

lie in pairwise different Borda cells. The mirror therefore does not collapse the problem to “compute one winner”; it asks about the geometry and computational accessibility of the entire winner map.

My expectation is Class A in an explicit-type representation: for a fixed profile, the winning conditions are linear inequalities, and the boundary-distance problem is an LP. With rankings represented implicitly by \(m\), however, finding a separating profile becomes a separation problem over permutations. That is precisely the kind of pricing problem the ChoCo programme is designed to expose. I would regard a polynomial separation algorithm as a plausible positive result, but I would not claim that Proposition 4 proves one.

A second useful anchor is Proposition 5, also proved in Appendix A. It states:

The Copeland partitions
\[ > \{E^{r_C}_{\bar w_1,m},\ldots,E^{r_C}_{\bar w_\xi,m}\} > \]
of \(\mathbb{N}_0^n\) coincide for every \(m\ge2\).

In other words, two weight vectors induce structurally equivalent Copeland committee games with \(m\) alternatives exactly when they are equivalent in the binary case. The number of alternatives does not create new equivalence classes.

The associated problem is Continuous Copeland Weight-Robustness. Its instance is \((n,m,\alpha,\delta)\), with the same mass-transfer action. It asks whether one can move at most \(\delta\) units of voting mass to a vector \(\beta\) for which some profile changes the Copeland winner. A solution is \((\beta,P)\), or, for structural equivalence, \((\beta,P,\pi,\sigma)\) witnessing the changed labelled or relabelled game.

For rational masses, Proposition 5 transfers directly after clearing denominators. Its proof explains why this problem should be easier: Copeland depends only on weighted pairwise majority comparisons,

\[ \sum_{i:aP_i b}\alpha_i \quad\text{versus}\quad \sum_{i:bP_i a}\alpha_i. \]

Thus the continuous question reduces to the geometry of binary threshold comparisons. I would expect this version to be Class A, or at least to admit a compact separation procedure whose complexity is independent of \(m\). Any remaining hardness would come from the number of bloc types and threshold structure, not from the continuum itself.

A third, somewhat weaker but still useful anchor is Proposition 6, proved in Appendix A:

The plurality partitions
\[ > \{E^{r_P}_{\bar w_1,m},\ldots,E^{r_P}_{\bar w_\xi,m}\} > \]
coincide for all \(m\ge n\).

Hence additional alternatives beyond \(n\) do not create new plurality equivalence classes. The continuous problem is Continuous Plurality Weight-Robustness, defined as above with \(r_P\). Proposition 6 says that, once \(m\ge n\), a separating profile can be sought using only \(n\) relevant top-ranked alternatives; unused alternatives can be added or removed without changing the induced equivalence class. I would expect this to be Class A through top-choice aggregation and linear mass constraints.

The paper’s Section 4.1 supplies an important computational bridge, although it is not a numbered anchor. Given a target choice rule \(\rho\), the paper uses an integer linear program whose variables are the weights \(w_i\), whose constraints require the prescribed winner at every preference profile, and whose objective minimizes \(\sum_iw_i\). The constraint set is nonempty exactly when \(\rho\) has a representation by the chosen scoring rule. The continuous mirror replaces integer weights by rational masses and can add a mass-transfer objective such as \(d(\alpha,\beta)\). This is not outcome-space continuity: the outcome remains one discrete candidate, while the electorate becomes continuous.

The main weakness is that the formal mirror is a family of societies \(\{\mu^{\alpha,P}\}_P\), because the paper itself studies a rule defined on every possible preference profile, rather than one fixed electorate. An opponent could argue that this continuizes voting weights or bloc sizes rather than the population. I think that objection is answerable: the paper defines \(r|_w\) by repeating each player’s preference \(w_i\) times, so replacing those repeated copies by a continuum of indistinguishable voters is exactly the high-multiplicity version of its own construction. The bloc label and ranking together form the complete voter type.

I would therefore submit this as a genuine, referee-recognizable continuous mirror of the paper’s weighted-committee equivalence and robustness problem, led by Proposition 4. But I would not present it as evidence that the paper already contains a Class A/B/C complexity result. It supplies a strong model and several structural anchors; the complexity landscape of their continuous versions remains the new ChoCo question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection comes before the modelling details: this paper contains no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Propositions 4–6 are structural classification statements, not complexity theorems. The ILP and branch-and-cut procedure are computational techniques for exhaustive enumeration, but the paper proves no membership result, hardness result, approximation theorem, or parameterized classification. The proposed continuous problems are therefore new robustness problems invented around the paper, not continuous versions of its computational results.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity story: many shareholders, party members, or delegates are partitioned into homogeneous blocs. So the objection should not be that multiplicity is impossible. The deeper problem is that, for this paper, continuization adds no distinct mathematical object. If \(w\) is replaced by normalized mass \(\alpha_i=w_i/\sum_jw_j\), every rational mass vector is already represented by the paper’s integer weights. More strongly, for fixed \(n\) and \(m\), every winner map is determined by finitely many rational linear comparisons of the form

\[ \sum_i w_i\bigl(s(P_i,a)-s(P_i,b)\bigr)\ge 0. \]

These comparisons partition weight space into finitely many rational polyhedral cells. Every nonempty real cell contains a rational point, which can be scaled to an integer weight vector. Thus allowing arbitrary real masses creates no new committee games or equivalence classes. It merely normalizes the parameter space the paper already studies.

A genuine distribution over voter types would instead be a measure \(\mu\) over rankings. But then the paper’s central object disappears. The paper compares two weight vectors as complete decision rules over every profile \(P\in\mathcal R(A)^n\). A fixed \(\mu\) gives only one election and one winner. Asking how far that \(\mu\) must move before the winner changes is a legitimate generic election-robustness problem, but it is not a mirror of the paper’s equivalence classes. Alternatively, one can retain the labelled blocs and require each bloc’s mass to have one common ranking; that recovers precisely the finite-dimensional normalized weight geometry above.

Proposition 4 does not rescue the case. It says that the Borda vectors \(\tilde w_j=(j,1,0,\ldots,0)\) induce different global mappings from all preference profiles to winners. The proposed “Continuous Borda Weight-Robustness” instead asks for the smallest movement from one mass vector to another together with some separating profile \(P\). That is an additional distance objective, absent from the proposition. If one uses ordinary continuous voter distributions, the \(\tilde w_j\) classes no longer exist as such; one has simply defined robustness of a Borda election. If one preserves the classes, the problem is distance to a cell in the same rational hyperplane arrangement already encoded by integer weights. The continuum has not exposed a new population-computational phenomenon.

Proposition 5 is still weaker as an anchor. Its content is that Copeland weight-equivalence is exactly binary weighted-majority equivalence. A mass formulation gives comparisons such as

\[ \sum_{i:aP_i b}\alpha_i \;\ge\; \sum_{i:bP_i a}\alpha_i, \]

but this is just the normalized form of the paper’s existing threshold geometry. If the profile is allowed to vary, the result itself reduces the question to a binary weighted game. If the society is fixed, one obtains ordinary Copeland winner robustness under changes to pairwise mass margins. Either way, the proposed mirror is either the paper’s finite weight space in different coordinates or a new generic robustness problem. Proposition 5 supplies no computational theorem whose continuous counterpart needs investigation.

Proposition 6 collapses even more sharply. Plurality depends only on top-ranked alternatives. Under a genuine continuous society, the entire ranking distribution reduces to the top-choice masses

\[ q_a=\sum_{t:\,a\text{ is top in }t}\mu_t. \]

The lower parts of rankings are irrelevant. The resulting problem is continuous plurality robustness over a simplex of top-choice masses, not the paper’s classification of weight vectors as complete rules over all profiles. Retaining the paper’s bloc structure again returns to the same finite arrangement of inequalities over normalized weights. The proposition’s statement that extra alternatives become irrelevant therefore removes, rather than creates, a population-level object to continuize.

Section 4.1 also does not provide the missing bridge. Replacing \(w\) by \(\alpha\) makes the constraints look like an LP only because scale and integrality are inessential: the original strict and weak comparisons are homogeneous, and rational solutions can be rescaled to integer ones. If the paper’s minimum-sum objective is retained, normalization makes it identically \(1\). If a movement objective is added to avoid that triviality, the result is a newly designed problem rather than a continuous version of the paper’s ILP.

So the best negative case is that every proposed anchor falls into the same trilemma: preserve the paper’s all-profiles weighted-game object and obtain a reparameterization of its already rational weight geometry; use a genuine distribution over voter types and lose the paper’s object; or add a new robustness objective and study a different problem. The absence of any named computational result makes this decisive under the programme’s gate.

The universal claim is not airtight: homogeneous shareholder or party blocs are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and a new paper on continuous winner robustness could be worthwhile. But that would be a ChoCo problem motivated by weighted voting, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of the computational results of this paper.
