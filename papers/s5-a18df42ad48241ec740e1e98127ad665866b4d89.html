The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Its OLFM theorem on satisfaction’s properties proves that \(\mathrm{SAT}\) satisfies properties 1, 2, 3, 4b, 5b, and 6b; its uniqueness theorem proves that \(\mathrm{SAT}\) is the unique measure satisfying the stated axioms. Both are proved here, but they are results about properties and axiomatization—not about an algorithm, complexity class, or computational problem. The OLF axiomatization results are cited from earlier work. The definition of \(\mathrm{SAT}\) counts the \(2^n\) initial decision vectors, and the paper gives an example calculation, but neither is a named computational result. So there is no numbered result I can responsibly use as an anchor or continuous problem to hand to a prover.

A plausible population regime would be many repeated, similar influence structures—for example, large organizations with a fixed set of roles and layered communication patterns. One could assign mass to those roles and define collective choice by the mass of final “yes” decisions. But to turn the paper’s satisfaction score into a continuous question, one would also need to specify how individual influence links and unanimity behave at population scale, and how the score for a representative actor is measured when that actor has zero mass. That is a substantive new model, not a continuous version of a result this paper establishes.

The foothold is that the model has a natural layered structure that might support such an extension. Its weakness is decisive for this task: the paper’s results are axiomatic, while ChoCo’s target is computational. A continuous-population study inspired by this paper may be worth pursuing, but I would not present it as a mirror of the paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case raises no positive computational anchor; it argues that none exists. Its strongest candidates are the OLFM theorem that satisfaction obeys the stated properties and the theorem that those properties uniquely characterize satisfaction. Both are axiomatic results. The OLF axiomatization is cited from earlier work. The formula defining \(\mathrm{SAT}(i)\) by counting \(2^n\) decision vectors suggests a counting problem, but the paper proves no complexity or algorithmic result about that problem. Computing \(\mathrm{SAT}\) on OLFM graphs could be a worthwhile new question; it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The proposed large-organization scenario does not resolve the more basic modeling issue. Satisfaction is defined for a named actor by comparing the collective outcome with that actor’s bit across all initial decision vectors. A continuous population supplies masses of types, but it supplies no canonical uniform distribution over continuum-wide binary decision profiles. Under independent fair initial choices, a nonatomic type has a yes fraction of one half almost surely, while any one actor’s bit has zero effect on majority. The resulting type-level satisfaction loses the individual pivotality that the paper measures. Giving each type one shared bit restores variation, but makes its members a coordinated bloc and changes the profile space. Other choices of microstate distribution are possible; the paper gives no basis for selecting among them.

A mass version of unanimity also has to represent an unusually sensitive feature of the model: a single dissenting predecessor can prevent a follower from changing. A measure over roles can erase that predecessor if it has zero mass; preserving it requires distinguished agents or atoms. A hybrid with a few named leaders and a continuum of followers may be sensible, and could produce interesting results about the leaders’ influence. Its focal quantity would then be a new hybrid power index, while averaging satisfaction over follower mass would again change the object the paper axiomatizes.

So the best-looking continuous extension is to define a type-level or hybrid satisfaction statistic and study its computation. That may be a good independent project. The case against a continuous *mirror* is that it requires supplying both a new profile-generating model and a new score, while the paper’s named results concern axioms for the finite, actor-indexed score. The negative case is strongest on this scope mismatch; it cannot establish that every adapted continuum model would be uninteresting.
