What the mirror covers

The proposed limit question would cover approximating type-level extended Shapley payoffs under Macho–Stadler marginality; it leaves the finite-player uniqueness theorem, the Shapley–Young axiomatic equivalence, and the comparison of other axiomatizations aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered result in the paper asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial-time solvability or hardness. The only arguable anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper, and I would use it only under the formulation exception: Section 4 computes from its formula. Theorem 1 says that, for every well-defined marginality \(\alpha\), there is a unique value satisfying Efficiency, Symmetry, Additivity, and Null-Player Axiom\({}^{\alpha}\), given by the expected marginal-contribution formula
\(\varphi_i^\alpha(v)=\frac{1}{|N|!}\sum_{\pi\in\Omega(N)}\sum_{P\in\mathcal P}pr^\alpha_\pi(P)[v(C_i^\pi\cup\{i\},P_{[C_i^\pi\cup\{i\}]})-v(C_i^\pi,P_{[C_i^\pi]})]\), where \(pr^\alpha_\pi(P)=\prod_{j\in N}\alpha_j(C_j^\pi,P_{[C_j^\pi]})\). Corollary 1 is an axiomatic equivalence, not a computational anchor. The Monte Carlo algorithm and its error discussion are in the paper, but are not stated as a numbered complexity result.

A plausible continuous mirror of the computation is Mass-Continuum Extended-Shapley Approximation. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a succinctly represented, efficiently evaluable partition-game kernel \(V\). Its input describes the value of an embedded coalition from the vector of type masses in that coalition and the multiset of type-mass vectors in the other coalitions. For each population scale \(q\) with \(q\mu_t\) integral, form a society with \(q\mu_t\) exchangeable players of type \(t\), and set \(v_q(S,P)=qV(\mathbf{x}(S)/q,\{\mathbf{x}(B)/q:B\in P\})\), where \(\mathbf{x}(S)\) counts each type in \(S\). Fix the paper’s Macho-Stadler marginality \(\alpha^{MSt}\). For a player \(i\) of type \(t\), let \(\Psi_t\) be the limit of that player’s extended Shapley payoff as \(q\) grows. Given \(\epsilon\) and \(\beta\), the task is to output estimates \(\widehat{\Psi}_t\) for every type, with \(|\widehat{\Psi}_t-\Psi_t|\le\epsilon\) for all \(t\) with probability at least \(1-\beta\).

The regime could be a large market of interchangeable suppliers or producers, grouped by a small number of complete types such as technology and contractual role. The mass of each type is the share of the population in that role; coalitions are alliances, and \(V\) lets an alliance’s payoff depend on the other alliances. This preserves the paper’s underlying task—allocating payoffs by expected marginal contribution when coalition values have externalities—and its sampled-order and sampled-partition computation. It continuizes the population, rather than the payoff space.

I would expect Class A for this approximation problem under explicit regularity promises: the limit exists with a polynomial convergence modulus, marginal contributions are uniformly bounded, and the limiting partition process can be sampled efficiently. Then finite-population sampling plus concentration bounds suggests randomized additive approximation in time polynomial in the type count \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), kernel size, \(1/\epsilon\), and \(\log(1/\beta)\). The paper motivates this route, but does not establish those continuous-limit conditions. Follow-on questions include which of the paper’s other marginalities admit such limits, whether the limits can be computed without finite replicas, and whether any version is hard.

The soft spot is substantial: the limit problem adds a mass-valued game and a convergence promise that the paper never studies. Its marginalities can depend on singleton coalitions or the number of blocks, notions that do not automatically carry over to a continuum. A referee might accept the large-population payoff question as theirs, yet reject these extra assumptions as defining a new problem. If the formulation exception is read narrowly to require an encoding such as an LP or SAT instance, then the paper has no admissible named computational anchor; its named results are axiomatic, and its approximation algorithm is unnumbered.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no numbered complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic uniqueness theorem with a formula, and the Monte Carlo procedure appears in Section 4 rather than as a complexity theorem. But the formula is exactly what that procedure computes, so the formulation exception makes Theorem 1 a defensible anchor. I would not treat the missing complexity classification as decisive.

The proposed mirror also survives the strongest modeling objections. Grouping interchangeable players into complete types is precisely high multiplicity, and scaling the game as \(v_q=qV(\cdots)\) is a natural way to keep per-player contributions from vanishing. For a smooth kernel, a one-player change has the form
\[ q\bigl[V(z+e_t/q)-V(z)\bigr], \]
which tends to a directional derivative. Under Macho–Stadler’s marginality, the random partition has a natural large-population limit. That gives a coherent question about expected type-level marginal payoffs. It is a better mirror than a literal atomless version of the finite-player axioms, and I cannot honestly argue that authors would reject it as their problem.

The case against is narrower: this mirror adds a mass-valued kernel, extensive scaling, and limit regularity that the paper does not study. It asks for a payoff functional defined by a limiting sequence, whereas the paper approximates payoffs in a specified finite game. That makes it a substantial extension, but the programme explicitly allows co-modifications needed to make population mass meaningful, so the difference alone does not defeat the mirror.

I can make the negative case strongest by saying that the paper supplies little evidence that its approximation result leads to a distinct continuous complexity landscape: the proposed problem needs its own input model and convergence analysis. But that is a reason to be cautious about the mirror’s eventual payoff, not a fundamental obstruction to asking it. The honest verdict is that the negative case is weak; I cannot defeat this anchor across plausible high-multiplicity scenarios.
