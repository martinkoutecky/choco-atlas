What the mirror covers

The proposed median-type mirror covers the Representative Voter outcome and the median choice rule’s incentive properties; it leaves the paper’s mechanism implementation and the single-crossing/order-restriction equivalence without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that on an order-restricted profile the median-type agent’s top alternative is the outcome on every agenda. Proposition 1, proved here, says the median choice rule is strategy-proof on single-crossing profiles; Proposition 2 says it is group-strategy-proof. These are outcome and incentive properties, not results classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise computationally tractable.

A continuous-population analogue is easy to imagine: represent voters by a distribution over ordered preference types and select the top alternative of a median type. But the paper neither studies that population model nor supplies a named computational result to anchor a continuous computational question. Presenting the incentive propositions as complexity results would overstate the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest candidate mirrors are the Representative Voter Theorem and Propositions 1 and 2. The case against them is that each is a statement about outcomes or incentives, not a computational problem: none gives an algorithmic input, an optimization objective, or a complexity question. For the natural population model, a distribution over the paper’s ordered preference types, the median-type outcome is found by accumulating mass to the median and reading off that type’s top alternative on the agenda. With support size \(\tau\), this is a direct scan of the support and the alternatives. It puts no hard computational object from the paper within reach of new machinery.

The best strategic mirror does not improve the case much. For an atomless society, a single voter’s report has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness is automatic. Positive-mass coalitions are more meaningful, but their effect on the outcome is still governed by a weighted median and a single crossing point. To get an optimization problem, one would need to add intervention costs and ask how cheaply to move enough mass to make a target policy win. That is a plausible extension, but it supplies an objective and cost model absent from the paper. Under the single-crossing restriction, the target’s top types form an interval, so the resulting mass-transfer problem has a short threshold description and a polynomial-size linear-programming formulation. It is hard to see this as a new complexity landscape extracted from the paper’s results.

This makes the paper a poor direct source for a continuous computational mirror. It does not make the universal negative airtight: a costed mass-manipulation problem could be close enough to the authors’ interest in strategic voting for them to recognize it as their problem. The honest case against is therefore a low-priority verdict, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists.
