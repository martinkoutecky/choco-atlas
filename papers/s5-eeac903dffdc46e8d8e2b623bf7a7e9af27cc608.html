What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns mixed-Nash implementation but covers no computational result; it leaves the paper’s other implementation characterizations and comparisons untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It says that an SCC \(F\) is mixed-Nash-A-implementable iff it is mixed-Nash-B-implementable iff it satisfies \(\widehat L^{A-B}\)-uniform monotonicity: for every pair of states \(\theta,\theta'\), if each agent’s modified lower-contour set at \(\mathrm{UNIF}[F(\theta)]\) in state \(\theta\) is contained in that agent’s lower-contour set at the same lottery in state \(\theta'\), then \(F(\theta)\subseteq F(\theta')\). Theorem 2 gives the simpler single-valued counterpart; Theorems 3 and 12 give related equivalence and implication results.

A possible population analogue would take types to be complete cardinal utility descriptions across states, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of society of type \(t\). A target rule \(F\) would map type distributions to sets of pure outcomes. The question would be whether a stochastic mechanism can make the union of mixed-Nash equilibrium outcome supports equal \(F(\mu)\) for every distribution in the specified domain. A plausible regime is a large organization whose members fall into a modest number of recurring preference types.

That is a candidate extension, not a qualifying mirror. Theorem 7 characterizes implementability; the paper gives no named result classifying an encoded computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. It also keeps the number of agents finite and makes lotteries the continuous object. With an atomless population, an individual deviation cannot change an outcome determined only by aggregate reports; preserving strategic force would require positive-mass blocs or a different deviation concept. Either choice risks changing the authors’ problem.

So there is no honest continuous problem to state *per qualifying anchor*, and no tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness expectation supported by this paper. The weak point is fundamental: the strongest theorem is a mathematical characterization, not a computational result, and the proposed population version would need extra modeling choices to preserve Nash incentives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is the strongest anchor, but it fails ChoCo’s first gate: it is a characterization of implementability, not a computational result. It gives no encoded decision or optimization problem, algorithm, running time, hardness result, or parameterization. The model even permits countably many states and cardinal utilities over \(\mathbb R\), with no input representation or length measure. Theorem 2 has the same issue; Theorems 3 and 12 give equivalences or implications, not complexity results. Their substance may be important, but there is no computational landscape here for a continuous-population mirror to chart.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s large-organization example: represent members by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference types, and ask which social choice correspondences \(F(\mu)\) are implementable. That is a possible new model, but it runs into the strategic unit at the centre of this paper. In an anonymous continuum whose outcomes depend on aggregate reports, one person’s unilateral report changes no mass and hence no outcome. Exact Nash incentives then stop disciplining the aggregate report; implementation reduces to restrictions on the mechanism’s range. If instead each positive-mass type acts as one player, deviations are by whole blocs. That changes the paper’s individual-deviation game. And if a bloc’s payoff is just its per-capita utility multiplied by its mass, the positive multiplier does not change its best responses. Mass matters strategically only after adding new mass-dependent effects or collective-action assumptions.

Keeping finite individuals who can each affect the outcome avoids that degeneracy, but then their reports or identities must enter the mechanism in a way that a distribution over types alone does not capture. The paper’s state is a profile of utilities for \(I\) agents, and its conditions reason about each agent’s unilateral deviations. Replacing that profile by \(\mu\) therefore needs a new account of how individual strategic force survives aggregation. The lotteries in the paper are continuous in the outcome space; that is not population continuity, and it supplies no such account.

One could define a computational problem around a chosen representation—for example, testing implementability for correspondences over rational type distributions. But that would require specifying how the rule \(F\) is encoded and what strategic unit and equilibrium notion the model uses. It would be a new computational project built around implementation, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. This is a strong case against counting any of the proponent’s anchors for ChoCo. It is not a proof that the authors could never find a worthwhile related project: a carefully motivated bloc or approximate-implementation model might be valuable, but its value would come from that additional model and question.
