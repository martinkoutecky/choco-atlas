What the mirror covers

The proposed task covers conditioning on a partial ranking; it leaves the paper’s EM structure learning, model estimation, and experiments outside the task.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 19 (proved in this paper): *every partial ranking is completely decomposable*. In full, for any partial ranking \(S_{\gamma\pi}=\Omega_1|\cdots|\Omega_r\), its indicator factors according to every binary hierarchy on the items. At each split into \(A\) and \(B\), the factors are the indicators for interleavings and relative rankings consistent with the observation. The theorem is structural; the paper’s Algorithm 1 uses it to condition a hierarchical riffle-independent distribution exactly in \(O(m|H|)\) time, where \(m\) is the number of candidates and \(|H|\) is the number of factor parameters. For thin hierarchies, \(|H|\) is polynomial in \(m\). There is no numbered theorem that literally states “this problem is in P”; the runtime comes from the algorithm built on Theorem 19.

A concrete mirror is Partial-Ballot Subpopulation Conditioning. Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\) candidates, and let a voter type be a complete ranking \(\sigma\in S_C\). A continuous society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types. Supply \(\mu\) as the paper does: rational factor tables for a binary hierarchy \(H\), rather than an expanded table over all rankings. Given a partial ranking \(O\) with \(\mu(O)>0\), compute the distribution of complete rankings within the subpopulation whose reported ballots are consistent with \(O\):
\[ \mu^O(\sigma)= \begin{cases} \mu(\sigma)/\mu(O), & \sigma\in O,\\ 0, & \sigma\notin O. \end{cases} \]
The solution is the exact factor-table representation of \(\mu^O\), together with the subgroup’s mass \(\mu(O)\). Here the “action” is filtering and renormalizing mass; there is no optimization objective. For a thin hierarchy, the exact computation is polynomial in \(m\), the factor representation size, and the rational input bit length \(L\), independent of the electorate size \(N\). If the support is expanded as a list of \(\tau\) rankings, it can instead be filtered directly in \(O(m\tau)\) time, but that loses the paper’s compact-model advantage.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large professional association or recurring election with a fixed five-candidate slate, where thousands or millions of voters share at most \(5!=120\) complete-ranking types and some submit only partial ballots. A ranking is a complete type for the inference task; mass is a fraction of voters, not a count to be rounded to one person. The paper’s APA and Irish election examples make the setting recognizable. Its assumption that partial reports arise through a censoring process independent of the underlying ranking is also a meaningful restriction on the mirror, not something to hide.

The paper’s Theorem 21 helps mark the boundary but does not supply a second complexity anchor: it proves that every completely decomposable subset observation is a partial ranking. Thus, partial rankings are exactly the report events whose indicators support factor-by-factor conditioning across *all* hierarchies. This does not prove that conditioning on other observations is hard, or rule out other algorithms; the paper leaves that complexity open. Further questions include whether non-partial report events admit efficient exact conditioning for a particular hierarchy, and how to handle noisy or preference-dependent reporting at the population level.

My weakest point is also the central prior-art risk: the paper already models distributions over rankings and performs inference on them. Read as voter-type frequencies, its distribution \(h\) is already a continuous society, and conditioning on a partial ranking is already the operation this mirror asks for. The positive case is therefore that the authors would recognize a natural population interpretation and that the paper’s exact inference result carries over directly—not that this is a new continuous relaxation or a new computational result. The mirror covers partial-report population inference, not winner robustness, campaigning, or the paper’s entire learning agenda.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 19 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and it has a direct collision problem. Set the type space to the rankings \(T=S_C\) and set the population mass \(\mu_t\) equal to the model probability \(h(t)\). For a partial ranking \(O\), the mass on compatible types is \(\mu(O)=\sum_{t\in O}\mu_t\), and the normalized mass distribution is exactly the posterior \(h(\,\cdot\mid O)\). Algorithm 1 computes that factorized posterior in \(O(m|H|)\) time; its local normalization constants also give \(\mu(O)\). The paper applies this machinery to election data, so reading its ranking distribution as electorate shares is a natural interpretation. This is not merely an answer found elsewhere: under that interpretation, the paper already performs the proposed continuous-population computation.

There is also a representation dilemma. If the society is supplied as the programme specifies, as a list of \(\tau\) ranking types and their masses, conditioning on \(O\) is a direct scan in \(O(m\tau)\) time. If the mirror instead uses hierarchical factor tables to retain the paper’s compact-model advantage, it uses the very representation and conditioning operation the paper already studies. Neither version gives Theorem 19 a distinct continuous computational question.

A stronger variant could model a reporting channel and ask for the mass distribution after noisy or preference-dependent reports. Independent censoring that reports only rankings consistent with the voter’s type reduces to the same conditioning operation. More general likelihoods define a different problem; Section 5.4 already treats a simple noisy likelihood as a mixture, while repeated noisy observations remain open. Theorem 21 does not establish hardness for those cases: it characterizes observations permitting complete decomposability, not the complexity of general conditioning.

So the negative case is strong against the anchor actually raised: its proposed mirror is already present in the paper under a population interpretation. The broader claim that no adjacent population question could be worthwhile is weaker; noisy or preference-dependent reporting is the clearest nearby extension, but it is not a new mirror of Theorem 19.
