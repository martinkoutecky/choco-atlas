Why no mirror

Theorem 1.5 is a quantitative impossibility inequality over arbitrary SCFs, not a named computational result under the programme's criterion. The proposed \(\Phi\)-over-\(\mu\) sensitivity problem is coherent, but adds anonymity, a society distribution, a rule representation, and an infinitesimal mass-flux objective. Since no qualifying result of this paper is being continuized, the mirror fails bit (a).

fails bit a — no named computational result to mirror

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the quantitative manipulation bound in Theorem 1.5 and uses Lemma 3.2 as analytic motivation; it leaves the arbitrary labelled-profile domain, impartial-culture law, and quantitative Arrow reductions outside the computational formulation.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which finite representation of \(\Phi\) and \(\mathcal D\) makes the sensitivity problem a genuine complexity problem?
  • Does the finite-\(N\) manipulation experiment converge to the proposed \(\liminf_{\delta\downarrow0}\) quantity under a specified high-multiplicity distribution?
  • Under neutrality and continuous distance hypotheses, does a lower bound analogous to \(C\epsilon^6\) or \(C'\epsilon^2\) hold?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper supports a plausible continuous-population mirror, but it does not contain a qualifying computational anchor.

None of its numbered Theorems, Lemmas, Propositions, or Corollaries proves NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), fixed-parameter tractability, or any comparable complexity statement. The paper cites external NP-hard manipulation results in its related-work discussion, but those are not results proved here. Indeed, it explicitly observes that with a constant number of alternatives, single-voter manipulation cannot itself be computationally hard. Thus there is no valid ChoCo complexity anchor in this paper.

The closest internal anchor is Theorem 1.5, proved in the paper. It says that there are universal constants \(C,C'>0\) such that, for every \(n\) and every \(\epsilon>0\), if an SCF \(F\) on \(n\) voters and three alternatives is at least \(\epsilon\)-far from a dictatorship and from having only two alternatives in its range, then \(\sum_i M_i(F)\ge C\epsilon^6\). If \(F\) is neutral, the stronger bound \(\sum_i M_i(F)\ge C'\epsilon^2\) holds. Here \(M_i(F)\) is the probability, under the impartial-culture distribution and a uniformly random replacement ballot for voter \(i\), that the replacement is profitable.

A defensible continuous analogue would use the six complete rankings of three alternatives as the type set \(T=L_3\). A society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\), and an anonymous homogeneous rule is a map \(\Phi:\Delta(T)\to\{a,b,c\}\). The high-multiplicity regime is a large electorate with \(N\gg 6\): millions of voters, but only six relevant preference types. The mass-transfer action corresponding to one voter changing their ballot is

\[ \mu^{t\to t'}_\delta=\mu+\delta(e_{t'}-e_t), \]

where \(t\) is the true type, \(t'\) is the reported type, and \(\delta\) is the transferred population mass. For finite \(N\), \(\delta=1/N\) gives exactly one voter.

To preserve the paper’s average-case structure, let \(\mu\) be drawn from a specified distribution \(\mathcal D\) over societies, then draw \(t\sim\mu\) and \(t'\) uniformly from \(T\). Define

\[ \operatorname{MP}_\delta(\Phi,\mathcal D) = \Pr\!\left[ \Phi(\mu^{t\to t'}_\delta)\succ_t\Phi(\mu) \right]. \]

The continuous problem is:

Continuous Manipulation-Susceptibility for Three Alternatives. Given a represented rule \(\Phi\), a society distribution \(\mathcal D\), and a threshold \(q\), compute or decide whether

\[ \mathsf S(\Phi,\mathcal D) = \liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{\operatorname{MP}_\delta(\Phi,\mathcal D)}{\delta} \]

is at least \(q\).

The scaling by \(1/\delta\) is important: for a finite \(N\), \(\operatorname{MP}_{1/N}\) is the average manipulation power of a random voter, so \(N\operatorname{MP}_{1/N}\) corresponds to the paper’s \(\sum_i M_i(F)\).

The continuous versions of the theorem’s hypotheses would be that \(\Phi\) is far from an individual-dictatorship benchmark,

\[ \Pr_{\mu\sim\mathcal D,\ t\sim\mu} [\Phi(\mu)\ne \operatorname{top}(t)]\ge\epsilon, \]

and that every alternative wins with probability at least \(\epsilon\). The conjectured mirror would then ask whether \(\mathsf S\ge C\epsilon^6\), or \(C'\epsilon^2\) under neutrality.

This is recognizable as the same question in a high-multiplicity regime: the types, preferences, random replacement report, winner, and profitable deviation are unchanged; only the electorate is represented by its type-mass vector. For an explicitly represented piecewise-polyhedral rule in the fixed five-dimensional simplex, I would expect finite-\(\delta\) susceptibility, and probably \(\mathsf S\), to be tractable by constant-dimensional polyhedral volume and boundary calculations. That would be a plausible Class A continuous-optimization problem.

The serious weakness is that a single voter has zero mass in the literal continuum. The derivative-like quantity \(\mathsf S\), or a positive-mass bloc deviation, is therefore necessary but is not literally the paper’s original manipulation experiment. A second weakness is that impartial culture collapses to the uniform population vector as \(N\to\infty\); a nontrivial \(\mathcal D\) over societies is an additional modelling choice. Finally, “dictatorship” is not native to an anonymous population rule, so the individual-dictator distance above is an inferred high-multiplicity limit rather than an exact object in the paper.

Those caveats make this a credible continuous-population reformulation of Theorem 1.5, but not a genuine ChoCo computational mirror anchored by the paper. The paper’s strongest contribution is analytic: Lemma 3.2 converts aggregate dependence on irrelevant alternatives into single-voter manipulation power, and Theorem 1.5 gives the quantitative impossibility bound. A computational continuization programme would need to turn this susceptibility problem into a new algorithmic result; the paper itself does not do that.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative verdict is that the paper has no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 1.5 is a quantitative probability inequality about an arbitrary function \(F:(L_3)^n\to\{a,b,c\}\). It specifies no computational input, representation, search problem, optimization problem, algorithm, or complexity bound. The NP-hardness results appear only in related work, and the paper explicitly notes that single-voter manipulation with constantly many alternatives cannot itself be computationally hard. Thus the proposed “continuous manipulation-susceptibility” problem is a new research problem inspired by the paper, not a continuization of one of its computational results.

The proposed mirror also fails to preserve the paper’s central objects. The paper’s profiles are labelled points in \(L_3^n\), and \(F\) may depend on voter identities. Replacing a profile by its six type masses retains only anonymous, homogeneous rules. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for voting, but it is a strict restriction of the theorem’s domain. The mismatch is visible on the paper’s own benchmark: if \(F(x)\) is the top choice of voter \(1\), its distance from a dictatorship is zero. Yet an anonymous \(\Phi(\mu)\) cannot reproduce that dictator. Under the uniform six-type society, any fixed winner disagrees with the top choice of a random voter with probability \(2/3\). The proposed quantity therefore calls a perfect dictatorship “far from dictatorship.”

One can repair this by putting voter roles into the type space, but then the role labels must remain distinguishable. With one type per identity, the number of types grows with the electorate and there is no fixed finite type space or high-multiplicity limit. With finitely many roles, the object becomes a bloc- or role-dictatorship model, not the individual-dictatorship notion in Theorem 1.5. That may be interesting, but it is a new social-choice model rather than a faithful mirror.

The manipulation quantity changes in the same way. \(M_i(F)\) concerns a named coordinate in a labelled product space and a uniformly random replacement ballot. In the proposed continuum, one voter has zero mass, so the event is replaced by a limit

\[ \liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{\operatorname{MP}_\delta(\Phi,\mathcal D)}{\delta}. \]

The \(1/\delta\) normalization is an invented boundary-flux sensitivity. It may be mathematically natural, but it is not forced by the theorem. A positive-\(\delta\) version instead studies a coalition or population bloc. Either repair changes the quantifiers and the object being measured.

The probability model is not inherited either. Impartial culture makes each labelled ballot uniform and independently sampled. The induced empirical mass distribution converges to the single point \(u=(1/6,\ldots,1/6)\), rather than to a nontrivial distribution \(\mathcal D\) over societies. Introducing \(\mathcal D\) is therefore an additional modelling choice. At \(u\), the paper’s global distances over all \(6^n\) profiles are not represented; they are replaced by local behaviour near one population vector. A Gaussian or large-deviation scaling could restore nontrivial fluctuations, but that would be a new asymptotic theory, not a continuous version dictated by the paper.

There is also no computational problem until \(\Phi\) is represented. An arbitrary map \(\Phi:\Delta(L_3)\to\{a,b,c\}\) has no finite input encoding. If \(\Phi\) is supplied as a piecewise-polyhedral rule, that is a substantial new restriction; if it is supplied by a circuit or oracle, the complexity question changes again. The proponent’s suggestion that fixed-dimensional polyhedral calculations might be tractable is a plausible research direction, but it is not an algorithmic consequence or mirror of Theorem 1.5.

Lemma 3.2 does not rescue the case. It is an analytic isoperimetric inequality relating two manipulation probabilities. A continuum analogue could become a boundary-measure or coarea inequality, but it would remain analytic and would not supply the missing computational anchor. The same is true of the quantitative Arrow results later in the paper.

The strongest possible rescue is thus an independently motivated study of anonymous voting rules on the six-ranking simplex: define a representation for \(\Phi\), choose a society distribution, and classify infinitesimal or bloc manipulation sensitivity. That is a coherent high-multiplicity voting project, and I would not object that voting lacks meaningful multiplicity. The objection is narrower and stronger: every route that makes the proposed problem computationally definite either restricts the theorem to a new anonymous subclass, changes individual manipulation into bloc sensitivity, adds a new probability model, or invents a new rule representation. Since the source paper supplies no computational result to mirror, the resulting project is not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper.

The universal claim that no related continuous voting problem could ever be valuable is too strong to prove from the text. But under the programme’s named-computational-anchor requirement, the negative case is decisive: this paper offers analytic material from which one might design a new continuous project, not a computational result whose population continuization the programme can study.
