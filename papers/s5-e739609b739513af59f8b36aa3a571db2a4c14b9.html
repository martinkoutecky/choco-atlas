The continuous mirror question

Given reservation shares \(\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^n\) summing to \(1\), a finite horizon \(H\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) department types \(t\), each specifying vacancies \(q_{t,s}\) per period, compute a finite-support lottery over integral roster paths for each type. Every unit of mass must receive one integral path that is monotone and, at every prefix \(h\), assigns category \(j\) either \(\lfloor\alpha_j Q_{t,h}\rfloor\) or \(\lceil\alpha_j Q_{t,h}\rceil\), where \(Q_{t,h}=\sum_{s\le h}q_{t,s}\); the mass-weighted assignment must equal \(\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_jQ_{t,h}\) for each \(j,h\). For finite implementations with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb{Z}\), do independent roster draws within each type recover Theorem 3’s university-level deviation bounds, and can the policy be computed efficiently when roster period \(k\) is explicit?

The model it lives in

A population of departmental hiring units, typed by their vacancy schedules, with \(\mu_t\) the mass of type \(t\). Each unit receives an integral monotone roster; a finite-support distribution over rosters per type gives fractional type averages and exact aggregate targets. For finite implementations with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb{Z}\), independent roster draws recover concentration bounds around those targets.

The objection that survived

In the limit, aggregate mass assignments are exact, so Theorem 3’s finite-\(N\) concentration phenomenon disappears after normalization; its deviation bounds must be understood as guarantees for finite-sample implementations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s cumulative randomized implementation with department quotas and high-probability university quotas. It leaves Theorem 1’s impossibility result and Theorem 4’s one-period controlled rounding result aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-support policy be computed in time polynomial in binary-encoded \(k\) when \(k\) is not explicit?
  • Can correlated roster choices improve finite-sample deviation bounds while preserving the department quotas?

The case FOR (proponent)

The anchor rule leaves no qualifying result in this paper: none of its numbered theorems, lemmas, corollaries, or propositions classifies a problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result, and Theorems 2–4 are existence results. Appendix A.2 describes a polynomial-time roster construction, but it is not a numbered complexity result. I would not present any of these as an “in P” anchor.

The closest substantive candidate is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that there exists an ex ante proportional randomized solution that is monotonic, stays within department quota, and almost stays within university quota with high probability: for each category and each \(b>0\), the upper and lower probabilities of a university-level deviation of at least \(b\) are bounded respectively by \(e^{-b^2/(3m)}\) and \(e^{-b^2/(2m)}\), where \(m\) is the number of departments. This is a proved constructive existence result, not a complexity classification.

If Theorem 3 is allowed as a nonstandard anchor, my strongest mirror would be Distributional Cumulative Roster Implementation. Consider a large multi-campus university governed by one reservation authority, with \(N\) departmental hiring units but only \(\tau\) distinct department templates, where \(N\gg\tau\). A type \(t\) is a complete department template: its category reservation shares, recruitment schedule over a fixed horizon, and relevant local rules. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of departments of that type. Each department still makes integral seat assignments; averages across a type may be fractional.

Given these types, their masses, and the reservation shares \(\alpha_j\), find a type-conditioned randomized policy over periodic rosters. Applying a roster to each department assigns every newly opened seat to a category. A valid policy must, at every period prefix, allocate to category \(j\) in each department either \(\lfloor\alpha_j Q_d\rfloor\) or \(\lceil\alpha_j Q_d\rceil\), where \(Q_d\) is that department’s cumulative vacancies; these roster assignments must be monotonic, so reservations already made are never undone. The policy must be ex ante proportional. Across the university, if \(A_j\) is the realized cumulative assignment and \(F_j\) its fractional target, require the Theorem 3 tail bounds with \(N\) in place of \(m\). The “solution” is a sampling procedure or finite decomposition that represents the policy by type. There is no optimization objective: as in the paper, the question is whether a policy with these properties exists and can be constructed.

I expect this version to be tractable when the roster period \(k\) is treated explicitly: the paper’s flow decomposition supplies the main machinery, and independent sampling across departments gives the concentration bound. A sharper algorithmic question is whether the policy can be sampled in time polynomial in the binary input length when \(k\) is not explicit. Further questions include whether correlated roster choices can improve the university-level deviation, and how the result changes when department types have different reservation rules.

The high-multiplicity scenario is many departments with few repeated hiring templates; the continuous mass is over departments, not applicants. That gives fractional type averages while retaining integral reservations for each department. The scope is Theorem 3’s cumulative randomized implementation, not the paper’s other results. The weak point is also the main plausibility risk: departments are allocation units in the paper, but they are not its agents in the usual population sense. A referee might see this as continuizing an administrative structure rather than the population. And even if the authors accept the mirror, the paper itself supplies no named computational-complexity anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor here. Theorem 3 is an existence and concentration result for randomized rounding; Theorem 1 is an impossibility result, and Theorems 2 and 4 establish existence. None classifies a computational problem’s complexity. Appendix A.2 describes a polynomial-time construction, but that is not a numbered complexity result, and its stated construction uses the roster period \(k\) explicitly. The proponent identifies this gap correctly; under the programme’s anchor rule, it is decisive.

Even granting Theorem 3 as an exception, the strongest mirror is a continuum of department templates. That makes departments—the table’s quota rows—the population, while the applicants and faculty the policy affects do not appear in the formal model. The question remains one of multi-period matrix rounding among administrative units, rather than computation over a continuous society of agents. Pooling departments across universities would not fix the mismatch: the university-level quota is specific to each university.

The department-template version is the best salvage, and it exposes the deeper limitation. At normalized scale, the bound in Theorem 3 makes aggregate deviations vanish as the number of departments grows; the limiting mass model has exact aggregate proportions. Keeping the finite-number fluctuations retains a finite rounding problem. Keeping distributions over roster paths at the continuum level preserves integral assignments for each department, but turns the aggregate into a fractional mixture. That is a sensible apportionment model, yet the population limit contributes no new computational axis: the roster construction depends on the reservation fractions and period \(k\), while grouping departments by template only batches its application.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo mirror, though not claim the universal negative is airtight. A very large system with many genuinely repeated administrative units could support a worthwhile high-multiplicity study. The weaker fit is to the programme’s focus on continuizing the society, and this paper supplies no named computational-complexity result for such a mirror to anchor on.
