What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue covers Theorem 1.2(a), with parts (b) and (c) as boundary cases, and suggests extending Lemma 5.1; it does not mirror a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a direct continuum version of Theorem 1.2, proved in this paper. Part (a) says that for every \(p>1/2\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), some \(\delta>0\) works uniformly over weighted-majority rules and joint distributions \(\mu\) on voter profiles: if every voter votes \(1\) with probability at least \(p\), and every voter’s effect is at most \(\delta\), then the probability the rule outputs \(1\) is at least \(1-\varepsilon\). Parts (b) and (c) give the boundary: for monotone antisymmetric rules that are not weighted majority, and for transitive such rules other than simple majority, the paper constructs distributions with every marginal above \(1/2\), zero effects, and output \(0\) with probability \(1\).

Here is the closest continuous-population question I can make from that result. Let voters be the unit interval with Lebesgue mass, with a measurable nonnegative weight function \(w\) normalized so that \(\int_0^1 w(a)\,da=1\). A random measurable signal field \(X(a)\in\{0,1\}\) determines the weighted-majority outcome \(F\), with \(F=1\) when \(\int_0^1 w(a)(2X(a)-1)\,da>0\); assume ties have probability zero. For almost every voter \(a\), require \(\Pr[X(a)=1]\ge p\), with both signal values having positive probability, and define \(e(a)=\mathbb E[F\mid X(a)=1]-\mathbb E[F\mid X(a)=0]\). The question is whether, for each \(p>1/2\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a \(\delta>0\) such that every such weighted population and signal law with \(\operatorname{ess\,sup}_a e(a)\le\delta\) satisfies \(\Pr[F=1]\ge1-\varepsilon\). A proof or counterexample would settle this proposed mirror; the natural further questions are whether the sharp bounds of Lemma 5.1 extend, and whether the counterexamples in Theorem 1.2(b,c) have continuum counterparts.

This is a plausible *analytic* mirror: the population has become continuous, and the theorem’s concern with weighted-majority aggregation remains intact. But it does not qualify as a computational mirror under the requested standard. Theorem 1.2 is not a complexity result; neither is Lemma 5.1, which proves tight probabilistic bounds using an LP argument. Theorem 1.1 is cited from Kalai rather than proved here, and is also an analytic result. None of these asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifiable by computational complexity.

The model also needs a joint probability law over the entire signal field to preserve the paper’s arbitrary correlations. A distribution over voter types such as weight and marginal bias alone does not encode that dependence. So the positive case is narrow: the authors would likely recognize the proposed question as a continuum analogue of their aggregation theorem, but it has no optimization objective or algorithmic output, and it belongs to the analytic line of work that ChoCo explicitly distinguishes from its computational focus.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror does not clear ChoCo’s computational gate. Theorem 1.2 is a statement about how a voting rule behaves under a probability distribution: small effects imply a high probability of a correct outcome for weighted majority, with counterexamples for other rules. Parts (b) and (c) are existence results about such distributions. Lemma 5.1 proves a tight probabilistic bound; the LP is part of that proof, not an algorithmic problem with a specified input and output. None of these results classifies a computational task.

The strongest repair would be to ask for an algorithm that computes or optimizes the aggregation probability over weighted populations. But preserving the paper’s arbitrary correlations means representing the joint law of the voters’ signals. A distribution over voter types alone does not capture that law: populations with the same type masses and marginals can have different dependencies and different outcomes. Encoding the full law, or a model that generates it, makes the representation of dependence central to the computational problem. Restricting the law to exchangeable, conditionally independent, or finite-latent models could produce a tractable input, but that adds structure absent from the theorem. The resulting algorithmic question would be a new problem about that chosen model, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The proposed random field on the unit interval is a plausible continuous *analytic* analogue. It still needs a law for the whole field to preserve correlation, and conditioning on \(X(a)\) for a single point is not a population-mass perturbation: that point has measure zero in the aggregate vote. A process model can make such conditioning meaningful, but it does so by adding a stochastic structure that the continuum population distribution does not specify. Even if the authors accept that formulation, it asks for an aggregation theorem, not a complexity result. The integral version of the paper’s expectation argument may be mathematically interesting, but it supplies no evident search, optimization, or separation problem for ChoCo to classify.

So the positive case establishes that this paper has a natural continuous-population *question*, not that it has a worthwhile continuous computational mirror. I cannot honestly prove the universal claim that no algorithmic problem could ever be built from these ideas; large elections plainly admit sensible high-multiplicity regimes. The narrower negative judgment is stronger: any computational proposal would need a new problem and a chosen encoding of signal dependence, and its computational content would come from those choices rather than from mirroring a named result here.
