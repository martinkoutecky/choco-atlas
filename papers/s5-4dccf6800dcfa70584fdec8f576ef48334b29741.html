The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Lemma 3.1, Proposition 3.3, Theorem 3.4, and Corollaries 3.5–3.6 are probabilistic or asymptotic results; none asserts that a problem is tractable, hard, or complete for a complexity class.

The strongest tempting anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved here: for a balanced market with \(n\) men and \(n\) women, where preferences on each side are independently drawn from Mallows distributions with fixed \(\phi_m,\phi_w<1\), there are events whose probability tends to \(1\) such that, in *every* stable matching, the maximum difference in central-order quantiles between matched partners tends to zero as \(n\) grows. This is a structural limit theorem, not a computational result.

It does suggest a natural population-continuum intuition: represent people by mass over preference types, retain their positions on the common central ranking as quantiles, and represent a matching by a mass coupling between the two sides. One could then ask whether every stable mass matching concentrates on pairs with similar quantiles. Large cohorts of applicants and positions sharing a small number of preference patterns offer a plausible high-multiplicity setting for that question.

But that would be a new, analytically motivated question, not a continuous problem anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The theorem’s random rankings and high-probability limit do not supply the required complexity anchor. So the honest positive case is only a structural analogy; there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover under the stated rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts with the decisive point: there is no named computational result here to continuize. Theorem 3.4 is a high probability limit theorem about stable matchings in randomly generated finite markets. Corollaries 3.5 and 3.6 are asymptotic welfare statements. None asks about the complexity of finding, verifying, or optimizing a matching. The paper’s own framing is probabilistic and structural, not computational.

The strongest possible mirror is the one suggested by Theorem 3.4: describe each side by mass over central-order quantiles and ask whether stable mass matchings concentrate on equal quantiles. But the continuum limit strips away the feature that makes the theorem informative. With fixed Mallows parameters below \(1\), rank displacements are \(O(\log n)\), so normalized displacements vanish. In a quantile model, those local deviations collapse, and the limiting preferences are simply aligned with the common order. Diagonal matching then expresses assortativity directly. Asking for an algorithm to recover that coupling would be a different problem; it would not mirror the paper’s high probability, uniform-over-all-stable-matchings claim.

There is a harder version: retain the full Mallows distribution over rankings as the population’s type distribution and compute stable mass matchings or their welfare range. But the rankings concern named counterpart identities, and their type space changes with \(n\); in the sampled markets, the realized support can grow with the population. Aggregating to a fixed set of quantile types loses the within-quantile ranking information that stability uses. Retaining that information preserves a large, \(n\)-dependent type space, while the paper still supplies no computational question or result for it. A marked continuum that keeps local rank offsets might make a new model, but it would need its own motivation and computational target.

The same problem affects the welfare corollaries. In a quantile limit, both sides’ average normalized ranks approach the same population average, so welfare equivalence becomes a direct consequence of the shared central order. Turning that into a complexity question would require adding an optimization problem the paper does not study.

So the proposed continuum analogy is coherent, but it is not a computational mirror of a result in this paper. The negative case is strongest at that boundary. The broader claim that *no* matching scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous computational problem is not established: one can imagine high multiplicity matching models. But that possibility does not rescue the anchors raised here; it would be a different research direction rather than a continuization of this paper’s results.
