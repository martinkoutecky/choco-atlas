What the mirror covers

The proposed model addresses equilibrium existence and construction; it does not mirror Theorem 1 as a computational result or the strategy-proofness impossibility in Proposition 1.

The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find a named result in this paper that classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It assumes weakly monotone utilities and says that a competitive equilibrium exists in the original finite exchange economy \(E\) if and only if the paper’s expanded coalitional game \(V\)—which adds a dummy seller for each commodity—is balanced. In full, balancedness means that for every nonnegative weighting \((\lambda_S)\) of coalitions satisfying \(\sum_{S\ni a}\lambda_S=1\) for each player \(a\), we have \(\sum_S\lambda_S V(S)\le V(N\cup\Omega)\). The proof connects the expanded game’s core to competitive-equilibrium payoffs and invokes the balancedness–nonempty-core theorem; it is an existence characterization, not a complexity result.

The most credible continuous mirror I can make from it is Finite-Type Continuum Competitive Equilibrium. Consider a large exchange market for standardized indivisible units—such as equipment or permits—with many participants but a small catalog of repeated valuation and endowment schedules. A type \(t\) completely specifies a participant’s utility \(u_t\), initial endowment \(e_t\), and wealth; let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s population share. A participant still receives an integral bundle \(A\) of units. The variable \(z_{t,A}\) records how much of type \(t\)’s mass receives bundle \(A\), so it may be fractional even though no individual’s bundle is.

An instance consists of finitely many commodity categories \(G\), rational masses \((\mu_t)\) summing to one, integral endowments \(e_t\), and explicit rational utility tables over each type’s feasible integral bundles. The per-capita supply of category \(g\) is \(s_g=\sum_t\mu_t e_{t,g}\). The problem is to return either a competitive price vector \(p\ge0\) and allocation masses \(z_{t,A}\), or a certificate that none exist. A valid output has \(\sum_A z_{t,A}=\mu_t\); aggregate use of each commodity is at most its supply, with equality whenever its price is positive; and every bundle receiving positive mass maximizes \(u_t(A)-p\cdot A\) for that type.

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime when, for example, thousands or millions of participants fall into tens or hundreds of complete preference-and-endowment types. Commodity supplies scale with population. Each person gets an integral bundle, while the type-level allocation can split mass across bundles. That co-modification is part of the market model, and the underlying question remains the paper’s central one: whether competitive equilibrium exists.

For explicit utility tables, the problem is in Class A: maximize \(\sum_{t,A}z_{t,A}u_t(A)\) subject to the type-mass and supply constraints. The LP dual supplies nonnegative commodity prices and type utility levels; complementary slackness gives the demand and market-clearing conditions above. Thus an optimal primal and dual solution yields an equilibrium in time polynomial in the explicit input length \(L\), including the number of types and listed bundles. If utilities are given succinctly instead of by tables, the bundle-optimization or pricing problem becomes a further question.

I think the authors would recognize this as a continuous version of their equilibrium-existence problem. The weakest point is also substantial: Theorem 1 is not a computational-complexity result, and the continuum model’s LP can make existence routine even where the finite economy has none. The paper’s finite-market balancedness criterion and its Section 4 strategy-proofness result are not thereby mirrored; in particular, a single agent’s report has no effect in an atomless population. This is a plausible positive case for continuizing the equilibrium question, but a modest one under the programme’s anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 is an existence characterization: equilibrium exists if and only if the expanded game \(V\) is balanced. It gives no algorithm, complexity bound, input encoding, or identified pricing problem. The paper’s proof establishes an equivalence; it does not chart the computational difficulty of deciding balancedness or finding an equilibrium.

The proposed finite-type market is still a credible population model. Repeated participants can share a complete preference-and-endowment type, and integral bundles for individuals can coexist with fractional allocations of type mass. That passes the programme’s high-multiplicity test. But the proposed task—compute prices and allocation masses—is a new algorithmic question adjacent to Theorem 1, not a computational claim the theorem makes. With explicit utility tables, a welfare-maximization LP gives a generic route, with table length \(L\) already including values for up to \(2^{|G|}\) bundles. With succinct utilities, the relevant bottleneck becomes finding \(\arg\max_A \bigl(u_t(A)-p\cdot A\bigr)\); this paper neither specifies an encoding that makes that problem meaningful nor develops a method for solving it. The resulting complexity story would depend on choices supplied after the fact.

The strategy-proofness result does not provide a stronger computational anchor. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero effect. Giving a positive-mass type group the power to report as a bloc could make deviations matter, but that changes the strategic players and still supplies no complexity result from this paper.

So the negative case is strong against treating Theorem 1 as an anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme. It is weak against the universal claim in the prompt: the large-market equilibrium question is plausible, and the authors could recognize it as their subject. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in that scenario; I can only say that its computational contribution would have to be built from modelling and algorithmic choices the paper itself does not provide.
