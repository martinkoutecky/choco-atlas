The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous population behind this paper, but it has no eligible named computational anchor under your rule. Propositions 1–4 establish equilibrium existence and characterize winners, payments, or large-\(N\) outcomes. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. So the strongest honest positive case is a well-motivated research question, not an anchored mirror of a computational result.

The closest result is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. For the budget-constrained game, assuming the paper’s large-budget conditions (2) and (3), it says that for sufficiently small \(\gamma\), \(X\) wins at no cost if
\[ B_X>B_Y-\sum_i V_i/2-V_N/2+(2m+1)\epsilon, \]
and \(Y\) wins at cost \(\bar V\), paid to legislators \(m\) through \(n\), if
\[ B_Y>B_X+\sum_i V_i/2+V_1/2+(2m+1)\epsilon. \]
Here \(\bar V\) is the least amount \(Y\) must commit to that median-through-nearly-indifferent group to secure a majority. This is a useful equilibrium characterization, but the paper does not state or analyze a computational problem, so I would not present it as an algorithmic anchor.

The strongest candidate follow-up is High-Multiplicity Up-Front Vote-Buying Equilibrium. Its instance is a finite support \(T\) of legislator types, each with a rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to one and a rational preference difference \(v_t=V^X_t-V^Y_t\), together with per-capita budgets \(b_X,b_Y\), per-capita winning values \(w_X,w_Y\), and a positive normalized cost for each bidding round. A unit of mass represents a fraction of delegates with the same relevant preferences; the lobbyists may target measurable portions of that mass with offers. Offers cannot decrease over time, delegates tender to the lobbyist whose offer plus their voting preference gives them greater utility, and a lobbyist’s budget counts only its outstanding commitments to delegates who would currently tender to it. The game ends under the paper’s two-consecutive-round stopping rule, with a winner holding more than half the mass.

The question is: given this rational finite-support instance, determine the pure subgame-perfect equilibrium terminal outcomes, reporting the winner and the distribution of payments across types—or decide whether a specified lobbyist wins in every such equilibrium. Each lobbyist’s objective remains the paper’s strategic payoff: its value for winning minus its payments and round costs, or its payment costs if it loses. The task is equilibrium computation, not minimizing total bribes.

A plausible regime is a very large union or association convention: thousands or tens of thousands of chapter delegates, but only dozens or hundreds of recurring constituency-preference profiles, so \(N\gg\tau\). Delegates who share a type are indistinguishable with respect to the preferences and costs the game uses. The paper itself notes the continuum-voter model of Groseclose and Snyder and Banks’s finite version. Those are supporting precedents for large voting populations, not collisions with this mirror: this paper’s repeated, reactive bidding game differs from their one-final-offer structure.

For the analogue of Proposition 2’s large-budget region, I would expect a tractable threshold test: the type-level input makes aggregate preference advantages and relevant order statistics computable from \(\tau\) types. That expectation is a research conjecture, not a result of this paper. The full equilibrium problem remains open; the propositions characterize only parts of the parameter space, and neither equilibrium existence nor the winner characterization automatically carries over to the nonatomic game. Further questions include whether equilibria exist, whether all equilibria select the same winner, whether the half-weight on preference advantages survives, and how the continuous outcomes approximate finite high-multiplicity instances.

The mirror’s weakest point is decisive for the requested grading: the paper supplies no named computational-complexity anchor. There is also a regime-fit question: a large convention can plausibly act like a legislature, but an ordinary national parliament is usually too small for this population limit. The proposed equilibrium problem is concrete and recognizable; it still cannot turn the paper’s economic equilibrium results into computational results the authors did not claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror is that it has no named computational result to continue. Propositions 1–4 establish equilibrium existence and characterize winners, payments, and large-population outcomes. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem or give an algorithmic result. Proposition 2 is the closest anchor, but its threshold depends on aggregate preferences and budget conditions. Replacing finite sums with weighted sums over types would restate an equilibrium characterization, not continue a computational result.

The convention scenario is plausible, and the high-multiplicity literature the paper cites supports taking large voting populations seriously. Neither is an objection. The difficulty is the proposed computational task: equilibrium computation in this dynamic game needs a precise representation of its states and outputs. Current offers determine both who tenders and which commitments count against each budget. Even legislators with identical preferences can end up with different offer histories. Allowing partial purchases of a type therefore requires tracking distributions of offers and histories within each type; the support size \(\tau\) alone does not give a finite state description. Restricting each lobbyist to one offer per type would simplify that problem, but would remove the option to target a portion of a type. The proposal does not yet specify an encoding or an algorithmic problem for the unrestricted game.

There is also a scaling choice tied to a feature the paper relies on. The positive per-round cost \(\gamma\) matters to its equilibrium analysis. In a population of size \(N\), keeping lobbyists’ budgets and stakes proportional to \(N\) while holding \(\gamma\) fixed makes that round cost vanish after per-capita normalization. Keeping it constant per capita requires scaling the round cost with \(N\), changing the model’s campaigning cost. Both choices can be studied, but neither supplies a canonical high-multiplicity version of the paper’s results.

A static question about the least cost of buying a majority would be easier to encode, but it would replace the paper’s strategic bidding game with a different optimization problem. The paper’s own large-\(N\) analysis and its references to continuum-voter models are relevant background; they do not supply the missing computational anchor.

So I would reject this paper from a programme seeking computational mirrors. The case against the stronger universal claim—that no worthwhile continuous question could be built from it—is less secure. A carefully specified computational study of equilibrium in a type-mass bidding game might be worthwhile. That would be a new project requiring its own model and computational target, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
