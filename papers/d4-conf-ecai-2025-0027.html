But the method relies on a specific social graph and user-level temporal data. Putting all the information needed to preserve those interactions into each type could make the support grow with the number of users. More importantly, this would be an unanchored new question, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper for a continuous mirror to carry over. It gives no named theorem, lemma, or corollary about complexity, approximation, or algorithms for a social-choice problem. Its equations define a forecasting model; its numbered contributions describe a method, datasets, and empirical accuracy comparisons. The “up to 6.4%” improvements and ablation results are experimental findings, not complexity results.

The strongest proposed mirror would group users by their opinion histories, disturbances, and social roles, represent interactions with a kernel, and predict population-level opinion trajectories. That could be a coherent continuous-agent forecasting problem. But it requires choosing a new computational target—such as training, prediction at a fixed horizon, or minimizing population-average loss—and specifying the interaction model and input representation. The paper makes none of those choices as a complexity question. Preserving its individual-user predictions instead requires preserving the network context that influences each user; complete types may then be nearly unique. Aggregating to type-level forecasts can restore multiplicity, but changes the output the experiments evaluate.

So the negative case is strong against calling this a *mirror of the paper’s computational results*: there are no such results to mirror, and the proposed forecast is an independently formulated problem. The universal claim is weaker. Since the paper is explicitly about opinion forecasting, a carefully specified population-level prediction problem could plausibly interest its authors and ChoCo. I can’t rule out that future direction; the paper simply doesn’t establish it as a worthwhile mirror. The paper’s references to continuous opinion values and opinion-dynamics models do not themselves supply a continuous-population computational result.
