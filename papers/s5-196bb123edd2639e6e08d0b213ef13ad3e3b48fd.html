The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), strict-ranking types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), Snowveil's local parameters, quorum \(Q>1/2\), horizon \(H\), and precision \(\varepsilon\), initialize \(y_{t,j}(0)=0\), where \(y_{t,j}\) is the mass of type \(t\) locked on candidate \(j\). Let \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_j y_{t,j}\), and let \(q_j(y)\) be the probability that the paper's Voter Update Process returns \(\mathrm{LOCK}(j)\) when its samples are drawn from the current reported-ranking distribution. Evolve \(\frac{dy_{t,j}}{ds}=\frac{u_t}{\sum_r u_r}q_j(y)\). Determine which candidate first reaches \(\sum_t y_{t,j}\ge Q\) by time \(H\), and return its hitting time within \(\varepsilon\), or report that none reaches quorum by \(H\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over strict-ranking types \(T\). The state \(y_{t,j}\) tracks type-\(t\) mass locked on \(j\), while unlocked mass \(u_t\) reports sincerely and locked mass reports its ranking with \(j\) promoted to first place. These reports induce a distribution \(\nu_y\); \(q_j(y)\) is the probability the \(\gamma\)-round update returns \(\mathrm{LOCK}(j)\) under samples from \(\nu_y\). In scaled time \(s=\text{update calls}/n\), the mass evolves by \(\frac{dy_{t,j}}{ds}=\frac{u_t}{\sum_r u_r}q_j(y)\), and the computational task is to approximate its first quorum hitting time.

With fixed local parameters, this may amount to standard density-dependent Markov-chain analysis and add little complexity-theoretic structure; that limits its novelty, not its status as a computational population mirror.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8.2's single-winner convergence-time result and models the associated lock dynamics. It leaves Proposition 7.1's coalition bound, CHB's axiomatic results, the full ranking iteration, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions does the fluid trajectory reach quorum, rather than exhausting unlocked mass below \(Q\)?
  • When does the fluid hitting time approximate the finite process's expected hitting time as \(n\) grows?
  • How does the cost of computing \(q_j(y)\) and approximating the hitting time scale with \(m\), \(\tau\), \(k\), and \(\gamma\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think there is a real, narrow mirror here: a large DAO or online community choosing among a fixed set of policies. It may have millions of participants but relatively few distinct strict rankings, so the input is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) ballot types, with \(\tau\ll n\). This setting preserves Snowveil’s random peer sampling and ranking aggregation while making population shares, coalition sizes, and locked cohorts natural quantities.

My strongest anchor is Proposition 7.1, proved in this paper. It says that to neutralize the probabilistic advantage of a truthful Borda winner \(p^\star\), with per-voter Borda margin \(\delta>0\) over a coalition’s target \(p_c\), a coordinated coalition must have size \(c\ge n\delta/(\delta+m-1)\), which is \(\Omega(n)\).

The continuous mirror is the Continuous Borda Coalition Threshold problem. An instance is a candidate set \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings, and candidates \(p^\star,p_c\), where \(p^\star\) is the truthful Borda leader and
\[ \delta=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(B(p^\star,t)-B(p_c,t)\bigr)>0. \]
A coalition may redirect mass from truthful reports to arbitrary reported rankings. Find the minimum coalition mass that makes the expected Borda margin of \(p^\star\) over \(p_c\) nonpositive, and give a report attaining it. The optimal attack ranks \(p_c\) first and \(p^\star\) last, so the answer is \(\delta/(\delta+m-1)\). This is a tractable, direct mass version of the proposition: the discrete lower bound becomes a minimum population share. The paper’s proof sometimes identifies \(p^\star\) with the CHB outcome while analyzing a Borda margin; I would identify them in this mirror only when the truthful CHB winner is also the truthful Borda leader.

A second anchor is Theorem 8.2, also proved here. It states that Snowveil converges on a single winner in an expected number of steps bounded by \(O(n)\). Its continuous mirror is the Fluid Snowveil Quorum problem. Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), candidates, CHB and update parameters, quorum \(Q\), a horizon \(H\), and precision \(\varepsilon\), determine which candidate first reaches locked mass \(Q\) by time \(H\), and return an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation of that hitting time—or report that no quorum is reached by \(H\).

Here the dynamic state records mass \(y_{t,j}\) of sincere type \(t\) locked on candidate \(j\), with unlocked mass \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_j y_{t,j}\). A locked voter of type \(t\) reports the ranking formed by moving \(j\) to the top of that type’s ranking; an unlocked voter reports the sincere ranking. Let \(\nu_y\) be the resulting distribution over reported rankings, and let \(q_j(y)\) be the probability that one execution of the paper’s \(\gamma\)-round Voter Update Process returns LOCK\((j)\), when its samples are drawn independently from \(\nu_y\). In time scaled as \(s=\text{steps}/n\), the fluid dynamics are
\[ \frac{dy_{t,j}}{ds}=\frac{u_t}{\sum_r u_r}\,q_j(y), \]
while unlocked mass remains. This represents the finite process’s random selection of an unlocked voter and its local sampling and lock decision. Under fixed local parameters and a promised separation from the quorum threshold, I would expect approximate trajectory and hitting-time computation to be tractable. Further questions are how the cost changes when \(k,\gamma,m\) vary, and whether the fluid hitting time approximates finite-population expected time.

Both mirrors are plausible extensions of the authors’ own model: the first turns their coalition bound into a population-share optimization problem; the second takes the density limit of their lock-state Markov chain while retaining CHB, sampling, and feedback. The scope is specific: the coalition result concerns the Borda signal, and the fluid problem concerns single-winner convergence. These questions do not continuize only an outcome or a noise model; the society’s ranking distribution is the input object.

The weakest point is the convergence anchor. The paper’s argument does not establish that every fragmented state reaches a quorum: agents can, in principle, all lock across candidates while no candidate reaches \(Q\), leaving no unlocked voter to update. Its lower bound on locking for the canonical outcome also assumes positive margins that are not guaranteed for every profile. So I would treat the fluid problem as a sound continuous analogue of the claimed scalability result, not as confirmation that the stated \(O(n)\) guarantee holds. There are no named NP-hardness or standard complexity-class results in this paper; these anchors are quantitative coalition and runtime results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The coalition anchor is the weaker mirror. Its proposed objective is only to make one expected Borda margin nonpositive. If a coalition controls mass \(x\), the best possible reports change the margin from \(\delta\) to \((1-x)\delta-x(m-1)\), so the threshold is \(x=\delta/(\delta+m-1)\). That is the paper’s calculation with the integer count \(c\) replaced by a share \(c/n\); for rational societies, the discrete formula gives the same threshold up to rounding. There is no pricing problem or complexity landscape left to chart in this formulation.

That objection does not dispose of a stronger coalition question. Asking for the mass needed to change CHB’s winner or to make Snowveil reach a target with a specified probability would retain the ranking distribution and add the protocol’s thresholds, repeated samples, and feedback. A DAO with many participants and repeated ballot types is a plausible setting, and the paper itself identifies attacks on the CHB thresholds as open work. Type-level costs or capabilities could also be modeled without losing the high-multiplicity interpretation. This could be worthwhile; it is simply a substantial extension beyond Proposition 7.1’s Borda-margin bound.

The fluid runtime mirror is harder to dismiss. Its state \(y_{t,j}\) records the mass of each ranking type locked on each candidate, and its transition probabilities account for locked agents’ altered reports. With fixed local parameters, the update probabilities \(q_j(y)\) can be computed from the sampling distribution, giving a finite-dimensional population dynamic. Asking for an approximate quorum hitting time is a coherent computational question. The case against it is that it turns the paper’s update-count bound into a fluid analysis of a gossip protocol, rather than exposing a difficult combinatorial intervention; much of the work may be standard density-dependent Markov-chain analysis. But that is a concern about the contribution’s novelty and scale, not a basic objection to the mirror. If the parameters and support size vary, the cost of computing the transition probabilities and hitting times could itself be worth studying.

So the negative case can reject the coalition mirror as stated and question whether the fluid mirror offers enough computational social choice. It cannot honestly establish that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists. In particular, the authors could recognize the fluid model as a useful population-scale analysis of Snowveil, and the paper’s own proposed attack directions admit a natural mass-based extension. The strongest defensible conclusion is that neither anchor, as currently framed, clearly opens the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo targets; the universal “no scenario” verdict is weak.
