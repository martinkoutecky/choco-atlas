The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(\mathcal T\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict rankings over acceptable partner types, and nonnegative rational pair costs \(w_{tu}\), find in time polynomial in the input size a stable mass assignment \(x_{tu}\) with cost at most twice the minimum stable-assignment cost. Require \(\sum_{u:tu\in E}x_{tu}\le \mu_t\), and require for every acceptable pair \(tu\) that at least one of the two types has zero mass unmatched or assigned below the other type in its ranking. Assume a stable assignment exists and that each type has at most two possible partner types across all stable mass assignments.

The model it lives in

A continuum of interchangeable agents grouped into types \(t\), each with mass \(\mu_t\), a shared strict ranking over acceptable partner types, and shared pair costs. The decision variables \(x_{tu}\) give the mass matched across each acceptable type pair; feasibility respects each type’s mass, stability rules out mutually willing positive masses, and the objective is \(\sum_{tu\in E}w_{tu}x_{tu}\).

The objection that survived

The theorem bounds each individual’s possible partners, while the mirror bounds partner types; symmetry among cohort members means the paper’s sparse-partner promise need not hold in the continuous model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the minimum-weight approximation result in Theorem 6. It does not cover Results 1–2 on bipartite reducibility and polynomial-time optimization, or the paper’s broader weighted stable matching results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s minimum-weight stable roommates problem on sparse partner supports.

Picture a large residential program that assigns compatible residents to pairs. A type records a resident’s strict ranking of acceptable *cohort types* and the costs of pairing with each type. Residents of one type are interchangeable for matching purposes. If there are \(N\) residents and \(\tau\) types, the regime of interest has \(N\) large and \(\tau\) fixed or moderate; each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). This makes the population continuous while keeping the matching decision recognizable: decide how much mass of each pair of types to match.

My anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It says that when each agent has at most two possible partners across all stable matchings, there is a polynomial-time \(2\)-approximation for minimum-weight stable matching, for any nonnegative edge weights. The paper’s proof describes the corresponding graph \(H\) as isolated edges and vertex-disjoint cycles, and reduces the weights to a U-shaped form. This is an approximation result, rather than a hardness result. The paper mentions general NP-hardness through Feder [7], but does not state it as a numbered theorem here, so I do not use it as an anchor.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover:

Two-Partner-Type Mass-WSR. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of types; rational masses \(\mu_t\); an undirected graph \(G=(T,E)\) of acceptable type pairs; a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of each type’s neighbors; and nonnegative rational costs \(w_{tu}\). The cost \(w_{tu}\) applies to every pairing between types \(t\) and \(u\). For each edge \(tu\), choose a mass \(x_{tu}\ge 0\), subject to
\[ \sum_{u:tu\in E}x_{tu}\le \mu_t \qquad(t\in T). \]
For an acceptable pair \(tu\), let
\[ b_{t,u}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{\substack{v:tv\in E\\v\text{ is ranked at least as high as }u\text{ by }t}}x_{tv}. \]
This is the mass of type \(t\) left unmatched or assigned to a type it likes less than \(u\). Call \(x\) stable if, for every \(tu\in E\),
\[ \min\{b_{t,u}(x),b_{u,t}(x)\}=0. \]
Thus no acceptable pair has positive mass on both sides willing to leave their current assignments for each other. The objective is to minimize
\[ \sum_{tu\in E} w_{tu}x_{tu}. \]
The promised class is that each type has at most two possible partner types across all stable mass assignments. A solution is a minimum-cost stable mass assignment—or, for the approximation version, a stable assignment of cost at most twice optimum.

I would expect this restricted problem to be Class A, as a research conjecture: the two-partner condition suggests cycle structure, and the paper’s U-shaped-weight argument is a concrete lead for an algorithm. But Theorem 6 does not establish an algorithm for this mass problem. The stability conditions above are complementarity conditions, and extending the paper’s approximation guarantee to binary-rational masses would require a new argument. Useful next questions are whether the sparse-support condition yields a polynomial-time exact algorithm or a \(2\)-approximation, how to find that support efficiently, and whether the mass optimum corresponds to limits of finite clone instances.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 6’s minimum-weight result and its sparse-partner regime, not the paper’s full characterization of weighted stable matching. The weak point is the cohort interpretation. It treats residents’ preferences as strict rankings of partner *types*, with members of a type interchangeable; the paper ranks individual neighbors. That is a plausible institutional matching model, but the “at most two partners” condition has become “at most two partner types,” which is not automatic from the theorem’s hypothesis. This is the part authors and a referee would need to accept as a meaningful high-multiplicity version.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 6’s “at most two possible partners” condition does not survive a high-multiplicity lift in its stated form. It concerns partner identities. In a symmetric clone model, if a positive mass of type \(t\) is matched with type \(u\), relabeling the \(u\)-agents can make each \(t\)-agent’s possible partner set grow with the size of the \(u\)-cohort. The theorem’s condition then fails as the population grows. Keeping it would require encoding partner identities in the types, making the number of types grow with the population and removing the intended high-multiplicity regime.

The proposed repair—at most two *partner types*—is natural, but it changes the theorem’s structure. A type’s mass may split between both partner types, producing a capacitated fractional matching with complementarity stability constraints. The theorem’s graph \(H\), its disjoint-cycle structure, and its U-shaped-weight argument concern individual agents and do not establish an approximation guarantee for that mass problem. This sharply limits Theorem 6’s value as a source of an algorithmic mirror.

It does not, however, defeat the stronger mirror. Large shared-housing cohorts with common rankings over resident types and common pair costs give a plausible high-multiplicity stable-roommates model. Rational masses, mass matching, and the rule that no positive masses on both sides wish to switch partners make a coherent computational problem. Its sparse partner-type regime could be worth studying even if Theorem 6’s guarantee does not carry over. The paper’s fractional stable-matching polytope is not prior continuous-population work; it represents a relaxation of a finite-agent problem.

So the negative case is limited: it shows that the proponent has not preserved Theorem 6’s actual promise or its algorithmic structure. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The type-level mirror remains plausible under the programme’s standard, and this is a weak case against the paper overall.
