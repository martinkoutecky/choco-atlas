What the mirror covers

The closest candidate is Theorem 4's enumeration of resolute rule families, with numerical comparisons in the appendix; the remaining theorems address axiomatic existence, incompatibility, and refinement.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest case for a continuous mirror is that the *setting* is natural: in a large electorate, types could be rankings and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of voters with each ranking. But the paper gives no named computational-complexity result to anchor that mirror. Its results concern axiomatic existence, impossibility, and refinement for finite \((m,n)\) social choice problems.

The closest result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It gives exact counts of resolute social choice rules, neutral resolute rules, and consequentially neutral resolute rules for finite electorates; Appendix A uses those counts for numerical comparisons. That is an enumeration of rule families, not a result classifying the complexity of an instance problem as, for example, polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard. The other named theorems likewise establish axiomatic conditions or constructions; Theorems 1 and 8 are cited from Moulin.

One could define a continuous society as a distribution over rankings and ask for an anonymous, neutral, resolute rule on those distributions. But that would be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. And the paper’s consequential-neutrality condition counts finite profiles; extending it to a continuum of societies would require an additional measure over those societies. That would define a new problem, not mirror a computational result in this paper.

So I have no defensible lead anchor, and no continuous problem to hand to a prover as the analogue of a named computational result. The population regime is plausible; the missing computational anchor is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its theorems describe which *functions* from finite preference-profile spaces exist, which axioms they satisfy, and how their outcomes are distributed across profiles. They do not classify the complexity of a task on a given society, such as finding a minimum-cost intervention or testing winner robustness.

Theorem 4 is the closest candidate, but its formulas count resolute social choice rules on the finite set \(L(A)^N\). Consequential neutrality is equality of the numbers of profiles assigned to each alternative. A continuous population gives a point \(\mu\) in a simplex of distributions over rankings; it does not supply a finite set of profiles to count. One could define a continuous version using a measure on the simplex and require equal measure for each alternative’s selection region. That would be a coherent axiomatic question, but it adds a measure and a global selector to the model. Counting or measuring those selectors is not a computational problem about an input society. Restricting to distributions with a fixed denominator restores a finite profile space; it does not turn Theorem 4 into a complexity result.

A stronger proposed mirror might take rational \(\mu\) as input and ask for an outcome under an anonymous, neutral, efficient, resolute rule, or ask whether such a rule exists over the whole distribution space. The first needs a specified rule: the paper studies classes of rules and gives no instance-level objective or action whose output an algorithm should compute. The second is again a global axiomatic design question. Encoding the rule as a circuit, or imposing a particular optimization criterion on its construction, could create a computational problem, but the encoding or criterion would supply the substance of that problem.

The same gap holds for the other named results. Theorems 2 and 3 characterize and refine the ties permitted by anonymity, neutrality, and efficiency; Theorems 5–9 give existence and incompatibility results under consequential neutrality. Their continuous counterparts could be interesting axiomatic results. They do not furnish a computational task from the paper to mirror.

A large electorate with relatively few ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, so the objection is not that a continuous society would be unnatural. The negative case is strong for this paper’s results: their object is axiomatic rule design, and no continuous-population complexity question is already present to carry over. The universal claim is less airtight: a researcher could devise a worthwhile algorithmic rule-synthesis problem inspired by the paper. That would require adding a computational objective and representation, though, so it would be new work rather than a continuous mirror of a result here.
