The continuous mirror question

Given finite advertiser types \(T\), slot formats \(J\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) and supplies \(q_j\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_jq_j=1\), and nonnegative binary-rational values \(v_{tj}\), find the coordinatewise greatest price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{J}\) admitting an allocation \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_tx_{tj}=q_j\), and \(x_{tj}>0\) only if \(v_{tj}-p_j=\max(0,\max_{k\in J}(v_{tk}-p_k))\). Return a clearing allocation as a witness.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic unit-demand assignment market with advertiser types carrying masses μ and slot formats carrying supplies \(q\). Each advertiser receives at most one indivisible slot; aggregate mass can split across formats only among indifferent advertisers of the same type. Prices are per format, and the objective is the coordinatewise greatest market-clearing price vector.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s maximum-MCP computation, with Theorem 2 supplying correctness. It leaves the paper’s revenue and strategic-equilibrium analyses aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does C-Max-MCP admit a strongly polynomial algorithm in τ and |J|, independent of the binary encodings of masses and supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead and only anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper, with Theorem 2 establishing the algorithm’s correctness. For any nonnegative valuation matrix in a balanced unit-demand market with \(m\) buyers and \(m\) goods, the skewed-set-aided descending-price algorithm computes the maximum market-clearing price vector in strongly polynomial time \(O(m^4)\), using Algorithm 2 to find the maximally skewed set. Theorem 2 states that the algorithm returns that maximum vector. This is a computational result about the paper’s central problem, rather than about auction revenue or equilibrium bidding.

A natural regime is a large ad platform with a fixed menu of slot formats. There are many advertisers, but repeated campaign templates give them only \(\tau\) distinct valuation vectors over those formats, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the advertiser population \(N\). Slot supply scales with the market: format \(j\) has a fraction \(q_j\) of total supply, while type \(t\) accounts for a fraction \(\mu_t\) of advertisers. The market can be balanced as in the paper, including by its dummy padding. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime only when advertisers really do share complete valuation types; if almost every advertiser has a distinct value vector, the compression disappears.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous Maximum Market-Clearing Price (C-Max-MCP). An instance gives finite sets of advertiser types \(T\) and slot formats \(J\); positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) and supplies \(q_j\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_jq_j=1\); and nonnegative rational values \(v_{tj}\), all encoded in binary. A solution is a price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{J}\) and an allocation of mass \(x_{tj}\ge0\), where
\[ \sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tj}=q_j, \]
and \(x_{tj}>0\) only when format \(j\) maximizes type \(t\)’s utility among the available formats and the outside option:
\[ v_{tj}-p_j=\max\!\left(0,\max_{k\in J}(v_{tk}-p_k)\right). \]
The task is to return the coordinatewise greatest price vector admitting such a clearing allocation, along with an allocation witness. The \(x_{tj}\) are shares of a type’s mass assigned to each format: each advertiser still receives at most one indivisible slot. Rationally scaling the masses gives a replicated finite assignment market, which supports the existence of a greatest price vector through the assignment market’s price lattice.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains unit demand, cardinal values, competitive clearing, and the maximum MCP objective they set out to compute. Only repeated buyers and repeated slot formats are represented by mass. I expect C-Max-MCP to be tractable (Class A) on these inputs: the aggregate assignment welfare problem has a compact transportation formulation over \(\tau\) types and \(|J|\) formats, and its optimal dual prices expose market-clearing prices; the greatest price can be selected from the optimal price set. The paper itself notes a two-LP route to the maximum MCP that is weakly polynomial, so tractability is not a novelty claim. Under the programme’s rules, that known answer does not make the mirror a collision; the continuous-population question still stands.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s maximum-MCP computation, not the paper’s discussion of strategic bidding, Bayesian equilibria, or revenue. Its weakest point is that Theorem 3’s \(m\) counts buyers and goods, not distinct types: its \(O(m^4)\) guarantee does not imply a bound polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(|J|\) for a binary-encoded population. The type-compressed LP supports a polynomial-time answer, but I would not claim the paper’s strongly polynomial algorithm transfers to that representation. The sharper question it leaves open is whether C-Max-MCP has a strongly polynomial algorithm in \(|J|\) and \(\tau\), independent of the mass encodings. A natural next extension is to ask which multi-unit-demand markets preserve the price lattice and tractability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a sound case that defeats Theorem 3’s anchor. It is a named, strongly polynomial result about computing the maximum market-clearing price vector, and that objective survives in a continuous population.

The strongest objection is that the paper’s \(O(m^4)\) bound counts buyers and goods. Its algorithm relies on unweighted Hall deficits, \(|S|-|N(S)|\), and on a buyer crossing an indifference threshold. With type masses and supplies, those become weighted deficits and mass transfers. The theorem’s algorithm and bound therefore do not automatically carry over to a runtime in \(\tau\), the number of goods, and the input bit length. That is a real limit on what the paper establishes.

But it does not defeat the mirror. A market with repeated complete valuation types and rational supplies is a coherent high-multiplicity version of the same unit-demand clearing problem. The aggregate allocation uses type-to-good masses, and the maximum MCP remains the target. The proposed transportation LP makes the computational question well-posed; even if it yields a straightforward answer, the programme explicitly treats that answer as a result, not a reason the question was unworthy. Exact repeated valuation vectors may be restrictive in some ad markets, but repeated cohorts or demand classes offer plausible regimes, and the objection is about a regime’s prevalence, not whether one exists.

So the honest negative case is weak: the discrete algorithm does not transfer directly, but that failure identifies the continuous complexity question rather than ruling it out. I would not count this anchor as defeated.
