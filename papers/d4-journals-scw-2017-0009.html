The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based positive case under your criteria. The paper has named propositions, but none asserts an algorithmic or complexity result.

The closest anchors are Propositions 7 and 8, proved in this paper. Proposition 7 characterizes when \(\tau=0\) is a global Condorcet winner—when
\[ 1+\pi[1-(F(\bar w/\pi)+F(\dot w))]\ge (1+\pi)/2, \]
—and when it is a local Condorcet winner—when
\[ 1-\pi+2\pi[1-F(\bar w/\pi)]\ge (1+\pi)/2, \]
under \(\beta<\bar w/\pi\) and \(\pi>1/3\). Proposition 8 gives conditions for an interior global or local Condorcet winner and characterizes the decisive voter and resulting pattern of family help. These are analytical characterizations of equilibrium outcomes, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise algorithmically classified.

There is a possible computational follow-on: given finite rational support for the productivity distribution, determine whether zero is a global or local Condorcet winner, or compute a Condorcet-winning tax rate. But that would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The population-continuum setting is already explicit in the paper: it models a continuum of families with productivity distributed according to \(F\). So the case for a new *population continuization* is especially weak here. The paper is useful evidence that this economic regime is sensible, and its analysis could motivate complexity work, but it contains no qualifying anchor to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Propositions 7 and 8 characterize majority-voting outcomes in an analytical model: when \(\tau=0\) is a Condorcet winner, and when an interior winner exists. Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

A reasonable attempt to turn Proposition 7 into an anchor would be: given rational masses for a finite set of income types, decide whether \(\tau=0\) is a global or local Condorcet winner. But the paper already gives the relevant conditions in terms of \(F(\bar w/\pi)\) and \(F(\check w)\). The complexity question would come from newly specifying how utilities, thresholds, and the distribution are represented as input—not from continuizing a computational result in the paper.

Proposition 8 has the same problem. One could ask an algorithm to find the equilibrium tax rate and decisive voter from finite income classes. Yet the paper already studies the continuum-population model and characterizes its equilibria. An algorithmic version would need its own input and output conventions, including how to represent utility functions and solve the resulting equations. That could be a worthwhile follow-on, but it is a new computational question, not a mirror of Proposition 8.

There is also a narrow “already continuous” point here: the paper begins with a continuum of families and a productivity distribution \(F\). That is a genuine collision for the population model itself, though not evidence that its complexity has been studied. The paper has no discrete-population computational result whose continuous counterpart remains to be discovered.

I can’t honestly defend the universal claim that no scenario could make related computational work worthwhile. Many families in a small number of income classes would be a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and computing their voting equilibrium could merit study. The negative case is narrower: none of the anchors raised survives as a *continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper*.
