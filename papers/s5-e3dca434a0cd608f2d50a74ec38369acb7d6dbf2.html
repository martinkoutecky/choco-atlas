The continuous mirror question

Given rationally encoded initial opinions \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\in\mathbb{Q}^d\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), output the exact type trajectory through the first fixed round \(T\), including \(T\) and the terminal opinions, under synchronous unit-radius mass-weighted averaging. Determine the output-sensitive bit complexity in \(d\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), including its dependence on the common mass denominator.

The model it lives in

A finite-support opinion measure \(\nu_0=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\delta_{p_i}\); type \(i\) has mass \(\mu_i\) and current opinion \(x_i(t)\), updated to the mass-weighted average of opinions within Euclidean distance \(1\). The task is to compute the exact run through fixation.

The objection that survived

The example with tiny rational mass takes exponentially many rounds in the mass bit length, but printing those rounds already requires exponential output; this does not establish intrinsic continuum-specific hardness.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the general-dimensional convergence bound, its one-dimensional refinement, and the polygonal convergence-time lower bound; it leaves the noisy and heterogeneous-radius variants aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the output-sensitive bit complexity of computing the exact run as a function of \(d\), \(\tau\), the mass denominator, and \(L\)?
  • Can the convergence time or terminal state be computed succinctly without enumerating every round?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a real mirror here: finite-support mass HK, the exact high-multiplicity version of the paper’s dynamics. It keeps the paper’s Euclidean opinions, unit confidence radius, and synchronous discrete rounds. The continuous object is the population mass; opinions and time are not what is being continuized.

An input is a dimension \(d\) and an initial opinion measure
\[ \nu_0=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\delta_{p_i}, \]
where each \(p_i\) is an exactly represented opinion vector and the positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) sum to \(1\). Type \(i\) means “has initial opinion \(p_i\)”; everyone of that type follows the same update. At round \(t\), its opinion becomes
\[ x_i(t+1)= \frac{\sum_{j:\|x_i(t)-x_j(t)\|\le 1}\mu_j x_j(t)} {\sum_{j:\|x_i(t)-x_j(t)\|\le 1}\mu_j}. \]
If types meet at the same point, their masses can be combined for computing later updates. The task, which I’ll call Mass-HK-Run, is to output the per-type trajectory through the first round \(T\) at which every type stops moving, together with \(T\) and the terminal opinions. There is no optimization objective: the output is the exact evolution and its first fixed round. Complexity should be stated in \(d,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), including the masses.

A plausible regime is a large opinion survey or platform population, with millions of participants but relatively few repeated answer profiles across a fixed set of issues. The profile is the type; its mass is its share of respondents. Requiring identical profiles is a real assumption, but discrete survey responses make repeated types credible. The population can be much larger than \(\tau\), while the dynamics remain exactly the paper’s bounded-confidence dynamics.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper: the homogeneous HK system converges in \(\operatorname{poly}(n,d)\) rounds; the proof gives \(O(n^{10}d^2)\). Its mirror is Mass-HK-Run for arbitrary dimension and rational masses. Every such instance has a clone interpretation: if \(D\) is a common denominator of the \(\mu_i\), replace type \(i\) by \(D\mu_i\) identical agents. Their updates exactly match the mass update. Thus the theorem gives a bound polynomial in \(D\) and \(d\). The continuous question is whether one can give an input-sensitive bound, or characterize the run, in \(\tau,d,L\).

For the explicit trajectory task, I expect a Class C boundary in the binary-mass encoding: a tiny positive type can force exponentially many rounds in its bit length. For example, put masses \(q/(2q+1),1/(2q+1),q/(2q+1)\) at opinions \(0,1,2\). The middle type stays at \(1\); each endpoint moves toward it by a factor \(q/(q+1)\) per round until the endpoints become neighbors, after which the system fixes. This takes \(\Theta(q)\) rounds with only three types and \(O(\log q)\)-bit masses. So the clone argument supplies a polynomial bound in population denominator \(D\), but not a polynomial-round guarantee in \(\tau,d,L\). That is a meaningful stopping point for continuization, not an NP-hardness claim about deciding the stopping time. The fixed-time decision problem and approximate-stability variants remain further questions.

Two other anchors sharpen the picture. Theorem 3.1, also proved here, says the one-dimensional system converges within \(O(n^3)\) rounds. Its mirror, Mass-HK-Line-Run, is Mass-HK-Run restricted to \(d=1\). Clone expansion gives \(O(D^3)\) rounds; the three-type example above shows why that does not become a polynomial bound in \(\tau,L\). The question for a prover is whether the dependence on mass granularity can be characterized or improved for exact runs.

Theorem 5.1, proved here, says that the system starting with one agent at each vertex of a regular \(n\)-gon of side length \(1\) takes at least \(\Omega(n^2)\) rounds to fix. Its mirror, Polygon-MassHK-Run, starts with mass \(1/\tau\) at each vertex of a regular \(\tau\)-gon and asks for the exact run through fixation. The same input can represent \(q\) identical agents per vertex for any \(q\), so this is a genuine \(N\gg\tau\) regime and the \(\Omega(\tau^2)\) lower bound transfers exactly. For this equal-mass family, \(D=\tau\), and Theorem 4.4 gives a polynomial round upper bound as well: the round-complexity question looks Class A, with a nontrivial lower bound still to tighten. The polygon coordinates can be given as exact real-algebraic inputs. [The numbered results and bounds are in the paper’s ITCS version.](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/pubs/itcs13.pdf)

The continuous-opinion literature is relevant background, but this mirror asks an input-sensitive computational question: how the exact convergence run depends on the number of opinion types and the encoded mass distribution. That is the part that fits the programme.

The weakest point is also the most important modeling fact: rational-mass HK is exactly clone-equivalent to a finite system, and the paper already tracks integer weights at coincident opinions. The mirror does not introduce a new update law. Its value is to ask for the complexity of that law when a population is represented by support and binary rational shares, and to identify which convergence bounds depend on the hidden denominator. That is a narrow but plausible question the authors should recognize as their problem. These anchors cover homogeneous synchronous convergence in one and higher dimensions and the polygon lower-bound construction; they do not cover the noisy variant or heterogeneous radii.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed Mass-HK-Run task as an exact trajectory problem. For rational masses, choose a common denominator \(D\) and replace each type of mass \(\mu_i\) by \(D\mu_i\) identical agents. This reproduces every update exactly. That equivalence does not by itself disqualify the mirror—high multiplicity is the point—but it exposes what the proposed input measure is changing: the paper bounds rounds in terms of population size, while the mirror asks about a population denominator that may be exponential in its bit length.

The three-type example makes the proposed “Class C” boundary especially weak. With masses \(q/(2q+1),1/(2q+1),q/(2q+1)\), the run lasts \(\Theta(q)\) rounds, but the requested trajectory has \(\Theta(q)\) rounds to print. Exponential time in \(\log q\) is therefore an output-size lower bound, not evidence of continuum-specific computational hardness. The one-dimensional theorem has the same issue. The polygon lower bound transfers to support size \(\tau\), but equal mass at each vertex makes the dynamics identical to the \(\tau\)-agent polygon regardless of how many clones there are. It supplies a lower bound on rounds, not a new high-multiplicity phenomenon.

One could avoid the output-size objection by asking only for the convergence time in binary or the terminal state. Those are plausible questions, but they are no longer the exact-run task proposed here. The paper’s upper bounds come from following the process, and its polygon construction shows that the process can take many rounds; none of those results tells us whether the time or terminal state can be computed succinctly. That might be worth studying, but these anchors do not establish its value.

So the three anchors do not provide three independent cases: they are bounds on the same mass-weighted recurrence, with the line and polygon results as special cases. That is the negative case I can defend against them. It is not a universal refutation, though. A finite-support society with rational masses is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and a succinct query about its convergence time or terminal state could be worthwhile. I cannot honestly rule out every such mirror; the case against is strongest against the proposed exact-run framing and its claimed complexity boundary.
