What the mirror covers

The closest candidates are Theorem 3 on dynamic Pareto efficiency and Theorem 4 on static AS-TTC properties; the paper's other results concern efficiency, acceptability, and incentive compatibility. None is a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: a large university or housing operator assigning recurring room types to large annual cohorts, where many residents share the same two-period preferences and tenure status. In that regime, the number of residents could be large while the number of preference and room types stays modest. Mass would represent a fraction of each type, and aggregate assignments could split type mass across rooms while every individual still receives an indivisible room.

But I cannot give this paper a qualifying anchor under your criteria. Its numbered results concern dynamic Pareto efficiency, acceptability, and incentive compatibility—not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another computational complexity class. The closest foothold is Theorem 3, proved here: in the general preference domain, a constant serial-dictatorship spot rule that favors existing tenants is dynamically Pareto efficient. That is a mechanism property, not a computational result. Theorem 4 is also a mechanism-property result, and the paper attributes it to Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez (1999).

A continuous population version of the housing model might be worth studying, but turning Theorem 3 into a question about aggregate assignments would be a new extension, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The weakest point is exactly that gap: the high-multiplicity scenario is credible, but the paper supplies no computational result for the continuous mirror to explain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The high-multiplicity setting is credible: a large housing operator could have many residents in recurring preference and room types. Letting room supply scale with the population and using type-level average assignments would be a reasonable co-modification. That does not, however, give the paper a computational result to mirror.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 3. It establishes that a constant serial dictatorship favoring existing tenants selects a dynamically Pareto-efficient plan. Its continuous counterpart would ask whether that efficiency property survives for a nonatomic population. That is a question about a mechanism’s welfare properties, not its computational complexity. The proof’s induction over a common priority ordering is also not an algorithmic bottleneck: with a finite description of the types and horizon, applying the priority rule is direct. An infinite horizon would require its own finite input representation; continuizing the population does not provide one.

Theorem 4 is no stronger as an anchor. It states familiar properties of the static AS-TTC rule and is cited from prior work; those properties likewise do not pose a complexity question. A project on computing aggregate assignments or testing efficiency could be worthwhile, but it would need a new computational target and modeling choices beyond these theorems. The proponent has made a good case that the population regime is sensible, not that this paper’s results supply that target.

So the negative case is strong on the missing computational anchor and weaker on the universal claim. One could formulate an interesting computational problem adjacent to the paper, but that would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of its named results.
