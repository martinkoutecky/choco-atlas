The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible anchors in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Table 2 reports experimental accuracy, false-negative rates, and AUC for voting and machine-learning methods; those are empirical findings, not named complexity results. So there is no honest anchor to quote, restate, or designate as the lead.

The strongest positive case I can make is a candidate mirror of the paper’s crowdsourcing-based logistic-regression pipeline, explicitly not an anchor. Consider repeated image-screening tasks with many annotators but a relatively small number \(\tau\) of stable response types. A type records its elicitation behavior on the image categories in the task, including its binary label, guess of the majority, and normalized confidence. For each training image \(i\), let \(\mu^i_t\) be the fraction of its annotators of type \(t\), and let \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) be its known label.

From those masses, calculate the paper’s four aggregate features: the fraction labeling the target absent, the fraction guessing the majority will label it absent, and the mean confidence among those labeling it present and absent. Given these feature vectors \(z_i\), one precise problem is: for rational \(\lambda>0\) and accuracy tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a logistic classifier whose regularized empirical logistic loss is within \(\varepsilon\) of the minimum over its coefficients, and use it to classify future images from the same task. Its action is the classifier; its objective is fit to the labeled training images. This preserves the paper’s use of confidence and majority guesses as features, while replacing individual annotator records with population masses.

A plausible regime is a large, repeated screening campaign—such as image review for medical or geospatial applications—with many annotations per image and recurring annotator behavior, so \(N\), the number of annotators, is much larger than \(\tau\). Authors could recognize this as a population version of their aggregation-and-classification pipeline. The continuous problem should be tractable by convex optimization. But this is a proposed extension, not a result the paper establishes; the paper does not analyze its complexity or claim that its discrete pipeline is hard.

The mirror would cover the paper’s use of elicited crowd inputs in machine-learning classifiers, including the reported comparisons in Table 2. It would not cover the synthetic image generator or the ResNet-34 baseline. Its weakest point is the type assumption: confidence and guesses can vary substantially between workers and across images. Unless types capture those differences while remaining few in number, \(\tau\) may grow with \(N\), defeating the high-multiplicity regime. And replacing sampled annotators with exact masses removes sampling variation that matters to the paper’s empirical questions. Thus there is a plausible population model here, but no qualifying named computational result to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named result to anchor a mirror to: the paper gives empirical comparisons of voting and machine learning methods, not a theorem about computational complexity or an algorithmic guarantee. The proposed logistic-regression problem is an extension of those experiments, as its proponent acknowledges. It asks what happens after crowd responses have been converted into features; it does not continue a computational result the paper establishes.

The strongest setting for that extension is a repeated, large-scale screening campaign with many annotations per image. In that setting, fractions of annotators reporting each kind of response are sensible quantities. But the paper’s inputs depend on workers’ image-specific judgments, confidence calibration, guesses, and assignment histories. A type that preserves those differences is a full response policy across tasks, so there is little reason to expect a small, stable support. Coarsening workers into a few reliability profiles could make the support manageable, but then the model needs assumptions about how each profile generates responses. That turns the proposal toward statistical modeling of crowd behavior, a question the paper does not formulate.

Even granting the type model, fitting logistic regression to the resulting aggregate features makes the population masses inputs to a conventional learning problem; it does not make the population itself the computational object. To study prediction on future images, one would also need a model for the image distribution and for how crowd responses arise on those images. Those are meaningful questions, but they are not mirrors of the paper’s reported accuracy and false-negative-rate comparisons. Treating the MMV rule or threshold adjustment similarly would yield a mass-based aggregation variant, not a complexity result in the paper.

That is the strongest negative case: the paper offers no qualifying computational result, and the plausible population extension shifts the subject toward statistical learning and response modeling. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A recurring campaign with many annotations per image and genuinely stable annotator types could support a sensible continuous-population study. I would call the no-anchor verdict strong, but the broader “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” case weak.
