The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), candidate stubborn types \(C\), rational interaction rates \(K_{uv}\), truth \(\theta\), fixed stubborn mean \(\eta\), and rational update parameters \(q\in(0,1)\) and \(\delta\), hold each candidate type \(s\in C\) at mean \(\eta\) and let responsive type \(v\) update by \(m_v[k+1]=q\bigl(m_v[k]+\delta\sum_{u\in T}\mu_uK_{uv}(m_u[k]-m_v[k])\bigr)+(1-q)\theta\). Assuming the responsive update matrix for each \(s\) has spectral radius below \(1\), compute its limiting mean vector \(\gamma^{(s)}\) and score \(D_s=\sum_{v\ne s}\mu_v|\gamma_v^{(s)}-\theta|\), then return a maximum-score candidate.

The model it lives in

Types are agent roles with masses \(\mu_t\); \(K_{uv}\) is a per-capita influence kernel whose aggregate effect scales with source mass. Pin a candidate stubborn type’s mean to \(\eta\), compute responsive type-level limiting means under the linear update, and report their mass-weighted deviation scores \(D_s\).

The objection that survived

The paper fixes one stubborn individual and does not define the scalar \(D_s\) or rank candidate blocs; those choices extend its centrality interpretation.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of choosing several stubborn types under a mass or budget constraint?

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Its theorems concern convergence, and its experiments are simulations. The closest anchor, and the one I would build on, is Theorem 4 (Convergence with stubborn agents), proved in this paper. It says that if \(\rho(A_{-1,-1})<1\), the expected means of the non-stubborn agents converge to \(\gamma=(I_{N-1}-A_{-1,-1})^{-1}(B_{-1,-1}\mathds{1}_{N-1}\theta+A_{-1,1}\mu^\dagger)\), with \(A=\Sigma(I+\delta_\mu W-\delta_\mu D)\) and \(B=I-\Sigma\). The authors interpret this limiting value as a measure of how much the stubborn agent can disrupt information flow. This is a useful analytic foundation for a computational mirror, but it is not itself a complexity result.

The strongest prospective mirror is Continuous Stubborn-Bloc Centrality. Consider a large platform or political information network with a small number \(\tau\) of interaction roles and millions of accounts or people. A type records everything relevant to the model: its belief parameters, whether its members are responsive or stubborn, and its interaction rates with every other type. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\). The scenario is plausible where platform design or organized communities create large cohorts with similar exposure and interaction patterns. A stubborn type represents a coordinated or otherwise persistently committed bloc, rather than a single person whose mass would vanish in a population limit.

For a precise instance, take a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a set \(C\) of candidate stubborn types, rational source-to-receiver interaction rates \(K_{uv}\), a rational truth \(\theta\), a fixed stubborn mean \(\eta\), and common rational update parameters \(q\in(0,1)\) and \(\delta\). Here \(K_{uv}\) is the rate at which an individual of type \(u\) influences a receiver of type \(v\); the source’s population mass scales its aggregate effect. Fixing a Gaussian-mixture component, let the expected mean \(m_v[k]\) of a responsive type evolve by \(m_v[k+1]=q\bigl(m_v[k]+\delta\sum_{u\in T}\mu_uK_{uv}(m_u[k]-m_v[k])\bigr)+(1-q)\theta\), while a selected type \(s\in C\) remains fixed at \(\eta\). This is the paper’s mean update with the finite graph replaced by type masses and an interaction kernel; its components can be handled separately as in Theorem 4.

For each candidate \(s\), require the resulting responsive-type update matrix \(A^{(s)}\) to satisfy \(\rho(A^{(s)})<1\). Let \(\gamma^{(s)}\) be its limiting expected means, obtained by solving \((I-A^{(s)})\gamma^{(s)}=c^{(s)}\), where \(c^{(s)}\) contains the truth-signal term and the influence of the stubborn type. Define its disruption score as \(D_s=\sum_{v\ne s}\mu_v|\gamma_v^{(s)}-\theta|\). The task is to return the candidate type \(s\) with largest \(D_s\), together with its score and equilibrium vector. This makes the action—choosing which bloc to hold stubborn—and the objective—maximizing the mass-weighted deviation among responsive agents—explicit. It computes a type-level version of the centrality the authors propose.

I expect this problem to be tractable: for rational inputs, each candidate’s equilibrium comes from a linear system of dimension at most \(\tau\), and testing all candidates takes polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. That complexity conclusion is a deduction for the proposed problem, not a result claimed in the paper. Further questions include whether one can choose several stubborn blocs under a mass budget, and whether a continuous-type or infinite-support interaction model admits comparably efficient exact or approximate algorithms.

The mirror covers Section 4’s stubborn-agent equilibrium and centrality interpretation, not the paper’s full Gaussian-mixture model or its non-stubborn convergence results. The positive case rests on a natural population scaling of the stubborn influencer and on Theorem 4’s explicit equilibrium formula. Its weakest point is also clear: the paper studies one stubborn node in a finite graph, while this mirror gives positive mass to a stubborn bloc and asks for a centrality ranking across possible blocs. That extension seems recognizable as a population version of their disruption question, but a strict reading of the requested anchor criterion still leaves this as a promising mirror without a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent already concedes: Theorem 4 is not a computational result. It gives the limiting expected means for a specified stubborn agent by solving a fixed-point equation. It states no complexity, algorithmic, or optimization result. The other numbered theorems concern convergence as well. So this paper supplies no computational anchor for ChoCo to classify.

The proposed *Continuous Stubborn-Bloc Centrality* is a coherent follow-on, and a population of accounts with repeated interaction roles is plausible. But it adds the central computational choice: selecting a stubborn bloc to maximize \(D_s\). The paper fixes one stubborn agent and interprets the resulting equilibrium as a centrality measure; it never asks which agent or bloc to choose. With finitely many types, evaluating each proposed bloc amounts to solving a \(\tau\)-dimensional linear system. Adding a budgeted search over blocs could make a richer problem, but that would be a new intervention problem whose combinatorics and objective need their own justification.

There is also a limit to how much of the paper’s setting the mass model preserves. Its influence depends on positions and weighted links in a named-agent network. Exact aggregation works for structured networks with repeated interaction roles; on a generic weighted graph, agents may need separate types, leaving \(\tau\) close to \(N\). A single stubborn hub presents a sharper issue: its influence can come from its links even as its population share tends to zero. Giving a whole type positive mass models a stubborn bloc; retaining the hub as an exceptional atom preserves the individual influencer, but leaves the proposed computational question to be defined.

That is a case against this paper as a continuous computational mirror, not proof that no useful research can grow out of its model. The negative case is weakest against the bloc interpretation: it is sensible, and a later paper could define a worthwhile optimization problem around it. The present paper itself, though, gives no named computational problem for that mirror to continue.
