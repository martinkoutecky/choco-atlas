What the mirror covers

The proposed block-detection question concerns the Aubin-core and linear-cost-share-equilibrium connection in Theorems 4.2 and 4.5; it leaves the other equilibrium characterizations and the atomless-economy results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the full-support characterization yield an exact polynomial-time feasibility algorithm for rational polyhedral improvement sets, including strict gains and positive participation?
  • How does the complexity change with the number of types and with richer encoded preference representations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper’s economic model already has a natural high-multiplicity form. In a large municipality considering public projects, let a type be a complete combination of preferences over projects and private goods and of initial endowment. If \(N\) households fall into \(\tau\) such types, with \(N\gg\tau\), type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Normalize resources and project costs per person; the paper’s finite-to-continuum construction does this by scaling a project cost \(c\) to \(c/n\). The authors themselves say they interpret finite economies as continuum economies with finitely many distinguishable agent characteristics.

There is, however, a limit on the case I can make under your anchor rule: the paper has no named computational result asserting a problem is tractable, hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The closest anchor is economic rather than computational. Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper, says that for a finite economy \(E\), any contribution measure \(\sigma\), and its corresponding cost distribution \(\varphi_\sigma\), the Aubin \(\sigma\)-core equals the set of linear cost share equilibria: \(C^A_\sigma(E)=LCE_{\varphi_\sigma}(E)\). That is a full characterization, but it gives no algorithm or complexity bound. Theorem 3.8, cited from Diamantaras and Gilles, gives the analogous core–equilibrium equality for atomless economies. So the continuum population is already part of the paper’s economics; what is absent is computational analysis of it.

The best *candidate* computational mirror, therefore, is type-mass Aubin-block detection. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, type utilities \(u_t\), endowments \(\omega_t\), project costs \(c(z)\), a fixed cost-share density \(\varphi_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t\varphi_t=1\), and a feasible allocation \((x_t,y)\). Ask whether there is a project \(z\), participation fractions \(\gamma_t\in[0,1]\), and bundles \(g_t\) such that every participating type strictly prefers its new bundle and

\[ \sum_t \mu_t\gamma_t g_t+ \left(\sum_t\mu_t\gamma_t\varphi_t\right)c(z) \leq \sum_t\mu_t\gamma_t\omega_t. \]

A “yes” solution is the project, participation rates, and bundles witnessing a block; core membership asks whether no such witness exists. Equivalently, one can maximize the common utility gain among participating types and ask whether it can be positive. This preserves the paper’s coalition, cost-sharing, and preference structure rather than making only the public project or outcomes continuous.

The prospect of tractability is the positive computational argument: for a fixed project and gain threshold, quasiconcavity makes each type’s better-than set convex. With \(a_t=\mu_t\gamma_t\) and \(q_t=a_tg_t\), the resource constraint is linear and the utility conditions use perspectives of those convex sets. That suggests convex-feasibility or separation methods, hence a provisional Class A direction when utilities have suitable separation oracles. It is not a result of this paper, and the exact treatment of strict improvement and positive participation needs a finite input model. Theorem 4.5, proved here, helps motivate the formulation: under its assumptions, the finite economy’s Aubin \(\sigma\)-core is characterized by the absence of blocks from full-support Aubin coalitions. Further questions include the bit complexity of the utility representation, dependence on \(\tau\), and whether equilibrium recognition can be reduced to the same convex program.

This candidate covers the paper’s Aubin-core and linear-cost-share-equilibrium connection, especially Theorem 4.2; it does not mirror every result. Its weakest point is also clear: Theorem 4.2 is not a computational anchor, and the paper has already formulated atomless economies and their equilibrium characterizations. The positive claim is therefore that this is a credible computational continuation of the authors’ continuous-population model—not that the paper already contains a complexity result for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 4.2 characterizes linear cost share equilibria as Aubin-core allocations; Theorem 4.5 reduces the blocking test to full-support coalitions. Theorems 5.3 and 6.3 give further characterizations. None states an algorithm, a complexity bound, or even a computational problem with an input encoding. The model allows an abstract project set \(Y\), general continuous utilities, and integrable allocations, so it supplies no input length \(L\) for a complexity question. A computational study of these results would begin a new research thread rather than continue a computational result in this paper.

The proposed type-mass blocking problem is the strongest candidate for that thread, but its computational content depends on assumptions the paper does not make. Fix a finite project set and represent each type’s preferences with rational polyhedral better-than sets, for example. Setting \(a_t=\mu_t\gamma_t\) and \(q_t=a_tg_t\) makes the blocking resource constraint linear:
\[ \sum_t q_t+\left(\sum_t a_t\varphi_t\right)c(z)\leq\sum_t a_t\omega_t. \]
For a fixed project, the type-level improvement constraints are perspective constraints over convex sets; with polyhedral representations, the full-support block test can be formulated as an LP. Theorem 4.5 supplies the full-support characterization under its assumptions. That is a plausible computational exercise, but its tractability comes from the chosen preference encoding. Leaving preferences and \(Y\) as in the paper leaves complexity undefined; enriching their encodings can make the algorithmic difficulty about those choices rather than the continuum population.

There is no honest basis for claiming that a high-multiplicity regime is nonsensical. A municipality with many households in a modest number of preference-and-endowment types is plausible, and the paper itself studies atomless economies and constructs a continuum representation of finite economies. That analytic work does not answer the computational question, so I would not call it a collision. It does mean that the proposed project is computationalization of an already continuous economic model, rather than a continuization of a named computational result.

So the negative case is strongest against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: it has no computational anchor, and its best candidate requires a new, consequential specification of the input model. It is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile project could arise: the household scenario is credible, and an algorithmic study could be worth doing on its own terms.
