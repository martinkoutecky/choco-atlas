The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered computational complexity result: none of its theorems, lemmas, propositions, or corollaries classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard. The nearest candidate is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It says that, in a regular two-agent problem, the multimatum—one agent proposes a closed set of outcomes, the other accepts an outcome or counters with a set of at least as large a measure, and the first agent then chooses from that set—fully implements the compromise solutions in subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium. That is an implementation result, not a computational result.

There is also a scope problem: the paper makes the outcome space continuous while keeping the society at two agents. That alone is outside ChoCo’s population-continuization scope. The strongest plausible positive case needs a new constituency scenario.

Consider two large political blocs negotiating over a policy in a compact, full-dimensional rational polytope \(X\subseteq\mathbb R^d\). Each citizen has one of finitely many policy-preference types \(t\in T\), represented by a nonconstant rational affine utility \(u_t\). Let \(\nu\) be normalized Lebesgue measure on \(X\). Bloc \(i\) is described by rational type masses \(\mu^i_t\), summing to one. Its population may be large while its support size \(\tau\) is small: the members of each type share the same preference, as high multiplicity requires.

For a type \(t\), define its canonical utility at policy \(x\) by
\[ v_t(x)=\nu\bigl(\{y\in X:u_t(y)\le u_t(x)\}\bigr). \]
Let each bloc’s policy preference be represented by its average canonical utility,
\[ V_i^\mu(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu^i_t v_t(x). \]
The Population-Compromise Threshold problem is: given \(X\), the type utilities, the two rational mass distributions, and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether
\[ \max_{x\in X}\min\{V_1^\mu(x),V_2^\mu(x)\}\ge q. \]
A “yes” instance has a policy attaining at least \(q\); a “no” instance does not. The associated strategic question is whether running the multimatum with these bloc preferences and the paper’s measure-based menu constraint implements the resulting compromise. If the aggregate preferences satisfy the paper’s regularity assumptions, Theorem 2 is a natural basis for that implementation claim.

This is a plausible instance regime: parties negotiate on behalf of large constituencies with relatively few shared preference types, rather than on behalf of two isolated individuals. But it is a proposed extension, not a result the paper proves. My tentative expectation for the threshold problem is continuum-specific difficulty in variable dimension, since evaluating the canonical utilities entails exact volume calculations; fixed-dimensional or more structured cases may be tractable. That is a research guess, not an anchor-backed hardness claim.

The weakest point is the change in the paper’s agents. Its two players have their own preferences; the mirror makes them representatives of blocs whose preferences are constructed by averaging constituents’ canonical utilities. That is credible in a political-party setting, but authors could reasonably object that the aggregation rule changes the problem. The mirror covers only the compromise and implementation result around Theorem 2. Theorem 1’s common-cardinalization characterization, and the paper’s application-specific comparative statics, do not become computational anchors through this construction.

So this is the best positive scenario I can defend, but it does not satisfy the requested anchor condition: Theorem 2 is relevant implementation theory, not a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no numbered computational result to carry over. Theorem 2 proves that the multimatum implements the compromise in subgame-perfect equilibrium. It gives no algorithm, complexity classification, finite input encoding, or running-time question. Its mechanism ranges over all nonempty closed subsets of \(X\), so the proof’s compactness and equilibrium arguments do not specify a computational procedure.

The proposed two-bloc scenario is the strongest candidate, and its mass distributions are a sensible high-multiplicity model. But it adds a substantive aggregation rule: each bloc’s preference is built from its members’ canonical utilities. In the proposed threshold problem, the objective is the minimum of the blocs’ *average* canonical utilities. The paper’s compromise instead canonicalizes each bargainer’s preference using \(\nu\). If a bloc’s aggregate utility is \(V_i\), the paper’s criterion would use
\[ \nu\bigl(\{y\in X:V_i(y)\le V_i(x)\}\bigr), \]
which generally differs from averaging members’ canonical utilities. Correcting the threshold to use the bloc-level canonical utilities yields a coherent extension, but the paper does not specify the aggregation that creates \(V_i\).

That corrected problem may be worth studying. Its computational content comes from the added population aggregation and the resulting optimization over \(X\). Theorem 2 applies afterward as a general implementation result for regular preferences; it says nothing about the complexity of finding the compromise or computing those preferences. Treating the voters themselves as the multimatum players would also require a new mechanism, since the paper’s protocol is specifically bilateral. Replacing the measure of outcome menus with population masses would change what the measure means.

So I would reject Theorem 2 as a computational mirror anchor. The bloc formulation is plausible, but the paper alone cannot establish that no such extension would be worthwhile; an author might recognize it as a natural new problem. The negative case is strongest if “mirror” means a population continuization of a computational result in this paper. It is weaker if a new computational problem built around the paper’s compromise concept counts.
