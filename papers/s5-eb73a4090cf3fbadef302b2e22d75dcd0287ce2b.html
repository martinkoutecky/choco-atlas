The continuous mirror question

Given τ bidder types with rational shares μ_t>0 summing to 1, and rational per-capita values v_t>0 and budget impact factors α_t>0, choose a common scaled allocation q_t≥0 for each type, with A=∑_t μ_t q_t≤1. In the atomless limit, each type-t agent has value v_t q_t and budget α_t A. Compute an allocation maximizing ∑_t μ_t min{v_t q_t,α_t A} and its optimal value.

The model it lives in

A divisible item of unit supply serves a population with type distribution μ. A type-t agent receives physical allocation q_t/N in an N-agent approximation, has value N v_t for the item, and has budget impact factor α_t. In the limit, the decision variables are the type allocations q_t, total per-capita supply is A=∑_t μ_t q_t≤1, each agent's budget is α_t A, and average liquid welfare is ∑_t μ_t min{v_t q_t,α_t A}. An epigraph LP computes an optimum.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: a large-market version of the paper’s liquid-welfare allocation problem, not a continuous version of its truthful mechanism.

The anchor is the Section 3 theorem labeled thm:opt-allocation. The supplied text prints it simply as “Theorem,” without a numeral, so I won’t invent one. It is proved in the paper, in Appendix A.1. Its content is that, for bidders ordered by decreasing valuation, the algorithm that gives bidder \(i\) up to \(\alpha_i/(v_i+\alpha_i)\) of the item, in that order, and assigns any remaining amount to a bidder with minimum \(\alpha_i\), returns an allocation maximizing liquid welfare. The paper proves the allocation is optimal; it does not state a formal “in P” theorem, though the displayed algorithm is polynomial in the number of bidders and uses basic arithmetic.

Here is a continuous population problem anchored on that result. There are \(\tau\) bidder types \(t\), each specified by a normalized value \(v_t>0\) and budget impact factor \(\alpha_t>0\). The input gives their population shares \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and binary rational \(v_t,\alpha_t\). Choose a common per-capita allocation \(q_t\ge 0\) for each type, with average allocation \(A=\sum_t\mu_tq_t\le 1\). Since an individual has zero mass in the continuum, their budget is \(B_t=\alpha_t A\). The objective is to maximize
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\min\{v_tq_t,\alpha_t A\}. \]
A solution is an allocation vector \(q\) attaining the maximum and the optimal welfare value. I’d call this Continuum Liquid-Welfare Allocation.

It has a direct large-population interpretation. Take \(N\) bidders, with type shares approaching \(\mu\), and let a type-\(t\) bidder’s value for the whole item be \(Nv_t\). If their physical allocation is \(x_i=q_i/N\), then the paper’s budget is
\[ \alpha_i\sum_{j\ne i}x_j=\alpha_i\left(A_N-\frac{q_i}{N}\right), \qquad A_N=\frac1N\sum_jq_j. \]
The value of their allocation is \(v_tq_i\). As \(N\) grows, the self-allocation term in the budget vanishes, leaving \(\alpha_t A\). Dividing total liquid welfare by \(N\) gives the population-average objective above. This normalization treats one \(1/N\) share of the item as a per-capita unit.

The regime could be a large platform auctioning a divisible pool of advertising inventory to many small advertisers. Advertisers with the same value per per-capita share and the same response to competitors’ allocations have the same complete type \((v_t,\alpha_t)\). There might be millions of advertisers but only dozens or hundreds of such types. The support size \(\tau\), rather than the number of possible types, is the relevant input size.

This mirror is tractable: introduce \(z_t\) for each type and maximize \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t\), subject to \(A=\sum_t\mu_tq_t\), \(A\le1\), \(q_t,z_t\ge0\), \(z_t\le v_tq_t\), and \(z_t\le\alpha_tA\). That is an LP with \(O(\tau)\) variables and constraints, so exact optimization is polynomial in the rational input size. This is a Class A expectation for the stated mirror, not a complexity claim made by the paper. It also echoes the theorem’s structure: with full allocation, type \(t\)’s welfare saturates at \(\alpha_t\), and its efficient allocation threshold is \(q_t=\alpha_t/v_t\), the limit of \(N\alpha_t/(Nv_t+\alpha_t)\).

The scope is only the optimal allocation result. I would not claim that this mirrors the paper’s \(1/3\)-approximation truthful mechanism or its \(1/2\) impossibility bound: those depend on individual reports and individual allocations, whose effects change in an atomless population. Follow-up questions include the finite-\(N\) error in the LP approximation, and what incentive guarantee—if any—survives for mechanisms in this population model.

The weakest point is substantial: at full allocation, \(A=1\), so every type’s continuum budget is simply \(\alpha_t\); the individual-allocation feedback central to the finite model disappears. The mirror preserves the type distribution, liquid-welfare objective, and allocation problem, but it blunts that feedback. If the authors regard the self-allocation effect as essential to their problem, they may reject this as their continuous analogue. The positive case is that the finite scaling above derives the limit from their own budget formula, and the paper’s optimal-allocation theorem supplies a concrete algorithmic anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is the Section 3 optimal-allocation theorem. Its continuous mirror has a basic problem: in the large-population limit, the paper’s defining budget externality disappears at any welfare-maximizing allocation.

With \(N\) bidders and type-\(t\) allocation \(x_i=q_t/N\), write \(A_N=\sum_t\mu_tq_t\). A bidder’s budget is
\[ B_i=\alpha_t\left(A_N-\frac{q_t}{N}\right). \]
The paper’s allocation algorithm uses the whole item, and its proof shows that some optimum always does. Thus \(A_N=1\), and as \(N\) grows each type’s budget tends to \(\alpha_t\), independent of how the item is divided. The budget impact is no longer allocation-induced in the relevant optimization problem.

The proponent’s value scaling, \(v_i=Nv_t\), keeps each bidder’s value for their share finite. But then the limiting objective is simply
\[ \max_{\sum_t\mu_tq_t=1}\ \sum_t\mu_t\min\{v_tq_t,\alpha_t\}. \]
That is a separable allocation problem with fixed per-type caps: give resource to higher-value types until their caps bind. The theorem’s special allocation to a minimum-\(\alpha\) bidder becomes unnecessary; leftover allocation can go anywhere once the caps are met. This is a legitimate LP, but it no longer studies the paper’s central interaction between one bidder’s allocation and other bidders’ budgets.

Keeping valuations fixed does not preserve that interaction either. Each bidder’s value for their \(1/N\)-scale share tends to zero, while their budget stays finite, so the budget cap ceases to bind and the aggregate objective reduces to ordinary welfare. Intermediate value scalings change whether the cap matters, but not the vanishing self-allocation term. Rescaling \(\alpha_t\) cannot keep both the budget and that term finite: if \(\alpha_t A_N\) stays finite, then \(\alpha_t q_t/N\) vanishes; scaling \(\alpha_t\) enough to retain the latter makes the former grow with \(N\).

One could preserve richer feedback by making budgets depend on allocations by type, or by treating positive-mass groups as single bidders. Either changes the externality rule or gives up the atomless population interpretation. The first is a new model rather than a limit forced by this paper; the second retains a finite set of strategic blocs rather than a high-multiplicity society.

So my case against the anchor is that its plausible continuous versions either lose the externality or reduce it to fixed budgets. That is the strongest objection available, but it is not airtight: the proposed LP is well-posed, and ChoCo’s standard allows a useful mirror even when the answer is simple. If the authors regard the fixed-budget limit itself as their problem, this anchor survives.
