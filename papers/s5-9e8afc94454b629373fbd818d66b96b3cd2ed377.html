The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of man types \(A\) and woman types \(B\), rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), a strict partial order over counterpart types for each type, and a rational threshold \(r\), decide whether the maximum entropy score over stable mass couplings is at least \(r\). A coupling \(\pi\) has marginals \(\mu\) and \(\nu\); it is stable if, for every \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\), \(\left(\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}\pi_{ab'}\right)\left(\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}\pi_{a'b}\right)=0\).

The model it lives in

The decision variable is a nonnegative mass coupling \(\pi=(\pi_{ab})\) with row sums \(\mu\) and column sums \(\nu\), subject to the type-level no-blocking condition in the mirror question. Its score is \(F(\pi)=\sum_{a\in A}\mu_a\log\mu_a+\sum_{b\in B}\nu_b\log\nu_b-\sum_{a\in A,b\in B}\pi_{ab}\log\pi_{ab}\), with \(0\log 0=0\). This is the candidate exponential rate of the fraction of labeled matchings that are stable when the type populations are replicated.

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror continues Theorem 2’s exact stable-matching count through its population-scale entropy rate. It leaves Theorem 1, Corollary 3, and the paper’s other finite-market results without continuous counterparts.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the threshold problem in the number of types and the input bit length, and does its feasible region admit an efficient optimization formulation?
  • Does the maximum entropy score equal the limsup of \(q^{-1}\log(S_q/q!)\) for every rational type distribution under the stated replication and preference lifting?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow, but credible. The paper’s setting has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large matching market run by a public service or a large firm, where thousands of managers and projects fall into a fixed set of categories. Members of each category share the same partial order over counterpart categories, and the number of people is much larger than the number of types. The paper’s project-manager example points toward this scenario. The type assumption is substantive, but it is exactly the grouping that makes high multiplicity meaningful.

There is an important limit on the anchors: the paper states no named complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The closest anchor is Theorem 2, an exact counting result stated in this paper, with its proof omitted rather than attributed as a result from elsewhere. For a market of size \(n\), it expresses the number of stable matchings by inclusion–exclusion over subsets of blocking-event tuples. In the paper’s notation, \(\mathcal F_s^k\) contains the subsets \(A\) of size \(s\) that use at most one tuple from each \(A_{i,j}\), and whose associated bipartite graph \(G_A\) has maximum degree one and \(k\) edges. The theorem gives
\[ |\sigma(M,W;P)|=n!+\sum_{s=1}^{n(n-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^s(n-k)!\,|\mathcal F_s^k(M,W;P)|. \]
Each such subset contributes the signed number of matchings containing its \(k\) required edges. This is a genuine exact-counting anchor, though not a complexity classification or an efficient algorithm.

My lead mirror is Stable-Matching Entropy Threshold. An instance has finite sets \(A\) and \(B\) of man and woman types, rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) on those sets, and for each type a partial order over counterpart types. For every scale \(q\) that makes all masses integral, form a market with \(q\mu_a\) men of type \(a\) and \(q\nu_b\) women of type \(b\). Lift each type order to the individuals in its counterpart classes, making members of the same class incomparable. Let \(S_q\) be the number of stable bijections in this market. Given a rational threshold \(r\), ask whether
\[ H=\limsup_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{q}\log\!\left(\frac{S_q}{q!}\right)\ge r. \]
The solution is the yes/no answer, with an optimizing mass coupling as a witness on the yes side.

The coupling \(\pi_{ab}\) is the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(b\); its row and column sums must be \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). It is stable if, for every type pair \(a,b\),
\[ \left(\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}\pi_{ab'}\right) \left(\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}\pi_{a'b}\right)=0. \]
Both factors being positive would leave positive mass on each side that strictly prefers the proposed pair. The count \(S_q\) groups by integral coupling matrices \(x\): a stable matrix contributes \(\frac{\prod_a(q\mu_a)!\prod_b(q\nu_b)!}{\prod_{a,b}x_{ab}!}\) individual matchings. This motivates the continuous objective
\[ H=\max_{\pi\ \mathrm{stable}} \left( \sum_a\mu_a\log\mu_a+ \sum_b\nu_b\log\nu_b- \sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}\log\pi_{ab} \right), \]
with \(0\log 0=0\). So the continuous object is the population distribution, the decision variable is a mass transport, and the objective is the per-capita logarithmic abundance of stable matchings relative to all bijections.

I would treat this as a Class C candidate, not a hardness claim: the stability conditions retain type-pair disjunctions even after the population is continuized, so the combinatorics may live in the support size \(\tau\), not in the number of replicated people. Whether those disjunctions admit a compact convex description is an immediate further question; so are approximation algorithms and parameterized algorithms in \(\tau\). If a convex description exists, entropy optimization could instead give a Class A result.

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s exact stable-matching count. It does not claim to continue Theorem 1 or Corollary 3’s shape-dependent bounds, or the paper’s extremal question over all size-\(n\) markets.

The weak point is real: Theorem 2 counts finite bijections, while this mirror replaces raw count by its asymptotic normalized logarithm. That normalization is a reasoned way to make a population-scale count meaningful, but the paper does not propose it. The authors may see the mass-transport entropy problem as a new question about their model rather than the continuous form of their exact-counting result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the boundary between the paper’s results and the proposed target. Theorem 2 gives an exact inclusion–exclusion formula for the number of stable matchings. It does not give an algorithm or classify the complexity of computing that number. Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 give finite-market bounds. So the paper supplies a quantity to study, but no named computational result whose continuous counterpart is already clear.

Multiplicity itself is not a persuasive objection. A large matching market with many agents in a small number of preference categories is plausible, and work on typed stable-matching problems treats that regime as meaningful.[Meeks and Rastegari](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109)

The harder objection is that the continuum does not inherit the paper’s count. The paper counts labeled bijections. A continuous population has mass couplings, and counting those directly does not yield the same kind of finite number. Replicating types \(q\) times and taking a normalized logarithmic limit is a sensible repair, but it changes the target. The proposed \(H\) measures the exponential rate of the fraction of labeled matchings that are stable; its objective is \(-\min I(A;B)\) over stable couplings. It discards subexponential parts of the count and reduces the problem to how much dependence stability forces between the two agents’ types.

That makes Stable-Matching Entropy Threshold a plausible new computational question. It does not make it a direct continuation of Theorem 2’s exact count. Counting distinct mass couplings would avoid labeled permutations, but then the object is a set of aggregate assignments rather than the paper’s stable matchings. Keeping the exact finite count retains the replication parameter \(q\). The entropy limit is the strongest bridge, and its choice is motivated rather than arbitrary; still, it is a substantive modelling step.

I would therefore argue against presenting this paper as a clear continuization-programme win: its only plausible anchor is an exact-count formula, and the strongest mirror shifts the quantity being studied. But that objection does not defeat every worthwhile mirror. The type regime is credible, and the entropy question survives as a coherent computational problem. The honest negative case is weak precisely there: I cannot support the universal claim that no scenario makes a continuous mirror worth the programme’s time.
