What the mirror covers

The proposed computation covers Proposition 3’s most informative interval equilibrium and the induced common platforms; it leaves the other equilibrium and welfare comparisons aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a qualifying anchor under your rules: the paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are equilibrium and welfare characterizations.

The closest positive foothold is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. It characterizes the most informative interval-partition equilibrium when the expert gives policy advocacy and later endorsements are influential whenever credible. There is a finite maximum number \(N^*(b_e)\) of intervals: advocacy is uninformative when \(b_e>1/3\), and informative otherwise. The proposition gives \(N^*\) and the interval cutoffs in cases determined by \(\lfloor 1/(4b_e)\rfloor\) and its remainder. Together with Lemma 3, it determines the parties’ common platform after each interval. This is a constructive analytical result, but the paper does not state or analyze a computational problem.

If one broadened the anchor requirement, a nearby problem would be: given rational \(b_e\), compute a compact description of the most informative equilibrium’s interval partition and platforms. Proposition 3 supplies the answer directly, so the compact-output version looks tractable. But that would be a computational question we formulate from the paper’s characterization, not a complexity result the paper proves.

There is also a collision problem for a continuous mirror: the paper already models voters as a unit mass with an atomless ideology distribution \(G\). A proposed society whose type is voter ideology \(b\), whose mass is \(G\), and whose decision variables are the expert’s message partition and the parties’ platforms would largely restate that model. My strongest honest case is therefore only this nearby equilibrium-computation question; it does not meet the requested anchor standard, and the paper’s starting population is already continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its numbered propositions characterize equilibria and compare welfare; none asserts the complexity of a computational problem. That is a decisive mismatch with ChoCo’s target.

Proposition 3 does not supply the missing anchor. It characterizes a selected equilibrium. One could define a new task—compute its interval partition from \(b_e\)—but the input would be a scalar preference parameter, not a society to aggregate, and the paper already gives a compact formula for the output. To turn this into a substantive computational social choice problem, one would need to add choices such as control or robustness, or change the strategic structure so the voter distribution matters in more than the way it does here. Those could be worthwhile projects, but they would add problems the paper does not study.

There is also a direct collision on the population axis: the paper already models voters as a unit mass with an atomless ideology distribution \(G\). Its electorate is continuous, not merely its policy or state space. And in the paper’s one-dimensional voting model, Lemma 1 reduces election outcomes to the median voter’s choice. Replacing that electorate with a list of rational type masses would not uncover a computational result hidden in the paper; it would introduce a finite representation for a society the paper already treats as a measure.

So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo mirror of this article: the population is already continuous, and the paper supplies no computational problem whose complexity a mirror could chart. I cannot rule out every richer model inspired by it. But adding enough structure to create a new algorithmic problem would make that an adjacent project, rather than a continuous mirror of these results.
