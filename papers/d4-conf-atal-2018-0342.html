The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor check yields no eligible anchor. This paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Sections 4 and 5 give an algorithm and simulation and robot experiments; Figure 6 reports that switching to the least certain feature before each observation helps in some pathological initial allocations. Those findings may motivate a mirror, but they are not named complexity results. The paper’s finite-time unanimity claims are not proved as formal guarantees. So I can’t honestly provide the required anchor-by-anchor case.

There is still a plausible continuous-population question behind the paper. Imagine a large deployment of standardized survey robots mapping an environment with a small number \(M\) of binary features. The robot population has a small number of complete types—say, classes defined by sensor and motion characteristics—and \(N\) is much larger than the number of types. Type-level mass records the fraction of robots in each task and decision state. This is a credible regime for swarm robotics, and it preserves the paper’s central problem: agents make noisy local observations, communicate locally, switch between features, and must lock in decisions.

One possible follow-up is Multi-Feature Lock-In Verification\(_\infty\). An instance specifies the feature truths, rational type masses, the initial distribution of task and decision states, the sensing and local-contact rules, and a discretized version of the paper’s update and switching protocol. The question is whether the induced population-mass dynamics reach, by a given finite horizon, a state in which all mass is locked into the correct decision on every feature. A yes-instance returns the trajectory and first such time; a no-instance establishes that the target state is unreachable. This asks about the underlying collective decision process, not an encoding of it. Its scope is the paper’s multi-feature allocation and finite lock-in objective, not the physical robot implementation generally.

That is a candidate research question, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper. Its weakest point is the population model: the robots communicate locally and track message IDs, so a finite set of type masses alone may not capture their dynamics. A contact model and finite-state abstraction would need to preserve those interactions well enough for the authors to recognize the result as about their protocol. Exact unanimity is also brittle under persistent observation noise: in a continuum model, even a small error rate can leave positive mass deciding incorrectly. Relaxing unanimity to an error tolerance may make the model workable, but weakens fidelity to the paper’s stated goal. With no named complexity anchor and these modeling issues unresolved, I would not assign this candidate a tractability or hardness class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own anchor check is decisive: the paper contains no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its main results are an algorithm and empirical performance claims from simulations and five robot trials. In particular, the finite-time lock-in claim is not established as a formal guarantee. There is no result here to classify as tractable, inherently hard, or continuum-specifically hard.

The proposed Multi-Feature Lock-In Verification\(_\infty\) is a new research question, not a mirror of one of those results. It also needs a population model that preserves the paper’s key dynamics. Masses by sensor type and task state do not determine who communicates with whom: local contacts, random-walk histories, and message caches keyed by sender ID affect the updates. An exact state may therefore need to encode neighborhood and history information that grows with the swarm. A well-mixed approximation could close the mass dynamics, but it would replace the local communication process the paper emphasizes. That could be worthwhile swarm modelling; it would not establish a continuous version of a result in this paper.

The strongest repair would ask about the probability of reaching an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate lock-in state by a horizon, or optimize a feature-switching policy against that target. Those are coherent questions. Their computational content, however, would come from a newly specified stochastic process or control model, and its complexity would depend on the abstraction chosen for space, memory, and contact. The paper gives no complexity problem whose high-multiplicity version that analysis would resolve.

I would grant that large fleets of standardized survey robots make a high-multiplicity regime plausible; robot identity alone is not a sound objection. The negative case is weaker as a claim that no useful continuous follow-up could exist. It is strong as a claim about this paper: the candidate follow-ups may be valuable, but none is anchored in a computational result the paper actually proves.
