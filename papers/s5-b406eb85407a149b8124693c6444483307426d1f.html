The continuous mirror question

Given a finite induced square-grid graph \(H=(P,E)\) of \(\tau\) population types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_p\) summing to \(1\), suppose every individual of type \(p\) is connected to every individual in each adjacent type \(q\). Let \(D_p=\sum_{q\in N(p)}\mu_q\). Choose seed masses \(x_p\in[0,\mu_p]\), initially active masses \(A_p^0=x_p\), and update synchronously: an inactive type \(p\) becomes fully active when \(\sum_{q\in N(p)}A_q^r\ge D_p/2\). What is the minimum \(\sum_{p\in P}x_p\) that eventually activates every type (equivalently, given a rational budget \(b\), is there a seed vector of total mass at most \(b\))?

The model it lives in

A rational mass distribution \(\mu\) on grid-position types, with complete bipartite contacts between adjacent types; the decision variables are initial seed masses \(x_p\), the objective is to minimize total seeded mass, and activation uses a majority of neighboring population mass.

The objection that survived

The paper's hardness concerns person-level grid graphs of bounded degree, whereas complete bipartite contact between adjacent cohorts makes each person's degree grow with cohort mass, so hardness on the quotient grid does not preserve the paper's network regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers majority-threshold Target Set Selection on grid graphs of types; it leaves the paper's constant, unanimous, and exact-threshold results and its other graph-class results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an optimal fractional seed vector be rounded to whole-type seeds without increasing total mass, for any natural restriction on the masses or grid?
  • What is the exact complexity in \(\tau\) and the rational input bit length, including for fixed \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 29, proved in this paper: Target Set Selection is NP-complete under majority thresholds even when the graph is a grid graph. In the paper’s decision problem, the input is a graph, a threshold for each vertex, and a budget \(k\); the question is whether at most \(k\) initially infected vertices can eventually activate the whole graph. Here “majority” means \(t(v)=\lceil\deg(v)/2\rceil\). The theorem is proved here by reducing from the paper’s planar majority-threshold result; that line of reductions ultimately uses a restricted planar SAT problem whose hardness is cited from elsewhere.

My lead mirror is Grid-Cohort Majority Target Set Selection. Imagine a large population in occupied square-grid zones: for example, a campus or urban district with many residents in each zone. People in a zone share the same role in the contact network and the same majority susceptibility. There may be millions of residents but only \(\tau\) distinct zone types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the population. Each type \(p\) has rational mass \(\mu_p>0\), with \(\sum_p\mu_p=1\). The type graph is an induced subgraph of the square grid. In this model, a person in zone \(p\) has contact with the residents of each adjacent zone; the neighborhood population mass is \(D_p=\sum_{q\in N(p)}\mu_q\).

An instance also has a rational budget \(b\). Choose seed masses \(x_p\in[0,\mu_p]\), minimizing \(\sum_p x_p\), or, in the decision version, ask whether seeds of total mass at most \(b\) suffice. Let \(a_p^0=x_p\) be the initially active mass of type \(p\). In each round, if \(\sum_{q\in N(p)}a_q^r\ge D_p/2\), all remaining mass of type \(p\) activates: \(a_p^{r+1}=\mu_p\). Otherwise \(a_p^{r+1}=a_p^r\). The question is whether there is a rational seed vector \(x\) whose cascade eventually activates every type. A successful cascade needs at most \(\tau\) rounds in which new types activate, so a prover can be given \(x\) and the resulting activation sequence.

This keeps the paper’s central decision recognizable: choose an initial infected population of minimum size so that threshold-based spread reaches everyone. Majority is a natural continuous threshold: with large cohorts, a fixed count such as two neighbors would cease to describe a stable rule, while a threshold of half the neighboring population retains its meaning. The grid geometry also remains visible in the contact pattern among types. When all type masses are equal and seed choices are restricted to whole types, the update condition matches the paper’s discrete majority rule: a type activates when at least half of its neighboring types are active.

I expect the fractional version to remain hard, provisionally as continuum-specific hardness. The theorem’s grid topology and cascade choices still create combinatorial structure across \(\tau\) types. But the fractional seed option matters: the discrete theorem does not by itself prove hardness when \(x_p\) can be any value in \([0,\mu_p]\). A rounding result showing that fractional seeds can be converted to whole-type seeds without increasing cost would instead give a route to hardness transfer. That is one of the first questions this mirror raises, alongside whether the problem is polynomial-time solvable for fixed \(\tau\), and whether an LP or another continuous-optimization method handles broader grid or unit-disk contact patterns.

The mirror covers the paper’s majority-threshold grid result, with its unit-disk setting as nearby motivation. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s fixed-count threshold results or its other threshold regimes.

My weakest point is the block-contact assumption. The paper studies sparse graphs whose individual vertices have a small number of neighbors; this mirror treats each type as a population cohort with a shared contact pattern and majority exposure measured by neighboring mass. That changes the micro-level network. The case for accepting it is that the population, seed action, spread rule, and grid-structured interactions all remain explicit, while the many residents within a zone become indistinguishable in exactly the respects the model uses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is a real computational result, though it is Corollary 29 in the paper: Target Set Selection is NP-complete on grid graphs under majority thresholds. The best objection is not that its continuous answer might be easy or unchanged. It is that the proposed mirror cannot preserve the feature that makes this result about grid networks while also making population mass a sufficient description.

The cohort model is mathematically coherent. With \(N_p\) people at each grid position \(p\), no links within a cohort, and complete bipartite links between adjacent cohorts, every person in \(p\) has degree \(\sum_{q\in N(p)}N_q\). The mass update then captures majority activation exactly in the scaling limit. But the grid is now only the *quotient* graph: each person has a number of contacts that grows with cohort size, so the underlying population graph is no longer the bounded-degree grid studied in the paper. Its geometry describes which entire cohorts mix, rather than local person-to-person contact.

The alternative—preserving sparse grid contacts—does not give a finite type-mass state in general. In a grid graph of maximum degree four, a large group of people with the same actual neighbor set is impossible: their shared neighbors would have degree at least the group’s size. Grouping people by structural role across repeated patches avoids that bound, but then a patch’s activation depends on which roles are active together. Marginal active mass by role does not record that correlation; tracking it requires types for patch configurations, which can grow exponentially with patch size. Thus the apparent choice is between a sparse, locally structured network whose aggregate dynamics do not close on a manageable set of masses, and a closed mass model with dense mixing that changes the network regime.

That is a concern about whether the model is a mirror of this result, not about what its complexity turns out to be. A dense block-contact Target Set Selection problem could be a sensible research question in its own right. The harder claim—that the paper’s result has no worthwhile continuous mirror in *any* scenario—is much less secure: authors studying general diffusion models might recognize that block model as their problem, and broadcasts or zone-wide exposure could motivate it. The paper also sits outside computational social choice proper, which weakens its priority for ChoCo, but that alone does not rule out useful adjacent work. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the proposed mirror changes the network regime, and preserving sparse geometry defeats simple mass aggregation; neither point proves that no worthwhile mirror exists.
