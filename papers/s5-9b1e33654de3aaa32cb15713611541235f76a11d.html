The continuous mirror question

Given finite schools \(X\), finite complete student types \(T\), rational masses \( \mu_t\ge0 \) with \( \sum_t\mu_t=1 \), rational per-capita capacities \( \kappa_x \), strict rankings \(R_t\) over \(X\cup\{0\}\), and weak priorities \( \succeq_x \) over types, compute a mass assignment \(y=(y_{t,x},y_{t,0})\) satisfying \( \sum_x y_{t,x}+y_{t,0}=\mu_t \) and \( \sum_t y_{t,x}\le\kappa_x \), with no positive mass assigned below a desired school \(x\) while \(x\) has slack or contains positive mass of a type \(u\) with \(t\succ_x u\), and such that no other stable assignment typewise first-order stochastically dominates \(y\). Determine an algorithm and running time in \( |X| \), \( |T| \), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity school-choice model with interchangeable type mass: \(y_{t,x}\) records aggregate assignment while each unit receives one indivisible school or outside option; capacities are per-capita, priorities are type-level, and the objective is a stable Pareto-undominated assignment.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and the stable-improvement-cycles algorithm; it leaves the strategic results in Theorems 2–4, random selection, simulations, and empirical comparisons outside scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Theorem 1 lift exactly to split type mass under typewise first-order stochastic dominance?
  • Can stable improvement cycles be selected with rational \( \varepsilon \) and implemented in time polynomial in \( |X| \), \( |T| \), and the encoding length?
  • Does acyclicity of the aggregate improvement graph certify Pareto-undominatedness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s weak-priority school-choice problem. It should be a Class A candidate: the population becomes a distribution over student types, while schools, priorities, stability, and Pareto efficiency remain exactly the paper’s objects.

The paper has no numbered NP-hardness theorem or numbered theorem explicitly saying “in P.” Its best computational anchor is therefore Theorem 1, proved in the paper (with proof in Appendix A.1), together with the polynomial algorithm built immediately from it.

Theorem 1 says:

Fix a weak priority structure \( \succeq \) and a strict preference profile \(R\), and let \( \mu \) be a stable matching. If \( \mu \) is Pareto dominated by another stable matching \( \nu \), then \( \mu \) admits a stable improvement cycle.

A stable improvement cycle consists of distinct students \(i_1,\ldots,i_n\), \(n\ge2\), each assigned to a school, such that \(i_\ell\) prefers the school currently assigned to \(i_{\ell+1}\) over her own school, and \(i_\ell\) is among the highest-priority students, under that destination school’s weak priority order, among all students who desire it. Executing the cycle gives another stable matching that Pareto improves on \( \mu \). The paper then gives an algorithm: begin with deferred acceptance after an arbitrary strict tie-breaking, construct a directed graph on schools, use a directed cycle to perform a stable improvement cycle, and stop when the graph is acyclic. The authors state that this procedure is polynomial in the number of students and schools, with at most \( \frac12 |N|(|X|-1) \) cycle searches.

My continuous problem is:

Weak-Priority Mass SOSM, denoted \( \mathrm{MassSOSM}_{\infty} \).

An instance contains:

  • a finite set of schools \(X\);
  • a finite set of student types \(T\);
  • a rational society distribution \( \mu=(\mu_t)_{t\in T} \), with \( \sum_t\mu_t=1 \);
  • a rational per-capita capacity \( \kappa_x\) for each school \(x\);
  • for each type \(t\), a strict ranking \(R_t\) over \(X\cup\{0\}\), where \(0\) is the outside option;
  • for each school \(x\), a weak priority order \( \succeq_x\) over types.

A type is complete: students of type \(t\) have the same school ranking and the same priority-relevant characteristics, such as walk-zone status, sibling status, or other policy categories. The mass \( \mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of that type.

A solution is a mass assignment \(y\), where \(y_{t,x}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(x\), and \(y_{t,0}\) is the unassigned mass. Each unit of mass still receives one integral outcome; \(y\) merely records the aggregate assignment. Feasibility requires \( \sum_x y_{t,x}+y_{t,0}=\mu_t\) and \( \sum_t y_{t,x}\le \kappa_x\).

The assignment is stable if there is no positive mass of type \(t\) assigned below school \(x\) in \(R_t\) while either \(x\) has unused capacity or \(x\) contains positive mass of a type \(u\) with \(t\succ_x u\). Thus a blocking pair is the continuous version of the paper’s priority violation: a positive mass of students wants the school and has strictly higher priority than some mass currently there.

A stable assignment \(y'\) Pareto dominates \(y\) if, for every type \(t\), the distribution of assignments under \(y'\) first-order stochastically dominates its distribution under \(y\) according to \(R_t\), with strict improvement for at least one type and one preference cutoff. The problem asks for any stable assignment that is not Pareto dominated by another stable mass assignment.

This is a faithful mirror rather than a fractional relaxation of the school-choice problem. If \( \mu_t=a_t/N\), \( \kappa_x=b_x/N\), and \(y_{t,x}=c_{t,x}/N\), then multiplying all masses by \(N\) produces an ordinary finite instance with \(a_t\) students of type \(t\) and \(b_x\) seats at school \(x\). Conversely, every finite matching projects to such a mass assignment. The school remains indivisible for each student; only the population is continuous. Scaling capacities to per-capita quantities is the natural co-modification required when the population itself is the continuum.

The relevant regime is a large urban school district with, say, tens or hundreds of thousands of students but far fewer distinct preference-and-priority signatures. A type might combine a geographic zone, sibling status, eligibility category, and one of a moderate number of common school-preference templates. Thus \(n\gg \tau\), where \(n\) is the number of students and \(\tau=|T|\) is the number of types. This is not a claim that every school district has low multiplicity. It is a plausible annual-cohort regime, and precisely the kind of instance-specific high-multiplicity scenario the programme calls for.

The paper’s stable-improvement construction has a direct mass analogue. For an assignment \(y\), let \(D_x(y)\) be the types having positive mass assigned below \(x\) and maximal priority among all types that desire \(x\). Construct a directed graph on schools, placing an edge \(x\to z\) when some positive mass of a type assigned to \(x\) belongs to \(D_z(y)\). A directed cycle \(x_1\to x_2\to\cdots\to x_k\to x_1\) permits moving a common positive amount \( \varepsilon\) of mass from \(x_\ell\) to \(x_{\ell+1}\) for each witness type. Choosing \( \varepsilon\) no larger than the available source masses preserves feasibility, improves every moved unit, and should preserve stability for exactly the priority reason used in Theorem 1.

The natural algorithm is therefore type-level deferred acceptance under a strict refinement of the weak priorities, followed by maximal-mass stable improvement cycles. The graph has schools as vertices and type witnesses as edges, so its combinatorial size depends on \(m\) and \(\tau\), not on the number of named students. I would expect \( \mathrm{MassSOSM}_{\infty}\) to be in Class A, probably via a combination of aggregate deferred acceptance, cycle detection, and flow or linear-programming subroutines. The paper does not prove this continuous result; it supplies the structural template.

The key open questions are whether Theorem 1 lifts exactly from individual matchings to mass assignments, whether an acyclic aggregate improvement graph certifies Pareto-undominatedness, how many maximal mass transfers are needed, and whether the whole procedure is polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of the rational masses rather than in the potentially enormous denominator used to realize them as individual students. One can also ask for the maximum aggregate welfare improvement over all stable assignments, rather than merely finding one constrained-efficient assignment.

The weakest point is that the continuous lift is not automatic. The finite proof uses distinct students and pointwise Pareto dominance, whereas a mass assignment can split a type across several schools and uses a coupling or stochastic-dominance interpretation of Pareto improvement. For rational mass assignments, scaling gives strong evidence that the finite theorem should transfer; arbitrary real masses and compressed bit complexity require a separate proof. A second vulnerability is empirical: if almost every family has an idiosyncratic ranking or lottery position, then \(\tau\) approaches \(n\), and the high-multiplicity interpretation loses force. But that is a limitation of the regime, not of the mirror itself.

This mirror covers the paper’s central algorithmic result and deliberately does not stretch to Theorems 2–4 on strategic manipulation and random selection. Those would require a separate continuum model of type-level reporting and random tie-breaking. The present question is already a genuine continuous-population version of the paper’s main problem: compute a stable, student-optimal-in-the-Pareto sense assignment when the society is given by masses of interchangeable student types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed “continuization” adds no genuinely new computational object. Theorem 1 concerns a finite matching, and its algorithm already depends only on priorities, preferences, school capacities, and the existence of witnesses for school-to-school edges. If students are grouped into complete types, replacing \(n_t\) by masses \(\mu_t\) merely compresses the same instance. For rational data, a common denominator recovers the finite market exactly; with binary-encoded denominators, the new question is principally whether the finite algorithm can exploit compressed multiplicities without expanding them.

That is a legitimate high-multiplicity question, but it is not a strong case for continuous computational social choice specifically. There is no uncertainty, continuum of alternatives, or infinite-dimensional optimization here. The proposed graph remains finite, with schools as vertices and type-level witnesses as edges. The “continuous” part only changes integer counts into rational capacities and transfer amounts.

The proponent’s claimed lift is also not established by Theorem 1. The finite proof uses a matching \( \mu:N\to X\cup N \), distinct students, and pointwise Pareto dominance. A mass assignment \(y\) is instead a transportation plan: one type may be split across several schools, and Pareto dominance is replaced by typewise first-order stochastic dominance. The aggregate graph records that a suitable type exists on each edge, but the finite proof does not by itself provide the required flow/decomposition argument, termination bound, or bit-complexity guarantee for successive mass transfers. An acyclic graph need not automatically certify maximality until those issues are resolved.

Still, this does not defeat the mirror. Splitting is not fatal: because each type has a common strict ranking, first-order stochastic dominance can be implemented by a measure-preserving reassignment of individual mass, and a finite cycle can use a sufficiently small positive \( \varepsilon \). Scaling capacities with population is also natural in a large district, and a plausible regime with many students sharing preference and priority signatures is easy to construct. Nor is existing high-multiplicity matching work a collision; under the programme’s rules it is evidence that the regime is sensible.

Thus the honest negative case can downgrade the proponent’s claim from “the paper supplies a continuous algorithm” to “the paper motivates a nontrivial open lifting problem.” It cannot establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 1 remains a valid computational anchor, and if the mass theorem fails, that failure would itself be an interesting boundary result rather than a reason to reject the mirror.
