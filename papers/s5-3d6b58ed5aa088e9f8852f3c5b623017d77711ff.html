The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(S\) of municipality types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), each type’s rational shock \(\theta_t\), threshold \(T_t\), political cost \(\omega_{T,t}\), and consent cap \(\bar b_t\), common rational \(\omega_b,c>0\), and rational per-district treasury supply \(R\), compute an exact maximizing vector \((b_t)_{t\in S}\) for \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t(\omega_b\theta_t b_t-\omega_{T,t}b_t-\tfrac{c}{2}b_t^2)\), subject to \(0\le b_t\le\bar b_t\), \(b_t=0\) when \(\theta_t<T_t\), and \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t b_t\le R\). Determine the bit complexity in support size and input length.

The model it lives in

A population consisting of mass \(\mu_t\) of interchangeable municipalities of each complete type \(t\). Choose one bailout amount \(b_t\) per type to maximize the mass-weighted quadratic net benefit, subject to each type’s eligibility and consent cap and a shared per-capita treasury limit.

The objection that survived

The continuous allocation moves from one municipality’s policy choice to a jointly budget-constrained portfolio; Section 5.1 points toward that extension but does not state it as a computational result.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the linear TLC policy in Proposition 3.3 extended to the heterogeneous-municipality treasury setting of Section 5.1. It leaves the general concave-benefit result, the no-bailout knife-edge, comparative statics, and the audit spline unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Establish an exact breakpoint-sweep algorithm and its bit-complexity bound in support size \(k\) and input length \(L\).
  • Determine which extensions, such as multiple treasury constraints or type-dependent quadratic costs, retain polynomial-time solvability.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a narrow continuous treasury allocation built from Proposition 3.3, proved in this paper’s appendix. In the linear-benefit case, it gives the optimal bailout for a municipality with shock \(\theta\) as
\(b^*(\theta;w)=\min\{\bar b(w),\max\{0,(\omega_b\theta-\omega_T(w))/c\}\}\), with \(b^*=0\) when \(\theta<T\). Equivalently, the policy is zero below \(\underline\theta=\max\{T,\omega_T/\omega_b\}\), rises linearly with slope \(\omega_b/c\), then reaches the consent cap at \(\overline\theta=(\omega_T+c\bar b)/\omega_b\). The proposition is not stated as a complexity result, but its closed-form optimizer is an algorithmic tractability result for the underlying bailout choice.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a province with many local service districts making bailout requests. Districts share a type when their shock, eligibility threshold, political cost, and weighted-consent rule are identical. The type’s consent rule determines its cap \(\bar b_t\). Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of districts of type \(t\); the regime of interest has many districts but relatively few types. This uses the paper’s own heterogeneous-municipality and treasury-constraint setting in Section 5.1, while making the type distribution the explicit population object. The bailout amount \(b_t\) remains the policy decision; continuity here is in the population of recipients.

I would name the lead problem Continuous Treasury-Constrained TLC Allocation. An instance gives a finite support \(S\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and for each type its realized shock \(\theta_t\), threshold \(T_t\), political cost \(\omega_{T,t}\), and WRD consent cap \(\bar b_t\). It also gives common \(\omega_b,c>0\) and a rational per-district treasury supply \(R\). The question is to choose a bailout \(b_t\) for each type to maximize
\(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\bigl(\omega_b\theta_t b_t-\omega_{T,t}b_t-\tfrac c2b_t^2\bigr)\),
subject to \(0\le b_t\le\bar b_t\), \(b_t=0\) if \(\theta_t<T_t\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t\le R\). A solution is an exact optimal vector of type-level bailouts.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). With a treasury shadow price \(\lambda\ge0\), each eligible type receives
\(b_t(\lambda)=\min\{\bar b_t,\max\{0,(\omega_b\theta_t-\omega_{T,t}-\lambda)/c\}\}\).
Choose \(\lambda\) to satisfy the treasury constraint and complementary slackness. The total allocation is piecewise linear in \(\lambda\); sorting the at most two breakpoints per type and solving on the relevant segment gives an exact algorithm polynomial in support size \(k=|S|\) and input bit length \(L\). This is an aggregate extension of Proposition 3.3, and it matches Section 5.1’s observation that a common treasury shadow price shifts each type’s effective political cost. I would not claim hardness transfer: this paper supplies no hardness anchor for the bailout problem.

This mirror covers the paper’s linear-benefit bailout result and its treasury-constrained heterogeneous-recipient direction. It does not claim to mirror the general concave case, the knife-edge result, or the audit spline. It also leads to natural follow-up questions: whether the same algorithm survives type-dependent quadratic costs, multiple treasury constraints, or equity floors.

The weak point is that the proposed recipients must be genuinely numerous and share a small set of complete types; ordinary municipalities may be too few or too distinct. Also, Section 5.1 already sketches a finite-\(N\) version of the budget-coupled allocation, and the paper already aggregates endorsement weights. The positive case therefore rests on treating explicit rational type masses and complexity in \(k,L\) as a new computational question, not on claiming a new policy geometry. If the referee regards the paper’s existing aggregation as already the continuous-population model, this mirror is too close to a restatement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3.3 is the only plausible anchor, but it is a characterization of a one-dimensional policy choice, not a computational result. Its input is a realized shock and a few parameters; it gives the maximizing bailout in closed form. The paper states no finite computational problem, complexity claim, or algorithmic question about an electorate. Calling the formula an “algorithmic tractability result” stretches that phrase: a formula can be evaluated, but that does not make the proposition a result about computational complexity or high multiplicity.

The strongest repair is the proposed many-municipality problem. It is a sensible optimization model, and Section 5.1 gives it a foothold. But it changes the task from choosing one municipality’s bailout to choosing a vector of bailouts jointly under a treasury constraint. That is a reasonable extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror of Proposition 3.3: the proposition supplies no discrete computational problem whose high-multiplicity version this allocation solves. The relevant agents also shift from citizens whose endorsements determine representation to municipalities whose requests compete for funds; the citizens’ preferences have already disappeared into the reduced-form caps.

A fuller model of citizens or representatives does not repair the connection. The paper already reduces their role to weights and a threshold distribution, then computes the policy from those summaries. Turning that calculation into a continuous-complexity question would require adding a new task, such as choosing representation weights or computing consent caps from richer inputs. That could be worthwhile work, but the paper does not pose or solve it.

So the case against this as a *mirror of a result in the paper* is strong: the computational anchor is missing. The case against the treasury allocation as an independent research question is weaker. Many similar districts with a per-capita treasury budget are a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s extension makes author recognition credible. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from this model; I would claim that the proposed one is a new computational extension, not evidence that this paper has a continuous mirror of its own result.
