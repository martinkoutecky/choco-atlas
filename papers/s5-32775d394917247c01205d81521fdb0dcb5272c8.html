The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest foothold is a clean high-multiplicity regime: a large committee could contain many experts with the same information technology, payoffs, and signal, giving two types, \(a\) and \(r\), whose masses are their population shares. That is a plausible population model. But the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it: it gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational formulation that it then uses.

The nearest result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. It says that if a non-babbling symmetric equilibrium exists, it is unique; if one exists under threshold \(q\), one also exists under every \(q'>q\); and at any equilibrium represented by \(z^*\ne1\), \(\partial R(n,z^*)/\partial z>0\). These are equilibrium-characterization claims, not a result about computing an equilibrium or the complexity of a problem. Proposition 4, also proved here, says that with positive disesteem penalty \(K\), under the state-of-the-art model, the approval probability tends to zero as committee size grows along any sequence of symmetric equilibria. That is a limit theorem, not a computational result. The paper’s other numbered propositions and corollaries likewise concern equilibrium properties, aggregation, or comparative statics.

One could define a new task: given a large, repeated-type committee, compute a symmetric equilibrium or its approval probability. But that would be a follow-up question, not a mirror of a computational anchor in this paper. There is also a model-fit problem: in a genuinely atomless committee, an individual vote has no effect on whether the approval threshold is crossed. The paper’s central strategic mechanism depends on an individual’s probability of being pivotal. Keeping that mechanism may require finite committees or positive-mass voting blocs, changing what the continuous model represents.

So I would not state a continuous problem per anchor or assign it a tractability class. The population setting is plausible; the computational mirror is unsupported by the paper’s named results. The weakest point is decisive under your criteria: the paper studies strategic information aggregation analytically, including its large-\(n\) limit, but does not study computational questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting exists: many experts could share the same payoff and signal technology. So I would not argue that committees can never have repeated types. The stronger objection is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes symmetric equilibria; Proposition 4 gives a large-\(n\) limit; Propositions 2 and 3 are also information-aggregation results. None specifies an algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or approximation task. Those results can motivate a new computational project, but they do not themselves provide its computational anchor.

The best version of a Proposition 1 mirror would ask for an exact or approximate symmetric equilibrium from a type distribution. But in the paper’s finite model, the strategic unknown is already compressed to the scalar \(z\): the non-babbling equilibrium is a root of \(R(n,z)=0\), built from binomial probabilities. With \(n\) explicit, that is a one-variable root problem with a direct binomial-sum representation, not an exponential-support LP or an uncontrolled pricing problem. If \(n\) is encoded succinctly, one could study finite-\(n\) equilibrium computation for very large committees; that is a possible follow-up, but it is a succinct finite-game problem, not a computational consequence of making the society continuous.

Proposition 4’s best mirror would treat the shares of signal types \(a\) and \(r\) as a continuum population. That exposes the deeper mismatch. In an atomless committee, one expert’s vote changes the approval mass by zero, so it cannot change the committee decision. If approval can occur, voting to approve adds the individual’s disesteem risk without improving the common payoff; with \(K>0\), rejecting is therefore optimal. The continuum model collapses the pivotality mechanism and gives the degenerate all-reject outcome. The paper already establishes the corresponding large-\(n\) limit. Preserving nontrivial pivotal incentives requires keeping finite-size fluctuations—potentially studying a scaling such as \(K\) of order \(n^{-1/2}\)—which is a finite-size asymptotics project, not a continuous-population computational problem.

One could instead make whole positive-mass blocs strategic, but then each bloc is a coalition whose action can move the threshold, not a mass of individually acting experts. Or one could add heterogeneous signal technologies and costs; in an atomless version, individual pivotality still vanishes. These may lead to worthwhile new models, but they change the strategic object or inherit the same degeneracy. The negative case is not a proof that no interesting extension can be invented. It is that no version considered here both preserves the paper’s central incentive and produces a nontrivial continuous computational mirror.
