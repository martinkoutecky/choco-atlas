The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive direction is continuous weightedness recognition, but there is a qualification: this paper has no named theorem asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Its strongest anchor is structural, not computational in that sense.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 2.2, proved here. It says that a simple game with \(n\) players is weighted if and only if it is \(\alpha_{n+1}\)-trade robust, where \(\alpha_{n+1}\) is the largest determinant of an \((n+1)\times(n+1)\) zero-one matrix. The paper proves the short-certificate direction using Farkas’ lemma; the converse relies on the characterization of weighted games by trading transforms from Taylor and Zwicker. This connects weightedness to whether winning and losing coalitions can be balanced player by player.

A plausible regime is a large federation, union, or shareholder body with many interchangeable members in each of a small number \(\tau\) of formal voting roles. Members in a role have identical effects on every coalition; the game is invariant to swaps within that role. A coalition is then described by how much of each role joins it. With total population normalized to one, its state is \(x\in\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}[0,\mu_t]\), where \(\mu_t\) is role \(t\)’s mass. The number of members can be very large while \(\tau\) remains small.

The candidate problem is Continuous Weightedness Recognition. An instance gives rational role masses \(\mu_t\) and a monotone winning region \(W\) in that box, described by a finite Boolean formula of rational linear inequalities; losing states are its complement. The question is whether there are nonnegative role weights \(a_t\) and a quota \(q\) such that, for every feasible coalition state \(x\),
\[ x\in W \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_t x_t\ge q. \]
A solution is such a weight vector and quota, or a certificate that no such threshold represents \(W\). This is a natural population-level version of the paper’s weightedness question: the members are divisible in the description of a coalition, while the outcome remains the same binary winning-or-losing decision. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in an atomless, role-symmetric voting regime.

I would expect this version to be tractable when the winning region has an explicit polyhedral description: checking whether a threshold separates the represented winning and losing regions reduces to linear optimization over their cells. That expectation depends on the input representation; a compressed rule or oracle access to \(W\) could change the complexity. The continuous question also leaves a useful bridge question: when does weightedness on the finite grid of coalition counts imply weightedness across the whole box?

This candidate covers Theorem 2.2’s weightedness characterization and its balanced-trade intuition. It does not cover the paper’s integer-weight bounds or rounding result. Theorem 4.1, proved here, says that for the paper’s linear relaxation of integer-weight representation, some scale in a specified interval yields an integer representation by flooring the scaled weights and quota. But the paper explicitly leaves open whether that scale can be found efficiently, so this is not an algorithmic anchor. Nor does Theorem 5.1’s bound on potent certificates supply a separate complexity result.

The weak point is substantial: an arbitrary simple game need not treat members in a role interchangeably, and the paper does not study a large-population regime. Moreover, Theorem 2.2 supplies a characterization and certificate-size bound, not a complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. So this is a credible *candidate mirror*, but it cannot honestly be presented as a compliant complexity anchor from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 2.2 is a structural characterization: weightedness is equivalent to trade robustness up to a determinant bound. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 5.1 similarly bounds certificate length, while Theorem 4.1 proves an existential rounding guarantee and leaves efficient scale-finding open. Those are mathematical results, but none supplies the complexity result the programme is meant to continuize.

The proposed role-symmetric federation is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and continuous weightedness recognition could be a sensible research question. But it needs extra structure the paper does not provide. A finite role-symmetric game specifies winning coalitions only at points on a grid of role counts. It says nothing about the fractional points between them. Giving the continuous winning region as a Boolean formula of rational inequalities fills that gap, but the formula and its encoding now define a new geometric decision problem. Its complexity would depend on that representation; Theorem 2.2’s bound on trades in a finite coalition system does not determine it.

Even the stronger atomless version—where every measurable coalition of each role is allowed and the winning region is specified directly—does not restore the missing anchor. It may yield a worthwhile problem about separating continuous winning and losing regions by a threshold. But that is a new computational problem built around the paper’s subject, not a continuous complexity result proved or classified by the paper. A continuous analogue of its trade certificate could still be interesting; it would not turn the source theorem into a complexity classification.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: this paper may inspire a continuous weightedness project, but the proposed mirror does not meet ChoCo’s stated standard for a computational anchor. The universal claim that no scenario could make such a project worthwhile is weaker. The role-symmetric regime is credible, and a carefully specified continuous recognition problem might be worth studying on its own.
