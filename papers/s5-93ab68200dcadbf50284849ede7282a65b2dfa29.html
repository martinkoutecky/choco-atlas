The continuous mirror question

Given platforms \(P\), a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita group caps \(\beta_{p,h}\), maximize expected assigned mass over lotteries on measurable assignments. Each person may receive at most one eligible platform; in every outcome, mass from group \(h\) assigned to platform \(p\) is at most \(\beta_{p,h}\); and each person of type \(t\) is assigned to one of their top \(k\) platforms with probability in \([L_{t,k},U_{t,k}]\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with \(\tau\) complete types of masses \(\mu_t\); variables \(x_{t,p}\) denote assigned mass. Maximize \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\), subject to \(\sum_p x_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), \(L_{t,k}\mu_t\le\sum_{p\in\mathrm{Top}_{t,k}}x_{t,p}\le U_{t,k}\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t:h\in H_t}x_{t,p}\le\beta_{p,h}\), with ineligible pairs fixed to zero. A randomized shift of each type’s partition realizes these masses while giving every individual the corresponding platform probabilities and preserving each group cap in every outcome.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.4’s expected matching-size objective, top-\(k\) individual fairness, and overlapping group caps; it leaves the other approximation regimes, strong group fairness with lower quotas, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s central problem: assigning people to platforms while giving each person a controlled probability of reaching a preferred platform and keeping every outcome group-fair.

The anchor is Theorem 3.4, the informal version of Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that for any \(\varepsilon>0\), when each item belongs to at most \(\Delta\) groups, a polynomial-time algorithm returns a distribution over group-fair matchings whose expected size is at least \((\mathrm{OPT}+\varepsilon)/f_\varepsilon\), where \(f_\varepsilon=O(\Delta\log(n/\varepsilon))\). For every item \(a\) and preference prefix \(k\), its probability of being matched into that prefix lies between \((L_{a,k}-\varepsilon)/f_\varepsilon\) and \((U_{a,k}+\varepsilon)/f_\varepsilon\). The algorithm reports infeasibility if no such distribution exists. This is the strongest anchor for my case because it concerns the paper’s main combination of individual fairness, group fairness, and matching size, including overlapping groups.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large regional placement system assigning applicants to schools or employers. Applicants can share a type when they have the same eligible platforms, ranking, overlapping group memberships, and individual-fairness thresholds. For example, a large annual cohort might contain many thousands of applicants but only hundreds of recurring composite types. If any of those attributes differ, the applicants belong to different types. This is a property of that regime, not a claim that every matching market has few types.

Call the mirror Continuous Top-\(k\) Fair Matching with Overlapping Group Caps. An instance gives a finite platform set \(P\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) specifies its eligible platforms, ranking, group set \(H_t\), and bounds \(L_{t,k},U_{t,k}\). It also gives a rational per-capita upper quota \(\beta_{p,h}\) for each platform \(p\) and group \(h\). These quotas scale the paper’s count bounds to a population of mass \(1\).

A solution is a lottery over measurable assignments in which every individual is assigned to at most one eligible platform. In every lottery outcome, the mass assigned from group \(h\) to platform \(p\) must be at most \(\beta_{p,h}\). For every individual of type \(t\), the probability of assignment to one of that type’s top \(k\) platforms must lie in \([L_{t,k},U_{t,k}]\). The question is to maximize expected matched mass, or report infeasibility.

The problem has a direct LP in variables \(x_{t,p}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to platform \(p\): maximize \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\), subject to \(x_{t,p}\ge 0\), \(x_{t,p}=0\) for ineligible pairs, \(\sum_p x_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), \(L_{t,k}\mu_t\le\sum_{p\in\mathrm{Top}_{t,k}}x_{t,p}\le U_{t,k}\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t:h\in H_t}x_{t,p}\le\beta_{p,h}\). The rational vector \(x\) and a sampler implement an optimal solution: represent each type’s mass as an interval, divide it into platform segments of lengths \(x_{t,p}\), and choose a uniform random shift for that type. Each individual then has the required platform probabilities, while every outcome assigns exactly those segment masses and so obeys the group caps. This gives an exact polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,\chi\), and the input bit length \(L\), through rational linear programming.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem’s continuous analogue. The same people-to-platform assignment, top-\(k\) preference guarantees, expected matching-size objective, and ex-post group caps remain. The population has become the continuous object; the allocation of each individual remains indivisible. The per-capita quotas are the natural scaling of count quotas, and type-level assignment masses can be fractional because each mass unit still receives one platform.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: its mass-allocation formulation is an LP. It covers the central objective and probabilistic individual fairness of Theorem 3.4, with overlapping groups and group-fair outcomes. It does not cover the paper’s experimental results or its separate strong-group-fairness setting with lower quotas. Natural follow-up questions include how well a solution rounds back to a finite cohort while preserving the caps and individual probabilities, and whether lower quotas admit an equally direct continuous formulation.

The soft spot is also clear: the paper’s algorithmic challenge is decomposing fractional solutions into integral group-fair matchings. The continuous mirror removes that obstacle by letting type mass split among platforms, so the exact LP may feel like a substantial simplification. That is the intended high-multiplicity change, but it means this case supports a mirror of the paper’s assignment-and-fairness question, not a claim that its finite decomposition technique survives unchanged. The hardness discussion in §3.1.1 is prose citing known results, not a numbered hardness theorem of this paper, so I do not use it as an anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed regime may be narrower than its example suggests. A type must preserve eligibility, the complete platform ranking, group memberships, and individual-fairness bounds. In school placement, those combinations can be highly varied; if most applicants have distinct types, then \(\tau\) is close to the number of applicants, and the high-multiplicity rationale weakens. Grouping them by broader categories would change the problem’s inputs.

That objection does not defeat the anchor. A standardized placement cohort with repeated complete profiles is plausible, and the proponent’s construction is sound: for each type, divide its mass among platforms according to the LP solution, then randomize the partition so every individual has the required assignment probabilities. Each outcome assigns every mass unit to at most one platform, and the assigned mass from each type is fixed, so overlapping group caps hold in every outcome. The LP therefore gives an exact polynomial-time solution to a continuous version of Theorem 3.4.

Calling that version too easy or saying it removes the paper’s decomposition challenge would attack the answer, not the question. Under this programme’s criteria, the resulting tractability is precisely a possible Class A result. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor; the negative case is weak here.
