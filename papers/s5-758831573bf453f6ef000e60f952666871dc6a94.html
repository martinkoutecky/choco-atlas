The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible numbered computational anchor in this paper: it states no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition about complexity. Its computational contribution is simulation with integer programming. The closest supporting finding is Table 1: on one generated instance, total transplants were 968 under individual collaboration, 1,108 under consecutive collaboration, and 1,255 under joint collaboration. Section 7.2 reports the same overall pattern in larger simulation batches. These are empirical findings, not named complexity results, so I can’t honestly present them as the required anchors.

The strongest candidate mirror I see is a continuous version of the quarterly matching run at the heart of those simulations. Call it Continuous High-Multiplicity Kidney Exchange Packing. A type is a donor–recipient pair, or altruistic donor, with all attributes that affect compatibility, eligibility, failure probabilities, country, and the programme’s scores. Two agents share a type only when those attributes make them interchangeable for the matching run. The instance gives each type a mass, the compatibility relation between types, and the programme’s cycle and chain length limits. For each allowed cycle or chain configuration \(q\), let \(r_{qt}\) count how many units of type \(t\) it uses. Choose a mass \(y_q\ge 0\) of each configuration, subject to \(\sum_q r_{qt}y_q\le\mu_t\) for every pair type \(t\), with corresponding capacity constraints for altruistic donors. The solution is the feasible allocation of cycle and chain mass that is lexicographically optimal under the programme’s criteria.

For the joint policy, the paper says it uses the UK optimisation policy on the merged pool. That gives a concrete objective: maximise effective 2-cycles, then transplanted recipients, selected cycles, and back-arcs; use the stated waiting-time, sensitisation, and donor-age scores as the final weighted tie-breaker. The output is a type-level allocation of cycle and chain mass. Its expected completed transplants can then be evaluated using the specified failure and internal-recourse rules.

This is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime for a large national or international registry with many pairs but a substantially smaller set of recurring, fully specified clinical and policy types. The types must preserve the exact compatibility and scoring information used by the run; grouping pairs merely by broad blood group or age category would change the problem. The mirror covers the per-run matching optimisation and its transplant objective. It does not, by itself, mirror the paper’s full five-year simulation of arrivals, departures, failures, and collaboration policies.

For fixed cycle and chain length limits, this proposed problem is tractable: there are at most polynomially many type configurations in the support size when the length limit is constant, and the allocation is a linear programme with a constant number of lexicographic stages. The high-multiplicity bridge would still need work: a useful next result would bound how closely the LP’s allocation and transplant count can be approximated by integral cycles in a finite pool. Further questions include what happens when the length limit is part of the input, and how to model multi-round arrivals and recourse while preserving tractability.

My weakest point is whether the required support-size regime is realistic here. The paper’s UK pool is described as roughly 220–250 pairs, and detailed immunological compatibility profiles may make almost every pair its own type. A larger registry with recurrent complete compatibility profiles could make \(N\gg\tau\) credible, but the paper does not establish that this holds for its simulated populations. I think the authors could recognise the single-run LP as a continuous analogue of the matching problem their simulator solves; they may reasonably say it does not reproduce the headline five-year simulation results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no named complexity result to continuize. Its findings are outputs of five-year simulations: policy choices are compared by completed transplants across changing pools, with arrivals, departures, failures, and recourse. The proposed cycle-packing LP is a sensible problem, but it is a new static, single-run question. It does not mirror the paper’s headline comparison of collaboration policies.

That static question also has a narrower computational gap than the proponent suggests. Kidney-exchange algorithms already use cycle and chain formulations whose continuous LP relaxations help prune the integer model; with the short cycle and chain limits used here, the number of configurations is polynomial in the number of pair types. [Delorme et al., “New Algorithms for Hierarchical Optimization in Kidney Exchange Programs”](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2022.2374) describe this use of LP relaxations and cycle/chain deactivation. A type-level mass LP could still yield a useful high-multiplicity result, but the paper neither establishes a need for that compression nor reports the complexity question it would answer.

The fragile point is whether the required types recur at useful scale. A complete type must preserve the compatibility and scoring information that affects a run, along with attributes such as waiting time and failure probabilities. Grouping by broad clinical categories risks changing the feasible cycles or priorities; grouping by exact matching-relevant features may leave nearly every pair in its own type. The paper’s simulated UK pools have roughly 220–250 pairs, and its generator does not supply HLA data. Its results therefore do not show a regime with \(N\gg\tau\).

A stronger dynamic mirror could model fixed clinical cohorts, arrival and departure rates, and failure processes, then ask for continuous aggregate throughput under each collaboration policy. That is plausible and could be worthwhile. It also means the negative case cannot honestly establish that no scenario supports a good mirror: a large registry with recurring complete profiles is possible, and the type-level LP is coherent there. The best case against is narrower—the proposed static LP is not anchored to a computational result in this paper, and the paper provides little evidence that its high-multiplicity regime occurs. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists remains weak.
