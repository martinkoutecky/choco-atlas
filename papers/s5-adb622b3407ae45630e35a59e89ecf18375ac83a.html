The strongest plausible mirror would treat legislators in a large, repeated committee system as a distribution over complete bargaining types: each type includes policy peaks, preference intensities, and any other traits that affect its choices. Mass is the share of legislators of each type. An offer specifies a policy and a type-dependent division of a per-capita pork budget; each type’s members vote according to their preferences, and passage depends on whether supporting mass reaches the required quota. One could ask whether a passing offer exists, or compute an equilibrium outcome.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no eligible computational anchor. It has a formal analysis and equilibrium predictions, but its reported results are experimental findings about bargaining behavior. H1–H4 are hypotheses, not complexity results; the paper states no algorithmic problem and proves no tractability or hardness result. So there are no named computational results to mirror here.

The strongest reconstruction would put legislators’ complete bargaining types into a distribution and randomly draw committees of three. One could then ask for aggregate outcomes over those committees. That is a coherent population model, but it turns the paper’s empirical question into a new aggregation or prediction task. The committee game stays finite, and its central object—how people actually propose, set voting thresholds, and compromise—is not a computation over masses. Replacing that behavior with an equilibrium or an optimized mass-contingent offer would ask a different question, especially since the paper documents departures from its equilibrium predictions.

Scaling the committees themselves to a continuum would be a more ambitious mirror, but it changes the strategic setting: members become measure-zero agents, and majority bargaining no longer works as it does in the three-person game. A model could repair that, but it would be a new legislative bargaining model rather than a computational version of a result in this paper.
