What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors in the supplied paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Equation (2) defines the objective, and Tables 2–12 report empirical comparisons. The Gale–Shapley result on stability is cited background, not a numbered result proved here. So I cannot honestly give a continuous problem *per anchor* or claim the paper proves tractability or hardness.

The strongest positive case I believe is for a continuous version of the paper’s underlying bonus-design problem. I would call it Robust Mass-Based Bonus Design. The paper’s objective and decision are clear: choose a bonus for a protected group at a program, balancing the gap in admission rates against the pre-bonus scores of admitted students, when next year’s applicants are uncertain. That is a recognizable computational question about admissions, not about making outcomes or probabilities continuous.

An instance has a finite set of programs \(P\), a target program \(p\), a protected-group label \(g_t\in\{0,1\}\), and a set \(T\) of complete applicant types. A type includes the applicant’s full program preference list and the scores used to rank them at every program. Uncertainty is represented by scenarios \(\omega\), each with rational probability \(q_\omega\) and a society \(\mu^\omega\), where \(\mu^\omega_t\) is the share of applicants of type \(t\) and \(\sum_t\mu^\omega_t=1\). Program capacities are given as per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\), scaled to the cohort. This scaling is part of the population mirror: the original programs’ seats remain scarce as the population grows.

For each allowed bonus \(b\) from the admissions system’s original bonus domain, add \(b\) points to protected applicants’ scores at \(p\), then run student-proposing deferred acceptance on the type masses. A type’s mass can be split at a priority cutoff, representing the share of otherwise identical applicants admitted; each individual still receives an ordinary admission decision. Let \(z^\omega_{t,p}(b)\) be the mass of type \(t\) admitted to \(p\), and define the group admission rates by
\[ R^\omega_g(b)= \frac{\sum_{t:g_t=g}z^\omega_{t,p}(b)} {\sum_{t:g_t=g}\mu^\omega_t}. \]
Let \(M^\omega(b)\) be the average *pre-bonus* score of those admitted to \(p\), and \(D^\omega(b)=R^\omega_1(b)-R^\omega_0(b)\). The question is to return a bonus \(b^*\) minimizing
\[ \sum_\omega q_\omega \left[ M^\omega(0)-M^\omega(b) +\lambda\lvert D^\omega(b)\rvert \right], \]
together with the resulting mass-based DA assignments and objective value. This expected-objective version makes the paper’s aim of choosing one policy for uncertain applicant pools precise; it differs from the paper’s implemented recipe of averaging bonuses optimized separately for sampled application sets.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large, recurring centralized admissions system whose applicants share complete score-and-preference profiles. The paper reports about 60,000 applicants and 1,500 programs in 2017, which supports the “many applicants” part of that story. Discrete score scales and repeated preference lists could also yield \(\tau\ll N\), but the paper does not report the number of distinct complete types. That compression is an assumption for the mirror, not an empirical finding. The authors should recognize the question: it preserves their bonus policy, DA assignment, uncertainty, and fairness-versus-score objective, while making the cohort itself a mass distribution.

I would expect this version to be tractable when the scenarios and permitted bonus choices are explicit: evaluate each allowed bonus by running type-level DA in each scenario. The likely running time is polynomial in the support, programs, scenarios, and number of allowed bonuses, assuming the fractional DA procedure is formalized and has polynomial complexity. That is a plausible Class A question, though the paper itself does not establish this algorithmic result. Natural follow-ups are how the answer changes with joint bonuses across programs, uncertainty in the scenario probabilities, or application behavior that responds to the announced policy.

The weak point is substantial: there is no named complexity result here to anchor the mirror, and the paper gives no evidence that the support of *complete* types is small. Further, for one program and a bounded bonus range, the resulting optimization may be a fairly modest computational problem. My positive case is therefore for the continuous form of the paper’s central policy-design problem—not for claiming that this paper has already mapped a complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary here that establishes a computational complexity result. The paper’s contribution is empirical: it compares ways to predict applicant pools and choose bonus policies. Gale–Shapley stability is cited background. So there is no result to anchor a complexity mirror to.

The strongest candidate is the proposed mass-based bonus-design problem. It preserves the paper’s decision and objective, but it has a serious support problem. A complete applicant type must include the protected-group label, scores at every program, and the full preference list. The paper reports 60,000 applicants, but gives no evidence that these complete profiles repeat often enough for the support size \(\tau\) to be much smaller than the applicant count. If profiles are mostly unique, the “continuous society” is just the same applicant-level instance written as a distribution. Coarsening scores or preferences to produce fewer types can merge applicants with different priorities or choices, changing the DA outcome the model is meant to preserve.

A deliberately standardized admissions system could have many applicants sharing a small set of complete profiles. That would make the population model sensible, but it still leaves a narrow policy decision: choose a bonus for one program, from the admissions system’s allowed bonus values, and evaluate DA under an explicit set of scenarios. The paper itself searches over bonus values and runs DA on sampled application sets. Replacing each scenario’s applicants with type masses would change the representation of those runs; it would not expose the kind of pricing or optimization bottleneck that motivates the programme’s continuous-optimization toolbox. In that regime, uncertainty and policy quality remain the substantive challenges: how to predict next year’s applicants, and how to handle behavior that changes after a policy is announced.

One could enlarge the problem to choose bonuses jointly across programs, optimize against an uncertain distribution rather than a supplied scenario list, or model strategic responses. Those could produce richer computational questions, but they add a new global policy or uncertainty problem; they are not established by this paper’s per-program experiments. The proposed expected-objective formulation also differs from the paper’s procedure of averaging bonuses optimized separately on sampled application sets.

So the negative case is strongest as a reason not to prioritize this paper for continuization: it supplies no complexity result, the data do not demonstrate a compressed complete-type regime, and the best faithful formulation looks like DA evaluation inside a small policy search. It is not airtight. A real admissions system with many repeated complete profiles and a consequential mass-based DA question could still make a worthwhile extension, but the paper gives little reason to expect that case.
