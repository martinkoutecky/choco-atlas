The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of roles \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) summing to \(1\), symmetric rational valuations \(w_{ts}=w_{st}\), and a rational threshold \(r\), decide whether an atomless society with role masses \(\mu_t\) can be measurably partitioned into coalitions so that its essential minimum utility is at least \(r\). Each type-\(t\) agent in coalition \(K\) has utility \(\sum_{s\in T} w_{ts}\operatorname{mass}(K\cap\Omega_s)\); require the graph of nonzero role interactions to have treewidth at most \(2\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic high-multiplicity symmetric ASHG with rational role masses and valuations, whose role-interaction graph has treewidth at most \(2\). A solution is a finite or countable family of coalition mass vectors \(q^j\), with \(\sum_j q^j_t=\mu_t\); maximize the least utility \(\sum_s w_{ts}q^j_s\) among roles \(t\) represented in each coalition.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s weak NP-hardness result for maximum egalitarian partitions in symmetric ASHGs. It leaves the other results, including those for Nash stability, maximum utilitarian, maximum envy-free, and other maximum egalitarian graph classes, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does threshold feasibility admit a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of roles and input bit length when the role-interaction graph has treewidth at most \(2\)?
  • Can optimal coalition mass profiles be represented with a finite support bound that yields an exact algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is maximum egalitarian coalition formation in a large organization whose agents fall into a small number of interchangeable roles. Think of project teams formed from employees in a few recurring job categories: everyone in role \(t\) has the same valuation \(w_{ts}\) for a teammate in role \(s\), and only a sparse set of role pairs interact. If the organization has \(N\) people but only \(\tau\) populated roles, with \(N\gg\tau\), the role categories are genuine high-multiplicity types.

My anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It shows that computing a maximum egalitarian partition in a *symmetric* ASHG is weakly NP-hard even when the interaction graph is series-parallel, has treewidth at most \(2\), and has pathwidth at most \(4\). The proof reduces from Partition. Thus the hardness holds on very restricted sparse graphs, and arises from numerical values rather than from large treewidth.

Here is the continuous question I would put to the authors. Call it Role-ASHG MaxEgal\(_\infty\) on TW2. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of roles, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and symmetric rational valuations \(w_{ts}=w_{st}\). Require the graph of nonzero role-pair valuations to have treewidth at most \(2\). The society is an atomless population \(\Omega=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}\Omega_t\), where \(\Omega_t\) has mass \(\mu_t\). A feasible solution is a measurable partition of \(\Omega\) into coalitions. An agent of role \(t\) in coalition \(K\) receives

\[ U_t(K)=\sum_{s\in T} w_{ts}\,\operatorname{mass}(K\cap\Omega_s). \]

The problem is to find a coalition structure maximizing the essential minimum utility,

\[ \lambda^*=\sup_{\mathcal P}\operatorname{ess\,inf}_{x\in\Omega} U_{\mathcal P}(x), \]

and return an optimal value and coalition structure. A structure can be represented by the role-mass vectors \(q^j_t=\operatorname{mass}(K_j\cap\Omega_t)\) of its positive-mass coalitions, with any remaining agents assigned to singleton coalitions. Each agent still belongs to one coalition; the continuum lets a role’s population be divided among different coalitions.

This preserves the paper’s underlying question: which coalition structure makes the least satisfied agent as well off as possible? The objective and additive utility are unchanged. What changes is that the input describes repeated role types by their masses, rather than listing every named agent and every edge. The authors should recognize this as a high-multiplicity Max Egalitarian ASHG question, though they may reasonably debate whether the role-level sparsity restriction is the right analogue of their individual-graph restriction.

I would tentatively expect Class A for this mirror, with complexity measured in \(\tau\) and the rational input length. The Partition reduction behind Theorem 5 depends on assigning whole item gadgets to one side or the other; atomless role mass can be split between coalitions. That makes it plausible that this particular numerical hardness dissolves and that a bounded-treewidth method can exploit the resulting fractional choices. It is a hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 5: the exact question is whether feasible coalition-mass profiles admit an efficient optimization method, or whether selecting compatible coalition supports creates new hardness. Further questions include whether the optimum can be computed in polynomial time for treewidth \(2\), whether algorithms extend to larger treewidth, and how an exact continuous solution can be rounded into a high-multiplicity discrete coalition structure.

The scope here is deliberately just Theorem 5’s Max Egalitarian result. I would not use it to claim a mirror for the paper’s Nash-stability or Max Utilitarian results.

The weakest point is that the paper’s sparse graph has edges between individual agents, while this mirror is sparse at the role-interaction level. Expanding repeated roles into named agents can make each nonzero role interaction a dense bipartite graph. So this is not a literal continuum limit of every bounded-treewidth graph instance in the paper. Its case rests on the large-organization regime being a sensible instance of the same ASHG problem: agents have additive utilities, form coalitions, and are indistinguishable within their complete role type. If the authors accept role-level interactions as their graph input, Theorem 5 gives a strong and concrete hardness anchor whose continuous fate is worth classifying.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a real computational result, so the “no named result to mirror” objection does not apply. The strongest objection is that its treewidth restriction belongs to the graph of individual agents, while the proposed mirror puts treewidth on a graph of roles. Those are different graph families.

The reduction in Theorem 5 assigns each Partition item its own three-vertex gadget, with weights determined by that item’s \(a_i\). To preserve those interactions in a high-multiplicity version, a type must encode the item and its gadget position. The number of types then grows with the number of items, so the reduction offers no population compression. If instead agents of a role interact uniformly with all agents of another role, the lifted individual graph has complete blocks between those roles. It can have large treewidth even when the role graph has treewidth two. The proposed mirror therefore does not continueize the paper’s sparse-instance result; it changes what sparsity means.

That objection survives a better formulation only if the target is specifically “Max Egalitarian on sparse individual graphs.” A role-based version could be made precise and computationally interesting, but its bounded-treewidth claim would concern a new, dense-at-the-individual-level ASHG. The paper’s Partition gadgets do not establish that this is the continuous fate of its treewidth-two hardness.

Still, I cannot honestly turn that mismatch into a convincing claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large organization with repeated roles and role-based valuations is a plausible high-multiplicity ASHG. Fractional role populations then pose a sensible Max Egalitarian question, even if Theorem 5 is a poor anchor for the role graph’s treewidth. Under the programme’s standard, that one plausible scenario may be enough. The negative case is therefore weak: it can challenge the connection to this particular hardness result, but not rule out the role-level mirror as worthwhile.
