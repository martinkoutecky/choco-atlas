The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(A\), a finite catalogue of preference types \(\theta=(U_\theta,\delta_\theta)\), and a rational uncertainty set \(\mathcal{M}\) for their masses, find an adaptive policy that queries pairs of alternatives within response-history cohorts. Each query returns the exact masses answering \(x\succ y\), \(y\succ x\), or indifference. The policy must identify, for every society \(\mu\in\mathcal{M}\) consistent with the answers, exactly the alternatives acceptable to at least an \(\alpha\)-fraction of society, while minimizing worst-case total mass queried.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity public-consultation model with a distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference types \(\theta=(U_\theta,\delta_\theta)\); each type answers pairwise RORi queries according to its utility and threshold, and the output is the set acceptable to at least an \(\alpha\)-fraction of the population.

The objection that survived

With a known type catalogue, aggregate responses measure the unknown mass distribution, so the computational core becomes distribution learning rather than the paper’s inference of one decision maker’s utility.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Proposition 4.1 and Algorithm 1’s acceptable-set target to a quota-acceptable set; it leaves Proposition 4.2, Proposition 4.3, Algorithm 2, the query heuristics, and the empirical comparisons aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the optimal adaptive policy as a function of the number of supported types and the encoding length of the mass uncertainty set?
  • Can useful approximation or parameterized algorithms identify the quota-acceptable set without recovering the full mass distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a group version of the paper’s pairwise elicitation problem. I would not claim that the paper already studies a continuous population: it studies one decision maker. The proposed mirror asks whether its RORi method extends naturally to a large group whose members share a small number of preference types.

A plausible regime is a public consultation on a finite set of transit or housing proposals. There may be hundreds of thousands of residents, but far fewer distinct preference types: each type has the same criteria, utility model and indifference threshold. A type is \(\theta=(U_\theta,\delta_\theta)\), and its mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of residents of that type. The mass of a type answers the same pairwise question in the same way. This makes the population, rather than the alternatives or the outcome space, the continuous object.

My anchor is Proposition 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that, if the decision maker’s true utility \(U^T\) belongs to the assumed utility-model class, Algorithm 1 terminates with exactly the set of truly acceptable alternatives. Here \(x\) is truly acceptable when \(U^T(x)\ge U^T(y)-\delta^T\) for every \(y\). The algorithm repeatedly uses LP1 to find possibly acceptable alternatives and LP2 to choose an unasked pair that is not necessarily indistinguishable, then updates its constraints from the decision maker’s answer. Proposition 4.1 is a correctness guarantee, not a polynomial-time or hardness classification; the paper gives no named result of that kind.

The corresponding continuous problem could be called Continuous Supermajority RORi Elicitation. An instance gives a finite alternative set \(A\), their criterion values, a finite catalogue \(T\) of possible complete preference types \((U_\theta,\delta_\theta)\), a rational uncertainty set for the type masses, an acceptance quota \(\alpha\), and the elicitation history so far. A query selects a pair \(x,y\) and a current response-history cohort. Each member of that cohort answers \(x\succ y\) if \(U_\theta(x)>U_\theta(y)+\delta_\theta\), answers \(y\succ x\) under the symmetric condition, and otherwise declares indifference. The answer reports the exact mass in each of the three response groups; those groups can then be queried separately.

For the actual, unknown society \(\mu\), call \(x\) acceptable to the quota if at least an \(\alpha\) fraction of society finds it individually acceptable—that is, \(\sum_{\theta\in T}\mu_\theta\,\mathbf{1}[U_\theta(x)\ge U_\theta(y)-\delta_\theta\ \forall y\in A]\ge\alpha\). The task is to construct an adaptive pair-query policy that returns exactly the quota-acceptable set for every society consistent with the observed answers, minimizing the worst-case total mass queried. A solution is the policy together with its terminal set of alternatives.

This preserves the paper’s alternatives, criterion-based utility models, personal indifference thresholds, pairwise questions and aim of identifying acceptable choices. It replaces the single DM with a society and makes the output a mass-based choice. I expect certification for a fixed query history to be tractable: each type’s acceptability is fixed, so its mass is a linear expression over the feasible mass distributions. I would expect the optimal adaptive policy problem to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C), because the query tree and its mass-weighted cost introduce combinatorics that Proposition 4.1 does not address. Further questions include how the complexity depends on the number of supported types, whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist, and whether aggregate responses can identify the quota set without identifying the whole type distribution.

The weak point is the change from one decision maker’s acceptable set to a quota-defined group choice. The authors might regard that as group MCDA rather than a mirror of their specific problem; moreover, Proposition 4.1 proves nothing about this population version. That concession matters. Still, this is a closer connection than merely applying their algorithms to fractional outcomes: the same pairwise RORi question is being asked across a high-multiplicity society, with each type’s mass determining how much of the population shares its answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4.1 is a correctness guarantee, not a complexity result: it says Algorithm 1 returns the right set when one decision maker has a true utility \(U^T\) and threshold \(\delta^T\) in the assumed model. Its key invariant is that every answer remains compatible with that same pair \((U^T,\delta^T)\).

Replicating that decision maker across a population makes the continuous dimension inert. If everyone has the same type, they give the same answer to every pair, so one response carries the information; representing the group by mass adds nothing. If there are \(\tau\) known types, running the procedure separately for each type is just \(\tau\) copies of the original problem. Neither case gives Proposition 4.1 a new population-scale computational question.

The proposed heterogeneous version is more substantive, but it changes the object being elicited. Different types do not share a single \(U^T,\delta^T\), so their pooled answers need not form the semiorder that RORi learns. A quota over individually acceptable alternatives supplies a new group-choice objective; a welfare ranking would require an aggregation rule the paper does not provide. And the proposed exact-mass response oracle makes the unknown quantity the distribution \(\mu\): with known types, pair responses are already determined by the catalogue, and each query supplies a linear measurement of their masses. That may be a worthwhile active-query problem, but its core is learning a distribution from aggregate responses, not the paper’s robust ordinal regression guarantee. If the types are unknown too, the task becomes a separate problem of learning both types and their prevalence.

There is a plausible public-consultation application, so I cannot rule out a worthwhile research direction in every scenario. The negative case is strongest at the boundary: preserving the paper’s single coherent decision maker leaves no meaningful population axis; introducing a heterogeneous society requires a new target and a new query model. That makes the proposed work look like group preference elicitation inspired by this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of Proposition 4.1.
