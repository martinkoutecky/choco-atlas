The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\lambda_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational entitlement rates \(b_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\lambda_t b_t=1\), and a non-atomic probability valuation \(\nu_t\) for each type specified by a finite rational piecewise-constant density, let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. For each \(N=kD\), form \(N\lambda_t\) agents of type \(t\), each requiring value at least \(b_t/N\), and let \(K_N\) be the minimum number of cuts in a division meeting every individual requirement. Determine or approximate to additive error \(\varepsilon\) the asymptotic cut rate \(\kappa(\lambda,\nu,b)=\limsup_{k\to\infty} K_{kD}/(kD)\).

The model it lives in

A society is represented by mass \(\lambda_t\) on each complete valuation-entitlement type \(t\). In its finite replication, the decision is an individual cake assignment meeting each agent’s scaled claim, the cost is the number of cuts, and the target is the limiting upper cut rate per agent.

The objection that survived

The rate is defined through finite replications, so it may be viewed as an asymptotic statistic of discrete divisions rather than a problem solved directly on an atomless society; the fixed-type distribution and normalized cut objective make this mismatch nonfatal.

The case FOR (proponent)

The only anchor I can defend is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper. It says: for every \(n\ge2\), every \(n\) non-atomic probability measures \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n\) on \([0,1]\), and every set of nonnegative demands \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\) summing to \(1\), there is a disproportionate division meeting every agent’s demand with at most \(3n-4\) cuts. The authors describe the proof as constructive and combinatorial. This is an algorithmic construction result, but it does not establish a runtime complexity class: the paper gives no finite encoding for arbitrary measures or running-time bound. It has no named P/NP-type result.

My proposed mirror is Asymptotic Type-Mass Disproportionate Division. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\lambda_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a non-atomic valuation \(\nu_t\) for each type, and a nonnegative entitlement rate \(b_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_t b_t=1\). For a finite computational version, take the \(\nu_t\) to have rational piecewise-constant densities. Let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. For each sufficiently large \(N\) divisible by \(D\), form a society of \(N\lambda_t\) agents of type \(t\), each with demand \(b_t/N\). These demands sum to \(1\). Let \(K_N\) be the minimum number of cuts in a division meeting every individual demand. The problem is to compute, or approximate to any requested additive accuracy,
\[ \kappa(\lambda,\nu,b)=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{K_{kD}}{kD}, \]
and give a procedure producing divisions whose cut rate approaches \(\kappa\).

This is a high-multiplicity regime: \(N\) grows while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct complete types stays fixed. Mass is the population share of a type; the action remains assigning cake to individual agents; the objective is asymptotic cuts per agent. The demand \(b_t/N\) expresses the paper’s normalization that total claims sum to one. Theorem 1.1 gives this mirror an immediate upper bound, \(\kappa\le3\), and a constructive family with at most \(3N-4\) cuts at every replication size. The exact-rate problem is a further question, not a result proved in the paper.

I think the authors would recognize this as their disproportionate-division problem viewed in a fixed-type, large-population regime: the paper itself defines the extremal cut function \(f(n)\) and asks how its linear bounds can be tightened. The mirror preserves individual guarantees and the cut objective; it does not turn fractional outcomes or random perturbations into the continuous object. Its scope is just Theorem 1.1’s bounded-cut construction, not the paper’s topological conjecture.

For this mirror, I tentatively expect continuum-specific difficulty for computing the exact rate \(\kappa\). The theorem supplies a tractable-looking rate-\(3\) construction, but it neither computes profile-specific optima nor proves a runtime bound. There is no discrete hardness result here to transfer. Natural follow-up questions are whether the limsup is always a limit, whether fixed finite type support changes the best universal rate, and whether the conjectured \(2n-2\) cut bound forces rate \(2\) in this regime.

The weak point is substantial: the paper proves a cut bound, not a complexity classification, and the exact asymptotic-rate question is an extrapolation. Also, this is a limit of finite divisions rather than an allocation to literally infinitely many agents. The case survives because it asks about the authors’ own cut-complexity objective in a credible high-multiplicity regime; it should not be presented as filling the programme’s full computational-complexity gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1.1, and it is the paper’s strongest possible one for the proponent: it gives a constructive cut bound. I would not dismiss it as a mere existence theorem. But it gives no finite input model or running-time bound for finding a division. The proposed rational piecewise-constant inputs supply those choices; they make a new computational problem well-posed, but do not turn the theorem itself into a complexity result.

The proposed scaling is sensible: with type mass \(\lambda_t\), each of its \(N\lambda_t\) copies can have demand \(b_t/N\), preserving the paper’s total-demand normalization and each individual guarantee. Repeated cohorts of agents with identical valuations are plausible. Those are not objections.

The deeper problem is what the limit retains. In the paper, the output is a partition into individual bundles, and the resource being bounded is the number of cuts. A continuous population has no individual atoms to receive those bundles. One can preserve the cut objective by studying finite replications and taking \(\limsup K_N/N\), but then the target is an asymptotic invariant of a sequence of discrete allocation problems; the type distribution supplies the sequence’s parameters, while the cuts and individual assignments still live at finite \(N\). Alternatively, one could ask for a type-level or fractional allocation. That gives a continuous object, but replaces individual guarantees or the cut-count objective, so it is a new model rather than a direct mirror of Theorem 1.1.

So I would reject this as an established continuous-complexity mirror and record it as a plausible extension to asymptotic cake-cutting complexity. That is a narrow verdict, not a strong universal negative: the fixed-type regime is credible, and a limit problem could be worth studying if ChoCo includes asymptotic objectives. The case against it rests on that mismatch in computational object, not on the answer the proposed problem might yield.
