What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers Theorem 29's majority-safety result for ALL; Theorem 22's MALL counterexample is a boundary comparison. It leaves the paper's other logics and agenda-safety characterizations aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper that meets your anchor requirement. It proves no result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar; Section 11 explicitly says computational complexity was not discussed. So I can’t honestly offer a qualifying anchor.

The nearest useful result is Theorem 29, proved in this paper: ALL is safe for the majority rule. In full, for any agenda in the additive fragment of linear logic, majority aggregation of complete, robustly consistent individual judgment sets produces an aggregate that is robustly consistent in ALL. The proof uses Property 28: every provable sequent in ALL contains exactly two formulas. Thus an inconsistent set cannot require three or more formulas, and a strict majority cannot accept both formulas of an inconsistent pair: their supporters would overlap, giving an individual judgment set containing that pair. Property 28 is attributed to Hughes and van Glabbeek (2003); the safety theorem is proved here. This is a judgment-aggregation theorem, but not a complexity anchor of the kind requested.

The continuous problem I would put to a prover is Continuous ALL Majority Safety. An instance is a finite, unit-free, complement-closed agenda \(\Phi\) of ALL formulas and a finite support of \(\tau\) distinct judgment types \(J^1,\ldots,J^\tau\). Each type is a complete, robustly consistent judgment set; its mass \(\mu_r\) is a positive binary rational, with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). Define \(\mu(\phi)=\sum_{r:\phi\in J^r}\mu_r\) and the aggregate \(M_\mu=\{\phi\in\Phi:\mu(\phi)>1/2\}\). The problem is to compute \(M_\mu\) and decide whether it is robustly consistent in ALL, returning an inconsistent submultiset if not. If completeness of the aggregate is required too, add the promise that no complementary pair has mass exactly \(1/2\). The expected answer is tractability: sum the rational masses and select the formulas above the threshold; the continuous version of Theorem 29 then guarantees consistency.

This is a credible population mirror. In a large membership organization or public consultation, a type is a complete pattern of judgments over a fixed agenda, and mass is the fraction of participants with that pattern. The regime is many participants but relatively few recurring judgment types, so \(N\gg\tau\). It preserves the paper’s agenda, logic, rationality assumptions, and majority decision; it changes the population from a finite list of people to a distribution over types. I expect the authors to recognize that as their majority-aggregation question. The direct continuous proof is also transparent: two formulas each supported by mass greater than \(1/2\) must co-occur in some positive-mass type.

A useful boundary check is Theorem 22, also proved here: MALL is not safe for majority. Its three-agent counterexample yields the inconsistent majority judgment set \(\{A,B,\neg(A\otimes B)\}\). Giving those three types mass \(1/3\) each produces the same continuous majority outcome, so continuization does not remove this failure. The proposed mirror covers the majority-safety result for ALL, with that MALL example as contrast; it does not cover the paper’s broader characterizations of safe agendas or its other logics.

The weak point is decisive for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme: this mirror is a simple weighted aggregation calculation, and its substantive guarantee follows from Theorem 29. It does not reveal whether discrete computational hardness dissolves in a continuum, because the paper supplies no such hardness anchor. Further work could ask how the paper’s agenda-safety characterizations extend to rational mass distributions and what complexity arises for succinctly represented agendas or types, but those would be new questions rather than mirrors of named complexity results here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is also the narrowest: the paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 29 is a logical safety theorem about majority aggregation; Theorem 22 gives a counterexample to safety. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem. Section 11 confirms that complexity was left open for future work. The proponent’s proposed anchor is therefore a coherent extension of one of the paper’s results, but it does not meet the stated computational-anchor criterion.

The continuous version of Theorem 29 is sensible as a high-multiplicity model. For an explicit support of \(\tau\) judgment types and an agenda \(\Phi\), compute each \(\mu(\phi)\) by summing the masses of types accepting \(\phi\), then compare it with \(1/2\). That takes \(O(\tau|\Phi|)\) rational additions and comparisons, with polynomial bit complexity in the input size. Given the promise that each type is robustly consistent, Theorem 29’s argument already guarantees that the resulting aggregate is robustly consistent. So the proposed task’s consistency decision always returns “yes”; its nontrivial computation is just a weighted tally. This is a valid aggregation problem, but it does not give ChoCo an algorithmic question about bribery, control, campaigning, robustness, or a difficult optimization problem.

The stronger version—asking whether an agenda is safe for every continuous mass distribution—does not supply a population-computation problem either. For ALL, safety is already the content of Theorem 29. The mass argument is immediate: two sets of supporters each having mass greater than \(1/2\) must overlap, so some positive-mass type accepts both formulas. In logics where safety depends on the agenda, asking whether all mass profiles are safe moves the computational work to checking an agenda property. That could be a worthwhile follow-up to this paper, but it is a different question from the computational landscape ChoCo is charting. Succinctly encoding exponentially many types might make tallying hard, but that would introduce a new representation assumption rather than mirror the paper’s results.

Theorem 22’s three-agent example also extends cleanly: assign its three judgment types mass \(1/3\) each. That demonstrates the continuous model can express the paper’s counterexample. It does not provide a computational anchor. One could instead study the region of mass distributions that produce inconsistent outcomes, but that is a new distributional-robustness question, not a computational result in the paper.

I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous judgment-aggregation project. A large consultation with recurring judgment types is plausible, and the proposed mass model is one the authors would likely recognize. The negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor; it is weak as a claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile in any broader research programme.
