The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the population model: a type \(\theta\) is a complete bundle of preferences, endowments, and observable labor skills, and its mass is its share of an atomless population. In a large metropolitan area, many residents could share a small number of such profiles while belonging to different jurisdiction types. The paper itself treats the population this way, so it is strong evidence that a high-multiplicity regime is sensible for community formation.

There is a strict limit, though: the paper has no named computational-complexity result to use as an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, establishes equilibrium existence under assumptions A1–A5 and B1–B3. Theorem 2, also proved here, establishes that equilibria are in the core and, with A5 and A6, that the core coincides with the set of equilibria. Proposition 1, proved here, gives a sufficient condition for equilibrium with heterogeneous communities. These are existence, welfare, and composition results; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Calling one a computational anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

The nearest computational follow-on I can make concrete is Approximate Berglas Equilibrium. An instance would specify a finite type set \(\Theta\), binary-rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\), and a finite set \(\Omega\) of allowable jurisdiction formats. Each \(\omega\in\Omega\) specifies a public project, production technology, and finite community profile \(n^\omega\). The input also gives endowments, public-good costs, and explicitly encoded utility and production functions. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), find jurisdiction masses \(\nu_\omega\), private-good bundles, and prices, wages, taxes, and transfers that satisfy the paper’s equilibrium conditions within \(\varepsilon\). In particular, type masses must match, \(\sum_\omega \nu_\omega n^\omega_\theta=\mu_\theta\); types assigned positive mass to a jurisdiction must be within \(\varepsilon\) of their best affordable choice; and goods markets, production, and public-good financing must clear within \(\varepsilon\). The solution is an approximate equilibrium allocation and supporting prices, not an encoding.

This is plausibly recognizable to Luque as an algorithmic question about his model. It is not, however, a mirror of a named computational result in this paper: the underlying population is already continuous, and the paper gives no discrete complexity result whose behavior could change under continuization. Its computational status would also depend heavily on how the utility and production functions are encoded. I therefore cannot honestly assign it to Class A, B, or C from the paper.

That is the case’s weakest point, and it is decisive under the requested anchor rule. The paper supports the *plausibility* of continuous population models, but it supplies no eligible computational anchor. The approximate-equilibrium question is a possible research extension, not a compliant continuous mirror of a result established here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper contains no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves equilibrium existence; Theorem 2 proves a core–equilibrium relationship; Proposition 1 gives a sufficient condition for heterogeneous communities. None defines an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity result. A computational question built from any of them would be an extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The best candidate is the proponent’s Approximate Berglas Equilibrium. It is a plausible research question, but its finite list of jurisdiction formats and explicit encodings of utility and production functions are extra choices needed to make the problem algorithmic. Different choices define different problems. More fundamentally, the paper already assumes an atomless population with finitely many complete types. Turning that model into a computational task would not make the population continuous; it would add a computational layer to a model that is continuous already.

The alternatives have the same gap. One could ask for an algorithm to find an equilibrium under specified encodings, check whether a core allocation exists, or test Proposition 1’s sufficient condition. But these are new computational formulations of analytic results. A finite-agent counterpart might support a high-multiplicity study, but the paper states no finite-agent complexity result for that study to mirror.

The positive case is strong on one point: the paper demonstrates that a high-multiplicity population regime is sensible for community formation. That supports the ChoCo programme’s modelling premise. It does not supply the computational anchor the programme needs. This is a strong negative case against calling any of these questions a continuous mirror of this paper, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile computational project inspired by the model.
