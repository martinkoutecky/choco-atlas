The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is the paper’s own closing suggestion: future work could exploit a population of users or tasks, whose utility functions for similar flights may be similar, using clustering within VEIL’s LP formulations.

A plausible mirror would be a large travel service handling many users choosing from the same flight inventory. A type would comprise the shared flight alternatives and feature vectors, current pairwise preferences, and utility-update objective; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of users of type \(t\). Given the support and rational masses, the task would be to find, for each type, bounded utility weights satisfying its expressed preferences and optimizing VEIL’s update objective, then return the highest-utility flight for that type. This is a credible scale-up of the paper’s method, and the authors might recognize the setting from their stated future-work direction. It would likely be Class A: the type-wise updates are LPs, and the aggregate problem is separable.

But this is only a plausible extension, not an anchor-based case. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its LP update scheme and convergence experiments are computational content, but neither supplies an eligible named anchor to restate. The proposed cohort problem also batches independent per-user updates, so it does not show that continuization changes the computational landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts at the requested anchor: the proponent names no numbered computational result, because the paper has none. It presents an LP update method and convergence experiments, but proves no complexity classification for them. The closing suggestion about populations is future work, not a result to mirror.

Taking that suggestion as generously as possible, let a type encode a user’s full VEIL state: the available flights and features, stated preferences, default utility, and current weights. A large travel service could plausibly have many users with identical states, so this is a credible high-multiplicity regime. For each type, run the LP update and return its preferred flight. But each update is determined entirely by that type’s state; the masses do not affect its constraints or answer. This formulation batches independent VEIL sessions rather than posing a computational question about a continuous society.

One could make the masses matter by adding a shared query budget, a clustering objective, or a policy for assigning common recommendations. Those could be worthwhile population problems, and the paper’s future-work remark points in that direction. They require a new objective or mechanism, though: VEIL lets users supply preferences and does not specify how to allocate questions or trade off one user’s outcome against another’s. The resulting computational problem would be an extension of the research agenda, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest against the proposed mirror, not against every project one could build from VEIL. Its weakness is the universal claim: the paper explicitly invites work on populations, and repeated cohorts with shared elicitation states are plausible. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous project there; I can say the proponent has not anchored one in a named computational result, and the straightforward population version leaves the masses out of the computation.
