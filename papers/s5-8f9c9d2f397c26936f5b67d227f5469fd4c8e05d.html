The continuous mirror question

Given supported ranking types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), school categories \(O\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_o\), and feasible type-level shares \(p_{t,o}\) satisfying \(\sum_o p_{t,o}=1\) and \(\sum_t \mu_t p_{t,o}=q_o\), does there exist a probability distribution over feasible measurable assignments of the atomless population such that every assignment in its support is Pareto-optimal and the expected share of type \(t\) assigned to category \(o\) is \(p_{t,o}\)?

The model it lives in

Use atomless sets of applicants with masses \(\mu_t\), one for each ranking type, and categories with supplies \(q_o\). A measurable assignment gives each applicant one category; its aggregate variables are \(x_{t,o}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(o\). Decide whether \(x_{t,o}=\mu_t p_{t,o}\) is the expectation of a lottery over feasible Pareto-optimal assignments. This is a feasibility question, with no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s test for ex-post-efficient implementation. It leaves aside the paper’s results on robust ex-post efficiency, random serial dictatorship, and the remaining geometric and complexity claims.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the membership problem admit an algorithm polynomial in the number of categories, the support size, and the input bit length?
  • Can a hardness reduction survive when each ranking type is atomless and its mass can be split among categories?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I see is the paper’s ex-post-efficiency test in a large school-assignment cohort. The agents are applicants, and a type is a complete strict ranking of the available school categories. A large cohort might have \(N\) applicants but only \(\tau\) distinct rankings, with \(\tau \ll N\). Each school category has a per-capita seat supply \(q_o\), with \(\sum_o q_o=1\). Each applicant still receives one indivisible seat; the continuous variables describe shares of each type assigned to each school.

Formally, let \(T\) be the supported ranking types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and let \(O\) be the school categories. The input includes a feasible type-level assignment \(p\), where \(p_{t,o}\) is the share of type \(t\) assigned to \(o\):
\[ \sum_{o\in O}p_{t,o}=1,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_{t,o}=q_o. \]
A deterministic assignment is a measurable map from the population to school categories that gives every applicant one seat and meets the supplies. It is Pareto optimal if no feasible reassignment weakly improves almost every applicant and strictly improves a positive mass of applicants. The continuous question is: does there exist a lottery over Pareto-optimal deterministic assignments whose expected type-level shares equal \(p\)? A yes-instance can be answered by such a lottery; a no-instance has no such implementation. The lottery preserves the paper’s implementation question. The population distribution, rather than that lottery, is what has been continuized.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: testing whether a given finite random assignment is ex-post efficient is NP-complete. In the paper’s model, the input is agents’ strict rankings and a feasible bistochastic assignment; the question is whether it is a convex combination of Pareto-optimal deterministic assignments. The authors prove membership in NP using a polynomial-size decomposition and prove NP-hardness by reduction from 3-SAT. My mirror asks that same implementation question for a population described by its ranking masses and for seat supplies that scale with population.

This seems like a problem the authors could recognize: it keeps the one-sided assignment setting, ordinal preferences, feasible assignments, and implementation by Pareto-optimal deterministic assignments. The school scenario also makes high multiplicity credible: many applicants can share a ranking, and capacities naturally scale with the cohort. The model does co-modify the object side: a school category represents interchangeable seats, and the allocation table records averages over type mass. That is important to the mirror, not a claim that the paper already studies continuous populations.

My forecast for this mirror is Class A, tentatively. In the finite proof, each named agent receives one named object in each deterministic assignment, and the reduction coordinates those discrete choices. In the mirror, any positive mass of a type can be split across schools while every individual still receives one seat. That seems to remove the reduction’s most immediate source of Boolean rigidity and makes continuous optimization a plausible route. It does not establish tractability: choosing among Pareto-optimal allocations may retain hard combinatorics, especially as \(\tau\) and \(m\) grow. The concrete question for a prover is whether this decision problem has an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or whether a hardness reduction survives type-level splitting.

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s ex-post-efficiency membership question; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s other results. It raises related questions about a two-way dictionary with finite high-multiplicity assignments, whether hardness returns when \(\tau\) is unrestricted, and the complexity of optimizing a linear objective over the convex hull of Pareto-optimal continuous assignments.

My weakest point is the change from individually named objects to categories with per-capita supplies. If the authors regard the identity of each object as essential to their model, they may reject that co-modification. The school-seat setting is why I think the question remains a credible mirror: seats at a school are interchangeable, while each applicant’s assignment remains integral.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor: testing ex-post efficiency is NP-complete. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes what the theorem’s input and decomposition mean. In the paper, a point is an \(n\times n\) matrix of probabilities for named agents and named objects, and its decompositions are lotteries over permutation matrices. With ranking types and per-capita school capacities, a deterministic assignment can split each atomless type across schools. Its aggregate matrix is already fractional. The mirror therefore asks about the convex hull of aggregate Pareto-efficient allocations, not the paper’s convex hull of Pareto-optimal permutations.

That gap is sharpest in the SAT reduction: its variable and clause gadgets are individual agents whose assignments coordinate discrete choices. A type-mass version can divide a gadget type among schools, so the reduction does not transfer as written. This is a warning about the bridge and the interpretation of any result, not an argument that the continuous question has an uninteresting answer.

The better version of the mirror survives the obvious objection. Large applicant cohorts with repeated rankings and school categories with capacities proportional to cohort size are plausible. Treating school seats as interchangeable does move from one-to-one assignment to a capacitated assignment model, but it is a natural population-scale formulation, and the paper’s broader resource-allocation setting gives its authors a credible reason to recognize it. Keeping every seat individually named would hew more closely to the paper, but makes the category-based cohort story less natural; that does not defeat the stronger formulation.

So I cannot honestly establish the requested universal negative. The mirror is less direct than the proponent suggests, and its link to Theorem 2 needs careful definition. But the theorem is present, a plausible high-multiplicity regime exists, and the continuous question remains well-posed. My case against the mirror is weak.
