The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper is a near-miss, not a qualifying positive case under the programme’s anchor rule. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting an algorithmic-complexity result such as membership in \(\mathrm{P}\), NP-hardness, or parameterized tractability.

The closest results are structural:

  • Proposition 17, proved in the paper, says that for deterministic mechanisms with dichotomous preferences, a constraint set \(S\) is sufficient if every non-singleton acceptable set \(Y\) has, for each \(x\in Y\), a proper subset \(Y'\subset Y\) containing \(x\) with \((Y,Y')\in S\), and all constraints out of singleton types are present.
  • Proposition 18, also proved in the paper, says that if \(x\in Z\), \(Z\neq\{x\}\), and \((\{x\},Z)\notin S\), then there is a deterministic mechanism satisfying \(S\) but violating \((\{x\},Z)\).
  • Proposition 16 gives the analogous strong independence result for probabilistic mechanisms.

These are not complexity anchors. The paper’s computational discussion—“easy manipulation” and computational versions of Gibbard–Satterthwaite—points to results cited from elsewhere; it does not prove or classify a computational problem itself. Lemma 11’s LP-duality argument is a certificate theorem, not a stated polynomial-time algorithm.

There is nevertheless a genuine continuous-population mirror nearby. Let \(X\) be a set of \(m\) possible meeting slots, and let a type \(Y\subseteq X\) mean that an agent accepts exactly the slots in \(Y\). A large scheduling service may have population distribution \(\mu=(\mu_Y)_{Y\subseteq X}\), with many agents sharing each acceptable-set type and \(|\operatorname{supp}\mu|\ll 2^m\). A deterministic mechanism is \(f:2^X\to X\). For a constraint set \(S\), define

\[ \mathrm{SIC}_S(f) \iff \forall (Y,Z)\in S,\quad f(Y)\in Y\ \lor\ f(Z)\notin Y, \]

and

\[ \mathrm{IC}_Y(f) \iff \forall Z\subseteq X,\quad f(Y)\in Y\ \lor\ f(Z)\notin Y. \]

A precise population problem is:

\[ \textsc{Mass-Supported Local Sufficiency} \]

Given \(X\), rational \(\mu\), \(S\), and \(q\in[0,1]\), decide whether

\[ \inf_{f:\,\mathrm{SIC}_S(f)} \sum_{Y\subseteq X}\mu_Y\mathbf 1[\mathrm{IC}_Y(f)] \ge q. \]

Thus the question is whether every mechanism satisfying the monitored constraints protects at least \(q\) of the population from all profitable misreports. A negative witness is a finite mechanism table \(f\), together with a type \(Y\) and report \(Z\) witnessing a violation.

This is a plausible aggregate version of the paper’s question: mass is an active objective, not merely a normalization. Proposition 17 would imply value \(1\) for every \(\mu\) on its structured family, while Proposition 18 shows that omitting \((\{x\},Z)\) can leave at least \(\mu_{\{x\}}\) of the population vulnerable. The authors would probably recognize the model, since they themselves motivate dichotomous preferences with meeting scheduling and discuss independently distributed multi-agent mechanisms.

The weakness is decisive: the paper studies worst-case incentive compatibility for every type, not population-weighted robustness. Introducing \(\mu\) and the threshold \(q\) creates a sensible new problem, but it is a co-modification rather than a computational result from the paper. Consequently, this is a defensible continuous analogue in substance, likely tractable on the Proposition 17 family, but it cannot honestly be presented as a paper-anchored ChoCo case under the stated rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case begins before the proposed mirrors: this paper contains no qualifying computational result. Its numbered results—Propositions 17, 18, and 16 included—are logical characterizations and independence statements about incentive constraints. They do not establish membership in \(\mathrm{P}\), hardness, approximation, or parameterized tractability. The “computational” Gibbard–Satterthwaite discussion invokes results proved elsewhere, and Lemma 11 is an LP-duality certificate argument, not a complexity theorem. Under the programme’s anchor rule, that is decisive: there is no computational problem from this paper whose population continuization is being tested.

Proposition 17 does not rescue the case. Its subject is whether a fixed set \(S\) of constraints forces every deterministic mechanism \(f:2^X\to X\) to be fully incentive-compatible. Replicating agents of type \(Y\) changes none of this. Every such agent receives the same \(f(Y)\), and the sufficiency statement remains exactly the same pointwise statement. If \(\mu\) has full support, the weighted condition is equivalent to the original condition; if \(\mu\) omits types, it merely ignores violations by types absent from the population. The numerical masses do not enter feasibility, the mechanism, or the local-to-global argument.

The proposed objective,

\[ \inf_{f:\,\mathrm{SIC}_S(f)} \sum_Y \mu_Y \mathbf 1[\mathrm{IC}_Y(f)], \]

is coherent, but it is not a continuization of Proposition 17. It replaces a worst-case logical requirement—every type must be protected—with a new population-weighted robustness objective. That may support a separate paper on approximate incentive compatibility, but the paper under review neither formulates nor analyzes it.

Proposition 18 is even less suitable as an anchor. It constructs a mechanism violating one omitted constraint \((\{x\},Z)\) while satisfying essentially all others. A population distribution can assign positive mass to type \(\{x\}\), turning that witness into a weighted vulnerability. But this remains a single omitted constraint attached to a single type. High multiplicity only repeats the same witness; it does not create a mass-transfer problem, an aggregate feasibility constraint, or a new algorithmic regime. Optimizing the total mass exposed by omitted constraints would again be a new weighted constraint-graph problem, not a continuous version of the proposition’s result.

The same objection defeats Proposition 16. Its “almost all constraints are independent” conclusion concerns probabilistic mechanisms and the logical relation among constraints. Adding \(\mu\) over true types does not change which misreports are available. An agent of type \(Y\) can still report \(Z\), whether or not any other agent has type \(Z\). Weighting the true types by \(\mu\) therefore produces an ex ante or approximate incentive notion absent from the paper. That is a legitimate new model, but not its continuous mirror.

The better possible repairs do not solve this problem. A Bayesian version could let \(\mu\) affect expected utility, but then the question becomes Bayesian incentive compatibility rather than the paper’s local-versus-full implication. A continuum of voters could make the aggregate outcome depend on the distribution, but an individual deviation has zero mass and hence typically no effect; incentive constraints become vacuous unless one introduces positive-mass blocs or coalition deviations. Those are substantial new strategic assumptions. A continuous distribution over cardinal utility vectors faces the opposite problem: exact incentive compatibility remains pointwise and \(\mu\) is inert, while almost-everywhere compatibility discards precisely the exceptional types on which the paper’s impossibility and independence phenomena live.

This is not an objection that high-multiplicity work is “already done”; high multiplicity is perfectly sensible for meeting-scheduling agents. The point is narrower: in this paper multiplicity is structurally inessential. The results concern the geometry of a type space and the logical sufficiency of constraints, not a society whose aggregate mass affects an outcome or an optimization problem. The population axis can be appended, but it does not arise from the paper’s mathematics.

The negative conclusion should therefore be stated carefully. The scheduling distribution is a defensible new population-weighted mechanism-design question, and it might be worthwhile independently. But it is not a paper-anchored ChoCo mirror, and none of the three proposed anchors survives that test. The universal claim that no imaginable reformulation could ever be interesting is not provable; the strong conclusion is instead that this paper is a near-miss with no qualifying computational anchor.
