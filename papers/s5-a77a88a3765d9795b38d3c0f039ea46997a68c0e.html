Why no mirror

The proposed bot-cancellation problem is well stated and recognizable as an extension of the paper’s analysis. But Theorems 2 and 3 characterize equilibrium behavior; neither states a computational result of the required kind, so the proposed question cannot qualify as a mirror of an anchored computational result.

What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers Theorem 3’s multi-bot cancellation result; it leaves Theorem 2’s single-bot threshold, the equilibrium and stability results, and the hub-and-spoke analyses aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest positive case is a continuous-population version of the paper’s bot-cancellation question. In a large platform, let \(\mu\) be the distribution over complete agent types: agents of a type share all update-relevant parameters, memory rules, and interaction behavior. For example, millions of users might fall into a small number of cohorts with the same exposure and memory settings. Let \(W(t,dt')\) describe type \(t\)’s ordinary-agent influence, and let \(b_k(t)\) be the weight type \(t\) gives bot \(k\), with \(\int W(t,dt')+\sum_k b_k(t)=1\).

A precise candidate problem is: given \(\mu\), \(W\), the bot weights and strengths \(\eta_k\), and homogeneous memory parameters \(c_{1,M},c_{2,M},M\), decide whether the bots leave every type’s limiting belief equal to the bot-free value \(p^{(*)}=c_{1,M}/(1-c_{2,M}M)\). The type-level equilibrium \(q\) satisfies
\[ q(t)=c_{1,M}\int W(t,dt')+c_{2,M}M\int W(t,dt')q(t')+\sum_k b_k(t)\eta_k. \]
In this model, \(q(t)=p^{(*)}\) for \(\mu\)-almost every \(t\) exactly when
\[ \sum_k b_k(t)\bigl(\eta_k-p^{(*)}\bigr)=0 \quad\text{for }\mu\text{-almost every }t. \]
This is a plausible population analogue of the paper’s counter-bot analysis: types represent fractions of a large society, while bot influence is specified per type. With finite rational support, the cancellation condition is directly checkable, so I would expect this decision problem to be tractable.

The nearest anchors are Theorem 2 and Theorem 3, both proved in this paper. Theorem 2 says that, in the stated homogeneous network with one bot, every agent’s asymptotic belief is strictly above the bot-free fixed point exactly when the bot strength exceeds \(p^{(*)}\), and the fixed point is unchanged exactly when the strength equals \(p^{(*)}\). Theorem 3 says that, with multiple bots, the asymptotic beliefs equal the bot-free beliefs exactly when the bot influence matrix annihilates the vector of bot strengths centered at \(p^{(*)}\). These results make the proposed mirror recognizable as their bot-influence problem.

But neither theorem asserts a computational-complexity result. They characterize asymptotic behavior; the paper gives no named result that classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, or otherwise. So this is a credible *new* continuous-population question suggested by their analysis, not a qualifying continuous mirror of a named computational result under your anchor rule. That absence is the case’s weakest point and prevents me from presenting it as a compliant anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that neither Theorem 2 nor Theorem 3 is a computational result. Theorem 2 gives an equilibrium threshold; Theorem 3 characterizes when bot effects cancel. The paper proves no complexity bound and poses no algorithmic problem. The proponent recognizes this: the proposed question is a new continuous problem suggested by the paper, not a mirror of one of its computational results.

Even granting the best population version, that distinction remains. Let types interact through a kernel \(W\), and let \(b_k(t)\) be type \(t\)’s weight on bot \(k\). The equilibrium shift from the bot-free baseline can be written as
\[ q-p^{(*)}\mathbf{1} = \bigl(I-c_{2,M}M W\bigr)^{-1} \sum_k b_k\bigl(\eta_k-p^{(*)}\bigr). \]
This is a sound continuous analogue of the bot analysis. With finite support, it is a matrix equation; with an unrestricted continuum of types, a computational question first needs a finite representation of \(W\) and the bot profiles. Neither representation nor complexity question appears in the paper.

One could ask a stronger question than the proponent does: choose bot placements or strengths to shift a specified fraction of the population, or to cancel an influence campaign at minimum cost. But that requires adding a target, feasible interventions, costs, and a finite input model. The paper’s theorems take bot weights and strengths as given; they do not define those design choices. Such a problem could be worthwhile research, but its computational content would come from the added design problem, not from continuizing a computational result in this paper.

The same issue applies separately to both anchors. Theorem 2’s type-level version remains an equilibrium-threshold statement. Theorem 3’s version remains a nullspace condition. Turning either into an optimization problem requires assumptions beyond the paper. A continuous population and repeated agent types are plausible here, especially for large platforms with shared interaction patterns, so lack of multiplicity is not the strongest objection. The sharper objection is that the paper’s contribution is analytical dynamics and control, not computational social choice: it supplies no named complexity result for ChoCo to mirror. I cannot rule out a valuable follow-on project, but under the stated mirror criterion, neither proposed anchor survives.
