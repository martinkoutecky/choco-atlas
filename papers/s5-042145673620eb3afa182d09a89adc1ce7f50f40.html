The continuous mirror question

Given two finite supported type sets \(A\) and \(B\), positive rational masses \((\alpha_a)_{a\in A}\) and \((\beta_b)_{b\in B}\) each summing to \(1\), and rational cardinal utilities \(u_{ab},v_{ab}\) with strict rankings over opposite-side types, decide whether there is exactly one stable mass flow. A feasible flow has \(x_{ab}\geq 0\), row sums \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\alpha_a\), and column sums \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\beta_b\); define \(\bar u_a(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha_a}\sum_b x_{ab}u_{ab}\) and \(\bar v_b(x)=\frac{1}{\beta_b}\sum_a x_{ab}v_{ab}\). Stability requires, for every \((a,b)\), that \(u_{ab}\leq\bar u_a(x)\) or \(v_{ab}\leq\bar v_b(x)\).

The model it lives in

A repeated one-to-one placement market with two populations represented by distributions over complete utility types. The decision variable is the mass flow \(x_{ab}\); its row and column sums match each type's mass, and stability is tested using each type's per-capita average utility. The question is whether exactly one such stable flow exists.

The objection that survived

Type homogeneity makes members indifferent among named partners within a type, even when rankings over types are strict; the paper's nonintegral construction is proved only for strict preferences, so the type-flow uniqueness question needs a fresh treatment of this tie structure.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Unique Stable Mass Matching recognition. The paper’s strongest numbered anchor for it is Theorem 3, proved here: a stable matching instance \(I\) has a unique stable fractional matching if and only if \(I\) is in CMFP, meaning Algorithm 1 repeatedly removes mutually first-preference pairs and ends with a perfect matching. In the strict-preference setting, the proof’s other direction uses Algorithm 2 to construct a non-integral stable fractional matching whenever the instance is outside CMFP. Since Algorithm 1 tests CMFP in polynomial time, the paper thereby gives a polynomial-time test for uniqueness. The displayed theorem is a structural characterization rather than a stand-alone complexity-class statement, so this is the closest computational anchor the paper offers. Its NP-hardness claim about maximizing social welfare is attributed to Caragiannis et al.; I would not use it as an anchor for this paper.

Here is the mass version I would hand to a prover. An instance has two finite sets of types, \(A\) and \(B\), with positive rational masses \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_b\), each side summing to \(1\). It also has nonnegative rational utilities \(u_{ab}\) and \(v_{ab}\): every member of type \(a\) assigns utility \(u_{ab}\) to a partner of type \(b\), and every member of type \(b\) assigns utility \(v_{ab}\) to a partner of type \(a\). Assume strict rankings over opposite-side types and that every pair is acceptable.

A feasible mass matching is a flow \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) with row sums \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\alpha_a\) and column sums \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\beta_b\). Its type-level utilities are
\[ \bar u_a(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha_a}\sum_b x_{ab}u_{ab}, \qquad \bar v_b(x)=\frac{1}{\beta_b}\sum_a x_{ab}v_{ab}. \]
Call the flow stable if every type pair \((a,b)\) satisfies \(u_{ab}\leq\bar u_a(x)\) or \(v_{ab}\leq\bar v_b(x)\), the paper’s blocking-pair test expressed in per-capita terms. The problem is: does this instance have exactly one stable mass matching? A solution reports yes or no; it can also return the unique flow in the yes case, or two distinct stable flows in the no case. The input size is measured by the number of supported types and the bit length of the masses and utilities.

The natural regime is a large, repeated one-to-one placement market—say, internship applicants matched with individual positions. There might be hundreds of thousands of applicants and positions but only tens or hundreds of complete types: each type fixes its members’ utilities for every opposing type, along with all other attributes the matching problem uses. The mass records proportions, and the flow records how much of each type pair is matched. This makes the population continuous; the fractional outcome in the original paper is not itself the continuization. The per-capita capacities and average utilities are the co-modifications needed to express the same stability question over a distribution.

I expect this recognition problem to be Class A, tentatively. The paper’s uniqueness result suggests looking for a mass-weighted analogue of its conditional mutual-first-preference structure, with an algorithm polynomial in support size and input bit length. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a transfer of Theorem 3: the weighted case needs its own characterization and proof. It also raises useful follow-up questions: does an exhaustive weighted peeling criterion characterize uniqueness; can the unique flow be recovered in polynomial time; and, as in the paper’s strict-preference corollary, does non-uniqueness imply an uncountable family of stable flows?

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s uniqueness characterization and its associated recognition algorithm. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s incentive-compatibility impossibility results or its social-welfare hardness discussion.

The weakest point is the move from individual agents to type-level flows. The paper’s uniqueness claim ranges over all edge-level fractional matchings, while this model identifies solutions by aggregate type flows. Arbitrary stable fractional matchings cannot necessarily be averaged into stable ones—the paper’s Observation 1 warns against that—so the aggregate problem may not preserve every feature of the original uniqueness question. I think the mirror is still plausible because it asks the same cardinal-utility blocking question for a high-multiplicity population, but whether the authors would accept this type-flow quotient is the main issue for a referee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proposed anchor is that “unique stable flow” is not the uniqueness question Theorem 3 answers. That theorem ranges over fractional matchings indexed by named pairs of agents. In a cohort model, it ranges over aggregate flows between types. The aggregate flow identifies schedules that differ only in which clone meets which clone, so uniqueness after aggregation can coexist with many distinct matchings in the paper’s sense.

That distinction is hard to avoid. Preserve the paper’s strict rankings over individually named partners, and agents with different rankings become different types, potentially leaving \(\tau\) as large as the population. Compress them into cohorts with shared preferences over partner types, and members are tied across the named agents in each opposing cohort. The paper explicitly limits its nonintegral-matching algorithm to strict preferences and gives a weak-preference example where its construction fails. The proposed mirror therefore needs a new type-level stability model and a new characterization; Theorem 3 does not carry over as a result about that model.

This also narrows the computational anchor. The paper’s polynomial procedure tests CMFP and constructs a matching for a finite instance. “Does a rational type-flow instance have a unique stable flow?” is a coherent new decision problem, but its connection to that procedure is a research lead rather than a mirrored complexity result. The paper’s incentive-compatibility results offer a closer match to its stated focus, though an individual report has zero mass in a nonatomic model if outcomes depend only on aggregate type shares. A useful version would need to specify how an individual’s report affects their own allocation.

That is the best case against the anchor: the natural compression changes the object being counted, the preference model, and potentially the strategic unit. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Large placement markets with repeated applicant and position types are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a uniqueness question for their stable mass flows would be computational and recognizable as a matching problem. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, those are substantial reasons the anchor may survive. The honest negative case is weak; I would not claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror.
