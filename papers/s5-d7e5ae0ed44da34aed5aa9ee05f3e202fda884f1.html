The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: the paper has a plausible continuous-population analogue, but it contains no admissible named computational anchor.

There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the supplied text, and no result asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT status, or an approximation algorithm. Item (7), “Approximate Pareto optimality under any scaling of the utilities,” is a numbered justification, not a theorem. The paper argues inline that if an outcome obtains an \(\alpha\)-fraction of maximum utilitarian welfare under normalized utilities, then it is \(\alpha\)-Pareto optimal; moreover, \(\alpha\)-Pareto optimality is unchanged by independently rescaling every agent’s utility function. This is proved here, but it is not a named result of the required kind.

Nevertheless, I would propose the following mirror candidate.

Let \(C\) be a finite set of alternatives and \(T\) a finite set of utility types. A type \(t\in T\) is a normalized utility vector \(u_t:C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) satisfying \(\sum_{c\in C}u_t(c)=1\). A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters of type \(t\). The action is to select one candidate \(c\in C\), not a lottery. Its per-capita utilitarian welfare is

\[ W_\mu(c)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_t(c). \]

The problem, which I would call Continuous Normalized-Utility Welfare Election, is: given \((C,T,\mu,(u_t)_{t\in T})\) and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), output a candidate \(c\) satisfying

\[ W_\mu(c)\ge \alpha\max_{d\in C}W_\mu(d). \]

The stronger exact version asks for a welfare-maximizing candidate.

The associated continuous Pareto question is whether the selected candidate is \(\alpha\)-Pareto optimal for the society: there must be no \(d\in C\) such that

\[ \alpha u_t(d)\ge u_t(c) \]

for every type with \(\mu_t>0\), with strict inequality for at least one positive-mass type. This condition is invariant under arbitrary positive type-specific rescaling, since replacing \(u_t\) by \(\lambda_tu_t\) cancels \(\lambda_t\) from every comparison.

This is a credible population mirror because the paper explicitly treats utilitarian voting and normalized utility vectors. The regime would be a large election in which many voters share one of a moderate number of preference-and-utility templates: for example, a national electorate with millions of voters but only \(\tau\) recurring survey-defined utility profiles. The number of agents \(N\) is large while \(\tau\ll N\), and the discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) is represented by \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Mass means population share, not uncertainty and not a fractional candidate.

The implied classification is Class A for this formulation. Computing every \(W_\mu(c)\) and selecting the maximum takes polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input precision. The paper’s inline argument then gives the welfare-to-\(\alpha\)-Pareto guarantee. The interesting follow-up would be to replace explicitly listed candidates and types by succinct combinatorial representations, or to study the attainable Pareto frontier of type utilities; those variants could introduce genuine pricing or separation questions.

This is not a novelty collision. The paper’s utilities are normalized, but the society is still described as a finite collection of agents; its “probabilistic perspective” concerns the interpretation of utility, not a continuum of population mass. Nor is the proposed mirror merely continuizing the outcome: the outcome remains one indivisible candidate.

The weakest point is decisive: this paper does not itself present a computational problem or a complexity result. The proposed mirror is therefore a natural reformulation of its central welfare claim, not a continuous counterpart of one of the programme’s preferred hardness or algorithmic anchors. Its computation is also too easy to expose the distinctive continuization phenomenon. I would submit it as a modest positive example of a sensible continuous-population question, but not as a strong ChoCo case under the anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no admissible computational anchor. It contains no computational problem, input/output specification, theorem, lemma, corollary, complexity classification, or approximation algorithm. Its numbered items are philosophical and technical justifications. In particular, item (7) is an inline observation, not a named computational result. Thus any continuous problem proposed here must be invented alongside the mirror; it cannot be a continuization of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed voting mirror is sensible as a high-multiplicity model, but that is precisely why it is too weak for this programme. For a finite electorate,

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_i(c) = \sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_t(c) \]

after grouping identical utility vectors. The continuous formulation is therefore a histogram or sufficient-statistic representation of the same welfare calculation. Selecting the winner requires only computing \(m\) weighted sums and taking their maximum. This is not objectionable merely because the answer is easy; the deeper problem is that the paper supplies no computational question whose population axis is being continued.

The Pareto part is even less population-dependent. For a chosen candidate \(c\), the relevant condition is whether there exists \(d\) such that

\[ \alpha u_t(d)\ge u_t(c) \]

for every type in the support, with strict inequality on a positive-mass set. The masses affect neither the inequalities nor the rescaling argument, except through support. Replacing a finite list of agents by a measure therefore contributes no substantive computational object to the paper’s central claim. The natural positive-measure version is perfectly legitimate, but it does not rescue the absence of an anchor.

The strongest repair would be to use an infinite or richly represented utility type space, or to formulate a continuum resource-allocation problem. That would either reduce to evaluating finitely many integrals for a finite candidate set, with difficulty coming from the representation of the distribution, or import an entirely new allocation model with supplies, feasibility constraints, and bundle structure. The latter could certainly be worthwhile research, but it would be a new computational social-choice problem motivated by the paper, not a mirror of one of its results.

There is also a loss of information in the proposed normalized-type model. Two populations may have the same distribution of normalized utility vectors but radically different distributions of actual utility scales. That is harmless for scale-invariant approximate Pareto comparisons, but it means the mirror no longer represents the paper’s discussion of “real” utilitarian or egalitarian welfare. Retaining scale as part of the type restores the information, while optimizing normalized welfare deliberately ignores it.

So the negative case is strong on programme scope: the paper is a normative/expository note, not a computational result to continuize, and its most natural population version is only an elementary aggregation identity. It is not an airtight claim that no useful continuum resource-allocation model could ever be built from the paper’s themes. If “worthwhile” means merely “a coherent continuous-population question can be posed,” the proponent is right. Under ChoCo’s computational-anchor standard, however, this paper should receive no mirror.
