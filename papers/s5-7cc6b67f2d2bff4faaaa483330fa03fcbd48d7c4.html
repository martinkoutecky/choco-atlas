What the mirror covers

The proposed question concerns detecting two disjoint \(\mathrm{TEQ}\)-retentive sets in an induced majority tournament; it does not cover the bound \(12 \leq n_{\mathrm{TEQ}} \leq 23\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered computational result: no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. Its central result is an unnumbered construction, not an eligible anchor under your rule. It gives a 24-alternative tournament with two disjoint, isomorphic \(\mathrm{TEQ}\)-retentive sets of size \(12\), and, together with earlier work, bounds \(n_{\mathrm{TEQ}}\) by \(12\le n_{\mathrm{TEQ}}\le23\). So there is no qualifying named result to anchor a continuous problem to.

The strongest plausible population lift is to treat a large electorate’s complete rankings of the 24 alternatives as voter types. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings; its mass on a ranking is the fraction of voters holding it. The pairwise majority relation induced by \(\mu\) gives a tournament. A precise associated problem is: given such a distribution with no pairwise ties, decide whether its majority tournament has two disjoint nonempty \(\mathrm{TEQ}\)-retentive sets, and return them if it does. Retentiveness is the paper’s condition: for every alternative \(x\) in a set, the \(\mathrm{TEQ}\) of \(x\)’s dominators lies inside that set. This is a decision and certificate-finding problem, not an optimization problem.

The electorate scenario is plausible: many voters can share one of relatively few complete rankings, so the population can be much larger than the support \(\tau\). Moreover, the paper’s tournament can be represented by such a distribution. For each directed edge \(a\succ b\), use equal mass on two rankings: one placing \(a\succ b\) before all other alternatives, and one placing the reverse order of the others before \(a\succ b\). Together these make \(a\) beat \(b\) while canceling their contributions to every other pair. For 24 alternatives this uses at most \(552\) rankings before duplicates are combined. The continuous population therefore supplies a standard source for the tournament while leaving alternatives finite. The proposed problem covers the paper’s counterexample construction and its nonuniqueness question; it does not add an explanation of the \(n_{\mathrm{TEQ}}\) bounds.

For variable numbers of alternatives, the natural expectation would be hardness transfer if a discrete complexity result for this tournament property were established: the mass-profile representation can encode arbitrary tournaments with polynomially many ranking types. But this paper establishes no such hardness result, so it supports neither a complexity classification nor a claimed Class A, B, or C verdict. The main weakness is that the population profile merely encodes the same dominance relation the paper already takes as its input. A tournament theorist could recognize the electorate interpretation, but the paper’s contribution itself is about structure in the alternatives, not computation over a large population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. It constructs one 24-alternative tournament with two disjoint \(\mathrm{TEQ}\)-retentive sets and bounds \(n_{\mathrm{TEQ}}\). The latter counts alternatives; neither result studies an algorithmic problem over societies. The proponent’s decision problem—given an electorate, detect the retentive sets—is a new computational question, not a complexity result from this paper.

The electorate model is plausible, and the proposed question is well-defined. But for a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, its answer depends only on the signs of the pairwise margins \(M_{ab}(\mu)\). Thus \(Q(\mu)=Q(\succ_\mu)\): within each region of the population simplex that induces the same tournament, the answer is fixed. The paper’s object is the tournament, and the population supplies one representation of it. As the proponent’s bounded-support encoding illustrates, arbitrary tournaments can be represented by ranking populations; increasing or rescaling that population adds no new instances of the paper’s structural question. This is a mismatch of what the paper studies, not an objection that the continuous answer would be dull. Even the closest extension of \(n_{\mathrm{TEQ}}\)—searching for the least order with nonuniqueness—varies the number of alternatives, not the population.

One could make mass matter by adding a robustness question, such as how much of a given electorate must change before its majority tournament loses TEQ uniqueness. That may be worth studying, but it adds a baseline society and a perturbation objective absent from the paper. It is a neighboring project, not a continuization of a result here. The negative case is therefore strongest for this paper as a ChoCo mirror; it cannot establish that every adjacent computational study of TEQ on large electorates would be worthless.
