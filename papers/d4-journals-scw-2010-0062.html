What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror; the mechanism characterizations, allocation derivation, and income-distribution comparisons are non-computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold for a population mirror is a large redistribution system with many people sharing the same skill–effort pair \((y,z)\). A type would be \((y,z)\), and \(\mu_{y,z}\) its fraction of the population. Pre-tax income and reference claims would aggregate as \(R=\int v(y,z)\,d\mu\) and \(C=\int v(\tilde y,z)\,d\mu\); the decision would be a per-capita transfer by type, with \(\int x(y,z)\,d\mu=0\). That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for this model.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a computational mirror. Proposition 1, proved here, characterizes \(S_{\tilde y\mathrm{MRE/E}}\) by Symmetry, Priority, and ETRS. Proposition 2, also proved here, characterizes \(S_{\tilde y\mathrm{MRE/P}}\) by Symmetry, Priority, and Participation. Lemma 1 derives how an income change is divided under those axioms. These are normative characterization results: none asserts a complexity classification, gives an algorithm, or formulates a computational problem that the paper solves.

One could ask how to evaluate either mechanism on a type distribution, but that would be a new computational task without a qualifying anchor in the paper. So I cannot honestly give a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor, or claim a hardness result here whose dissolution the continuum could test. The weak point is fundamental: a plausible population regime does not supply the missing computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed \((y,z)\)-type society is plausible: a large tax-and-transfer system could contain many people with the same skill and effort. But that establishes a possible population regime, not a computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize mechanisms by axioms; Lemma 1 derives how income changes are shared. None poses or solves an algorithmic problem. The clearest computational question one could add—evaluate one of the characterized mechanisms on a finite type distribution—is already answered by the paper’s explicit formulas. With encoded inputs and an efficiently evaluable \(v\), it reduces to aggregating incomes and sorting the supported types. That is not the complexity landscape the programme studies.

The strongest version of the population mirror also runs into a limit problem specific to the paper’s minimal-rights idea. Its thresholds depend on removing an individual’s claim from the total claims. In a per-capita, atomless society, an individual has zero mass, so removing them changes no aggregate. The successive thresholds that distinguish the finite-population mechanism collapse: below the claims total, individual minimal rights are zero; above it, they are full. The paper’s distinctive priority stages disappear in the limit. One can define a different rule using whole types or cohorts as claimants, but then the result depends on how identical people are grouped. That is a change to the model’s central rights concept, not simply compression of a high-multiplicity input.

A researcher could certainly build a new computational problem around redistribution, extra constraints, or choosing the reference skill. But those additions are not anchors in this paper, which treats \(\tilde y\) as exogenous and gives explicit allocations. So the negative case is strong for this paper as a source of computational mirrors, though it cannot prove that no worthwhile adjacent problem could be invented.
