The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of unit-density bundle types with rational item sizes and values \(v_i=s_i\), per-capita capacity \(C\), and a rational society distribution \(\mu\), determine the best worst-case expected-welfare ratio over mechanisms that assign each individual an integral subset of their reported bundle, satisfy aggregate capacity, and are truthful in expectation against every hiding report \(r\subseteq t\). The benchmark is \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}(\mu)=\min\{C,S(\mu)\}\), where \(S(\mu)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t s(t)\); give an optimal mechanism and its running time.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with mass distributed over bundle types; each individual receives an integral subset, while the mass of each type can be split among different subsets. The mechanism observes reported type masses, respects per-capita capacity \(C\), and must make truthful reporting optimal in expectation when an individual hides items.

The objection that survived

A unilateral deviation leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, removing the aggregate externality used in Theorem 4.15; the proponent flags this, but it still limits how directly the continuous frontier reflects the finite-agent incentive conflict.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.15’s randomized unit-density approximation ceiling; it leaves the paper’s general-density results, deterministic results, and other unit-density bounds outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The supplied paper has no numbered P/NP/FPT result. Its closest computational anchors are mechanism approximation theorems. My lead is Theorem 4.15, proved in this paper as an extension of a bound by Feigenbaum and Jaggard: for unit-density knapsack instances, if a randomized strategyproof mechanism is \(\alpha\)-approximate, then \(\alpha\le 1/(5\phi-7)\approx 0.917\). Here, unit density means every item’s value equals its size, and agents may hide items they own.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large funding programme with many local organizations, each submitting a portfolio from a small set of standardized portfolio types. An organization’s type is its complete item bundle, including the items’ sizes and values; type mass is the fraction of organizations with that bundle. There may be thousands of organizations but only \(\tau\) distinct portfolios. Assume the budget scales with the number of organizations, so the continuum has per-capita capacity \(C\). Each organization still receives an integral subset of its own items; the continuum lets different fractions of a type receive different subsets.

The precise mirror is the Unit-Density Mass Knapsack Truthfulness Frontier. The input is a finite set \(T\) of portfolio types, with each item having rational size \(s_i>0\) and value \(v_i=s_i\), a per-capita capacity \(C\), and any rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). A mechanism must map each reported population distribution \(\mu\) to feasible allocations of the reported bundles. Write \(\lambda^\mu_{r,A}\) for the fraction of agents reporting bundle \(r\) who receive subset \(A\subseteq r\), with \(\sum_{A\subseteq r}\lambda^\mu_{r,A}=1\). Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\sum_{A\subseteq t}\lambda^\mu_{t,A}s(A)\le C. \]
Expected strategyproofness requires that, for every true bundle \(t\) and every report \(r\subseteq t\), an agent does at least as well reporting truthfully:
\[ \sum_{A\subseteq t}\lambda^\mu_{t,A}s(A) \;\ge\; \sum_{A\subseteq r}\lambda^\mu_{r,A}s(A). \]
The question is to find the best worst-case approximation ratio over all \(\mu\), and a mechanism attaining it. Its welfare benchmark is \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty(\mu)=\min\{C,S(\mu)\}\), where \(S(\mu)=\sum_t\mu_t s(t)\).

In this model, the answer is \(1\). Let \(\rho=\min\{1,C/S(\mu)\}\) when \(S(\mu)>0\), and \(\rho=1\) otherwise. For every reported item, accept it with probability \(\rho\); equivalently, split each type’s mass among its integral subsets according to independent \(\rho\)-thinning of its items. Aggregate capacity used is exactly \(\rho S(\mu)\le C\), and welfare is \(\rho S(\mu)=\mathrm{OPT}_\infty(\mu)\). A single agent has zero mass, so hiding items does not change \(\rho\). Their expected utility from reporting a subset \(r\) is \(\rho s(r)\), no greater than the truthful utility \(\rho s(t)\). The rule is therefore truthful in expectation and exactly optimal. It can be represented compactly and computed in \(O(m+\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations, polynomial in the input bit length \(L\), where \(m\) counts the item descriptions in the supported types. I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A).

The mirror covers the randomized unit-density setting around Theorem 4.15; it does not claim a result for arbitrary densities or for deterministic individual allocations. Its weakest point is also the reason the finite bound may disappear: one agent’s report no longer changes the aggregate knapsack instance. Theorem 4.15’s proof uses a two-agent hide-an-item comparison, while this limit holds the population distribution fixed under a unilateral deviation. That makes the question a credible test of the high-multiplicity version of their strategic knapsack problem, but an author might object that atomlessness removes an externality central to their finite model. A finite-\(N\) approximation rate, extensions beyond unit density, and the distinction between expected and universal strategyproofness are natural follow-up questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor actually raised is Theorem 4.15, the randomized unit-density approximation bound. Its continuous mirror is formally sensible: repeated portfolio types make a high-multiplicity regime plausible, and assigning whole bundles to different fractions of each type respects integral allocations to individuals.

The trouble is that the limit removes the incentive conflict the theorem measures. Its proof compares two finite instances that differ when one agent hides an item. In an atomless population, that agent has zero mass, so the deviation leaves the population distribution unchanged. For unit density, a mechanism can accept each item with the same rationing probability, chosen to meet capacity. An agent then does at least as well by reporting every item they own. More generally, with arbitrary densities, the aggregate welfare problem becomes fractional knapsack; allocating items by density is unaffected by one person’s report, and hiding can only remove items from that person’s own allocation. The finite strategic tradeoff has disappeared in the continuum.

A stronger attempt to preserve it would let a positive-mass cohort coordinate its members’ reports. But that makes the strategic player a coalition or organization controlling a block of the population. The paper’s agents are individual owners, and the resulting question is about group deviations, not the strategyproofness in Theorem 4.15. A finite-population correction might be interesting, but exact finite-agent truthfulness does not follow from the atomless solution; an approximate-truthfulness or convergence-rate question would add a different target.

This is a case against the mirror’s value to ChoCo’s complexity programme, not an airtight claim that no worthwhile question can be asked. The proposed setting passes the high-multiplicity plausibility test, and the loss of strategic effects could itself be worth documenting. But the mirror appears to yield an ordinary fractional allocation problem, while the named theorem’s central phenomenon lives in the influence of an individual report.
