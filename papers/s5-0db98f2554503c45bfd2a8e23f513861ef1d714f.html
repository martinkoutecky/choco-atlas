The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Theorem 1.3, proved in this paper. The paper gives no NP-hardness, \(P\), W[1]-hardness, or FPT classification. Its computational contribution is instead a property-testing result: among IIA constitutions, transitivity can be tested by sampling an input profile and checking whether the outcome is transitive.

Theorem 1.3 says that for every \(k\ge1\) and \(\epsilon>0\), there is a \(\delta(\epsilon)>0\), which the paper bounds by \(\exp(-C/\epsilon^{21})\), such that for every \(n\), any IIA constitution \(F\) with paradox probability \(P(F)<\delta\) is within statistical distance \(k^2\epsilon\) of some exactly transitive IIA constitution \(G\in\mathcal F_k(n)\). The paper proves in Theorem 1.2 that this target class has an ordered partition of the alternatives into blocks: blocks of size at least three use a dictator or reverse dictator, and size-two blocks use arbitrary nonconstant pairwise functions. Theorem 1.3 is the anchor; Theorem 1.2 describes its target class.

Here is the continuous-population question I would put to a prover. Let \(T=S_k\) be the set of rankings, and let a society be a mass distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). For a pair \(a,b\), define \(p_{ab}(\mu)=\sum_{\sigma:a\succ_\sigma b}\mu_\sigma\). A continuous anonymous IIA constitution is a collection of rules \(\phi_{ab}:[0,1]\to\{-1,1\}\), with \(\phi_{ba}=-\phi_{ab}\), giving the social comparison of \(a\) and \(b\) as \(\phi_{ab}(p_{ab}(\mu))\). Its output is still a ranking or a cyclic tournament; only the society has become continuous.

Fix \(D\) as the uniform distribution over \(\Delta(S_k)\), representing variation in the composition of large electorates across places or elections. Let \(\mathcal G_k^\infty\) be the class of measurable aggregate IIA rules whose outcomes are transitive for every \(\mu\). Define
\[ d_D(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty) =\inf_{\Psi\in\mathcal G_k^\infty} \Pr_{\mu\sim D}[\Phi(\mu)\ne\Psi(\mu)]. \]
The problem is: given \(k\), query access to \(\Phi\), and \(\epsilon>0\), distinguish a rule in \(\mathcal G_k^\infty\) from one with \(d_D(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)>\epsilon\). A solution would establish a bound \(\delta_k(\epsilon)>0\) such that distance greater than \(\epsilon\) forces \(\Pr_{\mu\sim D}[\Phi(\mu)\text{ is nontransitive}]\ge\delta_k(\epsilon)\). Then sampling \(O(\delta_k(\epsilon)^{-1})\) societies and checking the outcomes gives a one-sided tester: it always accepts an exactly transitive rule and finds a cyclic outcome with high probability when the rule is far from the class.

The regime is plausible for large electorates with few alternatives: there may be millions of people but at most \(k!\) ranking types, so each type’s mass is more useful than one-person counts. For \(k=3\), there are only six possible types. The tester’s input and output remain computational objects; no outcome space is made divisible. I would expect a population-size-independent tester for fixed \(k\), though this paper’s very small \(\delta\) does not suggest a polynomial dependence on \(1/\epsilon\), and it says nothing about efficiency when \(k\) varies.

The weak point is substantial: the paper samples profiles of named voters, while my proposed tester samples mass distributions. Under independent ballots, empirical masses concentrate around the uniform distribution as the electorate grows, so that sampling model has no nondegenerate continuum limit of the kind proposed here. Also, Theorem 1.3’s nearby transitive rule may rely on a named dictator, which has no direct counterpart in an atomless population. So this is an honest continuous analogue of the paper’s property-testing question, not a limit theorem derived from Theorem 1.3. The new result would need to establish that the continuous, anonymous problem is still one the authors recognize as theirs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.3 is a real computational result in the property-testing sense, so there is no case against it on the ground that the paper lacks a computational theorem. The strongest objection is that its probability comes from random ballots *within one electorate*. If voters are anonymous and are grouped by ranking type, those ballots become an empirical mass vector \(\mu_n\). Under the paper’s independent uniform voting model, \(\mu_n\) concentrates at the uniform distribution as \(n\) grows. The population-level society therefore has no nontrivial paradox probability left: for a fixed mass vector, an aggregate constitution gives a fixed outcome. Recovering variation requires studying finite-size fluctuations, or choosing a separate distribution over societies.

The proponent’s distribution over the simplex is a coherent repair, and a better version would use a distribution \(\Pi\) grounded in variation across real constituencies. But that changes what is being measured: it asks whether a rule has cycles across sampled societies, rather than whether a random electorate produces a cycle. The choice of \(\Pi\) becomes part of the problem, and the uniform simplex distribution is not supplied by the paper’s model. Different plausible priors could give different paradox probabilities and distances. A theorem for a well-chosen \(\Pi\) might be useful; it would establish a new stability result whose central modeling choice comes from outside this paper.

There is also a mismatch in the theorem’s target class. In \(\mathcal F_k(n)\), a dictator is a named voter. In a high-multiplicity society where agents with the same ranking are indistinguishable, no individual has positive mass and can play that role. Keeping a named dictator preserves the individual-level model; replacing one with a type or group changes the target. The best mirror should therefore define its own class of exactly transitive mass-level IIA rules, rather than import \(\mathcal F_k(n)\). That makes the question coherent, but it does not restore the theorem’s original closeness target.

So the case against this anchor is that its probability and its central stability target both depend on individual-level structure that the proposed population model removes. It does not establish that every population-level property-testing question would be unworthy. A grounded cross-constituency prior could make one worth studying; on the material here, the negative case is strongest as an argument against treating that question as a direct, well-motivated mirror of Theorem 1.3.
