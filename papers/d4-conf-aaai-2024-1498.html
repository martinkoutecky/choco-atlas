The continuous mirror question

Given a finite graph of trader types with masses \(\mu_t\), where each type specifies role, valuation, initial awareness, and linked cohorts, does there exist a measurable direct mechanism assigning each trader an integral allocation and payment that balances traded mass among qualified traders, is individually rational, and makes truthful valuation reports and full neighbor invitations dominant strategies?

The model it lives in

A continuum population partitioned into finitely many role, valuation, and referral-cohort types with masses \(\mu_t\), connected by a finite type graph. Individual traders report valuations and measurable subsets of their linked neighbors; the mechanism returns pointwise integral allocations and payments, with mass-level feasibility and utility guarantees.

The objection that survived

The pointwise dominant-strategy semantics need careful formalization: an individual valuation report has zero mass in the aggregate, while an invitation may activate an entire linked cohort.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 2 and 3 for finite-support cohort networks. It leaves the experiments, the multi-unit one-sided application, and the proposed multi-item extension outside the question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can DTR’s reachability and price rules be extended to this cohort-mass setting while preserving pointwise dominant-strategy truthfulness and individual rationality?
  • What precise report and deviation semantics make individual valuation incentives meaningful when a single trader has zero mass but can invite a positive-mass cohort?
  • Can the mechanism and its verification be computed in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s diffusion double auction: a market where buyers and sellers invite neighbors, and the mechanism must preserve their incentives while trading mass of a single commodity. The natural regime is a large marketplace with many traders but relatively few recurring combinations of role, valuation, and referral access—for example, large buyer and seller cohorts with standardized price bands and links between occupational or market groups.

Let the population be \(X=[0,1]\), with mass measured by Lebesgue measure. Partition it into finitely many complete trader types \(X_t\), \(t\in\mathcal T\), with \(\lambda(X_t)=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type records whether its members are buyers or sellers, their common valuation, and their neighborhood profile. To keep the network information part of the type, take a finite undirected graph on the types: every member of one linked type is a neighbor of every member of the other. Let \(X_0\) be the initially aware traders. Each trader reports a valuation and any measurable subset of their true neighbors; qualification is reachability from \(X_0\) along reported invitations.

My lead mirror, anchored in Theorem 2, proved in this paper, is the continuous diffusion double-auction design problem with individual rationality and dominant-strategy truthfulness. For every report profile, a solution is a measurable allocation \(a:X\to\{0,1\}\) and payment \(p:X\to\mathbb R\). An unqualified seller keeps their unit, and an unqualified buyer receives none. Among qualified traders, the mass sold must equal the mass bought:
\[ \int_{S\cap Q}(1-a(x))\,d\lambda(x) = \int_{B\cap Q}a(x)\,d\lambda(x). \]
Utilities are \(a(x)v_x-p(x)\) for a buyer and \((a(x)-1)v_x-p(x)\) for a seller. The question is whether there is a direct mechanism such that, for every trader and every report profile of the others, reporting their true valuation and all their neighbors is a dominant strategy, and truthful valuation gives nonnegative utility even if they report only a subset of their neighbors.

This restates Theorem 2’s full guarantee: DTR is incentive compatible—truthful valuation and neighbor reporting are dominant strategies—and individually rational. It is proved here, not cited. The continuous question asks for the same strategic property of the underlying auction, with mass replacing head counts; it does not make the item divisible. Individual allocations remain integral, while type averages and traded quantities can be fractional.

I expect this finite-support, block-network version to be Class A, as a mechanism construction and verification problem: qualification closure and value ordering depend on the number of types \(\tau\), not the total population. The paper does not prove that bound or a continuous theorem, though. A further question is whether a mass version of DTR remains truthful when one person’s invitation can activate a positive mass of neighbors.

A second, narrower mirror is justified by Theorem 3, also proved here. It adds weak budget balance and the paper’s three comparisons with McAfee’s trade reduction run on the initial traders alone. A solution must give nonnegative revenue, \(\int_X p\,d\lambda\ge 0\), and achieve at least the benchmark’s traded mass, social welfare, and revenue. For the mass version of the comparison, sort by valuation using mass quantiles, permit proportional splitting at a marginal type, and take the one-trader reduction in McAfee’s rule as a vanishing-mass reduction. This mirrors Theorem 3’s claim that DTR is weakly budget balanced and that its number of transactions, social welfare, and revenue are each at least those of MTR on initial traders without diffusion. I would also expect this version to be tractable on finite-support instances, but the theorem’s discrete proof does not establish it.

The paper has no named result asserting NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or another complexity classification, and gives no running-time bound. The anchors here are named theorems about an explicit mechanism’s properties, not complexity results. If “computational result” is meant strictly as a complexity-class result, the paper has none.

The weakest point is the network restriction. DTR’s discrete proof reasons about identifiable traders who cannot trade and then invite identifiable neighbors. In an unrestricted atomless network, a pivotal individual can have zero mass, and reachability can change discontinuously when a negligible set forms a bridge to a large population. The block-network model preserves the paper’s invitation and competition problem while giving each type many members and meaningful mass effects, but it is narrower than the authors’ arbitrary finite networks. I think the authors would recognize it as their auction in a high-multiplicity regime; I would not claim that the finite DTR proof already transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorems 2 and 3 are not computational results in ChoCo’s sense. Theorem 2 establishes incentive compatibility and individual rationality for DTR; Theorem 3 establishes weak budget balance and comparisons with MTR on the initial traders. The paper gives algorithms, but no numbered result classifies the complexity of a computational problem or bounds their running time. A continuous restatement of those guarantees would be a mechanism-design theorem, not the complexity landscape this programme is meant to chart. The proponent acknowledges this gap, but then treats it as a caveat rather than a reason the anchors miss the programme’s target.

There is also a real tension between preserving the paper’s network problem and getting a useful high-multiplicity model. In an arbitrary social graph, a trader’s complete type includes their neighbors. Those neighbor sets are usually individual-specific, so the support can grow with the population. Replacing them with a small type graph is a better mirror than simply ignoring the network, but it changes the network regime. With dense links between cohorts, a single trader can still invite a positive mass, so it would be wrong to claim that invitation incentives must become vacuous. Yet this moves the problem toward weighted cohort reachability and valuation ordering. If instead one preserves sparse, local networks to keep the paper’s usual referral structure, the complete types tend to proliferate. Neither choice yields a clear new complexity question from the paper’s theorems.

Theorem 3’s benchmark does not fix the problem. Its MTR comparison is another guarantee about an existing mechanism, not a complexity result. In a mass market, the proposed “vanishing-mass” trade reduction also leaves open what the continuous benchmark is: taking the discrete one-trader reduction to zero is reasonable, but it makes the comparison depend on the chosen limiting model. That could be worth formalizing, but formalizing it would not by itself turn the theorem into a computational classification.

A stronger proposal would be to optimize social welfare over all diffusion mechanisms satisfying the paper’s incentive and budget conditions, perhaps with a cohort network or a fixed per-capita supply. That is a plausible computational problem, especially since the paper evaluates welfare and notes that optimal welfare is not generally attainable. But it is a new optimization problem, not a mirror of either named theorem; its objective, network regime, and admissible mechanism class would need to be specified first. So I would reject the two offered anchors for ChoCo. I would not claim the universal negative is airtight: a carefully chosen cohort or hybrid network model might lead to a worthwhile new question. The paper itself, however, does not supply a computational result that makes that mirror compelling.
