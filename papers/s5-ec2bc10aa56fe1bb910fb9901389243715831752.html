The continuous mirror question

Fix rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Given a finite support \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational reported coefficients \(\theta_t\geq 0\), with at least one positive coefficient, choose per-capita allocations \(x_t\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\theta_t x_t^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t\leq 1\), and report the common unit price. The total resource supply scales with population so this constraint remains per capita.

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over types that include each agent's relevant valuation and report; each type receives a per-capita allocation \(x_t\), with mass-weighted proxy welfare as the objective, a mass-weighted resource constraint, and common price \(\lambda\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 2's allocation and price computation. It does not extend the paper's individual Nash-equilibrium, price-of-anarchy, or revenue results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: a large-market mirror of the paper’s allocation computation, not of its equilibrium or price-of-anarchy analysis.

My anchor is Lemma 2, the second framework lemma, proved here. For \(n\) agents with bids \(\theta_i\) and \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), it gives the optimizer of the paper’s proxy-allocation program explicitly:
\[ x_i=\frac{\theta_i^{1/\alpha}}{\sum_j\theta_j^{1/\alpha}}, \qquad \lambda=\left(\sum_j\theta_j^{1/\alpha}\right)^\alpha. \]
Here \(x_i\) is agent \(i\)’s allocation and \(\lambda\) is the common unit price. This is a computational anchor because it solves the allocation problem the mechanism actually runs. The paper has no named hardness or complexity-class result; its headline Theorem 2 bounds the price of anarchy, which is an equilibrium-efficiency result rather than a computational one.

I would ask the prover to solve the following lead mirror, the *\(\alpha\)-Proportional Type-Distribution Allocation Problem*. Fix rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). An instance gives a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) complete agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational reported coefficient \(\theta_t\geq 0\) for each type, with at least one positive coefficient. A type includes the agent’s relevant valuation and report, so agents of one type are interchangeable for this allocation calculation. The population has \(N\) agents, with fraction \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\); scale the resource supply to \(N\) units so the per-capita allocation remains meaningful as the population grows.

Find per-capita allocations \(x_t\geq0\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\theta_t\frac{x_t^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t\leq1, \]
and report the common unit price. A solution is an optimal allocation and its price. Lemma 2’s calculation gives
\[ Z=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\theta_t^{1/\alpha}, \qquad x_t=\frac{\theta_t^{1/\alpha}}{Z}, \qquad \lambda=Z^\alpha. \]
Thus this mirror is tractable: the optimizer and price have a direct formula in \(\tau\) and the input data. The continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\), not merely the divisible good: the decision is an allocation level for each type, and the mass-weighted constraint accounts for the cohort’s share of the population.

A plausible regime is a large user base receiving compute, bandwidth, or another divisible service, with many users in a small number of valuation-and-report cohorts: \(N\gg\tau\). The mirror is close to the authors’ underlying allocation problem because it preserves their proxy objective, proportional allocation, and uniform price, while aggregating interchangeable agents by type. It covers Lemma 2’s allocation result, not the paper’s full mechanism-design results. Natural follow-up questions would ask whether equilibrium existence, uniqueness, welfare bounds, or revenue guarantees persist for a continuous distribution of types.

The weak point is the strategic layer. Identical valuations do not force identical bids away from equilibrium; individual deviations can split one valuation cohort into many reported types. So this is a good mirror for type-cohort reports or symmetric profiles, but it does not carry over every arbitrary finite bid profile, and it does not establish a continuum equilibrium theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has chosen the strongest defensible anchor: Lemma 2 really does compute the allocation used by the mechanism. But the proposed mirror does not expose a computational problem for the programme to study. With per-capita supply, the calculation is just
\[ Z=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\theta_t^{1/\alpha},\qquad x_t=\theta_t^{1/\alpha}/Z,\qquad \lambda=Z^\alpha. \]
That is Lemma 2 with a weighted sum in place of a sum over individuals. Given the support, it takes \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations. The paper has already solved this allocation problem in closed form; aggregating interchangeable agents does not create a pricing problem, an algorithmic bottleneck, or a complexity question. The mirror is a sensible large-market description, but its answer is immediate and adds no complexity result.

Making the type space richer does not improve that case. For a distribution over types, the same calculation replaces the sum by \(\int \theta^{1/\alpha}\,d\mu\), assuming that integral is defined. Any computational difficulty for a black-box or otherwise infinite description would come from representing or integrating the input distribution, not from the allocation problem the paper studies. With the paper’s finite-support input convention, the calculation remains direct.

The more tempting mirror would extend the equilibrium and welfare results to a large population. But atomlessness removes the strategic effect those results analyze: an individual’s deviation no longer changes the aggregate bid or unit price. In the natural per-capita extension, individuals take that price as fixed, so the model becomes a price-taking allocation problem. Alternatively, one can let each type cohort choose a common bid and treat the cohort’s mass as strategically significant; then the cohort, rather than an individual agent, is the player. That is a different strategic model, and the paper’s individual Nash-equilibrium claims do not transfer to it merely by replacing counts with masses. Either way, a proposed mirror leaves the mechanism’s strategic interaction behind or changes who acts.

So the negative case is strongest on the paper’s computational fit, not on whether large populations are sensible in resource allocation. This paper offers a closed-form mechanism allocation and analyses of equilibrium, welfare, and revenue; it states no computational complexity result. Its allocation lemma can be mirrored over types, but that mirror is already solved by the lemma’s formula. A separate computational question might be worth asking, but the paper does not supply one.
