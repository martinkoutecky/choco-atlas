The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and approval types \(A_1,\ldots,A_\tau\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_j\) summing to \(1\), compute a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) such that for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every cohort vector \(v\) with \(0\le v_j\le\mu_j\), \(\sum_j v_j\ge\ell/k\), and \(\left|\bigcap_{j:v_j>0} A_j\right|\ge\ell\), one has \(\sum_{j:|A_j\cap W|\ge\ell}v_j>0\).

The model it lives in

Represent the society by its \(\tau\) approval types and rational masses; keep the committee an integral size-\(k\) subset of \(C\). Run local search on weighted PAV, \(\mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_j\mu_j H_{|A_j\cap W|}\), accepting swaps with gain at least \(1/k^2\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 7’s LS-PAV computation and the average-satisfaction and EJR guarantees in Theorem 4 and Corollary 6. It leaves aside Proposition 9’s tightness result and the cited hardness of exact PAV maximization.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of exact maximum-PAV committee selection when approval types are listed with rational masses, parameterized by support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest numbered complexity anchor is Proposition 7, proved in this paper: for every election \(E\) and committee size \(k\), LS-PAV is computable in polynomial time. Its proof bounds the number of swaps by \(O(k^2\ln k)\): each swap raises the PAV score by at least \(n/k^2\), while a committee’s score is at most \(nH_k\), where \(H_k=1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1k\).

The guarantee that makes this algorithm a compelling mirror comes from two other results proved here. Theorem 4 says that the LS-PAV committee gives every \(\ell\)-cohesive group average satisfaction strictly greater than \(\ell-1\). Corollary 6 concludes that LS-PAV satisfies EJR. I treat Proposition 7 as the anchor; the theorem and corollary explain what its continuous counterpart should accomplish.

Continuous EJR Committee Selection (HM-EJR). An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(k\), and approval types \(A_1,\ldots,A_\tau\subseteq C\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\). A cohort is a vector \(v\) with \(0\le v_j\le\mu_j\), allowing a fraction of each type’s mass to belong to the cohort. For every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), require the EJR condition for each cohort with \(\sum_jv_j\ge\ell/k\) whose positive-mass types share at least \(\ell\) approved candidates: the cohort must contain positive mass of types \(A_j\) with \(|A_j\cap W|\ge\ell\). The problem is to compute a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) satisfying all these conditions.

This definition changes the population unit while retaining the paper’s committee choice and approval-based notion of cohesion. The committee remains an integral set of candidates; the satisfaction of each type remains an integer. Only the population and the cohort threshold become mass-based. The word “contains” in EJR becomes “contains positive mass,” since a single atomless person has no useful mass for this guarantee.

The continuous algorithm uses the weighted PAV score
\[ \mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j H_{|A_j\cap W|}. \]
Start from any size-\(k\) committee and swap candidates whenever the score gain is at least \(1/k^2\). The paper’s Theorem 4 proof carries over with voter counts replaced by masses: a cohort of mass at least \(\ell/k\) with average satisfaction at most \(\ell-1\) would force some swap to gain at least \(1/k^2\). Thus the terminal committee has average satisfaction greater than \(\ell-1\) for every qualifying cohort, which implies the positive-mass EJR condition. The score is at most \(H_k\), so there are at most \(O(k^2H_k)\) swaps. Each gain can be computed over the \(\tau\) supported types using rational arithmetic. The resulting running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), even when the represented population is far larger than the input’s support.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. It is plausible that the authors would recognize it: it keeps their approval ballots, fixed-size committee, cohesive groups, and EJR objective, replacing the number of voters by fractions of the electorate. A plausible regime is a large association or online membership selecting a fixed-size board from a modest candidate slate. If members submit approval sets and many submit the same set, the approval subset is their complete type for this problem; there may be millions of members but only hundreds or thousands of supported types. In the programme’s terms, the type count is small relative to population size.

The paper’s introduction also reports that finding a maximum-PAV committee is NP-hard, citing earlier work. That suggests a separate high-multiplicity question about exact PAV maximization, but it is not a numbered complexity result of this note, and it is not what Proposition 7 computes. The mirror here covers the paper’s polynomial-time EJR construction, not every result or objective in the paper. A natural follow-up is whether exact PAV maximization remains hard on binary-rational type distributions, alongside tractable EJR selection, and how far the guarantee survives when approval types gain additional relevant attributes.

The weakest point is that this continuation does not reveal a new tractability phenomenon: LS-PAV already runs in polynomial time on explicitly listed voters. Its gain is the high-multiplicity formulation and a runtime polynomial in support size and mass bit length, independent of the represented population’s magnitude. Since rational mass distributions can be realized by sufficiently large replicated elections, this is a compressed high-multiplicity model more than a new limit object. That makes the result modest as a novelty claim, but the mirror itself is direct and faithful to the paper’s computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it looks like a low-yield extension of a finite algorithm. LS-PAV’s proof is scale-free: replace each voter’s contribution by the mass of their approval type, replace \(n\) by total mass \(1\), and replace the improvement threshold \(n/k^2\) by \(1/k^2\). The potential is then at most \(H_k\), so the same swap argument gives a polynomial-time algorithm on the compressed input. That seems to add little algorithmic machinery or new structure.

But this does not defeat the mirror. The continuous formulation is coherent: repeated approval sets are complete types, large membership associations can have many members and relatively few types, and committee outcomes remaining integral is no objection to continuizing the population. More strongly, the proposed cohort version preserves the paper’s average-satisfaction guarantee. If a qualifying cohort of mass at least \(\ell/k\) had average satisfaction at most \(\ell-1\), the paper’s summed swap-gain argument would force some swap to improve weighted PAV by at least \(1/k^2\), contradicting termination. Since satisfaction levels are integral, average satisfaction greater than \(\ell-1\) implies that the cohort contains positive mass of members with satisfaction at least \(\ell\).

So Proposition 7 survives, with Theorem 4 and Corollary 6 carrying over as its guarantee. The criticism that this is mainly high-multiplicity compression does not make the question out of scope; compression is part of the programme’s stated bridge. It does make this a modest mirror rather than a difficult new tractability result. I cannot make a convincing case that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror here.
