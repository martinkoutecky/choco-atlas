The continuous mirror question

Given a rational type distribution μ over households with positive additive valuations \(u_{tj}\), and rational per-household supplies \(q_j\) of indivisible goods, set \(h_t=∑_j q_j u_{tj}\). A type-\(t\) bundle is a vector \(b∈ℤ_{≥0}^{J}\) with \(u_t·b≥ h_t\) such that removing any item makes its value fall below \(h_t\). Find assignment masses \(x_{t,b}≥0\) satisfying \(∑_b x_{t,b}≤μ_t\) and \(∑_{t,b} b_jx_{t,b}≤q_j\) for every good \(j\), that first maximize \(∑_{t,b}x_{t,b}\) and then maximize the worst preference-rank score among bundles assigned positive mass, with higher scores denoting more preferred bundles.

The model it lives in

A continuum of household types carrying mass, with repeated standardized goods supplied per household. Each unit of mass receives an integral minimal bundle; assignments may split mass within a type across bundles. The objective first maximizes the mass receiving a proportional bundle, then the worst preference-rank score among recipients.

The objection that survived

PR’s fallback rounds treat each player as a unit, while in Mass-PR an arbitrarily small positive mass can determine the worst assigned rank; the paper establishes no finite-population correspondence for this tie-break.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s guarantee of maximal proportionality and maximin preference rank. It leaves the paper’s separate claims about envy-freeness, Pareto optimality, manipulation, and two-person comparisons outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • When the number of good categories varies, can the configuration LP’s pricing problem over minimal integral bundles be solved efficiently?
  • What exact or additive finite-population guarantees relate a mass solution to populations with rational type counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s Theorem 2 (proved here). It says that PR returns a maximally proportional allocation—giving minimal bundles to as many players as possible, and to everyone if a complete allocation exists—and that the result is MX. Here MX is the paper’s maximin criterion over the ranks of the minimal bundles players receive. This is an algorithmic guarantee, not a runtime bound: the paper gives no named result that places a problem in P or proves it hard, and its conclusion says PR’s computational complexity remains to be studied.

A plausible regime is a large relief programme with many households and a stock of standardized, indivisible goods. Suppose the number \(N\) of households is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct valuation types. A type \(t\) specifies a household’s complete additive value vector \(u_{tj}\) for each good category \(j\). If there are \(Nq_j\) copies of category \(j\), then \(q_j\) is its supply per household. This is plausible when stock scales with the recipient population and many households share the same valuations.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Mass-PR for Indivisible Goods. The input is a rational type distribution \(\mu\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); rational per-household supplies \(q_j\); and positive rational values \(u_{tj}\). The proportional threshold for type \(t\) is
\[ h_t=\sum_j q_j u_{tj}. \]
That is the paper’s \(1/N\) share of the type’s value for the whole inventory, after scaling the inventory by \(N\).

An individual bundle remains integral: it is a vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\). It is minimal for type \(t\) when \(u_t\cdot b\ge h_t\), but removing any one item from it would put its value below \(h_t\). Let \(\mathcal M_t\) be the set of these bundles. The decision variable \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(b\in\mathcal M_t\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{b\in\mathcal M_t}x_{t,b}\le\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,b\in\mathcal M_t} b_jx_{t,b}\le q_j \quad\text{for every }j. \]
Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle; only identical agents’ assignments may be split across bundles. The question is to find a feasible \(x\) that lexicographically maximizes the mass receiving a minimal bundle and then maximizes the worst assigned bundle rank under the types’ rankings—the mass version of PR’s maximin refinement.

This mirrors the substance of Theorem 2: the goal is to make as large a share of the population proportional as possible, with the paper’s fairness tie-break. It does not make the bundles or goods continuous. It covers PR’s maximal-proportionality and MX guarantee, not the paper’s claims about envy-freeness, Pareto optimality, or manipulation.

My provisional expectation is that the fixed-\(J\) version is tractable when its minimal bundles can be listed, since the mass-assignment step is an LP. For unrestricted \(J\), I would treat the problem as open, with continuum-specific hardness a real possibility: pricing a minimal integral bundle appears knapsack-like even though assignment mass is continuous. A configuration LP makes the key follow-up precise: can its pricing problem be solved efficiently? Further questions include when the mass solution yields exact or additive guarantees for finite populations, and how the maximin tie-break behaves when a type’s mass is split across ranks.

The soft spot is the regime change. This mirror needs a genuinely large population and a per-capita supply of repeated goods; a small inheritance division among a few parties is not a convincing high-multiplicity setting. The paper does not analyze this scaled-stock model, nor prove a complexity result that would settle the mirror’s classification. That limits what Theorem 2 can establish in favour of continuization, but the proposed question still preserves its central fair-division problem in a plausible many-agent regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 2, and it is not a computational-complexity result. It proves that PR returns a maximally proportional, MX allocation; it says nothing about the running time of finding one. The paper explicitly leaves PR’s complexity for future study. So Mass-PR would not continuize a complexity result from the paper. It would define a new computational problem around a fairness guarantee whose original computational cost is unknown.

There is a real problem with the paper’s literal population limit. With a fixed stock of \(m\) items, at most \(m\) players can receive nonempty bundles, so the fraction served tends to zero as the population grows. Proportionality and MX then cease to describe a meaningful population-wide allocation. The proposed repair—scale supplies with population and treat goods as repeated standardized categories—is sensible, but it changes the setting materially. It replaces uniquely identified items with category copies, changes each player’s threshold to a per-capita supply benchmark, and allows mass of one type to be split across different bundles. That is a reasonable high-multiplicity fair-division model, but it is not simply the paper’s theorem with players replaced by mass.

The rank tie-break also needs a new definition. In a continuum, “worst rank among recipients” can depend on an arbitrarily small positive mass receiving a low-ranked bundle. One can repair that by enumerating rank cutoffs and maximizing served mass subject to each cutoff. But that construction is another modeling choice beyond Theorem 2, whose PR procedure ranks individual players’ bundles through discrete fallback rounds.

Those objections defeat the claim that Mass-PR is a direct continuous mirror of a named computational result. They do not defeat the better version of the proposal. Repeated goods and many households with shared additive valuations make a sensible high-multiplicity regime; letting identical households receive different integral bundles in different proportions is a natural population model. Its configuration LP, and the pricing problem needed when bundles cannot be listed, could produce worthwhile algorithmic questions. The relevant high-multiplicity literature supports that regime rather than counting against it.

So the strongest honest case against is a scope objection: Mass-PR is a promising new problem inspired by the paper, not a continuization of a complexity theorem the paper proves. That leaves the universal claim—no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario—weak. The scaled-stock model is plausible, and the paper’s unstudied computational complexity gives it a concrete question to pursue, even if the anchor is less direct than the proponent suggests.
