What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers computing the TAL awards from claim-class masses, but not the paper's results about equilibrium implementation of the game.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is a continuous version of the TAL allocation rule, but it cannot be anchored to a computational result of this paper: the paper contains no named complexity classification or algorithmic result.

A credible regime would be a large bankruptcy with many creditors in a small number of claim classes. Let type \(t\) mean a creditor with claim \(c_t\), and let \(\mu_t\) be that class’s fraction of creditors. With claims and the estate normalized per creditor, let \(e\leq\sum_t\mu_t c_t\). The Continuous TAL Allocation problem is: given rational \((\mu_t,c_t)_t\), \(e\), and \(\theta\), find each type’s per-creditor award \(x_t\), subject to \(0\leq x_t\leq c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=e\), according to
\[ x_t= \begin{cases} \min\{\theta c_t,\lambda\}, & \sum_t\mu_t\theta c_t\geq e,\\ \theta c_t+\max\{(1-\theta)c_t-\lambda,0\}, & \sum_t\mu_t\theta c_t<e, \end{cases} \]
where \(\lambda\) is chosen to meet the estate constraint. Mass represents shares of creditors; the award is per creditor, not the class total. This is a sensible continuous analogue of the allocation rule, and finding \(\lambda\) over finite support appears tractable by sorting claim thresholds. The authors would likely recognize the allocation question, though it does not preserve their strategic contribution.

The nearest named result is Proposition 3, proved in this paper: for every finite creditor set, bankruptcy problem, and \(\theta\in[0,1]\), \(T^\theta(c,E)\) is the unique Nash-equilibrium outcome of their game \(\Gamma^\theta(c,E)\). Proposition 2, also proved here, establishes a subgame-perfect equilibrium with that outcome; Proposition 1 proves the corresponding unique-equilibrium-outcome result for two creditors and shows it can be supported by a pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibrium. These are strategic implementation results, not results about computational complexity or algorithms, so none qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard.

The weakness is substantial: their game relies on individual creditors announcing permutations, selecting a coordinator, and reducing the problem to a two-creditor negotiation. In an atomless population, selecting one creditor has zero mass, and these strategic moves do not carry over directly. Replacing each class with a single negotiating bloc would change who the players are. Thus the allocation mirror is plausible, but it does not yet give an honest continuous mirror of the paper’s principal result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–3 establish strategic implementation: the TAL rule is the unique equilibrium outcome in the stated games, with the subgame-perfect equilibrium properties described in Propositions 1 and 2. They do not classify the complexity of a problem or present an algorithmic challenge.

The proposed continuous TAL Allocation problem is coherent. A bankruptcy with many creditors in a few claim classes is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and type masses with per-creditor awards preserve the rule’s allocation question. But this mirrors the rule’s formula in Section 2.1, not the paper’s contribution: the paper’s results are about why a particular game implements that rule. Computing the mass-weighted allocation is a direct threshold calculation over the supported claim values. Its simplicity alone would not make it unworthy; the problem is that it gives no computational mirror of the result this paper establishes.

A more faithful mirror would put the strategic game itself over a large or continuous creditor population. Yet the game’s mechanism is built around individual creditors announcing permutations, selecting a coordinator, reaching consensus, and then negotiating bilaterally. Turning claim classes into players changes the strategic roles and their force; retaining individuals leaves a game whose core object is still the finite-player strategic mechanism. Either route could inspire new game-theoretic work, but neither supplies a canonical continuous-population computational problem corresponding to Propositions 1–3. Adding a mass-based intervention on claims would be another possible research direction, but it would introduce a control problem absent from this paper.

So the proponent’s allocation scenario survives as a sensible continuous allocation exercise, and I would not dismiss it on high-multiplicity grounds. It does not, however, rescue this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror. The negative case is strongest on that scope boundary; it cannot rule out every worthwhile continuous bargaining model someone might devise, but such a model would be a new question rather than a continuization of this paper’s result.
