What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror covers Theorems 1–3: Theorem 1 counts tableaux, while Theorems 2 and 3 count profiles whose self-identity condition prevents a type-mass analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive hook is Theorem 3, but it does not meet your anchor rule: it is an exact enumeration, not a result classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. It proves that the number of narcissistic profiles on \(n\) voters that are single-crossing with respect to \(1\triangleright\cdots\triangleright n\) is \(2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\). The authors prove this here, using a bijection with tableaux and their Theorem 1. The other nearby results are also enumerative: Theorem 1, proved here using Stanley’s hook-content formula, gives \(2^{\binom n2}\) semi-standard Young tableaux of order \(n\); Theorem 2, proved here, gives \(\prod_{i=2}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i-1}\) narcissistic profiles single-peaked with respect to the fixed order \(1\triangleright\cdots\triangleright n\). None asserts a complexity classification.

I cannot write a continuous problem per qualifying anchor without changing what these theorems count. In the paper, voters and alternatives are the same \(n\) named individuals, and narcissism requires each individual to rank their own identity first. As \(n\) grows, the alternative set grows too, and each voter has a distinct top alternative. That leaves no regime of many agents represented by relatively few complete preference types. Grouping people by a shared “self” label would replace “ranks herself first” with “ranks her group first,” which is a different property. Making the identity set continuous instead would require a new domain of rankings over infinitely many alternatives and a new way to measure or count profiles; these theorems supply neither.

So the proposed mirror has no prover-ready question that I can defend as the continuous-population form of the paper’s result. The paper studies the number and structure of finite profiles, not computational complexity over a society represented by type masses. The weakness is decisive: even the best hook, Theorem 3, has no natural high-multiplicity regime under the paper’s defining self-identity constraint.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor problem is decisive. Theorem 3 counts single-crossing narcissistic profiles, Theorem 2 counts single-peaked narcissistic profiles, and Theorem 1 counts tableaux used in the proof. None asserts a complexity classification or studies an algorithmic problem over a society given by type masses. The paper mentions recognition algorithms for single-peakedness and single-crossingness, but those are cited prior results, not the results these theorems establish. Even treating the closed-form enumerations as counting problems, their objects are finite sets indexed by \(n\), not computational questions about a continuous society.

The natural population mirror runs into the paper’s defining constraint. Voters and alternatives are the same set, and each voter ranks their own identity first. Thus every voter has a different top alternative. A type that preserves the complete description of an agent must include that identity, so the number of types grows with the population; there is no regime of many people per type. Holding the alternatives fixed and assigning mass to shared preference types loses individual selfhood. Making each cohort rank its group representative first gives a group-narcissism problem, not the profiles counted in Theorems 2 or 3.

A more ambitious mirror could let identities and alternatives range over a continuum, with each agent ranking their own point first. That preserves the self-preference idea, but changes the object from a finite profile of permutations to a function or measure over an infinite alternative space. The paper’s exact counts then have no direct continuous counterpart: a count of profiles would need a newly chosen measure or sampling model, and the choice would supply a new problem rather than translate these theorems. Asking for probabilities under a random-profile model has the same gap: it adds a distributional question the paper does not answer, and does not make the population itself a finite-support mass input.

A weighted enumeration of group-narcissistic profiles might be interesting in its own right. I cannot rule out its value as new combinatorics. But it would require a new model and new results, without a computational anchor in this paper. The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is therefore that all three named anchors are enumerative results about identity-indexed finite profiles, and the identity condition prevents the high-multiplicity regime that the programme’s population continuization needs.
