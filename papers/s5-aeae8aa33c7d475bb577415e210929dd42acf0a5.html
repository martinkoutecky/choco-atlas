The continuous mirror question

Given a rational piecewise linear convex \(V:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}\) with \(V(0)=0\), choose \(q\in L^1([0,1])\cap L^2([0,1])\) to minimize \(\frac12\int_0^1 x^2q(x)^2\,dx\). Set \(p(x)=V'(x)-xq(x)\) and \(R(u)=p(u)+\int_u^1q(y)\,dy\); require \(R(u)\geq\frac{1}{1-u}\int_u^1R(y)\,dy\) for almost every \(u\in[0,1)\). Characterize the optimizer and determine how it can be represented from the finite input \(V\).

The model it lives in

An anonymous transferable utility game with unit mass of interchangeable members and coalition worth \(V(x)\) at coalition mass \(x\). A random priority assigns rank \(u\); when mass \(dx\) enters at mass \(x\), the entrant payment rate \(p(x)\) and incumbent transfer rate \(q(x)\) satisfy \(p(x)+xq(x)=V'(x)\). The selected voluntary flow minimizes the rank averaged quadratic adjustment, with payoff density \(V(1)\) as the Shapley target.

The objection that survived

The rank averaged objective may select a different flow from any limit of the paper’s edge counted Euclidean norm; the proponent acknowledges the metric change but does not resolve its effect on the optimizer.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4’s cardinality game formulation for the closest voluntary flow implementing the Shapley allocation, including the concave case and the small player examples. It leaves the general asymmetric case and the equal residual value, nonnegativity, and core tradeoff results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational piecewise linear \(V\), does the closest-flow optimizer have a finite exact representation, and can it be computed or approximated efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here, but it is a cooperative production problem rather than a voting problem. Imagine a very large membership pool in which every member has the same role, terms, and contribution technology. A coalition’s worth depends only on its mass. The society has one type, with total mass \(1\); for \(n\) members, \(n\) grows while the number of types stays \(\tau=1\).

Let \(V(x)\) be the worth of a coalition of mass \(x\), with \(V(0)=0\). A uniformly random priority order gives each member a rank \(u\in[0,1]\). At coalition mass \(x\), a newly entering mass \(dx\) creates \(V'(x)dx\) worth. Write \(p(x)\) for the entrant’s payment per unit mass and \(q(x)dx\) for the payment per unit mass to each incumbent. Budget balance is \(p(x)+xq(x)=V'(x)\). A member with rank \(u\) receives total path payment \(R(u)=p(u)+\int_u^1q(y)\,dy\). If that member waits, her eventual rank is uniform on \((u,1)\), so voluntary entry requires \(R(u)\geq\frac{1}{1-u}\int_u^1R(y)\,dy\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, “Stable allocation, unstable payment path,” proved here in part: for every convex game, the Shapley allocation is in the core; if the game is nonadditive, its Shapley flow is not voluntary; among convex games, the Shapley allocation is both core-stable and supported by voluntary entry exactly when the game is additive. The core claim is the classical result cited to Shapley (1971); the paper proves the payment-path conclusion. Its continuous mirror is the Continuum Shapley-Path Test: given a convex rational piecewise-linear \(V\), determine whether the random-priority marginal-payment flow is voluntary and whether its induced allocation is in the continuum core, returning violating ranks if any. The flow pays \(V'(u)\) at rank \(u\). Its average payoff per unit mass is \(V(1)\); a coalition of mass \(x\) receives \(xV(1)\), which covers \(V(x)\) by convexity. Voluntariness holds exactly when \(V\) is linear: any later increase in slope makes waiting profitable for earlier ranks. For this finite input representation, the test reduces to inspecting the slopes and is tractable.

Proposition 2, proved here, gives the supporting exact test: under the Shapley flow, entry is voluntary precisely when the current marginal contribution is at least the expected marginal contribution at the eventual entry coalition after waiting; every submodular game passes, while any strict increase in marginal contribution in a supermodular game creates a violated entry constraint. In the continuum model this becomes the comparison of \(V'(u)\) with its average over later ranks. This keeps the mirror attached to the paper’s actual timing incentive, rather than merely to its use of convexity.

A second, optimization anchor is Proposition 4, “Cardinality games,” proved here. For a finite cardinality game \(v(S)=h(|S|)\), let \(d_k=h(k+1)-h(k)\). The closest voluntary flow implementing the Shapley allocation is the unique solution in incumbent transfers \(q_1,\ldots,q_{n-1}\) minimizing \(\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom nk(n-k)k(k+1)q_k^2\), subject to \(r_k(q)\geq\frac{1}{n-k-1}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{n-1}r_\ell(q)\) for \(k=0,\ldots,n-2\), where \(q_0=0\), \(r_k(q)=d_k-kq_k+\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{n-1}q_\ell\), and entrant payments are \(p_k=d_k-kq_k\). If \(h\) is concave, the optimum is \(q_k=0\) at every level. The paper also solves the two- and three-player cases explicitly.

Its continuous counterpart is the Closest Voluntary Mass-Flow Problem. Given rational piecewise-linear \(V\), find a measurable incumbent-transfer rate \(q\) minimizing \(\frac12\int_0^1x^2q(x)^2\,dx\), with \(p(x)=V'(x)-xq(x)\), subject to the voluntary-entry inequalities above. The solution is the selected flow \(q^*\) and its entrant payments \(p^*\); budget balance makes the symmetric terminal payoff density \(V(1)\), the Shapley target. This is a convex functional optimization problem, and I would expect a Class A result if its optimizer can be represented finitely for piecewise-linear input. Establishing that representation and its bit complexity is an open question, not a result of the paper. The objective is a rank-averaged continuous version of the finite distance criterion: averaging by entry rank gives a meaningful measure once coalition size is continuous.

The weakest point is that this rank-averaged objective is not the literal limit of Proposition 4’s Euclidean norm, which counts every finite coalition edge and weights coalition sizes differently. The closest-flow mirror therefore requires a stated metric co-modification. The more direct Shapley-path test does not depend on that choice and is the firmer mirror. There is also a standard atomless-population issue: individual payments vanish with individual mass, so incentives are stated for payoff densities, equivalently as limits of per-member comparisons in large finite pools.

The paper reports no NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity theorem. The optimization anchor is a named formulation that the paper analyzes, rather than a complexity classification. This mirror covers the cardinality-game payment-path results; it does not claim to extend the paper’s equal-residual or multi-type results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchors are game-theoretic results, not computational results. The core claim in Theorem 1 is classical; its new claim concerns incentives on a payment path. Proposition 2 supplies the same incentive test, so it is not a second computational anchor. Proposition 4 gives a finite convex quadratic program and reductions for symmetric cases, but states no complexity classification or algorithmic boundary. A continuous formulation can be made from these results, but it would create the computational question rather than mirror one the paper asks.

For the Shapley path, the one-type model is sensible, but its proposed test is almost entirely a curvature check. For convex piecewise-linear \(V\), the continuum core condition follows from \(V(x)\leq xV(1)\), and voluntariness reduces to
\[ V'(u)\geq \frac{1}{1-u}\int_u^1 V'(y)\,dy. \]
With nondecreasing slopes, this holds at every rank exactly when the slopes are constant. A violation can be located by finding a later increase in slope. That is a clean continuous restatement of Theorem 1 and Proposition 2, but it supplies no computational landscape beyond inspecting a finite list of slopes. The fact that the test is easy is not itself a reason to reject the mirror; the concern is that the paper’s result is an incentive characterization, while the proposed mirror turns it into a small input-inspection task.

The stronger rescue is a finite-type worth function \(V(x)\), with \(x\) a vector of coalition masses, and an incentive test for each entrant type. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But its computational content depends on how \(V\) is encoded: explicit polyhedral pieces, an oracle, or some other representation produce different problems. Hardness could come from the dimension or representation of \(V\), rather than from replacing headcounts with masses. This does not make the question invalid; it makes the proposed extension a new computational model whose connection to the paper’s result needs more than the one-type rank test.

Proposition 4 is the best anchor, and also where the mirror’s main technical choice becomes visible. The finite objective weights level \(k\) by \(\binom nk(n-k)k(k+1)\), because it counts every coalition edge. A rank-averaged objective such as \(\int_0^1 x^2q(x)^2\,dx\) weights coalition sizes differently. A random-path average is a reasonable alternative, but it changes the nearest-flow criterion and can select a different flow. Moreover, the continuous optimizer is a function: an algorithmic version needs an input encoding for \(V\), an output representation for \(q^*\), and an exact or approximate solution standard. The proponent correctly identifies finite representability and bit complexity as open; until those are specified, the “closest voluntary mass-flow problem” is not yet a defined complexity problem.

This is a case against prioritizing this paper for ChoCo, rather than a proof that every mirror is worthless. A continuous cooperative-game model with mass-dependent coalition worth and a carefully chosen path metric could be worthwhile. The honest weakness in the negative case is that this model is plausible, and the paper’s cardinality setting makes it unusually natural. What is missing is a compelling computational problem already anchored in the paper: the path test has a direct slope criterion, and the optimization mirror needs a new metric and a finite computational specification before its value to the programme can be judged.
