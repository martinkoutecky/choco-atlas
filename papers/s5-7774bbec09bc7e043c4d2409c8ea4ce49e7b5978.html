What the mirror covers

The anchor covers only computation of the happy nucleolus; the paper’s determining-family and minimality results remain finite set-cover results as well.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I see is a narrow type-block Happy Nucleolus for public-service or logistics costs. Imagine a large population represented by a small number \(\tau\) of customer cohorts. Within each cohort, people have identical service eligibility, costs, prices, and bargaining arrangements. A service option covers whole cohorts, and its total cost scales with population size. The number of residents \(N\) is large compared with \(\tau\).

Let \(P\) be the cohort types, with masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and let \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^P\) be the explicit service options, with per-population costs \(c(T)\). For a coalition \(S\subseteq P\) of whole cohorts, write
\[ \kappa(S)=\min\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal U}c(T): \mathcal U\subseteq\mathcal T,\ \mathcal U\text{ covers }S\right\}, \]
and let
\[ \lambda=\min\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal T}c(T)x_T: x_T\ge0,\ \sum_{T\ni t}x_T\ge1\quad\forall t\in P\right\}. \]

The lead problem is: given \((P,\mu,\mathcal T,c)\), find the unique vector \(a\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{P}\) of per-person cohort allocations such that, with \(z_t=\mu_t a_t\), \(\sum_t z_t=\lambda\), every coalition is group-rational, and the sorted excesses
\[ \left(\kappa(S)-\sum_{t\in S}z_t\right)_{\emptyset\ne S\subseteq P} \]
are lexicographically maximized. The output is \(a\). For a population of size \(N\), cohort \(t\) receives total allocation \(N z_t\), and a service option \(T\) costs \(N c(T)\).

My formal anchor is the paper’s proved polynomial-time corollary: Corollary 6 in the numbered arXiv version says that, for any given set-covering instance \((P,\mathcal T,c)\), its happy nucleolus can be computed in time polynomial in \(|P|\) and \(|\mathcal T|\). Its proof uses the small determining family and implements the MPS scheme with polynomially many LPs. Applied to the cohort instance, this gives time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of service options, and the rational input length; dividing by \(\mu_t\) recovers per-person rates. The result is proved in the paper. The supplied starred restatement suppresses the corollary numeral, and theorem numbering differs across versions. [Numbered paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04040v1)

This is a computational population-mass question: the instance records cohort shares, and the output is a fair allocation rate for each share. The set-cover structure remains intact, including group rationality and lexicographic excess fairness; the mirror does not replace those with a simpler objective. I expect this version to be tractable, by direct reduction to the paper’s result. It covers the happy-nucleolus computation only. The paper’s discussion of the ordinary nucleolus being NP-hard is not a numbered hardness anchor here, so this case does not claim that a continuous mirror dissolves a named hardness result.

The weak point is the coalition model. I have restricted coalitions to unions of whole cohorts—plausible when customer associations or municipal blocs bargain as units. If individuals can form coalitions from arbitrary fractions of each cohort, the excesses become a continuum of values, and the paper’s finite sorted excess vector has no immediate canonical extension. That more faithful atomless version is a further question, not a result of Corollary 6. The type-block version is still worth testing with the authors: it preserves their underlying set-cover cost-allocation problem while making large cohort masses and per-person rates explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would oppose this anchor, though I cannot make the universal negative airtight. The corollary is a genuine computational result. The problem is that the proposed mirror does no computational work with the population distribution: in the variables \(z_t=\mu_t a_t\), every excess and every constraint is independent of \(\mu\). The theorem computes the happy nucleolus of an ordinary set-covering instance with \(\tau\) players; \(\mu\) only converts the resulting totals into per-person rates. That is a useful quotient model, but it does not show what happens when the original players occur with high multiplicity.

Allowing coalitions to contain arbitrary portions of each type exposes the harder issue. If identical members have the same incidence in every service option, an option covers either all or none of a type. The cost of a coalition of mass \(q\) then depends on its support, while its excess is \(\kappa(\operatorname{supp}q)-\sum_t q_t a_t\). For each support, these excesses fill a continuum of values. The paper’s sorted finite excess vector has no direct continuation: near the minimum there are arbitrarily many excesses with no next-smallest one. Optimizing only the minimum for each support reduces to the whole-cohort game again. A richer extension needs a new rule for comparing the continuum of excesses, such as a measure or quantile convention, and that choice is not supplied by the result.

A different rescue is to let service options cover fractional demand and define coalition cost by a fractional-cover LP. That is a plausible continuous model, but it changes the coalition cost function. Moreover, an optimal dual allocation for the grand demand \(\mu\) satisfies the fractional group-rationality constraints for every \(q\le\mu\), so the core is nonempty. The special “happy” relaxation that motivates the paper then disappears; this would be a new continuum nucleolus problem, not a mirror of the stated result.

So the strongest case against is that preserving the paper’s set-cover costs leaves either a finite type-block game or an undefined continuum fairness objective; making costs naturally scale with fractional demand changes the game and removes the paper’s defining core-empty case. The weakness is real: fixed-cost services negotiated by whole customer blocs are plausible, and the authors might recognize that as their problem. In that scenario the corollary applies directly, so I would argue against this anchor as a worthwhile population continuization, but would not claim to have ruled out every sensible continuous model.
