The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor under the rule you set: this paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. It does make complexity claims in prose, often summarizing or citing other papers. I can’t honestly give one of those claims a theorem number.

The strongest *unqualified candidate* is the claim in §3.4, attributed to Rothkopf et al. (1998): optimal clearing in a canonical combinatorial auction is NP-complete when there is one unit of each item, bids are on bundles, overlapping bids cannot both win, free disposal is allowed, and there are no XOR or other side constraints. The paper also reports that fractional bid acceptance can be solved by linear programming. These are useful claims to investigate, but neither is presented as a numbered result here.

A plausible population mirror would model a large procurement market with many suppliers but relatively few distinct supplier types. A type \(t\) specifies a supplier’s bundle bid \(a_t\) and price \(v_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of suppliers of that type. Given per-capita item supplies \(\rho\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) whose bid is accepted, to maximize \(\sum_t v_t x_t\), subject to \(\sum_t a_{ti}x_t\le \rho_i\) for every item \(i\). Each supplier still receives an all-or-nothing decision; the fractional quantity comes from accepting some of the mass of an identical type.

In markets with large cohorts of suppliers offering standardized bundles, \(N\) could be large while the number of distinct bid types \(\tau\) is much smaller. The continuous question is whether this clearing problem has an efficient algorithm in the number of items, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Its expected answer is tractability via an LP. The paper’s own discussion of fractional clearing makes this direction recognizable to its authors, though the mirror’s rationale is population mass, not fractional outcomes.

The weak point is substantial: the paper does not establish that the NP-hardness it reports is driven by population multiplicity, and the LP result it cites may already answer the proposed aggregate optimization. More basically, without a numbered computational result, this cannot be submitted as a compliant anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised one candidate: the canonical combinatorial auction clearing result in §3.4. Under the anchor rule given here, it is not an eligible anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its NP-completeness claim is prose attributed to Rothkopf et al. That is a decisive procedural objection to this anchor-based case, though it does not show that no continuous mirror exists.

The proposed formulation also needs a closer match to the auction problem. One variable \(x_t\) per bundle bid fits a restricted market where each type submits one bid. A fuller mirror would let \(t\) specify a bidder’s complete valuation or bid language, and use variables for the fractions of that type assigned each feasible bundle or allocation. That yields a continuous-population clearing problem whose LP may have exponentially many columns; its pricing problem depends on the type’s valuation representation. The large-cohort scenario is plausible, so I cannot rule out a worthwhile question on multiplicity grounds.

The negative case is therefore weak beyond the anchor-screening objection. The paper already gives LP results for fractional clearing in nearby variants, which makes the proposed one-bid mirror look easy to answer, but fractional outcomes alone do not settle the continuous-population question. I cannot honestly claim that this paper has no worthwhile mirror in any scenario.
