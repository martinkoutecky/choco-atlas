The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition that establishes a computational complexity result. The Appendix 1 lemma gives a Bayesian product rule, and Appendix 2’s Lemma 2 says a risk-neutral player maximizes expected log payoff by reporting their true beliefs. Neither says a problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another complexity classification. The reported forecasting improvements are experimental results, not named computational results. So there are zero anchor-indexed questions to give without inventing an anchor.

The strongest paper-inspired mirror I can offer is a proposed extension of its nonlinear aggregation mechanism. Call it Mass-Weighted Risk-Corrected Forecast Aggregation. Let \(S\) be the paper’s finite set of possible outcomes. A type \(t\) consists of a strictly positive forecast vector \(q^t\) over \(S\) and its market-derived risk-correction exponent \(\beta_t\). The input is the support of a society, with rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, plus an effective aggregation scale \(\lambda\) and an error tolerance \(\varepsilon\). The task is to return a forecast within \(\varepsilon\) of

\[ P_\mu(s)= \frac{\exp\!\left(\lambda\sum_t \mu_t\beta_t\log q^t_s\right)} {\sum_{r\in S}\exp\!\left(\lambda\sum_t \mu_t\beta_t\log q^t_r\right)} \quad\text{for each }s\in S. \]

Here mass is the share of the forecasting population with that complete report-and-calibration type. The output is a probability distribution over outcomes, as in the paper. The scale \(\lambda\) preserves the original aggregation’s strength: for a discrete panel of \(N\) people, set \(\lambda=N\) and \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), so each type contributes its original \(n_t\beta_t\) exponent.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization running recurring forecasts, as the paper itself discusses: many employees forecast the same finite-outcome events using shared information packets, a common reporting interface, and a limited set of market-calibrated profiles. For example, \(N\) could be in the thousands while the number \(\tau\) of distinct report-and-calibration types is much smaller. The paper’s equation (2) is the direct motivation: grouping identical contributors turns the product over people into a mass-weighted sum of log reports. Outcomes remain finite; it is the population that becomes the continuous object.

I would expect this proposed problem to be tractable: compute the weighted log scores and normalize them, using precision sufficient to meet \(\varepsilon\), in time polynomial in \(|S|\), \(\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\). It covers the paper’s nonlinear forecast aggregation mechanism, not the market stage that estimates risk attitudes or the experimental claim that the mechanism outperforms alternatives. Further questions include how forecast accuracy changes under small shifts in type masses, and how market-derived exponents should be estimated for a large population.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s experiments deliberately use small groups of 8–13 people, and in realistic large panels, forecasts and risk profiles may be so individualized that \(\tau\) approaches \(N\). Also, the proposed aggregation computation is a straightforward evaluation of the paper’s formula, not a complexity result the authors established. It is a plausible continuous-population extension they might recognize, but the absence of a named computational anchor keeps it from being a strong case under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no computational anchors in the paper: no named result classifies the complexity of a problem. Appendix 1 gives a Bayesian identity, Appendix 2 characterizes truthful reporting under risk neutrality, and the main results are experimental comparisons. So the proponent has not mirrored a computational result; the mass-weighted formula is a new proposal.

That proposal takes forecasts and calibration exponents as input, groups repeated types, and evaluates the paper’s aggregation rule. It leaves out the market stage that estimates the exponents and the experiments that assess forecast accuracy. A more ambitious mirror—such as finding the minimum mass of forecasters whose changed reports would move the aggregate past a decision threshold—would need a threshold, costs for changing reports, and a model of who can be influenced. None is supplied by the paper. That could be a worthwhile new problem, but it would be an extension rather than a mirror of a result here.

There is also a scaling problem in the proposed formula. Writing \(a_s=\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t\log q^t_s\), preserving the paper’s product over \(N\) people requires probabilities proportional to \(\exp(Na_s)\). If one state uniquely maximizes \(a_s\), this distribution concentrates on that state as \(N\) grows. Keeping a finite \(\lambda\) avoids that concentration, but introduces an effective population scale the paper does not define; setting \(\lambda=1\) changes the accumulation of independent evidence. The continuous version therefore needs a substantive modelling choice before its forecast has a clear interpretation.

A large forecasting organization with repeated report-and-calibration profiles is the proponent’s strongest scenario. It could yield a small support, but common information packets make repeated reports dependent, while independent signals may produce many distinct forecast types. The paper provides no type model that resolves this tension. Its small experimental groups and its examples of large communities with dispersed information do not establish a natural high-multiplicity regime for this mechanism.

So the case against is strong on the paper-level question: there is no computational result to mirror, and the proposed calculation is thin as a ChoCo problem. The universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile work is weaker. The paper itself points toward large forecasting populations, and a carefully specified mass-manipulation or robustness problem might be valuable; it would simply need to be developed as new work rather than presented as a mirror of this paper.
