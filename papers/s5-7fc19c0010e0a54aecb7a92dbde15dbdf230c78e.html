The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is the paper’s expert-poll scenario. Imagine a large survey on a fixed set of propositions, where respondents face the same argument map and can be grouped by their complete belief assignment. A type is one validity-respecting assignment; its mass is the fraction of respondents with that assignment. With the agenda fixed, the possible type set is finite, while the respondent population can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types actually observed. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, even though a committee of 51 experts need not be one.

A precise follow-on problem is Continuous Majority-Consistency. The input is a finite proposition set, an argument map whose constraints define the valid belief assignments, and an explicit support list of valid types \(t\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Promise that no proposition has exactly half the mass on either side. For each proposition \(p\), let \(b_\mu(p)=1\) when more than half the mass accepts \(p\), and \(0\) otherwise. Decide whether \(b_\mu\) satisfies every constraint in the argument map. A “no” solution can give a violated argument. This directly continuizes the paper’s sentence-wise majority aggregation: agents still have individually consistent beliefs, and the question is whether their mass-majority belief is consistent. With the support listed explicitly, the check is in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the map size.

This covers the paper’s group-level aggregation question, but not its central empirical claim that inconsistency prevalence changes with diversity and polarisation. A single mass profile has a determinate majority outcome; it does not supply the sampling distribution needed to talk about prevalence across groups. Recovering that claim would require specifying a further random model over mass profiles or argument maps.

There are no qualifying anchors in the paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity classification. Its findings are reported through simulations, figures, a table of consistency rates, and unnumbered regression results. So the problem above is an honest positive mirror of the underlying group task, but it cannot be presented as mirroring a named numbered result under your anchor rule. The weakest point is that the most faithful deterministic mirror is also a straightforward tally-and-check problem; the paper’s distinctive contribution is the empirical relationship between group composition and inconsistency risk, which a single continuum society does not capture.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational anchor in this paper. It reports simulation findings about how diversity, dispersion, and inferential density relate to the prevalence of inconsistent majority opinions. It states no complexity result for a computational problem. The proponent acknowledges this, so Continuous Majority-Consistency is not a mirror of a named result; it is a new task suggested by the paper.

That task is well-defined and its population interpretation is sound. For an explicit list of validity-respecting belief types with rational masses, tallying each proposition’s majority and checking the argument constraints answers whether that society’s majority opinion is consistent. But that answers a single society’s outcome. The paper’s central finding concerns inconsistency *prevalence across generated groups and argument maps*. A single mass distribution does not supply that sampling process. To recover prevalence, one must add a probability law over societies, maps, or both. In the large-population limit, sampling from a fixed type distribution generally settles into its deterministic majority; the finite-sample variation the paper measures then needs to be retained as another part of the model.

A stronger computational question might ask for the minimum mass of agents whose beliefs must change to make the majority consistent, or for the probability of inconsistency under a specified sampling model. Those could be worthwhile questions. But they add an intervention objective or a probability model absent from the paper. The authors do discuss alternative aggregation procedures and future work on deliberation, so an intervention question might connect to their interests; it still would not continuize a computational result they proved. And a probability law over populations risks shifting the computational object from the society itself to the sampling process that generated it.

The negative case is weakest at this boundary: the paper motivates adjacent computational questions, and a continuous population could be a sensible input to them. But on the stipulated anchor standard, no result survives. The proposed consistency check captures one group-level operation, while the paper’s contribution is an empirical relationship measured by simulation. Treating that relationship as a continuous complexity result would require adding the very computational problem the paper does not formulate.
