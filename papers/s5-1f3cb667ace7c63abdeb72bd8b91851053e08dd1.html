The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible high multiplicity setting is a large sensor network built from a small number of repeated sensor classes. A type would include an agent’s likelihood model for every hypothesis and its communication profile; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of sensors of type \(t\). The class-level interaction matrix would also have to preserve the network structure the results use. Such a regime is credible, but it does not give this paper an eligible computational anchor.

The nearest numbered results are analytic. Theorem 1, proved here, gives the almost-sure exponential rate at which an agent’s belief ratio for a wrong hypothesis decays when that hypothesis has positive transmission probability: the rate is the negative Perron-weighted sum of the agents’ KL divergences. Corollary 1, proved here, says that under global identifiability, giving every wrong hypothesis positive transmission probability suffices for all agents to learn the truth with probability one. Theorem 2, proved here, says that with positive initial beliefs, the true hypothesis retains positive belief at every finite time and its belief has positive limit inferior almost surely, so agents do not become fully confident in a wrong hypothesis. None asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm for a computational problem over continuous populations.

The introduction does say that computing a fully Bayesian group posterior is NP-hard, citing [13]. But that claim is unnumbered, comes from another paper, and concerns the fully Bayesian approach that this paper contrasts with its own algorithm. It cannot honestly serve as an anchor for this paper’s results.

So I do not see a continuous computational problem per anchor to hand to a prover. One could study whether a continuum or high-multiplicity version of this social-learning process learns the truth, but that would chiefly continue the paper’s analytic question. The weakest point in the positive case is therefore decisive: the paper has a plausible repeated-agent regime, but no named computational result connecting that regime to the programme’s target.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The repeated-sensor scenario is plausible: take a large network with a few sensor classes, each with the same likelihood models and communication role, and represent each class by its population fraction. That gives a sensible continuous population. But it does not supply the computational problem the programme needs. In the paper, the numbered results are about convergence and learning, not the complexity of a computational task.

Theorem 1 gives an asymptotic belief-decay rate for a fixed network and transmission rule. Corollary 1 gives sufficient conditions for truth learning. Theorem 2 proves a probabilistic robustness property. In a block-structured continuum model, each could motivate an analytic question about limiting behavior. To turn one into a computational question, one must add a design task—such as choosing sensor-class masses, communication costs, or a finite-horizon topic schedule. Those could be worthwhile projects, but they introduce the objective and choices the paper does not study. Theorem 1’s dependence on the topic distribution also does not itself supply such a task: it characterizes rates under a given distribution.

The strongest attempted mirror would be to choose the mix of sensor types needed to guarantee a target learning rate. That is recognizably related to the paper’s goal, but it is a new population-design problem: the paper fixes the agents, their likelihoods, and their network, then analyzes the learning rule. Similarly, optimizing topic schedules for finite-time performance changes the paper’s asymptotic question into a resource-allocation problem. Continuizing the population does not by itself make either one a mirror of a computational result.

The NP-hardness claim in the introduction is the nearest computational hook, but it is cited as a result about fully Bayesian group inference, the approach this paper contrasts with its own. It is not a numbered result of this paper, and the paper does not develop that computational problem.

So the negative case is strongest on scope: this paper offers a credible continuous *analytic* model, but no named computational result to mirror. Its weak point is that a new optimization problem over sensor-class masses could still be worth studying. I would call that a promising adjacent project, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
