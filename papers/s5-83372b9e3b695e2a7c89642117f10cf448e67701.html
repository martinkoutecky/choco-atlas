The continuous mirror question

Given a finite hospital set \(H\), resident types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), mutually acceptable preference lists over hospitals and types, and common rational quotas \(0<\lambda\le\kappa\), choose assignment masses \(x_{ih}\ge0\) with \(\sum_h x_{ih}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ih}\le\kappa\). For each acceptable pair \((i,h)\), let \(D_{ih}\) be the mass of type \(i\) assigned to a strictly worse hospital or unmatched, and \(W_{ih}\) the mass at \(h\) of types that \(h\) ranks strictly below \(i\); require that no pair has \(D_{ih}>0\) and either unused capacity at \(h\) or \(W_{ih}>0\). The score is \(S(x)=\sum_{h\in H}\min\{1,\sum_i x_{ih}/\lambda\}\). What is the best polynomial-time approximation factor \(\rho\), as a function of \(|H|\), \(\tau=|T|\), \(\theta=\kappa/\lambda\), and input bit length \(L\), for maximizing \(S(x)\) over stable assignments?

The model it lives in

A uniform-quota hospitals/residents market with a continuum of resident mass distributed over complete preference types. The decision variables are assignment masses \(x_{ih}\); each unit of mass receives at most one hospital, hospitals have common quota fractions \([\lambda,\kappa]\), and the objective maximizes the paper’s stable-matching satisfaction score.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper by reducing from Vertex Cover with a perfect matching. It states that HRT-MSLQ in the uniform model, where every hospital has quotas \([\ell,u]\) and \(\theta=u/\ell\), is inapproximable within \(\frac{1}{3}\theta+1-\epsilon\theta^2\) under UGC, and within \(\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{3}\theta+1-\epsilon\theta^2\) unless \(P=NP\), for any \(\epsilon>0\). The proof builds on earlier Vertex Cover hardness; the reductions connecting it to this matching problem are given here.

I would mirror the uniform model in a large residency market. Residents are a continuum of mass \(1\), distributed over a finite set \(T\) of complete preference types. A type includes its ranking over hospitals and acceptable pairs. Each hospital ranks resident types, so residents within a type are indistinguishable to every hospital. This is plausible in a large cohort with standardized specialty and credential categories, where hospitals use category-based priorities. The regime is \(N\) applicants with \(\tau=|T|\) recurring types, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\). Hospital quotas scale with the population: if the discrete quotas are \(\ell,u\) among \(N\) residents, the continuous quotas are \(\lambda=\ell/N\) and \(\kappa=u/N\).

Call the mirror Uniform Continuum HRT-MSLQ. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_i\ge 0\) for \(i\in T\), summing to \(1\); a finite set \(H\) of hospitals; resident-type and hospital preference orders with mutually acceptable pairs; and common rational quotas \(0<\lambda\le\kappa\) for every hospital. A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{ih}\ge0\) on acceptable pairs, with \(\sum_h x_{ih}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ih}\le\kappa\). The remainder of each type is unmatched. Here \(x_{ih}\) is the fraction of type \(i\) assigned to \(h\): every individual still receives at most one hospital.

Require stability in the population sense. For an acceptable pair \((i,h)\), let \(D_{ih}\) be the mass of type \(i\) assigned somewhere it strictly prefers less than \(h\), including unmatched mass. Let \(W_{ih}\) be the mass at \(h\) of types that \(h\) strictly ranks below \(i\). The assignment is stable if there is no pair with \(D_{ih}>0\) and either unused capacity at \(h\) or \(W_{ih}>0\). Its score is
\[ S(x)=\sum_{h\in H}\min\left\{1,\frac{\sum_i x_{ih}}{\lambda}\right\}. \]
The problem is to find a stable assignment maximizing \(S(x)\). The approximation question matching Theorem 13 is: for each fixed \(\theta=\kappa/\lambda\), can an algorithm return a stable \(x\) with \(S(x)\ge \mathrm{OPT}/\rho\), and what is the best achievable \(\rho\), as a function of \(|H|,\tau,\theta\), and the input bit length?

I think the authors would recognize this as their uniform HRT-MSLQ question at population scale. It keeps their stability condition, their soft lower quotas, and their objective; only the population counts and quotas are normalized. A discrete matching in a type-homogeneous instance maps to such a mass assignment by dividing counts by \(N\). The converse need not hold: the continuum can split a type’s mass across hospitals, which is part of the question rather than a change to what each resident receives.

My prior is Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction behind Theorem 13 encodes Vertex Cover in the preference and quota structure, so its combinatorics do not appear to come merely from the number of individual residents. But that is a prediction, not a transferred proof: fractional mass choices could soften the reduction into a fractional cover and improve the approximation ratio. The main follow-up is whether the reduction gadgets force effectively integral choices under continuous stability, or whether a tractable fractional formulation captures the optimum. The discrete-to-continuous identity and approximation gap are also natural questions to settle.

This mirror covers only Theorem 13’s uniform-model inapproximability results; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s general-model bounds or its Triple Proposal algorithm. The weakest point is the type assumption on hospital priorities. If hospitals rank individuals idiosyncratically, those ranks belong in the complete type, and \(\tau\) may approach \(N\). The proposed regime instead relies on plausible category-based priorities. That limits where the mirror applies, but does not reduce it to outcome-space continuity: the population itself is the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 13 is the anchor I cannot honestly defeat. It is a named computational result about approximability, and the proposed question continuizes the population. If fractional mass assignments weaken the Vertex Cover reduction, that is a possible answer to the mirror, not a reason the question is unworthy.

The strongest objection is to how closely this particular hardness result fits a high-multiplicity regime. In the reduction, residents have graph-dependent preferences and private options such as \(x_k^i\). Their complete types therefore encode the graph’s incidence structure; preserving that structure makes the type support grow with the reduction. Collapsing residents into a few recurring categories would erase information the reduction uses. The application story also needs care: with a fixed set of hospitals and fixed seat quotas, scaling population mass makes each quota fraction tend to zero, and the score approaches a count of hospitals receiving positive mass. Keeping positive quota fractions requires hospital capacity to grow with the population, which is a less direct model of residency matching.

That weakens the connection between Theorem 13’s hard instances and a compressed population model. It does not defeat the mirror. A scalable market with recurring resident types is still a sensible scenario, and whether fractional assignments preserve any of the reduction’s force is a substantive question. My negative case is therefore weak: I can challenge the anchor’s application and the reduction’s fit to high multiplicity, but I cannot support the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
