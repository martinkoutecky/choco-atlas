What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers only Example 6’s public-good allocation with a single project and finitely many benefit types; it does not cover the axiomatic characterizations in Theorems 1 and 2 or the paper’s broader bargaining-set applications.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive route is the paper’s public-good example: \(10{,}000\) households fall into three benefit types, with masses \(0.2, 0.4, 0.4\) and benefits \(20,10,0\). That is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many agents share the same benefit and hence are indistinguishable for this calculation. The mass would mean each type’s fraction of households; the quantity to compute would be the fair net payoff, or equivalent levy, per household of each type.

There is a strict limitation, though: the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. Its nearest result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It characterizes the unique solution satisfying efficiency, anonymity, compromise, continuity, additivity, and monotonicity in the extended model. For each player \(i\), it gives
\[ \phi_i(C)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}} \binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1} \left( \max_{x\in C}(x_{S\cup\{i\}}+x_{n+1}) - \max_{x\in C}(x_S+x_{n+1}) \right). \]
That is a formula for the characterized allocation, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Example 6 computes an approximation for the household case, but it is not a named complexity result either. So neither can honestly serve as a required computational anchor.

A concrete *candidate extension*, rather than an anchor-backed mirror, is Continuum Public-Good Shapley Allocation. An instance has a finite set of household types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-household benefits \(v_t\geq 0\), and a rational per-household project cost \(\kappa\), with \(0<\kappa<V=\sum_t\mu_t v_t\). The project is built; the output is the fair net payoff \(y_t\) per household of each type, equivalently the transfer \(y_t-v_t\) relative to that type’s project benefit. For coalition mass \(q\), its stand-alone claim is
\[ g(q)=\max\left\{\sum_{t\in T}q_t v_t-\kappa,\ 0\right\}. \]
Define the type payoff as the limit of the finite Shapley payoff for one type-\(t\) household in societies with \(n_t/n\to\mu_t\) and total project cost \(n\kappa\). Equivalently, in this model,
\[ y_t=\int_0^1 D_t^+g(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda =v_t\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{V}\right), \]
where \(D_t^+\) is the marginal change in the coalition claim from adding infinitesimal type-\(t\) mass. For the paper’s figures, \(V=8\) and \(\kappa=4\), giving \(y=(10,5,0)\), the approximation reported in Example 6. The mass-weighted payoffs sum to \(V-\kappa\), and the type-level levies fund the cost. This is an exact, finite-input computation with a simple closed form.

This is plausible as a population-continuous extension of the paper’s stand-alone Shapley allocation: it keeps the alternatives, claims, and fairness calculation, while replacing repeated households with type masses. The likely direction for this restricted question is tractable. Natural follow-ups are the convergence rate from finite electorates, cases with ties or more alternatives, and whether the same limit works for general bargaining sets.

The weak point is decisive for the requested anchoring standard. Theorem 2 is an axiomatic characterization, and Example 6 is an application; neither establishes a computational result that continuization can mirror. The proposed problem is therefore a credible extension of one worked application, not a continuous counterpart of a named complexity result in this paper. It also does not cover the paper’s general characterization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper states no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1 and 2 characterize a solution by axioms; neither gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or even a finite encoding for its input. Theorem 2’s formula sums over coalitions, but the bargaining set \(C\) may be an arbitrary convex set, so the paper does not define the computational task of evaluating that formula. Example 6 is an approximate application, not a complexity result. The proponent identifies this gap correctly, and it means none of the paper’s results supplies a valid computational anchor.

The household example is still a sensible high multiplicity scenario: benefit levels are complete types, and their population shares are meaningful. The proposed question is also coherent. But it adds a per-household cost, selects a single public good, and defines the payoff through a replication limit. Those choices yield a continuous version of that application, not a computational mirror of the paper’s general characterization. A stronger extension could allow finitely many alternatives, type-specific utilities, and alternative surpluses, then ask how to compute the limit allocation from the type distribution. That could be a worthwhile new problem. It would require choosing a finite input representation and adding a computational question the paper never formulates.

There is also a modeling boundary to that extension. In the baseline model, a type distribution over utilities for alternatives gives coalition claims of the form \(g(q)=\max_a q\cdot u(a)\). When one alternative is strictly best for the society’s type proportions, scaling the coalition in those same proportions preserves that alternative as the maximizer. The continuum then loses much of the finite-player marginal-claim variation that motivates compensation. Ties require a further rule or a more detailed limit model. Scaling costs with population can prevent this collapse, as in the proposed public-good example, but that is a defensible co-modification that produces a narrower application family; it does not supply the missing computational result.

So the strongest negative conclusion is about this paper and the programme’s mirroring standard: there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result here, because there is no such result to mirror. The broader claim that no useful continuous research question can arise from the paper would go too far. The household setting is plausible, and its scaled-cost extension could be worth studying as a separate computational project.
