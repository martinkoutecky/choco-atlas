The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass society with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), explicit coverage valuations \(w_t\), item classes \(J\), a known distribution \(p\), horizon \(K\), and packet mass \(\eta\), each of the \(K\) arrivals supplies \(\eta\) slots of a class drawn independently from \(p\). A causal policy may allocate each packet across advertiser mass, moving mass between integral-bundle states \(S\subseteq J\), with each unit of advertiser mass receiving whole slots. Does there exist, for each fixed \(\delta>0\), a policy polynomial in the encoded instance size, \(|J|\), the number of types, and \(K\), that achieves a \((1-1/e+\delta)\)-competitive ratio against the expected offline optimum on every such instance?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass advertiser population with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), each type having an explicit coverage valuation \(w_t\); over \(K\) i.i.d. arrivals of class \(j\sim p\), each packet has mass \(\eta\), and causal decisions move mass \(x_{t,S}\) among integral-bundle configurations \(S\subseteq J\) to maximize expected welfare against the expected offline optimum.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4.4 uses uncertainty from individual arrivals, while i.i.d. frequencies concentrate under fluid scaling and splittable packets can be spread across bundle states; its \(1-1/e\) barrier therefore needs a new argument in this model.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 4.4's i.i.d. coverage-valuation hardness. It leaves aside Theorem 2.1's adversarial lower bound, Theorem 3.3's budget-additive LP barrier, and Theorem 4.3's diminishing-returns guarantee.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(1-1/e\) hardness persist when each stochastic arrival packet can be split across advertiser states?
  • Can a finite-to-fluid reduction preserve the Max \(k\)-cover gap as packet mass shrinks?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper. It says that, unless \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{RP}\), no polynomial-time algorithm for online welfare maximization with coverage valuations in the i.i.d. model achieves a \((1-1/e+\delta)\)-competitive ratio for any fixed \(\delta>0\). The paper establishes the bound using a known uniform item distribution. Its reduction starts from hard Max \(k\)-cover instances, gives each of \(n\) coverage-valuation types \(t\) identical copies, and draws \(tm\) items i.i.d. from the \(m\) item classes. Thus the hard instance already has \(N=tn\) advertisers, with \(t=\mathrm{poly}(m)\) copies per type.

That suggests a plausible regime: a large ad market with many advertisers but relatively few campaign templates. A type \(t\) is a complete coverage valuation: it specifies the universe \(U_t\) of outcomes the campaign values and, for each query or slot class \(j\), the subset \(A_{t,j}\subseteq U_t\) it covers. An advertiser receiving the integral bundle \(S\) gets \(w_t(S)=|\bigcup_{j\in S} A_{t,j}|\). The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over these types. This keeps the individual bundle discrete while representing the distribution of bundles within each type; it does not replace the population by fractional outcomes.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. Call it Fluid Online Coverage Welfare. An instance gives a finite set \(J\) of item classes, \(\tau\) coverage types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), explicit coverage descriptions \(A_{t,j}\), a known rational distribution \(p\) over \(J\), a horizon \(K\), and a rational packet mass \(\eta\). There are \(K\) sequential arrivals. Each is a packet of \(\eta\) units of identical slots, with class drawn independently from \(p\). The society state is \(x_{t,S}\), the mass of type-\(t\) advertisers holding the integral bundle \(S\subseteq J\). On seeing class \(j\), a causal policy assigns the packet’s slot mass among current advertiser states, moving mass from \(S\) to \(S\cup\{j\}\); each unit of advertiser mass receives whole slots. The policy maximizes expected terminal welfare \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}w_t(S)]\), against the expected offline optimum for the same arrivals.

The algorithmic question is whether, for each fixed \(\delta>0\), a policy polynomial in the encoded instance size, \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(K\) can guarantee a ratio of \(1-1/e+\delta\) on every such instance. This is the lead mirror. I expect the answer to be hardness transfers: the reduction’s difficult structure is in the coverage sets and item classes, not just in having many separately named advertisers. In its cloned instances, each original valuation type becomes mass, and the NO-case coverage bound is concave in the number of items received, which is promising for extending the upper bound to distributions of integral bundles. The YES-case’s covering allocation also has a direct mass analogue.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the stochastic coverage-welfare hardness of Theorem 4.4, not the paper’s adversarial-arrival result or its budget-additive LP bound. The advertisers are the continuous population; item supply scales with population to keep per-advertiser supply finite. Existing work on high-multiplicity elections is not a collision here: it supports the modeling premise that many agents can share a small number of complete types.

The weakest point is the fluid arrival model. In a strict limit of vanishing i.i.d. packets, empirical arrival frequencies become deterministic, and allowing packet mass to move across advertiser states may add flexibility absent when one indivisible item goes to one named advertiser. Theorem 4.4 therefore does not itself prove the proposed fluid lower bound. A prover would need a finite-to-fluid dictionary, showing that a better causal mass-flow policy still distinguishes the underlying Max \(k\)-cover cases. The case remains worth making because the anchor’s set-system hardness survives the cloning construction; the key question is whether the added mass flexibility erases that gap.

That leaves useful follow-ups: establish the finite-to-fluid approximation error, determine whether the \(1-1/e\) barrier survives in the fluid model, and identify how the answer depends on \(m\) and \(\tau\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 4.4 as a *fluid online* mirror is a scaling problem. In its hard instances, \(tn\) cloned advertisers face \(tm\) individual arrivals. Normalize the population to mass one, and each arrival has mass \(1/(tn)\). For fixed \(m\), the number of arrivals of each class, divided by \(t\), concentrates around its expected value. The arrival path therefore becomes deterministic in the fluid limit. Keeping single arrivals makes each one’s per-capita welfare effect vanish; keeping arrivals at macroscopic mass makes them bulk deliveries that the policy can split across advertiser states. The paper’s lower bound does not directly establish a barrier for that bulk model.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror. The reduction already has \(t\) copies of each of \(n\) complete coverage types, so it supplies unusually direct evidence for a high-multiplicity regime. A mirror can retain finite arrivals as mass quanta, or define bulk arrivals and ask for a fresh complexity result. In either version, the paper’s NO-case coverage bound is concave in the number of items received, while its YES-case allocation covers the universe. Splitting a type’s mass across integral bundles does not by itself erase that gap. There is no identity dependence to obstruct aggregation.

So the negative case can challenge the claim that Theorem 4.4’s *online stochastic* barrier survives a fluid limit. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. In the limit, the online uncertainty may disappear while the computational question becomes continuous welfare optimization with configuration choices; the Max \(k\)-cover hardness could still make that a meaningful Class B result. The proponent’s packet model needs a finite-to-fluid argument, but that gap is not a fundamental obstruction. I would not recommend rejecting this anchor.
