The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive idea is a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s Intersection rule, but this paper contains no named computational result to anchor that case. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor complexity direction to report.

The nearest named results are Theorem 2, proved here, and Theorem 3, also proved here. Theorem 2 says that the Intersection rule \(I(v,\Omega)=\operatorname{Sh}(v^\Omega)\) is the unique allocation rule satisfying efficiency, additivity, equal treatment of equal agents and the null-agent axiom on games unchanged by restriction, and equality of payoffs when every feasible coalition has worth zero. Theorem 3 says that, for an efficient and additive allocation rule \(f\) on ordinary TU-games, the unique extension satisfying efficiency, additivity, equality, \(f\)-consistency, and invariance under irrelevant changes to the intersection-closed system is \(\Phi_f(v,\Omega)=f(v^\Omega)\); Corollary 2 gives the Shapley-value case. These are axiomatic characterizations, not complexity or algorithm results. Propositions 1–3 and Theorem 1 give structural formulas, but do not analyze an algorithm or its complexity.

A plausible follow-on question would be to compute a continuum limit of the Intersection allocation. Consider a large cooperative organization with finitely many roles, such as workers with a small number of skills. A type records everything relevant to coalition worth and feasibility; agents of that type are interchangeable in every coalition. Let \(\mu_t\) be the population share of type \(t\), let \(x\) be a vector of coalition masses, and let \(W(x)=V(c_\Omega(x))\) be the worth after applying a closure operator for feasible coalitions. Under smoothness assumptions, the candidate output is each type’s per-unit allocation
\[ q_t=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial W}{\partial x_t}(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda, \]
so type \(t\) receives total mass allocation \(\mu_t q_t\). This is a concrete continuous analogue of computing the Shapley allocation of the restricted game, and it covers the Intersection rule rather than the paper’s broader family \(\Phi_f\).

The weak point is substantial: the paper allows arbitrary worth functions and intersection-closed systems over named agents. They need not be representable using type masses, and the derivative formula is an added continuum construction, not a result of this paper. The authors might recognize it as a limit question about their rule, but this manuscript supplies no computational anchor or basis for predicting whether that question is tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive gap: there is no qualifying computational result here to mirror. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 characterize allocation rules by axioms; Propositions 1–3 and Theorem 1 give structural identities. None states the complexity of a computational problem or specifies an algorithmic input model. So there are zero anchors, and neither theorem supports a continuous complexity claim.

The strongest rescue is the proposed high-multiplicity Shapley limit. With finitely many interchangeable types, an anonymous worth function \(W(x)\) over type masses, and suitable smoothness, the formula
\[ q_t=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial W}{\partial x_t}(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda \]
is a coherent continuous allocation. But it needs substantial structure absent from the paper. Its arbitrary coalition worths and intersection-closed systems need not be determined by type counts: pairwise synergies, permission links, or other relations can distinguish agents with the same individual attributes. Preserving those distinctions can make the number of types grow with the population; suppressing them changes the game. In a symmetric, succinctly represented model, the formula may define a worthwhile new computational question, but its complexity depends on that model’s representation and regularity assumptions, not on a result established here.

That rescue is plausible, so the negative case is not airtight as a claim about every possible follow-on project. It is strong as an assessment of this paper: its contribution is axiomatic, and the proposed continuum allocation would be a new model and research question rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result.
