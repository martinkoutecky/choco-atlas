The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) resources and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete workload and entitlement types, with per-capita demands \(a_{tj}\ge 0\), entitlement intensities \(w_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t=1\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{tj}\ge 1\) for every resource \(j\), find service rates \(x_t\in[0,1]\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t a_{tj}\le 1\) for every \(j\), and for each type \(t\), either \(x_t=1\) or there is a saturated resource \(j\) with \(\sum_s\mu_s x_s a_{sj}=1\) and \(x_t a_{tj}\ge w_t\).

The model it lives in

Types carry complete per-capita resource-demand vectors and entitlement intensities; the society is their rational mass distribution \(\mu\). Each type receives a common service rate \(x_t\), aggregate resource use is bounded by capacity, and every type either receives its full request or meets its entitlement on a bottleneck resource.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible population mirror, but it fails the required anchor test. The paper has no named theorem or lemma classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, hard, or parameterized. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: given entitlements \(e_i\ge 0\) summing to \(1\) and requests \(r_{ij}\in[0,1]\) whose total is at least \(1\) on each resource, there exists an allocation \(x_i\in[0,1]\) that respects every resource capacity and gives each user either their full request or at least their entitlement on some saturated resource. The ODE proof gives a numerical construction, but no running-time or exact algorithm guarantee; the paper leaves polynomial time in \(N\) and \(m\) open. So I would not call Theorem 1 an “in P” result.

The strongest candidate is Mass-NJC Allocation, a search problem mirroring only Theorem 1. Let each type \(t\) specify a complete per-capita resource request vector \(a_t\) and entitlement intensity \(w_t\). The instance gives \(m\) resources, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational \(a_{tj}\ge0\), and \(w_t\ge0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{tj}\ge1\) for each resource \(j\). Equivalently, in a society of \(n\) users, \(n\mu_t\) users have type \(t\), each requests fraction \(a_{tj}/n\) of resource \(j\), and has entitlement \(w_t/n\).

The task is to output rational type-level service rates \(x_t\in[0,1]\) such that
\[ \sum_t\mu_t x_t a_{tj}\le1\quad\text{for each }j, \]
and, for every type \(t\), either \(x_t=1\), or some saturated resource \(j\) satisfies
\[ \sum_s\mu_s x_s a_{sj}=1 \quad\text{and}\quad x_t a_{tj}\ge w_t. \]
A credible regime is a large cloud or compute cluster with many interchangeable tenants but relatively few distinct workload and service-agreement profiles, so \(n\) is large and \(\tau\ll n\). This changes the population representation; the paper’s already-divisible resources alone would not make it a population-continuous mirror. The ODE construction appears to support equal treatment of duplicate users, since its trajectory respects permutations of users with identical inputs; that is an inference from the proof, not a separate theorem.

For this newly posed search problem, guessing the saturated-resource set gives \(2^m\) rational LPs; guessing a witness resource or full-service status for each type gives \((m+1)^\tau\) LPs. Thus fixed \(m\) or fixed \(\tau\) has a direct tractability route. With both variable, these bounds do not establish polynomial time, and I would leave its trichotomy classification open rather than claim hardness transfer or continuum-specific hardness. This is a plausible problem for the authors: it asks how to find the fair allocation when bottlenecks are unknown, with computation measured in \(m,\tau\), and input bit length.

The case is limited to Theorem 1’s fair-allocation question; it does not mirror the paper’s comparisons with dominant resource fairness, its fairness properties, or its online scheduling discussion. Its weakest point is also decisive under your rules: the precise Mass-NJC question is a new high-multiplicity search formulation, while the paper supplies no eligible named complexity anchor. So the positive case is credible as a follow-on research direction, but not as a qualifying anchored mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result. Theorem 1 proves that a fair allocation exists. Its ODE construction gives a numerical method, but the paper gives no complexity bound for computing an allocation; it explicitly leaves polynomial time in \(N\) and \(m\) open. Under ChoCo’s stated test, that makes the theorem an ineligible anchor.

The best version of Mass-NJC is a sensible high-multiplicity model: many cloud tenants can share a workload profile and service agreement. But for rational masses its existence claim is just Theorem 1 in compressed notation. Choose a common denominator \(q\), expand each type into \(q\mu_t\) users, and give each user request \(a_{tj}/q\) and entitlement \(w_t/q\). The constraints and complaint condition become exactly those of the paper. The computation question—especially its dependence on support size \(\tau\) and input bit length—is still open, and may be worth pursuing. It is a follow-on to the paper’s open algorithmic question, not a mirror of a complexity result the paper established.

That distinction defeats the offered anchor under the required test, but it does not establish the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The paper’s open question and plausible repeated tenant profiles make that stronger claim hard to defend honestly. The negative case is therefore strongest as an objection to calling this an anchored mirror, not as evidence that the continuous-population problem lacks research value.
