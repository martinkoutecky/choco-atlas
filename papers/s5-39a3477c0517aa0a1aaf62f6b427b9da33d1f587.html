The bargaining set \(S\) is already continuous in outcome space; that is distinct from making the population continuous.

The proponent’s three large constituencies are the best apparent population story, but they expose the mismatch. If the three constituencies are the three bargaining agents, their masses do not create more bargaining agents or a new population parameter for Theorem 3. The theorem still concerns a bargaining problem in three utility coordinates. If instead each constituency contains members who bargain individually, the model needs a new feasible set, payoff semantics, and rule for aggregating their outcomes. That is a plausible separate research question, but it no longer mirrors a computational result in this paper. Extending the theorem to many agents and then studying a continuum limit would likewise add both a new theorem and a new model.

There is a further obstacle to that extension: ordinal preferences do not by themselves give a canonical way to aggregate members’ utility levels. A type-level payoff can be defined by imposing shared representations or equal treatment, but those are modeling choices that need justification. Even if they yield a sensible collective bargaining model, the paper’s uniqueness result does not tell us which computational question about that model to ask.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: its results are axiomatic, and its continuous mathematics concerns outcomes rather than population. The universal claim is less secure. Bargaining among large, internally homogeneous groups could motivate worthwhile work; the honest point is that it would be a new computational bargaining programme, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s results.
