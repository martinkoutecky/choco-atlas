The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchored positive case under your criteria: the paper contains no named computational result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified by complexity.

The closest candidate is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: for a static interaction graph, a first-order alliance, whether a pair or triplet, can never lose members. Corollary 3.1, also proved here, says the coalition size can only get larger. Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 establish limits on which hybrid-automaton states can be reached in one transition. These are results about the model’s dynamics, not the computational complexity of a problem.

A continuum model of those dynamics may be a worthwhile analytic extension, but it would not supply a valid anchor for the requested computational mirror. So there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover without inventing a complexity result the paper does not claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.1 proves that, on a static interaction graph, an alliance cannot lose members; Corollary 3.1 restates the consequence for coalition size. Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 rule out particular one-step transitions in the hybrid automaton. None defines a decision, search, or optimization problem with an input encoding and a complexity claim. A continuous version of those statements could be an analytic extension, but it would not mirror a computational result from this paper.

The best population-level scenario is a large robotics cohort with many agents sharing positions, initial conditions, and behavioral parameters. It is sensible to ask what fraction forms pairs or triplets. The obstacle is that the proved claims depend on who is connected to whom and on pair-specific familiarity and rejection values. A distribution over agent types alone does not tell us which typed pairs are within interaction range, who ranks highest in whose candidate set, or whether two agents reciprocate a request. Preserving that information calls for a joint spatial and dyadic state, such as a graphon-based hybrid system. That may be a worthwhile new model, but it is not a continuization of the paper’s theorem in the programme’s computational sense.

There is a further mismatch at the level of the result: each alliance has only two or three members, so an individual alliance has zero mass as the population grows. One could instead track the fraction of agents in alliances or the distribution of local alliance states. That is a reasonable reformulation, but it needs the same evolving relational information, and the paper provides no computational problem about such fractions to carry over. If the network is simplified into repeated finite templates, the aggregate is just a weighted collection of the original local automata; if the spatial and pairwise structure is retained, the proposed task becomes a new networked-dynamics problem.

A natural reachability question—whether a given initial network eventually forms a specified coalition—could be introduced, then given a continuous-population version. But that would be a new computational agenda, not one of the results raised by the proponent. It would need its own input model and a reason to treat a distribution over agent types as a faithful representation of the interaction network. On this paper, the case against an anchored ChoCo mirror is strong. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous computational project inspired by it; I can say that none of the cited results supplies one.
