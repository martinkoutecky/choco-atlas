The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no continuous mirror anchors; its central point is right. The paper’s numbered propositions concern existence and properties of allocation rules, or existence and nonexistence of stable partnership structures. None states an algorithmic result or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. There is no computational theorem here for ChoCo to continuize.

The strongest possible computational hook is Section 6: given a game and an allocation rule such as the Shapley value, find or test a stable partnership structure. But the paper allows an arbitrary characteristic function \(v\), with a worth for every coalition, and its stability definition tracks whether each named player benefits. Turning this into a population problem requires a new model: worths must be generated from type masses, allocations must be defined over those masses, and partnership deviations must retain a meaningful stability criterion. In an atomless population, an individual’s gain has zero mass; preserving the paper’s criterion therefore takes additional structure that the paper does not provide.

One could build a cohort model for a particular production or cost-sharing setting, as the paper’s examples suggest. That may be a worthwhile separate project, but its computational question and results would be new, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. So the strongest defensible negative verdict is that this paper offers no worthwhile computational mirror as written. I would not claim that no related continuous partnership model could ever be worthwhile; that universal claim is where the negative case is weakest.
