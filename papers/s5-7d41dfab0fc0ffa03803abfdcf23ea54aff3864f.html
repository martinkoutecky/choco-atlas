The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind required for a ChoCo anchor: it proves no complexity classification or algorithmic result such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or fixed-parameter tractability.

The nearest named result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that when \(\frac{379}{523}\alpha_2 < \alpha_1 < \alpha_2\), the two-market model has a unique core-stable configuration, up to firm relabeling: the same two firms form a cartel in both markets, while the third remains a singleton in both. This is an analytical stability result for a model with exactly three firms and two markets; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem.

A high-multiplicity extension with many otherwise identical firms is imaginable. But it would need to specify how demand and costs scale and how cartel deviations work for a continuum of firms. In the resulting atomless model, individual firms’ incentives also differ from those of the three strategic Cournot players in the paper. So I would not present that extension as a mirror anchored in Proposition 1. The positive case is therefore limited: the paper suggests a possible economic question about continuum-population cartel stability, but supplies no qualifying computational anchor for the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only plausible anchor, and it is not a computational result. It characterizes the unique core-stable configuration for exactly three firms and two markets when \(\frac{379}{523}\alpha_2<\alpha_1<\alpha_2\), using payoff comparisons across a fixed set of configurations. The paper gives no algorithmic or complexity claim. Asking an algorithm to check stability on this fixed-size model would add a computational wrapper to an analytical result, not continue its contribution. The paper’s quantities are continuous, but its population is not.

The strongest proposed repair is to replace the three firms with many otherwise identical producers, scale demand and costs with population, and ask which cartel membership patterns are stable as the number of firms grows. That could be an interesting economic project, but it changes the strategic object. In an atomless-firm limit, an individual firm has no effect on market price, so the singleton deviations central to the paper’s stability analysis lose their force. Allowing positive-mass coalitions restores market power, but then the deviators are measurable blocs rather than the paper’s coalitions of firms. Defining their incentives, cross-market membership, and cost aggregation would require a new game and a new stability question.

So the negative case is strong on whether this paper supplies a worthwhile ChoCo mirror: it does not supply a computational problem to mirror, and the most plausible population extension changes the stability model. It is weaker as a universal claim: a computational study of core stability in a deliberately generalized continuum oligopoly might be worthwhile in its own right. That would be a new research lead, not a continuous mirror of Proposition 1.
