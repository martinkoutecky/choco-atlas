The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based case for a continuous computational mirror: this paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar.

The numbered results are axiomatic characterizations. Theorem 1 characterizes geometric rules by scale invariance, upstream invariance, equal treatment of equal single polluters, top consistency, and budget additivity. Theorem 2 characterizes generalized geometric rules after dropping scale invariance; Theorem 3 characterizes the proportional rule; Theorem 4 characterizes the full-transfer rule. Lemma 1 extends agreement between budget-additive rules from redistribution problems to the full domain. None classifies a computational problem. The allocation formulas and case-study tables make particular allocations calculable, but do not establish a complexity result.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many permitted outfalls grouped into a small number of ordered river reaches, with each type sharing its reach and per-outfall claim. Type mass would be the fraction of outfalls in that cohort, and the decision would be its per-outfall pollution quota. But turning that into a computational question would be a new problem, not a mirror anchored in a result of this paper. A continuous axiomatic characterization would be closer to the paper’s contribution, but it would not meet the programme’s computational scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that this paper has no computational result to continue. Theorems 1–4 characterize allocation rules by axioms; Lemma 1 extends an identification result to a larger domain. None asks for, or proves the complexity of, a computational problem. A continuous version of those characterizations would remain axiomatic work, outside ChoCo’s scope.

I would grant that many permitted outfalls could plausibly be grouped into ordered river-reach types with shared per-outfall claims. That makes a high-multiplicity allocation model sensible; it does not provide a missing computational result. In that model, evaluating the geometric rule is a direct pass over the \(\tau\) reach types: the augmented claims satisfy \(a_i=c_i+(1-\gamma)a_{i-1}\), and the quotas follow from them. With rational inputs and fixed rational \(\gamma\), this is \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations. The generalized rule is similarly a pass over the types if \(\Gamma\) can be evaluated; without a finite representation for \(\Gamma\), its computational complexity is not even specified. There is no exponentially large search space or pricing problem for continuization to unlock.

A stronger proposal might optimize \(\gamma\), for example to enforce claims-boundedness or balance a fairness and environmental objective. But the paper supplies no objective for choosing \(\gamma\); it explicitly leaves that choice open. Adding one could produce a useful new problem, but it would not mirror a result in this paper.

So I would not reject the proposed setting on high-multiplicity grounds. Its weakness is more basic: a plausible continuum population does not turn an axiomatic characterization into a computational question. The material leaves room to invent an adjacent optimization problem, but gives little reason to treat that as a ChoCo mirror of this paper.
