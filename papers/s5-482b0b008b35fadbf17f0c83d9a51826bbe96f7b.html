The continuous mirror question

Given three non-atomic spatial measures \(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3\) for three preference cohorts along an ordered corridor, together with the paper’s online measure-access model and reveal-point bound, what is the minimum worst-case number of cuts a deterministic online allocator needs to assign each resulting interval wholly to panel \(A\) or \(B\), while ensuring \(\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}|\mu_i(U_A)-\mu_i(U_B)|\le\epsilon\)?

The model it lives in

A society is represented by three ordered, non-atomic spatial mass profiles, one per preference type. As each part of the corridor is revealed, the allocator irrevocably decides whether to cut and assigns each resulting interval wholly to one of two panels; the objective is to minimize the worst-case cut count subject to discrepancy at most \(\epsilon\) for every type.

The objection that survived

Location and order affect which assignments remain possible, so three preference labels alone are not complete types for this online problem; the proponent acknowledges this by retaining ordered type-mass profiles.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the online consensus-halving algorithm and cut lower bound, including Theorems 1.2 and 5.3. It leaves the offline algorithms, positive-share approximation, and necklace-splitting results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(\Theta(1/\epsilon^2)\) cut bound persist when the ordered cohort profiles are encoded as finite spatial cells with rational masses?
  • Which parts of the bounds remain if online arrival order or contiguous interval assignments are relaxed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is Online Cohort Consensus Halving. Take a large, geographically ordered electorate with three complete preference types, such as three ranking groups. Each type has a non-atomic distribution of residents along a corridor, and the electorate is streamed from one end to the other. Two representative panels must each receive a contiguous collection of intervals. As each interval arrives, the algorithm decides whether to cut and which panel receives it; those decisions are final. The goal is to make the two panels’ shares of every preference type differ by at most \(\epsilon\), using as few cuts as possible.

Formally, let \(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3\) be the normalized location distributions of the three types on \([0,1]\). An instance gives \(\epsilon\) and the online measure access used in the paper, including its bound on how much of any measure can lie between consecutive reveal points. A solution is a finite set of allowed cuts and an assignment of each resulting interval to panel \(A\) or \(B\), such that
\[ \max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}} |\mu_i(U_A)-\mu_i(U_B)|\le\epsilon. \]
The optimization question is to determine the smallest worst-case cut guarantee \(K_3(\epsilon)\) for a deterministic online algorithm. Each resident gets one whole panel assignment; only the type-level shares are fractional.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: \(N\) residents may fall into just three preference types, so \(N\gg\tau=3\). The one-dimensional order is part of the scenario—think of forming geographically contiguous panels from a streamed population—not an attempt to make outcomes divisible. The paper’s measure profiles become distributions of people by type, and its interval assignments become assignments of whole residents to panels.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper: for Online \(\epsilon\)-Consensus Halving with three measures, an adversary can force every deterministic algorithm to make \(\Omega(1/\epsilon^2)\) cuts. In the cohort mirror, this says no online panel-forming rule can guarantee the required type balance with asymptotically fewer cuts in the worst case.

The matching algorithmic anchor is Theorem 1.2, also proved here: for Online \(\epsilon\)-Consensus Halving with \(n\) measures, there is an efficient deterministic online algorithm that returns a proper solution with \(O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)\) cuts. For three types this is \(O(1/\epsilon^2)\). Together the results put this mirror in Class A in the paper’s oracle model, with optimal worst-case cut complexity \(\Theta(1/\epsilon^2)\) for three types. The lower bound is a cut-count bound, not a claim of PPAD- or NP-hardness; the algorithm remains polynomial in the paper’s parameters.

The authors should recognize the underlying question: it uses the same ordered interval, cuts, two recipients, and per-measure discrepancy condition as their consensus-halving problem. The social interpretation changes what the measures represent, while preserving the computation being studied. This mirror covers those online consensus-halving results, not the paper’s offline algorithms, its positive-share approximation, or its full necklace-splitting results.

The weakest point is that the paper’s original measures are measures on a resource interval, while this interpretation treats them as spatial distributions of people. Also, the online order carries information beyond the aggregate three-type proportions in ChoCo’s basic type-mass model. I think the scenario remains credible because contiguity and arrival order are explicit constraints of the online problem; the population is still the continuous object being divided, and the panels receive indivisible assignments at the resident level. But a formal ChoCo version would need to retain those ordered type-mass profiles, rather than encode the society only by three scalar masses. Further questions include whether the cut bounds survive a binary-rational step-density input, what changes when the number of types grows, and how much of the lower bound remains without online or contiguity constraints.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is not that the anchors are noncomputational: Theorem 1.2 gives an online algorithm, and Theorem 5.3 proves an online cut lower bound. Nor is it that their answers would be uninteresting. The question is whether they yield a new continuous-population problem.

In the proposed corridor electorate, a resident’s location matters: it determines when they arrive, which cuts are available, and which assignment decisions are already irreversible. So “three preference types” is not a complete type description for this problem. Keep only rankings and the ordered spatial profile disappears; without that profile, the online problem changes. Keep location as part of the type, and the model has a continuum of location-specific types rather than a population over three types. The lower bound in Theorem 5.3 relies on the evolving profile along the interval, not just on the total mass of each ranking.

The obvious repair—represent each preference cohort by its spatial measure—does produce a sensible continuous population model. But its input, permitted cuts, discrepancy objective, and online protocol are precisely the paper’s consensus-halving model under a new interpretation. Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 5.3 already answer the corresponding cut-count questions. That makes this a strong application of existing results, but a weak candidate for a new continuous mirror: the paper already studies the relevant non-atomic measure problem.

Discretizing the corridor into ordered cells could give a finite-support, high-multiplicity version. Yet then the cell profile and its resolution become essential parameters; the three-type summary alone cannot preserve the online problem. This is the best route to a distinct ChoCo formulation, but it would need a precise dictionary showing which discretized profiles retain the paper’s bounds. The case against is therefore limited: the stated mirror either omits information the anchors need, or reproduces a problem the paper already solves continuously.

I cannot honestly make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large, ordered electorate with repeated preference-and-location cohorts is plausible, and the paper’s results may transfer to it. My negative case is strongest as a novelty objection to this particular anchor, not as a rejection of every population-continuous scenario.
