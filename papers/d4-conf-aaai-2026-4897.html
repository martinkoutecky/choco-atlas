Turning voters into masses would not, by itself, make any of those findings a ChoCo problem.

The best proposed mirror would give each geographic unit a rational mass of voters, retain location and demographic or partisan attributes in the type, and study the choose-and-freeze process over those masses. That is a plausible model, and the location information must remain in the type because it determines which districts are connected. But the paper’s outcomes are driven by the finite map space and the agents’ choices within it. Keeping that space fixed makes the population change largely an input representation; allowing arbitrary continuous regions changes the map space, legal constraints, and candidate-generation problem. Either way, there is no named computational result here whose continuous counterpart the paper supplies.

The fairness guarantee is attributed to earlier work, and the paper’s own contribution is an empirical implementation of that protocol. A continuous fair-partition or redistricting-complexity project might be worthwhile on its own, but it would need a new formal problem and new results. The case against a mirror is therefore strong under ChoCo’s result-anchored standard, though it cannot establish the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous redistricting problem could ever be formulated.
