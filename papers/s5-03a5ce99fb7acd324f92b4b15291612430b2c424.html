Why no mirror

Theorem 13 and Theorem 15 are semantic equivalences, not named algorithms, complexity bounds, hardness results, or computational formulations with guarantees. The proposed high-multiplicity stability evaluation is a sensible extension and likely a Class A finite calculation, but it is newly posed rather than a computational result of this paper. Therefore bit (a) fails and the mirror is red.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the zero-loss interpretations of Theorems 13 and 15, but leaves the neural-network training method, empirical frontier, welfare, entropy, similarity, and the absence of formal complexity guarantees untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can optimization over an anonymous type-level mechanism kernel \(K\) yield a nontrivial exact, approximation, or parameterized complexity problem for \(\lambda S_\infty(K)+(1-\lambda)R_\infty(K)\)?
  • Can the strict named-agent preference and reporting semantics be preserved under a formal high-multiplicity limit without introducing a new solution concept?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity matching market with many workers and positions but relatively few complete preference types. The paper’s \(4\times 4\) experiments are not themselves a continuum regime, but the underlying mechanism-design problem has a natural one.

Let \(\mathcal A\) be a finite set of worker types and \(\mathcal B\) a finite set of firm or position types. A worker type \(a\) specifies a complete ranking \(P_a\) over \(\mathcal B\cup\{\bot\}\); a firm type \(b\) specifies a complete ranking \(Q_b\) over \(\mathcal A\cup\{\bot\}\). The type also includes every other feature relevant to the mechanism. Let \(\rho_a\) and \(\sigma_b\) be the masses of the two sides. A matching is a mass coupling \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le \rho_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le \sigma_b\). The residual mass is unmatched.

This is genuinely a population continuization: \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of agents matched across two types, not a fractional assignment of an individual. A nonatomic matching can implement such a coupling with every individual receiving one partner or remaining unmatched.

A plausible regime is a large residency, school-choice, or hiring market with hundreds of thousands of applicants and positions, grouped by a modest number of specialty, geography, qualification, and preference types. Each type may contain \(\Theta(N)\) agents, while \(|\mathcal A|\) and \(|\mathcal B|\) remain fixed or grow much more slowly than \(N\). This is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. The paper already motivates matching through large markets and correlated preferences, so the authors should recognize the setting, although one must replace rankings of named agents by rankings of anonymous counterpart types or impose a common within-type tie-break.

The lead anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper. It states that a randomized matching mechanism \(g^\theta\) is ex ante stable, up to zero-measure events, if and only if its expected stability violation \(\mathit{STV}(g^\theta)\) is zero. Here \(\mathit{STV}\) is the expectation of the paper’s \(\mathit{stv}\), whose term for a worker-firm pair is the product of the firm-side probability-weighted preference advantage and the worker-side probability-weighted preference advantage. Thus the theorem says that the paper’s numerical stability loss is exactly zero precisely when there is no ex ante justified envy, assuming the mechanism is individually rational as in the paper’s network construction.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuum Ex-Ante Stability Evaluation. Given finite type sets \(\mathcal A,\mathcal B\), rational masses \(\rho,\sigma\), type rankings \(P,Q\), and a rational feasible coupling \(x\), compute the continuum stability violation and decide whether the coupling is ex ante stable.

For \(\rho_a,\sigma_b>0\), define the worker and firm lotteries by \(r^W_{ab}=x_{ab}/\rho_a\) and \(r^F_{ab}=x_{ab}/\sigma_b\), with analogous probabilities for being unmatched. For a pair \((a,b)\), define \(\phi_{ab}(x)\) as the product of the two sums \( \sum_{a'}r^F_{a'b}\max\{Q_b(a)-Q_b(a'),0\}\) and \( \sum_{b'}r^W_{ab'}\max\{P_a(b)-P_a(b'),0\}\), including \(\bot\) as a possible partner. The objective is the natural mass-weighted sum \(S_\infty(x)=\sum_{a,b}\rho_a\sigma_b\phi_{ab}(x)\).

A solution is the exact value of \(S_\infty(x)\) and either a YES answer when it is zero or a certificate \((a,b)\) with \(\phi_{ab}(x)>0\). The expected complexity is Class A in an explicit type representation: this is a finite calculation over the type table. A natural design version chooses the type-level coupling or mechanism kernel \(x\) to minimize \(S_\infty\), possibly subject to a strategy-proofness or welfare constraint.

This is not merely making the outcome fractional. The population is the continuous object; the coupling records how mass is matched. It is also not a novelty collision: the paper studies finite named-agent profiles and learns a mechanism over them. The type-level continuum question is a new high-multiplicity formulation of the same ex ante-stability problem.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 15, also proved here. It states that a randomized mechanism is ordinal strategy-proof, up to zero-measure events, if and only if its expected regret \(\mathit{RGT}(g^\theta)\) is zero. The regret for each agent maximizes, over all misreports and all preference cutoffs, the increase in probability of obtaining an option above that cutoff; the overall quantity averages this regret over workers and firms and then takes the expectation over preference profiles.

To retain a nonvacuous incentive question in a continuum, the mechanism must be represented as a direct type-level kernel. For a worker, let \(K_W(\hat a,\cdot;\rho,\sigma)\) be the lottery assigned to a tagged worker who reports \(\hat a\), while the population masses remain \((\rho,\sigma)\). Define \(K_F\) analogously. Under truthful reports these kernels must induce the coupling \(x\).

The corresponding problem is:

Continuum Ordinal-SP Evaluation. Given the type masses, type rankings, and a feasible direct mechanism kernel \(K\), compute

\(R_\infty(K)=\frac12(R_W(K)+R_F(K))\),

where \(R_W\) is the \(\rho\)-weighted maximum, over worker types \(a\), reports \(\hat a\), and acceptable cutoffs \(b^\star\), of \( \sum_{b\succ_a b^\star}(K_W(\hat a,b)-K_W(a,b))\), with the analogous expression defining \(R_F\). Decide whether \(R_\infty(K)=0\), and if not, return a positive-mass type, a misreport, and a cutoff witnessing the violation.

For explicit type and report tables this is again Class A: enumerate the finite reports and rank cutoffs. The full continuous analogue of the paper’s learning objective is then to choose \(K\) to minimize \( \lambda S_\infty(K)+(1-\lambda)R_\infty(K)\). The paper’s experiments are empirical searches for this tradeoff; the continuum version asks for exact or approximate optimization over type-level mechanisms.

Theorem 5, cited from Erdil (2014), supplies the conceptual bridge: ordinal strategy-proofness is equivalent to FOSD. The continuum regret above is therefore not an arbitrary surrogate; it is the mass-weighted FOSD violation for type-level lotteries.

The weakest point is the strategy-proofness anchor. If one naively lets a continuum mechanism depend only on aggregate masses, an individual deviation has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate outcome, making individual strategy-proofness vacuous. The tagged-agent kernel avoids that collapse, but it is a price-taking, type-level formulation rather than a literal limit of the paper’s named-agent profile mechanism. For that reason, Theorem 13 is my strongest anchor: ex ante stability survives the continuization directly and nontrivially. Theorem 15 is a credible secondary mirror, but only with the direct-kernel interpretation made explicit.

Finally, this paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar complexity theorem. I would not pretend otherwise. The positive case is that its two central computational quantities—stability violation and FOSD regret—have precise, natural high-multiplicity versions, with explicit-type evaluation in Class A and a meaningful mechanism-design frontier left for further complexity analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case begins with a basic mismatch: this paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 13 and Theorem 15 are semantic characterizations of two losses, and Theorem 5 is the standard equivalence between ordinal strategy-proofness and FOSD. None gives a complexity bound, an exact algorithm, a hardness result, or even a formally stated optimization problem over mechanisms. The substantive contribution is empirical SGD on \(4\times4\) markets. Thus there is no computational theorem here whose high-multiplicity analogue could chart the programme’s intended complexity landscape.

The proposed stability mirror is mathematically definable, but it does not repair that gap. Given explicit type tables and a coupling \(x\), computing the proposed \(S_\infty(x)\) is just evaluating finitely many sums and maxima, in time polynomial in \(|\mathcal A|\) and \(|\mathcal B|\). Theorem 13 itself contributes no algorithmic content: it says that a nonnegative expected loss is zero exactly when the underlying violation is zero almost everywhere. The same observation remains true for a mass coupling. A certificate consisting of a violating type pair is useful bookkeeping, but not a computational result of the kind this programme seeks.

More importantly, \(x\) is an allocation, not a mechanism. The paper’s \(g\) maps every reported preference profile to a randomized matching. The proponent’s question fixes one type distribution and one coupling, thereby discarding the paper’s central object: the rule that responds to reports. Optimizing over \(x\) would be a new anonymous capacitated matching problem; optimizing over a family \(G(\rho,\sigma)\) would be a new mechanism-design paper. Either could be worthwhile, but neither is a mirror of Theorem 13’s computational content, because Theorem 13 has no such content.

There is also a modelling cost hidden by the phrase “preference types.” In the paper, preferences are strict rankings of named counterparties. Repeating agents while retaining that formalism does not produce a small finite type space: rankings over an expanding set of identities carry the identities with them. Replacing those rankings by preferences over anonymous counterpart types is plausible, but it changes the market into an anonymous capacitated or many-to-many model, with different tie-breaking, blocking, and mechanism semantics. That is an admissible new model, not evidence that this particular paper has a natural continuous mirror.

The strategy-proofness anchors are weaker still. In a genuinely aggregate continuum mechanism, one agent’s report has zero mass and therefore cannot change the aggregate outcome. If the mechanism is \(G(\rho,\sigma)\), every unilateral report produces the same \(G\), so the paper’s regret is identically zero. This is not an interesting tractability gain; it is the disappearance of the strategic interaction being measured.

The proposed tagged-agent kernel \(K_W(\hat a,\cdot;\rho,\sigma)\) avoids that collapse only by adding a new primitive. The aggregate mechanism \(G\) does not determine the tagged agent’s response: many different kernels can induce the same mass coupling and have different regrets. If \(K\) is supplied explicitly, evaluating its regret is again a finite enumeration of reports and rank cutoffs. If one optimizes over \(K\), one has formulated a new direct-mechanism design problem for anonymous markets, not continuized the paper’s learned mechanism. If instead one permits deviations by positive mass, the deviation is no longer ordinal strategy-proofness of an individual; it is a coalition or population-manipulation concept.

Theorem 5 does not provide an independent escape route. FOSD is simply another representation of the same individual-lottery incentive condition, so it inherits the same dichotomy: with only aggregate outcomes it is vacuous, and with tagged-agent kernels it is a finite type-level mechanism problem introduced from outside the paper.

The negative case is therefore strongest as a scope and contribution objection: the paper studies empirical neural search over finite named-agent mechanisms, while the proposed mirrors either reduce to trivial finite evaluation or require constructing an entirely new anonymous mechanism model. I would not claim the universal impossibility literally holds. A carefully developed type-level kernel and an exact Pareto-frontier problem could become a worthwhile new matching paper. But that would be an extension inspired by the paper, not a continuous computational mirror of any result the paper actually proves.
