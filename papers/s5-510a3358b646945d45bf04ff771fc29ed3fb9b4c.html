The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large DAO referendum or online poll where many participants share a small set of complete types: for example, stance, voting cost, and arrival propensity. Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population fraction. At each turn, informed agents of a type choose whether to vote, wait, or abstain based on the normalized public tally; arriving swing mass votes for the current leader, splitting at a tie. A continuous timing-equilibrium problem would take rational type masses, costs, and arrival fractions as input and ask for a type-symmetric WPBE: strategies for every type and public-tally history, plus beliefs consistent with the hidden origins of past votes. Its solution is an equilibrium profile, not an optimized objective.

That is a recognizable population-level extension of the paper’s setting. The paper itself discusses large sequential elections, including Mamageishvili and Tejada’s work, and its DAO motivation supplies a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But this proposed problem is not anchored by a qualifying result in the paper. Theorem 4.10, proved here, is the closest match: the paper presents it as showing that when \(q_1>0\), equilibria can have informed voters mix between early and late voting. Its printed statement says \(q_1\in(0,1)\), while the proof and surrounding text concern the early-voting probability \(p_1\). Either way, it is an equilibrium-existence result, not a result that classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The other numbered results likewise characterize equilibrium behavior; none gives the required complexity anchor.

The main obstacle is strategic. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot changes neither the tally nor the later swing voters’ behavior. With positive voting costs, the individual incentive to vote that drives Theorem 4.10 disappears. The straightforward continuum game may therefore have a simple, degenerate equilibrium rather than the paper’s timing trade-off. Keeping that trade-off might require finite atomic blocs, coordinated type-level decisions, or a noise model that preserves pivotality. Each is a substantial modeling change, and the authors might reasonably reject it as a mirror of their individual-voter game.

So there is a plausible continuous-population research question here, but not a sound anchor-based case that this paper already contains a continuizable computational result. I would present the equilibrium-computation problem only as an unanchored follow-on, and name the loss of pivotality as the case’s weakest point.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.10 proves existence of a mixed weak perfect Bayesian equilibrium in a two-period game with three voters. Theorem 4.6 and Theorem 4.7 characterize other equilibrium regimes; the propositions derive best responses and payoff thresholds. None asks for, or establishes the complexity of, an algorithmic problem. A question about computing equilibria in a continuum game could be worthwhile, but it would be a new equilibrium-computation project, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed DAO setting is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime: stance, voting cost, and other problem-relevant attributes could define types. The difficulty is more fundamental than choosing those types. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot has zero mass, so it cannot move the public tally or sway later voters. That removes the strategic influence behind the paper’s timing trade-off. Treating an entire type’s mass as able to deviate would restore influence by making the type a coordinated bloc; adding common shocks to preserve pivotality would introduce a different source of uncertainty. Either could motivate a new model, but neither follows by simply continuizing this game.

The paper’s WPBE analysis also relies on uncertainty about who cast a small number of observed votes. With known type masses and symmetric mixed strategies, aggregate actions in an atomless population are deterministic, so that hidden-origin uncertainty generally disappears too. These are losses in the strategic structure the theorem studies, not objections based on whether the resulting equilibrium would be interesting.

I would vote against a ChoCo mirror on the stated anchor requirement. The negative case is weaker as a universal claim: a continuum equilibrium-computation study, or a separate control problem built around turnout, might be worthwhile. But those would be adjacent research questions, not continuous versions of a named computational result in this paper.
