What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers Proposition 5's PR and random-recognition \(MR\)-viability condition; it leaves the other bargaining results, ASB cases, plurality voting, and strategic voting aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: it characterizes bargaining equilibria and conditions for viable coalitions, but does not classify a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. The closest useful anchor is Proposition 5, proved here. It says that under proportional representation and random recognition, an ex ante \(MR\) coalition is viable exactly when
\[ x_R(LM)-x_R(M)>\alpha\left(\frac12-v(M)\right). \]
If it forms, \(M\) gets a majority government; no other ex ante coalition is viable. This is a precise equilibrium result, but it is not itself a computational-complexity result.

A narrow continuous problem suggested by Proposition 5 is PR Random-Recognition Coalition Viability. Consider a large electorate whose voters have the paper’s three complete ranking types, \(L\succ M\succ R\), \(M\succ L\succ R\), and \(R\succ M\succ L\). Let \(\mu_P\) be the fraction of voters of type \(P\), with \(\mu_P=v(P)\). The input also gives the paper’s rational payoff parameters, including the seat value \(\alpha\) and \(R\)’s externalities. The regime is plausible: \(N\) voters can be large while the support size stays at \(\tau=3\).

An \(MR\) joint list chooses a seat transfer \(q\) from \(R\) to \(M\), giving them seat shares \(\mu_M+q\) and \(\mu_R-q\). The question is whether some transfer makes both parties strictly better off than with no agreement; a solution is such a \(q\). The decision objective is joint profitability. To give \(M\) a majority, the transfer must exceed \(1/2-\mu_M\). \(R\)’s gain is \(x_R(LM)-x_R(M)-\alpha q\), so a jointly profitable transfer exists exactly under Proposition 5’s inequality. Under binary rational inputs, that gives a direct polynomial-time decision procedure; the expected direction is tractable.

The mirror would be recognizable to the authors because it is their own PR coalition-viability question expressed as a mass-transfer problem. Its scope is just this \(MR\)-viability result; it does not claim to mirror all the paper’s bargaining protocols or plurality results.

That recognition is also the case’s weakest point. The paper already represents voters and seats by fractions and studies their redistribution. This proposal therefore looks like an algorithmic restatement of an already mass-based model, rather than a new continuous mirror of a discrete problem. And Proposition 5 supplies no complexity anchor of the kind the programme prioritizes. The natural follow-up would be to formalize the finite-\(N\) counterpart and determine exactly how seat granularity and boundary cases affect the viability threshold.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 5 is already on the continuous-population side of the bridge. Its electorate is summarized by fractions \(v(L),v(M),v(R)\), with the three voter rankings as types; seat shares are fractions too. The proposed \(MR\)-viability question uses the same distribution and the same continuous seat transfer. The paper already gives its answer: viability is equivalent to
\[ x_R(LM)-x_R(M)>\alpha\left(\frac12-v(M)\right). \]
That is a collision with the paper’s own model and result, not just a similar result found elsewhere.

There is also no computational-complexity result here to mirror. Proposition 5 characterizes equilibrium outcomes; it does not analyze an algorithmic problem. Turning its condition into a decision procedure amounts to comparing rational inputs, while finding a profitable transfer amounts to checking an interval. Those computations are immediate from the proposition. This is not an argument that a tractable answer would be uninteresting; the more basic problem is that the proposed continuous question restates an already continuous result.

The best rescue is to start with a finite electorate and ask how seat granularity changes viability. With three voter types, counts map directly to the fractions in the paper; if seats must also be indivisible, the transfer threshold just gets rounded. That could make a useful dictionary between finite and fractional variants, but it does not supply a new continuous mirror of Proposition 5. Broader questions—say, coalition formation with many parties or heterogeneous districts—could have real computational substance. The paper’s own discussion points toward heterogeneous districts. But those extensions change the bargaining model and need their own anchor; they do not rescue this one. So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchor, though the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror could arise from any extension of the paper is harder to defend.
