The continuous mirror question

Given the fixed signal model, a focal voter’s signal \(w\), an effective peer scale \(M\), and rational masses \((\mu_0,\mu_1)\) over peers’ first-round votes with \(\mu_0+\mu_1=1\), compute the second-round posterior log-odds \(Y=x(w)+M\sum_{h\in\{0,1\}}\mu_h\log\!\left(p_1(h)/p_0(h)\right)\) and return vote \(1\) if \(Y>0\), and \(0\) otherwise. Here \(p_s(h)\) is the probability of a peer’s first-round vote \(h\) given state \(s\); integer counts \(M\mu_h\) recover the finite-panel likelihood, while rational masses define its mass-weighted extension.

The model it lives in

Represent peers by their observed first-round vote types \(h\in\{0,1\}\), with mass \(\mu_h\) and effective scale \(M\). The focal voter’s signal contributes \(x(w)\); each peer type contributes its log-likelihood ratio, weighted by \(M\mu_h\). The task is to compute the resulting posterior odds and vote.

The objection that survived

For non-integer \(M\mu_h\), the mass-weighted likelihood is not the ordinary likelihood of independent finite observations; an exact continuum of independent signals can instead reveal the state.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the second-round specialization of Theorem 1.7. It leaves the all-round vote calculation and Theorems 1.4–1.6 on unanimity, monotonicity, and asymptotic learning untreated.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s vote calculation, not its convergence claims. The lead anchor is Theorem 1.7, proved here; §2.1 restates it as Theorem 2.7 and proves it. Under the paper’s signal model, fix \(\mu_0,\mu_1\) and \(n\). If the log-likelihood ratio \(X\) and the cumulative distribution functions of its conditional laws \(\nu_0,\nu_1\) can be evaluated in constant time, then an \(O(nt)\)-time algorithm takes agent \(i\)’s private signal \(W_i\) and all votes through round \(t-1\), and calculates \(V_i(t)\). That is a named tractability result for the underlying problem of calculating an agent’s Bayesian vote.

A plausible regime is a large jury or hiring panel using a small, standardized set of evidence categories. Let a peer’s type at round \(t\) be their publicly observed vote history \(h\in\{0,1\}^{t-1}\): for this calculation, peers with the same history make the same likelihood contribution. Write \(p_s(h)\) for the probability that one peer has history \(h\) conditional on state \(s\). Give the focal agent signal \(w\), with log-likelihood ratio \(x(w)\), and let \(\mu_h\) be the fraction of peers with history \(h\), where \(\sum_h\mu_h=1\). Let \(M\) be the effective number of peers represented by this society. The continuous vote-evaluation problem is: given the signal model, round, \(w\), \(M\), and the rational masses \((\mu_h)\), compute the posterior log-odds
\[ Y(w,\mu)=x(w)+M\sum_h\mu_h\log\frac{p_1(h)}{p_0(h)} \]
and return vote \(1\) if \(Y(w,\mu)>0\), otherwise vote \(0\), using the paper’s tie-breaking convention. A solution is that vote and its posterior log-odds. When \(M\mu_h\) are integer counts, this is the finite-population likelihood calculation: conditional independence makes the likelihood contributions add, and the multinomial factor cancels between the two states. Allowing rational masses extends those counts to high-multiplicity proportions.

This is a meaningful regime when many panel members share a small number of evidence or voting-history types, so the number of people is much larger than the number of distinct types. Everyone still observes peers’ votes and makes the paper’s Bayesian, myopic decision; aggregation just replaces repeated identical likelihood contributions by their mass. In the finite-type version, the per-peer history probabilities can be computed from the signal model as in the paper. I expect this mirror to be Class A: its central calculation is a mass-weighted sum, with the paper’s likelihood and distribution computations supplying the per-type terms. The corresponding algorithm should depend on the number of represented types, the horizon, and the input bit lengths, rather than on the number of people. This extends the paper’s stated \(O(nt)\) result; it is not a theorem the paper itself proves for rational masses.

The scope is only Theorem 1.7’s Bayesian vote calculation. I would not use Theorems 1.4–1.6 as additional anchors: they assert unanimity, monotonicity, and exponential-probability asymptotic learning, rather than a computational complexity classification. Useful follow-up questions are whether the weighted update admits exact polynomial bit complexity for rational masses, how errors in estimated masses affect the vote, and how the calculation changes under a sparse observation network.

The weak point is real: the paper assumes a finite group and, for its main model, a non-atomic likelihood-ratio distribution. A panel with a finite standardized signal alphabet changes that assumption and can create ties, so the mirror needs the paper’s deterministic tie rule and a finite-type extension. Also, a literal infinite population of independent signals can reveal the state through exact aggregate frequencies, making later voting trivial. The proposed problem instead continuizes the finite model’s extensive likelihood evidence: \(M\) records population scale, while \(\mu_h\) records type proportions. That is a defensible high-multiplicity analogue of the calculation the authors study, though it is not a claim that their finite theorem already covers a literal continuum of independent agents.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only real computational anchor is Theorem 1.7. It is a genuine algorithmic result, so I would not argue that the paper has nothing to mirror. The stronger case against is that its Bayesian calculation does not have a nondegenerate continuous-population version that remains the same problem.

The agents’ complete types in this model include their private signals. Because the log-likelihood ratio is non-atomic, almost surely every agent has a different signal. A distribution over those types therefore has support size \(n\), so it gives no high-multiplicity compression. Worse, if the population’s exact signal distribution is made known in a literal continuum, it identifies the state: \(\mu_0\ne\mu_1\). Even the first-round vote fraction distinguishes the states, since the paper establishes that \(P[X>0\mid S=0]\ne P[X>0\mid S=1]\). The uncertainty that makes the agents’ calculations interesting disappears.

The proponent’s history-mass formula does not fix this for all rounds. It works at the second vote, when peers’ histories are just their first-round votes and are conditionally independent given the state. From the third vote onward, those histories depend on one another through the shared voting dynamics. Their likelihood is not, in general, the product of the marginal probabilities \(p_s(h)\).

There is a better repair. Given an observed transcript, the paper’s proof turns each peer’s history into an interval constraint on that peer’s private likelihood ratio. The posterior then sums the corresponding interval log-likelihoods. Agents whose histories induce the same interval can be grouped, and their contributions multiplied by a count. This could compress the finite calculation. But it makes the “types” classes of an already observed transcript, not stable types of the society. With integer counts it is exactly a finite-panel calculation in histogram form. With fractional counts, the usual Bayesian model of independent signals supplies no fractional observations; weighting likelihood terms by fractional counts defines a generalized likelihood model instead.

That distinction is decisive for the proposed mirror. To retain genuine Bayesian uncertainty, the masses must encode a finite sample, with integer multiplicities. To take an exact continuum of independent signals, the aggregate reveals the state. Intermediate fractional masses require a new evidence model. The valid histogram version may be a useful finite-data compression, but it does not establish a continuous society whose members retain the paper’s information structure. And its computation is a recurrence of likelihood sums and thresholds, with no evident role for the continuous-optimization machinery that motivates ChoCo.

The negative case is not airtight. A reader could reasonably treat transcript-history classes as types and study a carefully defined mass-weighted update. That is the strongest surviving version, and the paper’s complete-network symmetry makes it plausible. My objection is that this version either reproduces an integer finite transcript or changes the Bayesian model; the proponent’s formula alone does neither correctly.
