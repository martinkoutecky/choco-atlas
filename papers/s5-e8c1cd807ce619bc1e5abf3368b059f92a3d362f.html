The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a mirror of the paper’s project winner computation. There is a natural continuous population version of the participatory budgeting problem: the electorate becomes a distribution over cumulative ballots, while the projects remain indivisible.

For a fixed slate \(P\) of \(m\) projects, let a type be a complete ballot \(v\in\{0,\ldots,10\}^m\) that assigns 10 points across at least three projects. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_v\) the fraction of residents using ballot \(v\). Given project costs scaled per resident, a per-resident budget, and fixed tie-breaking, ask for the exact set \(W\subseteq P\) selected by the Method of Equal Shares with the paper’s Add1U completion. The solution is \(W\) and a certificate of the rule’s funding steps. Ballot mass is continuous; projects stay indivisible.

A plausible regime is recurring, city-wide participatory budgeting with a fixed ballot format and many residents relative to the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots actually submitted. Exact ballot vectors make legitimate types because voters with the same vector are indistinguishable to this computation. The paper’s own Aarau setting—1,703 voters and 33 projects—illustrates the kind of decision involved, though it does not report \(\tau\), so it does not establish that the observed instance has high multiplicity. In a larger electorate with repeated ballot patterns, the continuous input is a sensible object and preserves the paper’s core task: selecting funded projects from citizen votes under a fixed budget.

My expected direction is Class A, pending formal analysis: the rule may be executable directly on the \(\tau\) weighted types, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. A key question is whether the weighted execution exactly matches the finite election formed by replicating each type according to rational masses, including the Add1U completion and ties. Further questions concern the precise bit complexity, how winner sets change under small shifts in \(\mu\), and whether the computation remains efficient as the project slate grows. This mirror covers winner determination only; it does not model the paper’s survey findings about legitimacy, causal explanations, project ideation, or implementation.

There is a crucial limit to the case: the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. It has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting membership in P, hardness, FPT, or another complexity classification. Its numbered key findings are empirical claims, including that equal shares selected 17 projects in Aarau versus 7 under the standard method. The reported 85.92% classifier accuracy is also an empirical model-performance result, not a complexity result. Those findings can motivate the winner-determination mirror, but none qualifies as an anchor under the brief. The candidate problem above is therefore unanchored; presenting it as a mirror of a theorem would invent evidence the paper does not provide.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that none of the paper’s three stated results is a computational complexity result. They are empirical findings about project selection and representation, voters’ legitimacy judgments, and survey traits associated with shifts in preference. The paper reports applying existing voting methods and software; it does not claim a new algorithm or classify a computational problem. The proposed winner-determination question therefore has no qualifying result to mirror.

The strongest version of that question is coherent: represent residents by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete cumulative ballots, scale the budget per resident, keep projects indivisible, and ask which projects Equal Shares with Add1U selects. Repeated ballots form legitimate types, and a large-city participatory budget is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. I cannot defeat that model by arguing that individual identity is essential or that the continuum degenerates.

But this formulation changes the paper’s contribution. It turns an empirical study of what happened under a voting rule into a worst-case analysis of how to execute that rule on weighted inputs. The paper’s result is about comparative outcomes and voter responses, not the computational difficulty of finding winners. The method itself already has a formal algorithmic literature and public implementations, including variants with Add1U completion; that is not a continuous treatment, and I do not treat it as a novelty collision. It does show that the proposed task would be a new computational project prompted by the paper’s application, rather than a mirror of a computational result the paper establishes. [Peters and Skowron’s study of Equal Shares](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/69f8ea31de0c00502b2ae571fbab1f95-Paper.pdf), [the Equal Shares computation tool](https://equalshares.net/tools/compute/).

One could make the follow-on project more ambitious—for example, ask how much ballot mass must move between types to change the winner set. That is a plausible continuous robustness question, but it is further from the paper’s reported findings. Computing representation statistics from \(\mu\) would track its first empirical result more closely, yet would still introduce a new general-input question rather than mirror a complexity claim in the paper.

So the against case succeeds on the paper-anchoring requirement: the proponent’s sole candidate does not survive as a mirror of a named computational result. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise from this paper is much harder to defend. The weighted Equal Shares question is sensible and may be worth pursuing independently; the honest negative verdict is that this paper does not supply the required computational anchor.
