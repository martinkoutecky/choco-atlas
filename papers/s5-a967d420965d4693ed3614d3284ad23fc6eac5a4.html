The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of asylum-seeker types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type records a complete ranking over \(M\times W\), a priority class, and common burden-size \(1\), and given member-state quotas and wait-time capacities scaled per capita, can a type-mass version of cumulative offers compute a stable allocation \(x_{t,m,w}\ge 0\)? Require \(\sum_{m,w}x_{t,m,w}=\mu_t\), respect the scaled capacities and quotas, and define stability to exclude any positive mass of a type that prefers a contract its member state would accept under the mass choice rule.

The model it lives in

A large recurring asylum cohort represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete seeker types; each state has per-capita quotas and wait-time capacities. The decision variables \(x_{t,m,w}\) are masses of type \(t\) assigned to integral state-wait-time contracts. The task is to compute a stable allocation with a mass version of cumulative offers.

The objection that survived

Individual strategy-proofness has no canonical type-mass analogue: a single seeker has zero mass, while a positive-mass cohort deviation changes the incentive unit.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s stability guarantee for homogeneous burden-sizes. It leaves individual strategy-proofness unresolved, as well as Theorem 1’s choice-rule characterization, Theorem 3’s heterogeneous-size impossibilities, and Proposition 3’s non-obvious manipulability result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a type-mass cumulative-offer procedure compute a stable allocation in time polynomial in the number of states, wait times, support size, and input bit length?
  • Can individual strategy-proofness be meaningfully retained for tagged infinitesimal seekers, or should the incentive question concern positive-mass cohorts?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a paper-anchored positive case under ChoCo’s criteria: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes the unique member-state choice rule satisfying feasibility, early filling, and priority respect. Theorem 2, proved here, shows that the cumulative offer mechanism is stable and strategy-proof when all asylum seekers have identical burden-sizes. Theorem 3, also proved here, gives instances with heterogeneous burden-sizes where no stable mechanism exists and where no strategy-proof mechanism can select a stable outcome whenever one exists. These are mechanism-design results; none establishes a complexity class or analyzes the running time of computing an outcome.

Theorem 2 is the closest possible anchor because it concerns an explicit mechanism, but its conclusion is about stability and strategy-proofness, not computational tractability. A continuous version would naturally ask whether those properties survive when seeker types carry mass; that would continuize the paper’s mechanism-design guarantees, which are outside this programme’s scope. Treating it as a computational mirror would therefore stretch the anchor requirement.

So there is no qualifying continuous problem per anchor to specify. A separate computational study of finding stable mass allocations could be inspired by the model, but it would be a new question, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes a choice rule; Theorem 2 proves stability and strategy-proofness for a mechanism under homogeneous burden-sizes; Theorem 3 gives impossibility results for mechanisms under heterogeneous sizes. These are results about which rules and outcomes have desirable properties, not about the complexity of computing them. That makes the paper a poor fit for ChoCo’s computational question.

The best attempt to build a mirror is a large recurring cohort: group seekers by preference, burden-size, and priority characteristics, then ask whether a mass version of cumulative offers produces a stable, strategy-proof allocation. That is a coherent model. But its central questions still concern mechanism properties. To turn them into computational questions—say, finding or testing a stable mass allocation—would add a problem the paper does not analyze. Strategy-proofness also needs careful treatment: the aggregate distribution records mass, while a deviation is made by an individual. A tagged individual or positive-mass group can handle that, but each changes what the theorem is about.

Theorem 3 admits the same move: ask whether stable mass allocations exist, or whether a stable, strategy-proof mass mechanism can be constructed. Those could be worthwhile questions in their own right. The paper’s theorem, though, establishes impossibility for a mechanism; it gives no complexity claim about deciding existence or computing an outcome. Theorem 1’s explicit procedure likewise supplies no computational result to mirror.

I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity asylum regime exists: recurring cohorts make one quite credible. The negative case is narrower. Under ChoCo’s stated target, that credibility supports a new continuous matching study, but does not supply a paper-anchored computational mirror.
