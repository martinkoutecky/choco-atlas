The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper has no computational result to continue. Theorem 13 is an impossibility from symmetry, Theorem 25 characterizes a partition by two axioms, and Theorem 34 decomposes strict rules into a canonical part and arbitrary choices. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or an optimization problem. There is therefore no named computational claim whose complexity landscape a continuous mirror could chart.

The voting example is the best candidate for a population model: keep the candidates finite and represent voters’ rankings by a rational distribution \(\mu\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but it adds little to this paper’s model. Its profile \(h\) is already a finite-support histogram of rankings with integer multiplicities. For rational \(\mu\), choose a common denominator \(D\) and set \(n_t=D\mu_t\). This gives a finite electorate with exactly the same normalized profile. Scaling the counts preserves both the candidate symmetries and the primary scoring order, so the orbit partition and the paper’s axiomatic conclusions are unchanged. Irrational masses would require a new computational input model. Either way, this supplies no new complexity result.

A stronger proposal would ask for an algorithm to compute \(\Omega\), or to decide whether an anonymous strict refinement exists, from a support-encoded rational voter distribution. That could be a worthwhile algorithmic question, but it studies computation of an axiomatic object the paper defines; it does not mirror a computational result in the paper. And for rational profiles, normalization to integer histograms preserves the instance exactly, so any complexity comes from the finite profile’s symmetry structure, not from continuization.

The other examples do not provide a clearer route. In chess, sports, cooperative games, and networks, the finite set \(N\) is the set of entities being ranked. Replacing those entities by mass types would change what the output ranks. A continuous model might be designed, but it would be new work without a computational result here to anchor it.

So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo mirror of this paper: its most plausible population model is already represented by the paper’s histogram input, and the paper has no computational claim to mirror. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could be built around its ideas is weaker; computing orbit structure from profiles could be worthwhile as a separate project.
