The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a paper-anchored positive case under the stated anchor rule: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Propositions 1–5 and Lemma 1 establish characterizations or possibility and impossibility results about Brexit anomaly; none classifies a problem as being in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Proposition 5, for example, proves that completely mixed random mechanisms avoid the anomaly with strict preferences. That is a substantive result, but it is not a computational anchor.

The closest candidate is Proposition 2, proved in the paper. It says that in simple voting with at least two voters and candidates, every unanimous and monotone mechanism suffers Brexit anomaly when each voter’s preference domain is all complete orderings, all strict orderings, or all non-strict orderings. A natural high-multiplicity setting would be a large referendum electorate with relatively few distinct expressive-preference types. A type would be a complete ordering over vote/outcome pairs; its mass would be its share of the electorate.

One possible research question—not a qualifying anchor—is Mass-Brexit Witness. An instance gives candidates \(C\), a finite support of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and an anonymous voting rule \(F\) represented by a finite rational polyhedral partition of the action-share simplex. For each type \(t\), an allocation \(y_{t,a}\) records the mass choosing action \(a\in\{\text{abstain}\}\cup C\), with \(\sum_a y_{t,a}=\mu_t\); \(F\) maps the aggregate action shares to a winner. The question is whether there are a type \(t\), actions \(r\ne\ell\), a positive cohort mass \(\delta\le\mu_t\), and two feasible residual allocations such that moving that cohort from \(r\) to \(\ell\) leaves the winner unchanged in the first context, type \(t\) prefers \((\ell,z)\) to \((r,z)\) there, and \(r\) is weakly best among its actions in the second context. A solution is such a witness. This asks about the underlying anomaly, not its polyhedral encoding.

The case for this extension is that it puts mass shares and a finite type support at the center of a familiar voting mechanism question. Its central weakness is also decisive: the paper’s anomaly is witnessed by one individual, while an atomless voter has no effect on aggregate action shares. The proposed version therefore gives a positive-mass cohort coordinated action; it changes the unit of deviation and is not the paper’s result. I would not assign it a tractability class on the evidence here. Proposition 2 is the best lead for a follow-up question, but it cannot support the requested anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive problem: the paper has no computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes mechanisms that avoid Brexit anomaly; Propositions 2–4 establish impossibility or exhibit the anomaly; Proposition 5 gives a sufficient condition for random mechanisms. None asks for an algorithm or classifies a computational problem. A continuous-population version could be an axiomatic result, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

Proposition 2 is the strongest candidate to rescue. A large referendum electorate with a small support of expressive-preference types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The difficulty is what happens to the paper’s individual deviation. Under a mechanism \(F\) on aggregate action shares, one person has zero mass and cannot change the shares. Keeping the deviation infinitesimal removes the individual’s pivotal role. Giving it positive mass \(\delta\) restores a possible effect on the outcome, but changes the question to whether a coordinated cohort deviates. That may be a reasonable new question; it is not the individual Brexit anomaly Proposition 2 characterizes. Keeping voters as finite atoms of size \(1/n\) preserves the individual deviation, but leaves a finite-population problem represented by rational frequencies.

The proposed Mass-Brexit Witness is the best repair: it makes that cohort question explicit and gives the mechanism a polyhedral representation. But the question’s computational structure comes from assumptions supplied for the repair—anonymous \(F\), a finite type support, polyhedral cells, and a positive-mass cohort. With an explicit list of cells, checking witnesses reduces to enumerating finite type/action/cell choices and testing linear feasibility. A different, succinct representation could induce a different complexity problem. The paper supplies neither a mechanism encoding nor a reason to prefer one, so this is a newly designed verification problem rather than a computational mirror grounded in one of its results.

The negative case is strong under ChoCo’s computational scope, but it cannot honestly establish that every continuous extension would be worthless. A mass version of Proposition 2 could be a credible axiomatic project, and a high-multiplicity voting regime is sensible. Those points do not supply the missing computational anchor.
