What the mirror covers

No results are covered by a computational population mirror; Theorem 4.2 and Lemma 2.1 and Propositions 3.3 and 4.3 concern implementation structure and genericity.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest foothold is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper: when both agents have signal spaces of dimension at least two, for a residual set of relative valuation functions—and, with greater smoothness, a residual and finitely prevalent set—only constant deterministic social choice functions are ex-post implementable. That is a structural impossibility theorem, not a result about computational complexity. Lemma 2.1 and Propositions 3.3 and 4.3 are structural characterizations and steps toward that theorem. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. So there are no eligible computational anchors for the requested case.

One could propose a follow-on problem about deciding ex-post implementability for rationally encoded polynomial valuations, but it would not be anchored in a computational result of this paper, and the paper’s theorem does not settle its complexity. Turning its agents into a continuum creates another obstacle: if the mechanism depends only on aggregate reports, one agent’s deviation has zero mass and the paper’s incentive constraints can become vacuous. Keeping each agent’s influence preserves the paper’s question but leaves the population finite.

So the positive case does not hold up under the stated anchor requirement. The paper already has continuous signal spaces; it does not study a continuous population, nor does it report a named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.2 is the strongest possible anchor, but it does not meet the programme’s test: it asserts generic impossibility, not the complexity of a computational problem. Lemma 2.1 and Propositions 3.3 and 4.3 supply structural and geometric arguments for that theorem; they do not give an algorithmic question either. A mirror of the theorem could ask whether a specified mechanism is implementable, or whether a nonconstant rule exists, for rationally encoded polynomial valuations. But that would introduce a new computational problem. The paper gives no computational result to motivate it, and its genericity claim over smooth valuation functions is not itself a finite input or an algorithm.

The obstacle to a population version is also structural. The main model has two strategically distinct agents, and its incentive constraints and transfer functions depend on their signals separately. The key geometric conditions compare derivatives with respect to those named agents’ signal coordinates. Replacing them with a distribution over types discards which agent has which signal and the profile information those conditions use. A more ambitious mirror could posit many similar firms whose valuations depend on the aggregate distribution of signals. That may be a sensible new model, but it changes the payoff and incentive structure; the paper’s generic impossibility result does not carry over as a computational result about that population.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor: it supplies no named computational result, and its central object is a small set of strategic agents with interdependent signals, not a high multiplicity society. It is weaker against the universal claim. One could build an interesting large population mechanism design problem from a suitable economic setting. The case against is that this would be a new research direction inspired by the paper, rather than a worthwhile continuous mirror of a result it establishes.
