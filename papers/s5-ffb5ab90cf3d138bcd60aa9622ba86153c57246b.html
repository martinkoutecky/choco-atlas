The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set, respondent types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), item assignments, and an explicitly represented finite-state model specifying each type's beliefs about its binary signal and the resulting per-item report shares, is truthful play a strict Bayesian Nash equilibrium of the nonatomic top-flop game? Each respondent observes \(T\in\{0,1\}\), chooses top or flop, and ratings are per-capita shares of reports equal to \(1\).

The model it lives in

Types \(\theta\) carry mass \(\mu_\theta\), item assignments, and finite-state signal-belief data. Truthful reports induce per-item shares \(r_k(z)\) in state \(z\); each nonatomic respondent chooses top or flop. Test strict equilibrium by comparing each type-signal's conditional probability of winning under truthful play and deviation.

The objection that survived

With only independent idiosyncratic signals, aggregate reports become deterministic in the population limit, so the signal may no longer predict the rating; the proposed case does not specify the common state or other source of persistent aggregate correlation.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive scenario is a large crowdsourcing panel or consumer study divided among a fixed set of items. A type would specify a respondent’s item assignment, belief over the signal process, and relevant preferences; after receiving a binary signal, the respondent chooses a top or flop bet. Mass is the share of respondents of each type, and an item’s endogenous rating is the mass share of its respondents choosing action \(1\). Replacing counts by shares is a natural population limit of the paper’s game, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their setting.

A precise *prospective* question would be: given finitely many respondent types with rational masses and explicitly represented finite-support beliefs about others’ signals, does truthful play—choosing \(0\) on signal \(0\) and \(1\) on signal \(1\)—form a strict Bayesian Nash equilibrium of the nonatomic top-flop game? A solution is an exact yes/no answer, with a profitable deviation as a certificate if the answer is no. This looks amenable to exact computation from the represented belief supports. But it is a new computational question suggested by the model, not a computational result established by the paper.

The closest named results are Theorem 3, proved here, which says that under Assumptions 1–4 for every agent and common-knowledge Assumption 5, truthful play is a strict Nash equilibrium of the finite top-flop game; and Theorem 4, also proved here, which gives the corresponding strict-equilibrium result for the threshold-\(y\) game when each agent’s threshold lies in \(S_i'\). Neither asserts a complexity classification, gives an algorithm, or analyzes a computational formulation. The paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria.

So the population limit is plausible, and the underlying game is already aggregate in character. But I cannot honestly present it as a continuous computational mirror of an anchored result: the paper’s contribution is an equilibrium characterization, not computational social choice. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 proves that truthful play is a strict Bayesian Nash equilibrium under the stated belief assumptions; Theorem 4 proves the corresponding result for threshold betting. Neither studies an algorithmic problem or a complexity classification. A continuous version could be a worthwhile mean-field game question, but that would extend the paper’s equilibrium analysis rather than continuize a computational contribution.

The crowd-panel scenario is credible, and multiplicity is not the problem. Many respondents can receive signals about a fixed set of items, with their assignment and other relevant features represented by types. The best version of the proposed mirror would give those types rational masses and include common item-level latent qualities so a respondent’s signal predicts the aggregate reports for that item. Then one can ask whether truthful strategies form a strict equilibrium in the nonatomic game.

That model also shows what must be added. With independent idiosyncratic signals and no common item-level uncertainty, the law of large numbers makes each item’s aggregate rating deterministic; an individual’s signal no longer predicts it, so the paper’s truth-revealing incentive can disappear. A common latent quality can preserve the association, but specifying its distribution and how beliefs about it vary by respondent becomes part of the new model. Under an explicit finite-state specification, checking truthful incentives amounts to calculating each type’s conditional win probabilities and comparing its two actions. With a more succinct or richer belief model, the computational question depends on that newly chosen encoding of population correlations. Neither version is supplied by the paper.

The same limitation applies to Theorem 4: a finite set of thresholds gives another set of incentive comparisons, while choosing thresholds or designing a robust payment scheme introduces an optimization objective the paper does not study. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are still further from a population computation: they characterize one agent’s choice against exogenous ratings, so duplicating respondents does not change the individual decision problem. Coalition resistance or equilibrium selection could motivate computational work, but the paper does not analyze those problems.

I would therefore treat the proposed panel model as a plausible new research lead in continuous mechanism design, not as a worthwhile continuous computational mirror of this paper. The negative case is not universal: a continuum equilibrium analysis could interest the authors, and the paper itself gives a convincing high-multiplicity setting. Its weakness is exactly that point. What fails is the computational anchor ChoCo requires, not the possibility of a sensible population limit.
