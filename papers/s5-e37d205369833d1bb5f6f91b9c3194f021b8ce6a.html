What the mirror covers

The proposed census would tally Theorem 15’s exact outcome counts and related expected-value calculations; it leaves the fairest-policy search and partition-dominance results untouched, and no population mirror survives.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest anchor is Theorem 15, proved in this paper. It gives an exact count for a concrete computation in the paper, though the authors do not state a complexity classification such as “in P.” For a policy \(S\), let \(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_n\) be Luis’s turn positions and \(r_1,\ldots,r_n\) Rita’s. For a specified outcome \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\) of Luis’s choices, Theorem 15 says the fraction \(P\) of Rita’s preference permutations that produce that outcome is
\[ P=\frac{(\ell_1-1)_{\ell_1-1}\left(\prod_{j=2}^{n}(\ell_j+v_{j-1}-2n-2)_{\ell_j-\ell_{j-1}-1}\right)(v_n-1)_{2n-\ell_n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2n-r_i+1)}, \]
where \((x)_q=x(x-1)\cdots(x-q+1)\). Thus \((2n)!P\) is the number of preference permutations yielding \(v\). The paper derives this by counting choice paths and dividing by the total number of paths.

Here is the continuous mirror I would defend: Mass Outcome Census. A large network runs many independent allocations, each giving a pair of participants its own copy of the same \(2n\) item types. Every pair follows one common policy \(S\). A type \(\pi\in S_{2n}\) records the pair’s relative preference ranking: relabel the items by Luis’s ranking, and record Rita’s ranking in those labels. The instance gives a finite support of types and rational masses \(\mu_\pi\), summing to one. The question is: for each possible Luis outcome \(v\), what fraction \(\nu^L(v)=\sum_{\pi:\,L(S,\pi)=v}\mu_\pi\) of the allocation population produces it? The solution is the exact rational outcome-mass table. Under the uniform type distribution, Theorem 15 gives this mass directly as \(P\); for arbitrary listed support, simulating each type computes the table in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\). This is a Class A evaluation problem.

The regime is many actual allocation pairs, with many pairs sharing each relative-preference type; \(\tau\) may be small compared with the number of pairs, especially when the item set is fixed. The masses describe the population’s composition, not uncertainty about one pair. The choice policy is the decision rule applied across that population. The paper’s average-advantage criterion can be recovered by also tallying Rita’s outcomes and summing the two role-specific mean utilities. Under the uniform society, the resulting mean signed advantage is the paper’s \(Ladv(S)\) divided by \((2n)!\), so minimizing its absolute value selects the same policies.

This covers the paper’s exact outcome-counting and expected-value calculations, not its harder question of finding the fairest policy. That creates a natural next problem: given \(n\) and \(\mu\), choose a balanced policy \(S\) minimizing the absolute population-average advantage. The paper supplies no named hardness or complexity result for that optimization. I would expect its search over \(\binom{2n}{n}\) policies to remain difficult as \(n\) grows, since making the population continuous does not relax the policy itself; but a Class B verdict would need a hardness result for the finite optimization problem.

The soft spot is the unit of mass. It is a population of allocation pairs, with each pair’s joint preference profile as its type, rather than a population of isolated voters. That is a real modeling choice, and the authors would need to accept repeated pairwise allocations as a natural deployment of their policy. The case survives because it preserves their two-player allocation rule, Borda utilities, common policy, and aggregate fairness calculation exactly; it extends the population regime rather than simplifying the local problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 15, but its proposed mirror changes the unit being modeled. The theorem counts preference permutations for one two-player allocation. In “Mass Outcome Census,” a unit of mass is instead a whole allocation instance: a Luis–Rita pair with a joint preference profile. Replicating that pair many times gives many independent copies of the game, each with its own items. Nothing about one copy’s choices or feasible outcomes depends on the mass in another. The result is an average over scenarios, mathematically equivalent to a distribution over discrete preference profiles. The paper already studies that kind of uncertainty through its uniform-permutation model; calling the scenarios a population does not make the society itself continuous.

That distinction matters because the joint profile is essential. Replacing the pair by separate masses of Luis and Rita types loses the pairing and correlation that determine the outcome. Keeping the joint profile preserves the computation, but makes each “type” a two-agent instance rather than an agent type. The alternative—a single shared allocation involving many agents—would require a new model of how the paper’s two-player turn sequence extends to that society. It would not be a continuous version of Theorem 15’s problem.

The census also offers little computational substance as stated. With an explicitly listed support of \(\tau\) pair types, simulate the policy on each type and add its mass to the resulting outcome; at most \(\tau\) outcomes appear. Under the paper’s uniform distribution, Theorem 15 gives the mass of a specified outcome, while Lemma 4 gives expected values recursively. A full table may be exponentially large, but that is an output-size issue. None of this yields the kind of complexity question the programme targets.

The stronger follow-up—choose one policy to minimize average advantage over a distribution of pair types—is more interesting, and I would not claim it is mathematically empty. But the distribution still weights independent allocation scenarios; it does not create a high-multiplicity society whose mass changes the allocation problem. Whether that policy-design problem deserves study is a fair question. The negative case is weakest there: a broad enough notion of “population” could include a large cohort of repeated pairwise allocations. I would argue that this is a scenario-distribution mirror rather than the programme’s population continuization, but the boundary depends on whether the authors themselves would recognize the cohort as their problem.
