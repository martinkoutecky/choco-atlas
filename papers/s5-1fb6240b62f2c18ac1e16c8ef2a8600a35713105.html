The continuous mirror question

Given two types \(A,B\), each with mass \(1/2\), and two arms, suppose type \(B\) has utility vector \((1,1)\) each round and type \(A\) has an adversarially chosen vector \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^2\). The learner chooses \(p_t\in\Delta_2\), samples an arm, and observes that arm's utility for each type. With \(F_{\mu,t}(p)=\sqrt{\langle p,v_t\rangle}\), is worst-case expected regret \(\max_{p\in\Delta_2}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p)-\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p_t)\) necessarily \(\Omega(T)\), or can an algorithm guarantee \(o(T)\) regret uniformly?

The model it lives in

A finite support \(\Theta\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\); each round has a utility vector \(u_{t,\theta}\in[0,1]^K\) shared by the members of that type. The learner chooses an arm distribution \(p_t\), samples an arm, and receives that arm's utility for each type. Welfare is \(F_{\mu,t}(p)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\log\langle p,u_{t,\theta}\rangle\right)\), set to \(0\) if a positive-mass type receives zero utility. The anchored case has two types of mass \(1/2\), with one type's utility fixed at \((1,1)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 4.1 and 4.2. It leaves the stochastic results in Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 and the other full-information guarantees in Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For general rational \(\mu\) and support size \(\tau\), characterize minimax regret under adversarial bandit feedback for mass-weighted NSW, including whether the linear-regret obstruction persists and how it depends on \(\mu\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: this paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification results. Its named computational results are about online regret. But its central problem—choosing among actions to maximize fair welfare across agents—has a direct population version, and the paper’s adversarial results show that this version preserves a meaningful distinction between feedback models.

Take a platform choosing among \(K\) services for a large population. A type \(\theta\) is a cohort whose members have the same utility for each service under each round’s public conditions. Its mass \(\mu_\theta\) is its fraction of the population, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Let \(u_{t,\theta}\in[0,1]^K\) be that cohort’s utilities at round \(t\). If the platform uses action distribution \(p\in\Delta_K\), cohort \(\theta\)’s expected utility is \(\langle p,u_{t,\theta}\rangle\). The natural population version of NSW is

\[ F_{\mu,t}(p) =\exp\!\left(\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta \log \langle p,u_{t,\theta}\rangle\right), \]

defined as \(0\) if a positive-mass cohort gets zero utility. This is exactly the geometric mean across agents when each cohort is expanded into identical members. The mass-weighted objective is a necessary co-modification: the original unweighted mean over named agents becomes a mean over population mass.

The regime is plausible where millions of users fall into a small number \(\tau\) of stable response cohorts. In the bandit version, the platform samples an action and observes that action’s utilities for each type; in the full-information version, it observes the full type-by-action utility table. Utilities may change with the public conditions each round. This preserves the paper’s online decision problem, its NSW objective, and its feedback distinction while making the population the continuous object.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It states that with bandit feedback, for \(f=\mathrm{NSW}\), every algorithm has an adversarial environment with expected regret \(\Omega(T)\). The proof already uses just two agents and two arms. One agent always has utility \(1\) for both arms; the other’s arm-utility vector is drawn each round from either \(E=(0.4,0.2,0.1,0.3)\) or \(E'=(0.3,0.3,0.2,0.2)\), in the order \((00,01,10,11)\). The two environments have identical marginal observations for each arm, but different optimal mixtures. The theorem is established here, not cited.

Continuous-Cohort Adversarial NSW Bandit is the direct mirror. An instance has two types \(A,B\), each of mass \(1/2\), and two arms. Type \(B\) has utility vector \((1,1)\) each round; type \(A\)’s vector \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^2\) is selected adversarially. The learner chooses \(p_t\), samples an arm, and observes the selected arm’s utility for both types. Regret is

\[ \max_{p\in\Delta_2}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p) -\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p_t). \]

A solution is a minimax regret classification for this problem: either a sublinear-regret algorithm or a lower-bound proof ruling one out. The paper’s construction gives the latter. Here \(F_{\mu,t}(p)=\sqrt{\langle p,v_t\rangle}\); the two environments give exactly the same bandit observations, while their expected welfare-maximizing mixtures are \((0.2,0.8)\) and \((4/13,9/13)\). The expected direction is linear regret, transferred from the two-agent instance. More precisely, this is an information-theoretic lower bound, not an NP-hardness result. Its force is that the obstruction remains with fixed support size \(2\): increasing the population multiplicity does not remove it.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 4.2, also proved here. It says that for any concave, Pareto-optimal welfare function \(f:[0,1]^N\to[0,1]\), Algorithm 2 with the log-barrier regularizer and \(\eta=\sqrt{K\log T/T}\) achieves adversarial full-information regret \(O(\sqrt{KT\log T})\). For finite support, \(F_{\mu,t}\) is itself a concave, Pareto-optimal welfare function, so this theorem applies to the mass-weighted NSW mirror.

Continuous-Cohort Full-Information NSW has the same input and regret objective, but reveals the entire utility table after each round. A solution is an online algorithm and a proof of a regret bound uniform over adversarial utility sequences. The expected direction is tractable regret minimization: Theorem 4.2 supplies the bound, independent of the number of agents; with a finite type list, the welfare and its gradient can be computed from that list. As with the source theorem, this is a regret guarantee, not by itself a bit-complexity classification.

Together these questions mirror the paper’s adversarial feedback separation: the bandit population problem has linear regret even with two types, while full information admits sublinear regret. This case covers Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 only; it does not claim a continuous analysis of the paper’s stochastic results or its other full-information guarantees.

The weakest point is the cohort assumption. The lower-bound mirror treats members of a type as sharing the same round-by-round utilities, as under a common cohort-level shock. If members instead receive independent noisy rewards, observing a large cohort may reveal much more than observing one agent, and the bandit lower bound need not survive. That limits the scenario, but not its plausibility: a shared public condition affecting a small number of large cohorts is a sensible online welfare problem, and it is recognizably the authors’ problem with population mass in place of agent counts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that these mirrors change the labels on the welfare coordinates more than they change the computational problem. The paper’s learner chooses a distribution over arms; it does not alter the population. In the proposed mirror, masses enter only as weights in
\[ F_{\mu,t}(p)=\exp\!\left(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta\log\langle p,u_{t,\theta}\rangle\right). \]
That gives a coherent population objective, but the population is not where the paper’s main computational difficulty lies.

For Theorem 4.1, the proposed two-cohort example preserves the lower bound by making every member of a cohort share the same realized utility vector each round. Then the “large population” gives exactly the same observations and welfare as two agents with weights \(1/2\): the hidden information is about the relationship between an individual’s possible arm utilities, not about the number of people. If cohort members instead have independent reward realizations, the feedback changes with population size; and with NSW, a positive mass of users receiving zero utility can make welfare zero. So the mirror’s cleanest transfer depends on common shocks and exact replication. That limits how much it says about an ordinary large user population. Still, shared cohort-level shocks are plausible, and showing that a lower bound survives them is a legitimate result.

For Theorem 4.2, the population objective with finite support is itself a concave, Pareto-optimal welfare function. The theorem therefore applies directly, with the type masses built into the function. This leaves little new algorithmic work: the continuous model inherits the regret guarantee by instantiating the paper’s general theorem. But that is not a genuine collision under the programme’s standard. The paper’s agents are finite; it does not formulate a society as a distribution over types. A direct corollary may make the mirror easy, but it does not make the population question already studied.

So the negative case is mainly about limited new structure: the bandit mirror is an exact replicated instance under a restrictive feedback regime, and the full-information mirror is an immediate application of a generic theorem. I cannot make that into a strong argument that no worthwhile scenario exists. A platform serving large cohorts subject to shared public conditions is plausible, and the two theorems then give a meaningful comparison between bandit and full-information feedback. The honest case against is weak: it questions the mirror’s incremental yield, but does not defeat its well-posedness or its value as a boundary result.
