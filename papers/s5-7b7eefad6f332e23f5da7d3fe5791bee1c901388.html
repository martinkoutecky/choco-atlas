The continuous mirror question

Given \(p\), rational arrival shares \(a_0=1-p\lambda\) and \(a_j=\lambda\), density \(d\), horizon \(H\), and precision \(\varepsilon\), compute \(\varepsilon\)-approximations to each type’s and the total perished fraction under the Patient rule, starting from an empty pool in the sparse mass limit \(m\to\infty\), \(\alpha=d/m\).

The model it lives in

Use types \(T=\{A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_p\}\), with time-varying waiting masses \(x_i(t)=\lim_{m\to\infty}|S_t(A_i)|/m\). Arrivals follow the stated type shares; compatibility has probability \(d/m\) for the allowed pairs and zero otherwise. The Patient rule prioritizes compatible hard-to-match types at critical events. The objective is to return the per-type and total perished fractions over \([0,H]\).

The objection that survived

The paper already solves these ODEs numerically and analyzes their large-market sparse regime, so asking for approximate loss values may duplicate its existing analysis; the proponent does not supply a new complexity guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers finite-horizon perishing losses for the Greedy and Patient policies, including the type-level and total losses. It leaves policy optimization, waiting-time trade-offs, and finite-market error bounds outside the stated question.

Open questions for a prover

  • What running-time bounds in \(p\), input bit length, \(H\), and \(1/\varepsilon\) hold for finite-horizon loss evaluation?
  • Can the error between finite-market stochastic losses and the mass-limit ODE losses be bounded uniformly over the horizon?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible but narrow positive case. The paper has no theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its best anchors are Theorem 2 and Theorem 4, which give ODE formulations that the paper then uses to analyze matching performance. I take Theorem 4 as the lead: it gives the clearest type-level flow for a continuous-population version of the Patient Algorithm.

A plausible regime is a large kidney-exchange program with many patient–donor pairs but a moderate number of compatibility and patience profiles. A type records the pair’s compatibility probabilities with every other type, its critical-time distribution, and its treatment by the matching rule. Agents in one type are indistinguishable in those respects. Let \(N\) be the market scale, with \(N\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types. The paper’s own example has \(1{,}097{,}058\) OPTN records, though that alone does not establish that real compatibility profiles can be grouped into small \(\tau\).

In the paper’s particular model, \(T=\{A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_p\}\): \(A_0\) is easy to match, and each \(A_j\), \(j\geq1\), is compatible with \(A_0\) and with its own type, but not with other hard-to-match types. Arrival shares are \(a_0=1-p\lambda\) and \(a_j=\lambda\); each waiting agent has critical-event rate \(1\). Set the compatibility probability to \(\alpha=d/N\). A continuous state \(x_i(t)\) can then represent the mass of waiting type-\(A_i\) agents per unit market scale. Masses may be fractional while every individual pair still receives an integral match.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, is the Greedy formulation anchor. Writing \(n_i(t)=|S_t(A_i)|\) and \(\pi_t^*(A_i,A_k)\) for the probability that the event agent of type \(A_i\) is matched to a waiting type-\(A_k\) agent, it gives
\[ \dot n_0=(1-p\lambda)m\left(1-\sum_{k=0}^{p}\pi_t^*(A_0,A_k)\right) -(1-p\lambda)m\pi_t^*(A_0,A_0) -\lambda m\sum_{k=1}^{p}\pi_t^*(A_k,A_0)-n_0, \]
and, for \(j\geq1\),
\[ \dot n_j=-(1-p\lambda)m\pi_t^*(A_0,A_j) -\lambda m\pi_t^*(A_j,A_j) +\lambda m\left(1-\pi_t^*(A_j,A_0)-\pi_t^*(A_j,A_j)\right)-n_j. \]
The terms account for arrivals that remain in the pool, arrivals that remove a waiting partner, and agents who become critical and leave. The paper computes with this formulation in its performance analysis.

The corresponding continuous question is Greedy-Fluid-Eval. An instance gives \(p,\lambda,d\), a horizon \(H\), and a requested precision \(\varepsilon\), with an initially empty pool. In the large-market limit \(N\to\infty\), \(\alpha=d/N\), the state consists of type masses \(x_i(t)\). The Greedy rule makes the same local choice as in the paper: on arrival, match to a compatible hard-to-match neighbor if one exists, otherwise to a compatible \(A_0\) neighbor, and otherwise let the arrival join the pool. A solution returns \(\varepsilon\)-approximations to each type’s perished fraction and the total perished fraction over \([0,H]\). The objective is the paper’s loss: expected perished mass divided by arriving mass.

Theorem 4, also proved here, is the Patient formulation anchor and my lead. It gives
\[ \dot n_0=(1-p\lambda)m-\sum_{k=0}^{p}n_k\pi_t^*(A_k,A_0)-n_0, \]
and, for \(j\geq1\),
\[ \dot n_j=\lambda m-n_0\pi_t^*(A_0,A_j)-n_j\pi_t^*(A_j,A_j)-n_j. \]
Here all arrivals enter the pool; when an agent becomes critical, it leaves and may remove a compatible partner, with hard-to-match partners prioritized. The paper again uses the resulting system to evaluate performance.

Its mirror, Patient-Fluid-Eval, has the same instance format and output, but applies the Patient rule at critical events. In the sparse large-market limit, a type-\(i\) event agent sees a Poisson number of compatible type-\(k\) pool agents, with mean \(d x_k(t)\) for allowed types. The rule specifies which compatible type is selected; that determines the mass flow between types. The objective is again perished mass over arriving mass. For example, the limiting perishing rates are \(x_0(t)e^{-d\sum_k x_k(t)}\) for \(A_0\) and \(x_j(t)e^{-d(x_0(t)+x_j(t))}\) for \(A_j\).

For these fixed policies, the expected direction is Class A for the paper’s symmetric type regime: the Poisson neighbor probabilities are explicit, and equal hard-type masses reduce the dynamics to a low-dimensional ODE. A computational study could bound the cost of approximating finite-horizon losses and steady-state losses in \(p,\tau,d,H\), and \(1/\varepsilon\). The paper’s named results do not establish such a complexity bound. A natural next question is how the answer changes when the planner may choose among causal type-level partner-selection rules, perhaps with a waiting-time constraint.

The main weakness is how close this mirror sits to the paper itself. Theorems 2 and 4 already replace discrete pool counts with deterministic type-level ODEs, and the paper also studies a large-market sparse limit. The positive case depends on treating the new question as the population-mass computational problem—with complexity measured in type support and precision—not as the paper’s ODE analysis under another name. Finite-market error bounds are also a substantial open bridge: the authors themselves identify the finite-time gap between the discrete process and the ODE as future work. The mirror covers only Greedy and Patient matching and their loss objective; it does not continuize outcomes or replace pairwise matches with fractional assignments.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither proposed anchor is a computational result of the kind this programme mirrors. Theorem 2 derives the Greedy Algorithm’s type-count ODE; Theorem 4 derives the Patient Algorithm’s. Theorem 3, Theorem 5, and Proposition 6 then analyze those systems’ solutions, losses, and waiting times. None classifies a computational problem or gives an algorithmic complexity result.

The proposed Greedy-Fluid-Eval and Patient-Fluid-Eval largely restate the paper’s own analysis. Its loss is already expressed through integrals of the type counts, and the paper computes those dynamics over a finite horizon and studies their steady-state behavior. Under the proponent’s scaling, setting \(x_i=n_i/m\) and \(\alpha=d/m\) gives \((1-\alpha)^{n_i}\to e^{-d x_i}\). The proposed Poisson neighbor rates are thus the large-market form of the probabilities already used in the paper’s ODEs. For the paper’s symmetric regime, the systems reduce to two dimensions. Asking for an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to their output is numerical evaluation of the continuous model, not a new landscape of computational social-choice problems.

A stronger successor might optimize over causal matching policies, perhaps with a waiting-time penalty. That could be worthwhile research, but it changes the question: the paper evaluates two fixed policies, rather than presenting a discrete optimization problem whose continuous mirror could reveal a new algorithm or hardness boundary. With the same exchangeable compatibility assumptions and type-level policies, the natural formulation is a continuous control problem over the existing aggregate dynamics. If policies instead depend on the realized compatibility graph, the type masses generally cease to capture the relevant state; restoring that information could require a type space so detailed that the moderate-support premise is lost.

The negative case should not rest on there being no plausible high-multiplicity setting. Large matching pools with repeated agent profiles are sensible, and the paper’s own model supports that regime. Nor is “the continuous answer might be easy” an objection. The difficulty for a mirror is that this paper has already made aggregate ODE dynamics its central method and already analyzes the proposed losses. A finite-market error bound could be a valuable follow-up, as the authors themselves note, but it would sharpen the bridge from the stochastic process to their ODEs, rather than supply a missing computational result for continuization to classify. My case is strongest against these two anchors; it cannot rule out every worthwhile computational question adjacent to dynamic matching.
