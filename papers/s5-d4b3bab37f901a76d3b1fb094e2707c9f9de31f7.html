The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(p\in(0,1]\), given finite valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), finite item types \(G\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and additive values \(v_t(g)\), choose masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) for integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=q_g\). Require that whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{u,C}>0\), \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(C\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in C\) with \(v_t(g)>0\), and maximize \(W_p(x)=\left(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}v_t(B)^p\right)^{1/p}\).

A high-multiplicity course-allocation model with finite valuation types, continuous population masses \(\mu\), per-capita item supplies \(q\), indivisible individual bundles \(B\), configuration variables \(x_{t,B}\), support-wise \(\mathrm{EFX}\), and aggregate \(p\)-mean welfare.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity model of fair allocation for large course-allocation or bonus-distribution systems.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete valuation types, with \(\mu_t\) the population fraction of type \(t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(G\) be a finite set of item types, and let \(q_g\) be the per-capita supply of item type \(g\). An individual still receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\); only the aggregate population is continuous. Write \(v_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}v_t(g)\).

The decision variables are

\[ x_{t,B}\ge 0, \]

where \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(B\). They satisfy

\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni g}x_{t,B}=q_g. \]

Thus \(x\) is a distribution of integral bundles across a continuum of agents, not a lottery over outcomes for one agent. A natural regime is a large university with millions of students, a few hundred valuation profiles, and course capacities specified per student. The number of agents is enormous while \(|T|\) and \(|G|\) are comparatively small.

For EFX, the correct almost-everywhere translation is support-wise: whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{u,C}>0\),

\[ v_t(B)\ge v_t(C\setminus\{g\}) \]

for every \(g\in C\) with \(v_t(g)>0\). For EFX\(_0\), the condition is imposed for every \(g\in C\), including zero-valued goods. This preserves the paper’s individual-agent notion rather than replacing it by an average-utility condition.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.6. Printed in the paper, it states:

Fix any \(p\in(0,1]\) and integer \(c\le 3\). Given an additive-valuation instance \(\mathcal I=(N,G,\mathbf v)\) with \(m=n+c\ge 1\), computing an EFX allocation maximizing \(p\)-mean welfare among all EFX allocations is NP-hard; the same holds for EFX\(_0\).

The theorem is proved in the paper, in Appendix A.6, by a reduction from Partition. The construction forces two agents to divide goods of values \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), and an EFX welfare optimum exists exactly when those goods can be divided into two bundles of equal total value.

I would mirror it with the following problem.

\(\textsc{Continuous-EFX-}p\textsc{-Mean}\): given \((T,\mu,G,q,\mathbf v)\) and fixed \(p\in(0,1]\), find a feasible \(x\) satisfying the support-wise EFX constraints and maximizing

\[ W_p(x) = \left( \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\subseteq G} x_{t,B}v_t(B)^p \right)^{1/p}. \]

The solution is the exact mass assignment \(x\), not merely its value.

This is recognisably the same question: how much generalized-mean welfare can be achieved while every agent is EFX? It is not outcome-space continuity, because bundles remain indivisible for every individual, and it is not merely a probabilistic social-choice problem. The continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\), together with per-capita item supplies.

My forecast is that the discrete Partition hardness will not automatically transfer. In the paper’s reduction, the crucial equality \(U_1=U_2=T/2\) is enforced by assigning whole goods to one of two agents. In the population model, item supply and agent mass can be split continuously, so the subset-sum obstruction may dissolve. The likely boundary is instead continuum-specific: the welfare part is linear in the configuration variables \(x_{t,B}\), but choosing which integral bundle configurations have positive mass while maintaining pairwise EFX compatibility is a nonconvex support problem. I would therefore expect tractable configuration-LP or separation results in restricted regimes, and potentially a Class C hardness result for the unrestricted problem. That is a substantive continuous-computational question even if the paper’s NP-hardness itself disappears.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 5.2. It states:

Fix any integer \(c\le 3\). Given an additive-valuation instance with \(m=n+c\ge1\), deciding whether there exists an allocation that is both EFX and Pareto-optimal is NP-hard; deciding whether there exists an allocation that is both EFX\(_0\) and Pareto-optimal is \(\Sigma_2^P\)-complete.

This theorem is also proved in the paper, in Appendix C.1. The NP-hardness uses a cited NP-hard EFX-plus-PO problem and padding gadgets. The \(\Sigma_2^P\)-completeness result reduces from the cited \(\Sigma_2^P\)-complete EF-plus-PO problem.

The corresponding continuous problem is \(\textsc{Continuous-EFX/PO}\): decide whether there exists a feasible mass assignment \(x\) that is EFX and Pareto-optimal. Pareto-optimality must quantify over measurable reassignment of the population. Formally, \(x\) is not PO if there is another feasible assignment \(y\) and, for each type \(t\), a coupling \(\eta_{t,B,C}\) moving mass from old bundle \(B\) to new bundle \(C\), with

\[ \sum_C\eta_{t,B,C}=x_{t,B}, \qquad y_{t,C}=\sum_B\eta_{t,B,C}, \]

such that every positive-mass move weakly improves utility,

\[ v_t(C)\ge v_t(B), \]

and some positive mass improves strictly. The EFX\(_0\) version replaces EFX by EFX\(_0\).

This is a particularly natural mirror for large-scale course allocation: fairness is EFX, while PO means that no reassignment of seats can make every positive-mass subgroup weakly better and some subgroup strictly better. For a fixed support pattern, the Pareto-improvement test is a transportation or linear-feasibility problem, so I would not expect the paper’s \(\Sigma_2^P\) hardness to transfer verbatim. The remaining difficulty is choosing an EFX-compatible support of integral bundles; that could yield a genuinely continuum-specific Class C problem. A useful follow-up would be whether EFX\(_0\) still causes a complexity jump over EFX once Pareto improvements are represented by flows.

The paper’s Theorem 6.1 is useful supporting evidence but not an anchor I would rely on: its polynomial algorithm enumerates a constant number of named heavy agents when \(m-n\le3\). In a continuum, a positive mass of agents may receive non-singleton bundles, so that proof does not survive without a new structural argument.

The weakest point is the surplus scaling. With a finite population of \(n\) agents, \(m=n+c\) becomes per-capita supply \(1+c/n\). If \(c\le3\) is held fixed while \(n\to\infty\), the surplus density tends to zero and EFX may become nearly trivial. A meaningful continuum model therefore uses a per-capita surplus \(1+\delta\), with \(\delta\) small but positive; this corresponds to \(c\) growing proportionally with the population, not literally staying at three global goods. I would state that explicitly rather than pretend the finite-\(c\) theorem has an automatic limit.

That caveat does not undermine the mirror. The authors’ underlying problem—combining EFX with welfare or Pareto efficiency for additive indivisible-goods allocation—survives intact, and the high-multiplicity scenario is credible. What changes is precisely what the ChoCo programme wants to study: whether the hardness comes from named-agent multiplicity, from indivisible item structure, or from the support geometry of a continuous society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors are tied to a finite, balanced market whose defining parameter disappears under continuization. This is not an objection that the answer might become easier; it is an objection that the proposed limit does not preserve the computational object being studied.

Normalize population mass to \(1\). An instance with \(n\) agents and \(m=n+c\) goods has total per-capita supply

\[ \sum_g q_g=\frac{n+c}{n}=1+\frac{c}{n}. \]

For fixed \(c\leq 3\), the surplus tends to zero. If the goods remain one-copy atomic objects, every individual good has vanishing population mass in the limit, so the special goods driving the reductions disappear from both welfare and feasibility. If instead goods are replicated into item types with positive per-capita supply, then there are \(\Theta(n)\) copies and the surplus is no longer \(c\leq 3\). The paper’s “few surplus items” regime has been replaced by a positive-surplus-density market.

The proponent’s proposed repair, using total supply \(1+\delta\) for fixed \(\delta>0\), is a legitimate new model, but it is not a continuization of the stated theorems. The paper’s key structural fact is that only \(c\) agents can have non-singleton bundles and that their union contains at most \(2c\) goods. In the repaired model, a positive mass of agents may receive multi-good bundles, so that entire finite-heavy-part structure vanishes. Theorem 6.1 has the same problem: its algorithm enumerates finitely many named heavy agents, which has no analogue when the heavy population has positive mass.

Theorem 3.6 therefore does not provide a stable anchor. Its Partition reduction relies on each \(g_j\) being assigned whole to agent \(1\) or agent \(2\). In the high-multiplicity version, copies of each \(g_j\) must be supplied at positive density. The mass assignment can then divide the supply of \(g_j\) between the two valuation types, achieving the required value \(T/2\) fractionally without selecting a subset. That is not a defect in the continuous answer; it means the original subset-selection object has disappeared. Keeping each \(g_j\) atomic preserves Partition only by retaining finitely many exceptional goods, whose mass is zero and whose effect cannot be represented by the population distribution.

The support-wise EFX definition does not fully repair this. It is faithful to finite EFX, but it makes feasibility depend discontinuously on support: a bundle held by mass \(1/2\) and the same bundle held by mass \(\varepsilon>0\) impose exactly the same EFX constraints, while at mass \(0\) they impose none. Thus a vanishingly small exceptional population can constrain the entire society while contributing nothing to \(p\)-mean welfare. The alternative, imposing EFX only almost everywhere in the limiting measure, removes precisely the finite agents and bundles used by the reduction. There is no canonical limit preserving both the paper’s welfare problem and its EFX predicate.

Theorem 5.2 is even less portable. Its PO hardness depends on named-agent Pareto comparisons and on padding gadgets consisting of a constant number of dummy agents, private goods, and one-unit utility gaps. In a population model, a constant-size gadget has zero mass and disappears. If it is replicated to positive mass, the private goods become divisible supplies, and the even-integer argument converting EFX\(_0\) into EF no longer applies to a single named bundle. A mass assignment can split a valuation type across several bundle supports, so there is no longer one bundle whose value is rounded by the private-good increment.

The proposed continuous PO notion also changes the predicate in a fundamental way. For a fixed support, Pareto improvement becomes a transportation or flow-feasibility test over types and bundles. That is a sensible notion, but it is not the paper’s individual PO problem with its existential allocation followed by a universal search for an improving allocation. If one insists that a single measure-zero exceptional agent can block Pareto optimality, identity has been reintroduced and the model is no longer determined by the population distribution. If one counts only positive-mass improvements, the paper’s padding gadgets are invisible.

The support-selection problem left after these changes may certainly be interesting. But its nonconvexity comes from selecting compatible supports of integral bundle configurations; the population masses enter linearly once that support is fixed. That is a new finite-support fair-division problem with fractional multiplicities, not a continuous mirror of either named theorem.

So the negative case defeats both proposed anchors in their stated few-surplus form, and it defeats the obvious repairs that preserve positive-density goods only by changing the regime. It is not, however, an airtight universal impossibility claim. A positive-density course-allocation model with support-wise EFX is coherent and may be worthwhile in its own right. The honest conclusion is that the universal “no scenario” case is weak; the strongest objection is specifically that the paper’s computational phenomena are generated by one-copy goods and constant surplus, and those features cannot survive as a genuine population continuum.
