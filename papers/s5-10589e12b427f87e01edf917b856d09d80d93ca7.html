The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: a large centralized school or residency market in which many participants share the same preference type. Mass would be the fraction of each side in each type, and the matching decision would be a flow of mass between types. But the paper gives no named computational result to anchor a computational mirror on.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved by the authors: on a domain satisfying unrestricted top pairs for at least one side, every stable matching rule is strategy-proof if and only if it is group strategy-proof. That is an incentive-property theorem, not a result about complexity or algorithms. Theorem 3 is likewise a characterization of domains compatible with stability and strategy-proofness, not a computational result. The other numbered results concern manipulation structure, impossibility, or existence.

So I cannot state a qualifying continuous problem per anchor without stretching your anchor rule. A continuous analogue of Theorem 2 could ask whether individual and group strategy-proofness coincide for stable rules over type-mass matching markets, but that would continuize an axiomatic question rather than the population for a computational question. It is a possible theoretical mirror, not a case for this programme’s computational continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 compares two incentive properties of stable rules under a richness condition; Theorem 3 characterizes domains on which suitable rules exist. The other results describe manipulation, welfare, and stability. None asks for an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization problem. Deferred acceptance is an algorithm, but the paper does not study its computational complexity. On the programme’s stated terms, the paper therefore has no qualifying anchor.

The most convincing mirror would use a large school or residency market with finitely many preference types on each side. Represent each side by type masses, let preferences rank opposite-side types, scale capacities per capita, and describe the matching as a flow of mass between types. One could then ask whether individual strategy-proofness still implies group strategy-proofness for stable rules, or characterize the single-peaked type domains that admit such rules. That is a coherent high-multiplicity model, but its central questions remain axiomatic. It could be worthwhile matching theory; it does not fill the computational gap ChoCo targets.

Turning that mirror into a computational question would require choices the paper does not motivate: how domains and matching rules are encoded, what input an algorithm receives, and whether it should find a stable flow, detect a profitable deviation, or decide whether a suitable rule exists. A type-level flow can also preserve matched mass without preserving which named agents are matched or which coalition members benefit. Adding report-dependent assignments or identity-sensitive outputs may repair that, but it changes the object enough that the resulting complexity problem is a new proposal, not a computational mirror of Theorems 2 or 3.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo computational target, but weaker as a universal claim that no continuous matching mirror could be worthwhile. A continuum axiomatic study is plausible. The case against is that this paper offers no computational result to anchor it, and the natural repairs lead to a different research question.
