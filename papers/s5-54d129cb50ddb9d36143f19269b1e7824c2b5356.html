The continuous mirror question

Given finite worker and project type sets with rational masses, capacities, acceptable type pairs, and strict rankings of counterpart types, represent feasible mass assignments by distributions over integral partner-type bundles and compatible pairings. For each pair of assignments \(M,N\), couple the bundle distributions within each type in the way that minimizes the integrated paper-style vote: cancel common partner types, pad with nulls, and use the least favorable bijection, treating copies of one type as equivalent. Find a robustly popular assignment, whose worst-coupling margin against every feasible \(N\) is nonnegative, with maximum matched-pair mass, or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic bipartite market with finite complete types, rational masses, capacities, rankings over counterpart types, integral bundles for individuals, and an adversarial within-type coupling when comparing assignments; maximize matched-pair mass among robustly popular assignments.

The objection that survived

The paper compares bundles for the same named person, while type-level mass data do not specify that person’s alignment across assignments; worst-case coupling adds a robust voting semantics that may differ from the authors’ intended one.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, given a finite many-to-many bipartite instance \(G=(A\cup B,E)\), with integral capacities and strict rankings of acceptable neighbors, one can compute a popular matching of maximum cardinality in linear time, \(O(|E|+|A|+|B|)\). Popularity uses the paper’s vote rule: each vertex compares its two partner sets after removing common partners, padding with nulls if needed, and takes the least favorable vote over bijections between the remaining partners. The theorem is about the largest matching *among popular matchings*, not the largest feasible matching. The paper proves it here; it is not a cited result. I would use no second anchor: the paper gives no comparable named hardness result for its own problem.

The mirror I believe is Max-Popular Mass Matching. It models a large two-sided market, such as a workforce platform where workers can take several projects and projects can accept several workers. The instance has finite worker types \(I\), project types \(J\), acceptable type pairs \(E\), and rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\). A type specifies an agent’s capacity, acceptable counterpart types, and strict ranking of those types. The masses describe atomless populations of such agents; the number of people can be huge while the number of types \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) remains modest.

A feasible mass matching pairs units of mass along acceptable type pairs. Each individual still receives an integral bundle of partners, within their capacity; it is the fraction of a type receiving each bundle that can be fractional. For two matchings \(M,N\), each individual votes by the paper’s rule, applied to the multiset of partner *types*: cancel common entries, pad with nulls, and choose the most adverse bijection. Copies of one counterpart type are treated as equivalent. Integrating those votes over both populations gives \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mass}}(M,N)\); \(M\) is popular if this is nonnegative for every feasible \(N\). The objective is to find a popular mass matching maximizing its total matched-pair mass. A finite output can describe the mass assigned to each integral bundle pattern, together with pairings that realize those bundles.

The expected regime is a market with many repeated profiles—perhaps millions of participants but dozens of preference, eligibility, and capacity types. The result would answer a natural high-multiplicity version of the authors’ question: which popular assignment uses the most matching capacity when voters are population mass rather than individually counted vertices?

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A), though that is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 3. The paper’s two-level Gale–Shapley algorithm and its LP proof suggest operations that might be batched by type and mass instead of performed person by person. The continuous question is whether that compression gives an algorithm polynomial in the type graph and the bit length of the masses.

The weakest point is the preference change forced by anonymity. In the paper, strict rankings distinguish individual neighbors; agents grouped into a repeated type cannot distinguish copies of another type, so the mirror has indifference among those copies. That can affect popularity and may prevent the discrete algorithm or proof from carrying over. Also, a linear-time algorithm in the number of named agents does not by itself give an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and mass bit length. These are real gaps, but they are exactly the questions the mirror poses: whether the paper’s matching problem admits a useful high-multiplicity form, and whether its structure survives there.

This case covers Theorem 3’s maximum-cardinality popular-matching problem only. Immediate follow-ups are whether a finite type-level primal–dual certificate exists, whether the continuous optimum agrees with limits of large finite replications, and whether the within-type indifference changes existence or complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper proves that a maximum-cardinality popular matching can be found in linear time. I do not see a sound argument that the result itself fails the programme’s test.

The strongest objection to the proposed mass mirror is that its input does not determine how to evaluate popularity. Popularity compares the assignments of the *same individuals* in two matchings. Masses on type pairs give edge totals, and masses on bundle patterns give each matching’s bundle distribution, but neither says which people receive which bundles in both matchings. The proponent’s rule—treating partners of the same type as interchangeable—supplies that missing information by changing the paper’s strict rankings over named neighbors into indifference among type copies. The paper’s adverse bijection compares partners within one person’s two bundles; it does not choose how bundle assignments in two population matchings are aligned across people.

There is a better version of the mirror: explicitly model type-level preferences, integral bundles for individuals, and the distribution of those bundles across each type. Then define popularity using a specified coupling of people across alternatives, perhaps requiring the incumbent to withstand the worst feasible coupling. That is a coherent computational question. It is also a substantive choice of voting semantics: a different coupling rule can change which mass assignment is popular. Preserving the paper’s exact preferences over named partners instead generally requires retaining those identities and their rankings, which defeats the hoped-for compression in many markets with individualized priorities.

This exposes a real gap in the proposed formulation, but it does not defeat the best mirror. Category-based preferences and anonymous cohorts are plausible, and a precise coupling rule could make the problem well-posed. Those choices may lead to an interesting complexity question even though the result would extend the paper’s model rather than simply scale its input. I can make the case that the proponent has not yet specified a unique mirror; I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The negative case is weak here.
