The continuous mirror question

Given two oceanic weighted voting games with the same number \(m\) of named major players, a designated player \(i\), rational major-player weights and quota, and an atomless homogeneous ocean of rational total weight \(A>0\) in each game, decide whether \(\Phi_i(G')>\Phi_i(G'')\). For each game, \(\Phi_i\) is the limiting Shapley–Shubik power under random arrival priorities: \(\Phi_i=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]\setminus\{i\}}\int_{\ell_{i,S}}^{u_{i,S}} t^{|S|}(1-t)^{m-1-|S|}\,dt\), where \(\ell_{i,S}=\max(0,(q-w(S)-w_i)/A)\), \(u_{i,S}=\min(1,(q-w(S))/A)\), and empty intervals contribute zero.

The model it lives in

A mixed oceanic population: finitely many named major players retain individual weights and power, while a homogeneous atomless voter type carries total voting weight \(A\). The decision compares the designated major player’s exact limiting Shapley–Shubik values in two such games.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2.11’s Shapley–Shubik comparison result in an oceanic voting model; leaves the paper’s Banzhaf results and raw-index function-complexity results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does PP-hardness persist when the ocean has enough voting weight to change major players’ pivotality?
  • What is the exact complexity of comparing the oceanic indices for rational inputs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is an oceanic Shapley–Shubik comparison. The anchor is Theorem 2.11, proved in this paper: given two weighted voting games with the same number \(n\) of players and a designated index \(i\), decide whether player \(i\)’s Shapley–Shubik index is higher in the first game. That comparison problem is PP-complete.

The setting is a large membership organization or federation with a vast class of rank-and-file members who share the same tiny voting entitlement, alongside a finite set of major stakeholders. The ocean members form one homogeneous type with atomless mass; the major stakeholders remain named agents because the question is specifically about an individual’s power. A useful precedent is the established oceanic-game model, which studies voting power for finite major players amid a continuous population of individually insignificant voters. That literature supplies an analytic model; the exact complexity of comparing the resulting power values is the computational question here. [Milnor and Shapley, “Values of Large Games II: Oceanic Games”](https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.3.4.290)

Precisely, an oceanic game has \(m\) major players with nonnegative rational weights \(w_1,\dots,w_m\), quota \(q\), and a homogeneous atomless ocean with total voting weight \(A>0\). Under random arrival priorities, the fraction of the ocean that has arrived by time \(t\) is \(t\), so its arrived weight is \(At\). Write \(w(S)=\sum_{j\in S}w_j\). For a designated major player \(i\), define the limiting Shapley–Shubik power

\(\displaystyle \Phi_i(A,\mathbf w,q)= \sum_{S\subseteq [m]\setminus\{i\}} \int_{\ell_{i,S}}^{u_{i,S}} t^{|S|}(1-t)^{m-1-|S|}\,dt, \)

where \(\ell_{i,S}\) is \((q-w(S)-w_i)/A\) clipped to \([0,1]\), and \(u_{i,S}\) is \((q-w(S))/A\) clipped to \([0,1]\); an interval with \(\ell_{i,S}\ge u_{i,S}\) contributes zero. The integrand is the probability density that exactly the major players in \(S\) have arrived before \(i\) at time \(t\). This is the continuous arrival version of the paper’s random-permutation interpretation of the index; large-game limit theory is established prior work. [Shapiro and Shapley, “Values of Large Games, I: A Limit Theorem”](https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.3.1.1)

Call the resulting decision problem Oceanic Shapley–Shubik Power Comparison: given two such games with the same \(m\) and designated index \(i\), compute whether \(\Phi_i(G')>\Phi_i(G'')\). Its answer is yes or no, and its inputs and exact rational-valued objective are fully specified.

This is a particularly close mirror of Theorem 2.11. In fact, the finite hardness embeds exactly. Given the theorem’s two integer-weight games, add an ocean of total weight \(A=\tfrac12\) to each. For any predecessor set \(S\) of major players, let \(r\in[0,\tfrac12]\) be the ocean weight that arrived before \(i\). Player \(i\) is pivotal precisely when \(w(S)+r<q\le w(S)+r+w_i\). Since the original weights and quota are integers, this holds for every such \(r\) exactly when \(w(S)<q\le w(S)+w_i\); otherwise it never holds. The ocean therefore leaves the focal player’s pivotal status unchanged, and the relative order of the major players remains uniform. Both indices, and hence their comparison, are exactly preserved. The mirror is PP-hard. The displayed values are sums of exponentially many exactly computable rational terms with a polynomial-bit common denominator, which gives a natural PP upper-bound route; I would expect this problem to be PP-complete.

The expected class is therefore Class B: hardness transfers. That is a useful outcome for the programme: the mirror asks the same recognizable question about relative power in weighted yes/no decisions, while identifying a regime where a continuous population does not erase the hard combinatorics. This case covers Theorem 2.11 only; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s raw-index function theorems or its Banzhaf results.

The soft spot is that this is a mixed model: a continuous ocean plus finitely many atomic major players. Also, the simple hardness embedding uses an ocean too light to change any major player’s pivotality. A referee could regard that as preserving the finite core more than continuizing it. I think the mirror remains worth posing because the oceanic model is a recognized voting model and the general comparison problem includes cases where ocean arrivals do affect pivotality. But whether hardness persists when the ocean has substantial voting weight is a natural next question, alongside parameterizing by the number of major players or seeking approximation algorithms for the limiting power.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the anchor, Theorem 2.11, is that a power index measures an individual’s chance of being pivotal. In an all-atomless population, an individual has no mass to change the outcome. Under the Banzhaf coalition model, each type’s aggregate participation concentrates on a deterministic fraction; under Shapley–Shubik arrival, the population follows a deterministic mass path. An infinitesimal voter therefore has zero power. Treating a whole type as one bloc gives it power, but changes the question from an individual’s power to a group’s.

That objection does not defeat the proponent’s oceanic mirror. It retains finite major players, so their individual power remains meaningful, while the ocean is continuous. The reduction using ocean weight \(A=\tfrac12\) attaches mass that cannot change any integer-weight player’s pivotality. That establishes a hard boundary case, not that the ocean drives the complexity. Still, a larger ocean gives a well-defined comparison problem in a recognized model. The finite major players carry the combinatorial complexity, but that is a limitation on what the mirror teaches about high multiplicity, not a reason the question is unworthy.

There is also a plausible all-population repair: compare the aggregate Shapley–Shubik power of a type, as the limit of the sum of its members’ indices in a replicated finite society. That preserves a meaningful type-level influence. It changes the unit of comparison, but it is a natural high-multiplicity power question; I cannot rule out that the authors would recognize it as theirs.

So the negative case can challenge the fidelity of the proposed mirror and the claim that its hardness comes from continuization. It cannot honestly show that no worthwhile mirror exists. The oceanic comparison survives as a credible computational question, and the continuous treatment of power is not already answered merely because an analytic model exists.
