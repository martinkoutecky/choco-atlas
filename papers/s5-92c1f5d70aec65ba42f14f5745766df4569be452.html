The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor under your rule. The paper contains no numbered result classifying the computational complexity of a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its results characterize convergence rates of a stochastic process; they do not classify the complexity of computing those rates.

The closest candidate is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that for reversible asynchronous dynamics on \(G\), the low-noise hitting time of the all-\(+1\) state satisfies
\[ \tau_+(G,h)=\exp\{2\beta\Gamma^*(G,h)+o(\beta)\}, \qquad \Gamma^*(G,h)=\max_{F\subseteq G}\Gamma(F,h^F) =\max_{F\subseteq G}\Delta(F,h^F), \]
where \(h_i^F=h_i+|N(i)|_{G\setminus F}\). Here \(\Gamma\) is the minimum, over vertex orderings, of the maximum tilted cut along the ordering; \(\Delta\) is the corresponding maximum over monotone separating sets. This is a strong mathematical characterization, but it is not a computational-complexity result. So it can motivate a mirror; it cannot serve as the required anchor.

The most credible mirror I see is a continuous version of that barrier calculation for a block-structured population. Take \(\tau\) agent types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where agents of a type share the same coordination-game parameters and interaction pattern. There are \(N\) agents, with \(N_t/N\to\mu_t\); interactions between types \(t,u\) have weight \(J_{tu}/N\), for a symmetric nonnegative rational matrix \(J\). The types might be organizational roles adopting a technology, with many agents per role and relatively few roles.

For \(r_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), let \(r_t\) be the mass of type \(t\) retained in a candidate subpopulation \(F\), and let \(x_t\in[0,r_t]\) be its mass playing \(+1\). Define
\[ \Phi_r(x)=\frac12\sum_{t,u}J_{tu} \bigl[x_t(r_u-x_u)+(r_t-x_t)x_u\bigr] -\sum_t x_t\left(h_t+\sum_u J_{tu}(\mu_u-r_u)\right). \]
This is the type-block analogue of the tilted cut in Theorem 3, normalized per agent. Let
\[ \gamma(r)=\inf_{x(\cdot)}\ \max_{s\in[0,1]}\Phi_r(x(s)), \qquad \Gamma_{\mathrm{cont}}=\sup_{r\in[0,\mu]}\gamma(r), \]
where the infimum is over coordinatewise nondecreasing continuous paths from \(x(0)=0\) to \(x(1)=r\). The candidate computational question is: given \((\mu,J,h)\) and rational \(\varepsilon>0\), compute \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{cont}}\) to additive error \(\varepsilon\), and return a subpopulation \(r\) and path whose barrier is within \(\varepsilon\) of optimal. It asks for the limiting per-capita tilted-cutwidth barrier in a society where type masses, rather than named individuals, are the state variables.

I would provisionally expect this problem to be hard when \(\tau\) is part of the input: the objective retains a global path-ordering bottleneck, though allowing fractional mass may dissolve some discrete ordering hardness. Whether it is tractable for fixed \(\tau\), and whether the finite-\(N\) barrier converges to this value, are natural follow-up questions. I would not present that expectation as a result of this paper.

This is the strongest positive case I can make, but it is an extension suggested by Theorem 3, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result. Its weakest point is that the block-symmetry regime smooths away much of the paper’s arbitrary network structure; the authors might recognize it as a useful new model of their barrier, but not as the continuous form of a computational problem they solved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection comes before choosing a continuum model: this paper has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror. Theorem 3 characterizes a low-noise hitting-time exponent through tilted cutwidth and tilted cut. It gives no algorithmic input-output problem, running-time bound, approximation guarantee, or hardness result. Theorem 1 and Propositions 4–5 likewise characterize dynamics on graph families; they do not classify the complexity of computing anything. So Theorem 3 can inspire a new computational problem, but it does not anchor a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{cont}}\) is a coherent optimization problem. Its finite-type interaction matrix, however, assumes exchangeable agents with dense, block-constant interactions scaled by \(1/N\). The paper’s central quantity,
\[ \Gamma^\ast(G,h)=\max_{F\subseteq G}\Gamma(F,h^F), \]
depends on cuts of induced subgraphs and on vertex orderings. Those quantities can depend on how edges are arranged among agents who share the same block labels. A block-mass description loses that information. Keeping it generally requires refining types until the support grows with the number of vertices, which removes the small-support high-multiplicity regime that makes the mirror attractive.

A graphon-style model could preserve more network structure, but it creates a separate problem. Dense graph limits suit some networks and lose the local structure of bounded-degree networks. A representation rich enough to retain arbitrary sparse neighborhoods needs additional data and a specified finite encoding before its computational complexity is even defined. Either way, the proposed optimization studies a restricted mean-field network game, or a new graph-limit problem, rather than continuizing the graph-structural result across the regimes the paper compares.

The honest weakness in this negative case is that the block model may still be worthwhile. Theorem 3 gives it a principled barrier objective, and the paper’s authors might recognize computing that objective as a useful extension of their work. The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower: the case for a continuous mirror has not produced a qualifying computational anchor, and the proposed block model changes the network regime enough that it should be treated as a new algorithmic metastability question. If ChoCo admits adjacent questions of that kind, this objection may not defeat it.
