But Theorems 1 and 3 bound statistical error, while Theorems 2 and 4 give information-theoretic limits from insufficient samples; none establishes a computational guarantee such as a running-time bound, approximation ratio, or computational hardness result. The mirror therefore fails bit (a), even though the population interpretation is statable.

What the mirror covers

Theorem 1’s exact top-\(k\) upper bound and Theorem 3’s approximate top-\(k\) upper bound concern sampling accuracy; Theorems 2 and 4 give corresponding statistical lower bounds. The proposed population framing carries over these inference questions, but none of the results addresses computational resources.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow population interpretation, but this paper has no named computational-complexity anchor of the kind the ChoCo brief requires. Theorems 1–4 are statistical error bounds: Theorems 1 and 3 upper-bound Borda’s probability of selecting the wrong top-\(k\) set, while Theorems 2 and 4 give information-theoretic lower bounds for *any estimator*. In particular, Theorem 2’s error floor of \(1/7\) under its stated gap and sampling conditions is a limit from insufficient observations, not a computational hardness result.

A plausible adjacent model would be Contextual-Judge Borda Recovery. Let a type \(\theta\) specify a ranking \(\theta(A)\) for every \(m\)-item subset \(A\), and let \(\mu_\theta\) be its population mass. This represents judges whose rankings may depend on the panel they are asked to assess. The induced ranking law is \(M_A(\pi)=\sum_{\theta:\theta(A)=\pi}\mu_\theta\), and the population’s normalized Borda score for item \(a\) is

\[ \tau_a(\mu)=\frac{1}{\binom{n-1}{m-1}}\sum_{A\ni a}\sum_{\pi} M_A(\pi)\,\beta_{\operatorname{rank}_{\pi}(a)}. \]

The target is the \(k\) items with largest \(\tau_a(\mu)\). In a panel with many judges and relatively few repeated response types, the observed \(r\) rankings per subset can be treated as samples from this continuous population, with each observation revealed with probability \(p\). The decision is to return a \(k\)-set from those observations; the loss is whether it differs from the population top-\(k\) set. This is a coherent high-multiplicity *statistical* analogue, and the paper’s error guarantees are relevant to it.

The limit is that the paper does not give a computational result for this model. If the rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) are supplied, the scores can be summed and sorted directly, so the exact population top-\(k\) problem is straightforward. If the masses are hidden and only samples are available, the paper’s question and its bounds carry over, but that is a sample-complexity problem. Neither version yields a computational anchor for a ChoCo trichotomy.

The weakest point is also the main modeling choice: ordinary voters have stable rankings of the alternatives, whereas these types have context-dependent rankings across subsets. That choice can represent the paper’s unrestricted, independently specified \(M_A\) distributions; restricting types to stable complete rankings may exclude its hard statistical instances. So this is a plausible population framing for the paper’s *inference task*, but it does not supply an anchored continuous-complexity question. Theorems 1–4 cover the statistical top-\(k\) and approximate top-\(k\) results; none supports a claim that discrete computational hardness dissolves or transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a ChoCo mirror is strong for this paper’s results, though it does not establish that no population interpretation could ever be worthwhile. Theorems 1 and 3 bound statistical error for Borda’s top-\(k\) and approximate top-\(k\) estimates. Theorems 2 and 4 give information-theoretic error lower bounds for any estimator. Their parameters \(p\), \(r\), and \(\Delta_k\) describe observation and sample difficulty. In particular, Theorem 2’s \(1/7\) floor is a limit from insufficient data, not a lower bound on computational resources. The paper gives no computational-complexity result to continue.

The best population model is plausible: many judges could share response types, with each type assigning rankings to item subsets. A more conventional version could give each type a stable ranking of all items. If the type masses are known, the associated Borda scores are weighted sums, and finding the top-\(k\) items amounts to sorting them. If masses are hidden and rankings are sampled, the paper’s bounds speak to statistical estimation. The contextual type construction also reproduces the paper’s unrestricted subset-specific ranking laws, but the target depends only on their marginals; lifting them to a latent population of whole response maps adds no computational task.

Making the mass distribution subject to bribery, control, or another intervention could create a computational problem. Those objectives are absent from the paper’s results, so they would be new questions rather than mirrors. The probability vectors \(M_A\) are already continuous objects, but they are distributions over ranking outcomes; that is outcome or noise continuity, not a continuous society.

I would therefore reject the proposed mirror as a computational mirror of this paper. The negative case is weaker if “in any scenario” includes any adjacent research question: a high-multiplicity population of judges is sensible, and a new computational objective could be posed over it. What the paper does not supply is a computational result for that objective.
