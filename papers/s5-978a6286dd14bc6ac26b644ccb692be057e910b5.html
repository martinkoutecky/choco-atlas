The continuous mirror question

Given bipartite type sets \(A,B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\mu_b\) summing to \(1\), eligible pairs \(E\), and rational surpluses \(w_{ab}\), compute a polynomial-size description of all stable mass outcomes. Each outcome has contract masses \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) with incident mass at each type bounded by its \(\mu\); its allocation maximizes \(\sum_{(a,b)\in E}w_{ab}x_{ab}\), and its per-agent payoff rates \(p_a,p_b\geq 0\) form a dual optimum satisfying \(p_a+p_b\geq w_{ab}\).

The model it lives in

A bipartite continuum matching market in which each type carries mass, \(x_{ab}\) is matched mass, and \(p_a,p_b\) are per-agent payoff rates. The primal maximizes total surplus subject to type-mass capacities; the dual minimizes \(\sum_a\mu_a p_a+\sum_b\mu_b p_b\) subject to \(p_a+p_b\geq w_{ab}\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2.8 for bipartite unit-capacity markets with a small number of types and type-symmetric payoffs; it leaves general networks and capacities, and the later core, prekernel, and nucleolus results, outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational masses, what is the exact relationship between mass-stable outcomes and type-symmetric discrete stable outcomes after scaling the masses to integer multiplicities?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered hardness result to anchor a mirror on, so the strongest available anchor is its algorithmic one: Theorem 2.8, proved in this paper in Section 3.1. It says that the set of all stable solutions to the network bargaining game can be constructed in polynomial time. The proof builds stable outcomes from optimal solutions to a contract-selection LP and its dual, and shows that complementary slackness recovers every stable outcome.

I would mirror its one-contract-per-agent, bipartite matching case in a large standardized procurement market. There are many suppliers and buyers, but only a few distinct types. A type specifies an agent’s side of the market, eligible counterpart types, and surplus from each eligible contract. Agents of a type are interchangeable in every respect relevant to bargaining. For example, a large annual procurement round might have many vendors and projects, with only a small number of standardized vendor and buyer classes.

Call the continuous problem Stable Mass Bargaining. Its input is finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an eligible-pair set \(E\subseteq A\times B\), and a nonnegative rational surplus \(w_{ab}\) for every \((a,b)\in E\), all binary encoded. Each agent can enter at most one contract. A variable \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) is the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(b\). Feasible mass contracts satisfy
\[ \sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\mu_a \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{a:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\mu_b. \]
The market forms contracts to maximize total surplus, \(\sum_{(a,b)\in E}w_{ab}x_{ab}\).

The question is to compute the whole set of type-symmetric stable outcomes: contract masses \(x\), and nonnegative per-agent payoff rates \(p_t\). A solution consists of an optimal feasible \(x\) and an optimal feasible solution to the dual
\[ \min\ \sum_{a\in A}\mu_a p_a+\sum_{b\in B}\mu_b p_b, \qquad p_a+p_b\geq w_{ab}\quad ((a,b)\in E). \]
This gives the bargaining interpretation directly: every used contract divides its surplus between its two types, and no eligible pair can block by forming a more valuable contract. The full set can be represented by the optimal faces of these two LPs, rather than by listing its possibly many points.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable: the LPs have size polynomial in the number of supported types \(\tau=|A|+|B|\), the number of eligible type pairs, and the input bit length \(L\). When masses are discrete proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), scaling the flow by \(n\) gives a bipartite matching problem with integer capacities, so an integral optimal contract allocation exists. That offers a concrete bridge to high-multiplicity discrete markets, as well as further questions about the exact dictionary between type-symmetric stable outcomes and discrete stable outcomes.

The scope is deliberately limited to Theorem 2.8’s stable-outcome computation in this matching regime. It does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s nucleolus result, Theorem 2.12: extending its lexicographic ordering over coalitions would require a principled way to order the continuum of fractional coalitions.

My weakest point is the restriction the mirror needs. The paper allows arbitrary individual-level networks; this version assumes compatibility and surplus are constant within type pairs, and it focuses on unit-capacity agents. If a market’s important opportunities depend on named counterpart identities, the number of types may grow with the population and the high-multiplicity advantage may vanish. In the standardized cohort scenario, though, the restriction describes a plausible market regime while preserving the paper’s core question: which contracts form, and how is their surplus divided?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2.8’s mirror survives the strongest objection I can make. The proposed mass model is essentially a bipartite matching market whose vertices have been grouped by complete type. Its allocation variables are flows between type classes, its stability prices are dual variables, and positive flow uses only tight type-pair constraints. This is a coherent continuous population question: in a standardized procurement round, for example, many interchangeable suppliers and buyers can be matched while total surplus and blocking conditions remain meaningful per unit of mass. Neither individual identity nor vanishing mass makes the model collapse.

The serious limitation is that the proposed LP represents type-symmetric outcomes. The paper’s stable solutions can assign different earnings to named agents, so the type-level price vector should not be presented as an automatic representation of every possible individual-level stable solution. But that does not defeat the mirror: the continuous question can ask for the set of aggregate stable mass allocations and type-level payoff rates, or for an exact correspondence between those outcomes and symmetric discrete outcomes. Both are questions the authors would recognize as network bargaining.

The stronger, more general mirror—allowing rational type masses, arbitrary bipartite compatibility between types, and type-pair surpluses—has the same issue and the same repair. If neighborhoods or surpluses vary between agents, those differences belong in their types; when there are few such types, the mass-flow model remains meaningful. Calling the resulting LP easy or observing that the paper’s primal-dual method is nearby would limit the novelty, but it would not show the question is unworthy.

So the best case against this anchor is weak. The proponent should qualify the claim that their formulation captures the *whole* stable-outcome set, but the continuous mirror itself is plausible and survives.
