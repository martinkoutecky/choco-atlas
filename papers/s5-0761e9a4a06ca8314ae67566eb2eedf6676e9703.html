What the mirror covers

The proponent raised Theorem 25 and Proposition 23, and discussed Theorem 16 as a possible computational foothold; the opponent addressed all three. The other named results were left without proposed mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 25, proved in this paper: for Concave-Sum valuations satisfying single crossing, M-GVA welfare approaches half of optimal welfare as the number of agents grows. Proposition 23, also proved here, says M-GVA is welfare optimal among deterministic, anonymous, cursed ex-post IC, ex-post IR, ex-post budget-balanced mechanisms under the stated conditions. Both are substantial mechanism-design results, but neither asserts a computational complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 16 gives an optimal threshold by a pointwise maximization, but with general continuous functions and no input encoding or running-time claim, it does not establish that the underlying problem is in P.

So I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor*: this paper has no named result asserting hardness, tractability, or another computational classification. Its continuous signals and signal distributions also do not make the population continuous; there are still \(n\) bidders. Theorem 25’s large-\(n\) limit is a useful clue, but its welfare guarantee relies on finite-sample fluctuations that a fixed mass distribution can erase. Meanwhile, preserving the single indivisible item in an atomless population makes deterministic anonymous selection of one winner problematic; scaling supply to serve positive mass changes the auction being studied.

That is the weak positive case: the asymptotic welfare analysis suggests a natural direction to investigate, but the paper supplies neither a qualifying computational anchor nor a convincing, ready-to-hand-to-a-prover continuous-population question anchored in one of its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Theorem 25 proves an asymptotic welfare guarantee, Proposition 23 characterizes a welfare-optimal mechanism, and Theorem 16 gives an optimal threshold rule. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem; the paper gives no finite input encoding or running-time question. Its signals and signal distribution are continuous, but its society still consists of \(n\) bidders. Turning these results into algorithmic questions would be a new research direction, not a continuous-population mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The best population mirror of Propositions 23 and Theorem 25 would represent bidders by a mass distribution over signal types and study the masked auction on that society. But the paper sells one indivisible item and selects one bidder. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass; with full support, the highest signal may be a supremum that no bidder attains. A type-mass allocation cannot select one individual while preserving the paper’s deterministic, anonymous, single-item model. If the mirror instead allocates to a positive mass of bidders or scales supply with population, it becomes a multi-unit mechanism with different feasibility and incentive constraints. That could be interesting, but it does not rescue these anchors as stated.

Theorem 25 has a further problem as a continuum-limit anchor: its half-welfare guarantee comes from finite-sample fluctuations. The proof uses the event that the sample mean exceeds \(\lambda+b/n\), whose probability tends to \(1/2\) by the central limit theorem. In an exact continuum society the mean is \(\lambda\); the fluctuation that produces the half disappears, leaving a boundary condition. Even if one resolves that boundary by convention, the mechanism still needs an attained highest-signal bidder to allocate its single item. Retaining randomness over aggregate societies would make uncertainty the object being continuized, rather than the population.

Theorem 16 is the strongest possible computational foothold, but its threshold depends on the full vector of the other bidders’ signals. Replacing that vector by an aggregate distribution makes one bidder’s deviation invisible. Keeping a distinguished bidder’s report restores the incentive question, but returns to selecting an individual of zero mass. The paper also gives no representation for the distributions and valuation functions on which an algorithmic complexity claim could be based.

This is a strong case against a ChoCo mirror of this paper, not a proof that no related continuous mechanism-design problem could be worthwhile. A per-capita-supply model might be sensible and might interest these authors; it would need its own formulation and computational question. On the paper’s stated results, though, the proposed anchors are mechanism-design and asymptotic results, and the most faithful atomless version runs into the single-item allocation problem.
