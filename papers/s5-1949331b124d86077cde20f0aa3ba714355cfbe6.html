The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational anchor in this paper under your rule. Theorem 5, proved here, says that when \(\alpha_+,\alpha_-\ge 0\) and they are not both zero, the mechanism is DSIC and gives the dominant report
\[ m_i^*= \begin{cases} \hat v_i/\alpha(p_i^A),&\hat v_i>0,\\ \hat v_i/\alpha(p_i^B),&\hat v_i<0, \end{cases} \quad \hat v_i=v_i^A-v_i^B+\beta(p_i^A)-\beta(p_i^B). \]
That is a strategic-property theorem, not a complexity result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, or parameterized tractable. The other numbered results concern symmetry, budget feasibility, individual rationality, and scale invariance. The \(O(n)\) implementation cost appears only in an unnumbered note. So there are no valid per-anchor mirror questions to supply.

The strongest adjacent lead I can offer is Continuous-Population Belief-Aware Design. Its instance would contain a finite set \(T\) of rational complete types \(t=(v_t^A,v_t^B,p_t^A,p_t^B)\), a finite rational distribution \(\Pi\) over society mass vectors \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), a per-capita budget \(b\), and reservation level \(\bar u\). A society has \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\) types, with \(N\gg\tau\) members. The designer chooses feasible parameters \(\eta=(\alpha_+,\alpha_-,\beta_+,\beta_-)\), where \(\alpha_\pm\ge0\), \((\alpha_+,\alpha_-)\ne(0,0)\), and \(\beta_\pm\in[\bar u,b]\). Using the report \(m_\eta(t)\) from Theorem 5, the resulting decision is \(A\) if \(\sum_t\mu_t m_\eta(t)\ge0\), and \(B\) otherwise. The benchmark chooses \(A\) if \(\sum_t\mu_t(p_t^A-p_t^B)>0\), and \(B\) otherwise. The task is to return feasible parameters maximizing the \(\Pi\)-weighted fraction of societies on which these decisions agree.

The regime would be a large DAO governance body with many members in a small number of genuinely shared role, preference, and forecast classes. The paper’s large-DAO motivation supports that setting, and its equal-weight pooling objective carries over directly to mass-weighted pooling. The paper’s reported simulations already optimize classification accuracy over mechanism parameters, so this extension stays close to its design question. I would tentatively expect this parameter-selection problem to be Class A: scale invariance lets us normalize the two \(\alpha\) parameters, and the allocation depends on only two effective parameters—the \(\alpha\) ratio and \(\beta_+-\beta_-\). With finite rational input, one could investigate the resulting piecewise-rational decision boundaries.

This is an unanchored extension, not a mirror of a named computational result. Its weakest point is also substantial: in an atomless population, one person’s report has zero effect on the aggregate decision. The pivotal transfer then loses its elicitation force, while Theorem 5’s strictness requires a positive chance of being pivotal. Exact repeated preferences and beliefs are another narrow assumption, and the paper studies small committees. A useful further question would be whether finite, high-multiplicity versions have classification guarantees that remain meaningful as the committee grows while retaining the mechanism’s incentive properties.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the closest thing to an anchor, but it establishes dominant-strategy incentive compatibility and a reporting formula, not the complexity of a computational problem. The propositions on symmetry, budget feasibility, and individual rationality likewise do not give complexity results. The \(O(n)\) implementation note is an unnumbered runtime calculation. Under the stated criterion, there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The proposed continuous-population design problem is the best remaining lead, but it runs into a basic tension. In an atomless population, one expert’s report cannot change the aggregate decision. The pivotal transfer then has no elicitation force, so the truthful-report foundation for the proposed classification objective disappears. Giving reports positive influence by making experts or blocs carry positive mass changes the strategic model; keeping a finite committee preserves the mechanism, but leaves a parameter-selection problem over finite committees rather than a continuous-population version of Theorem 5.

A finite high-multiplicity design problem could still be formulated: choose reward parameters to maximize agreement with the belief benchmark across a supplied distribution of societies. That is a plausible extension of the paper’s simulation objective, and the authors might recognize it as useful. But it is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. Its appeal depends on treating an implicit, empirically optimized design objective as sufficient basis for a new complexity problem.

So the negative case is strong against an *anchored* ChoCo mirror of this paper. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise: exact parameter optimization for large, repeated-type committees is a coherent exception. I would not claim the paper rules out that research direction; I would say it does not provide the computational result the requested mirror exercise needs.
