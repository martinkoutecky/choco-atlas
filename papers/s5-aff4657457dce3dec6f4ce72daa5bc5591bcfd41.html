The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many student types, where each type specifies school preferences and priority categories, and school capacities measured as fractions of the cohort, define continuum DA and full-consent EADA by allowing type mass to split at priority cutoffs. For each society, compute the maximum mass \(I^*(\mu)\) that can be reassigned from continuum DA so every unit weakly prefers its new school, and the mass improved by continuum EADA and DA+TTC. Characterize the worst-case ratios \(I^*(\mu)/I_M(\mu)\), over societies with support at most \(\tau\) and positive denominator, for those mechanisms and for Pareto-efficient mechanisms that weakly Pareto-dominate continuum DA.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity school-choice model with rational mass \(\mu_t\) on each preference-and-priority type, per-capita school capacities, and type-to-school mass assignments; the objective is to maximize strictly improved mass relative to continuum DA and compare it with the mass improved by continuum mechanisms.

The objection that survived

The proposed question computes only the maximum improved mass, while Theorem 1 compares that optimum with EADA's output; a faithful mirror also needs continuum versions of DA and EADA and their improvement ratio.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's improvement ratio for EADA and DA+TTC; it leaves Theorem 2's setwise-minimal-instability claim and Theorem 3's cardinal blocking-pair comparison aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can continuum DA and full-consent EADA be defined canonically for finite preference and priority categories with mass splitting at cutoffs?
  • What are the worst-case improvement ratios as a function of support size \(\tau\); are they finite or unbounded when type masses can be arbitrarily small?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying case from this paper under your anchor rule: it contains no named result classifying a computational problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for \(n>4\) and every Pareto-efficient mechanism \(M\) that Pareto-dominates DA,
\[ \rho(\mathrm{EADA};n)=\rho(\mathrm{DA+TTC};n)=\frac{n-1}{2}\geq \rho(M;n). \]
Theorem 2, also proved here, says every setwise minimally unstable Pareto-efficient mechanism that Pareto-dominates DA has that same improvement ratio. These are sharp mechanism-performance results, not complexity results. Theorem 3 is likewise a mechanism-existence result: another Pareto-efficient mechanism can improve more students than EADA while generating fewer blocking pairs. Lemma 1 is a cycle characterization credited to Tang and Yu (2014), not a computational classification. The paper itself identifies the computational complexity of alternative mechanisms as future research.

There is a credible *prospective* continuization near Theorem 1’s numerator, \(I^*(P)\), the maximum number of students who can be helped without harming anyone. In a large school district, let a type specify a student’s school preferences and priority category at each school; let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of applicants of type \(t\), and let school capacities scale with the cohort. A continuous DA baseline assigns mass to schools, splitting a type’s mass at a priority cutoff when needed. The question would be: given that baseline, find a feasible reassignment of its mass that respects capacities, sends no mass to a less-preferred school, and maximizes the total mass sent to a strictly preferred school. A solution is the reassignment and the maximum improved mass. Repeated cohorts with relatively few preference-and-priority profiles compared with the number of applicants make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

That is an honest extension of the paper’s improvement objective, but it is not a mirror of a named computational result. Nor should one assume Theorem 1’s \((n-1)/2\) bound survives: fractional mass can permit improvements of arbitrarily small size, so the discrete lower bound of two students per trading cycle no longer applies. This is the weak point, and the reason I would not present the extension as a qualifying positive case under the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1 and 2 compare how many students mechanisms improve; Theorem 3 establishes that an alternative mechanism can improve more students while creating fewer blocking pairs. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm or hardness result. The paper itself lists the complexity of alternative mechanisms as future work. A continuous model could be interesting, but it would be a new computational project prompted by the paper, not a continuization of a computational result in it.

The best version of the proposed \(I^*(P)\) question would use a large district with repeated preference and priority profiles, quotas scaled to population, and a continuous DA baseline. It could ask for the greatest mass that can be reassigned to strictly preferred schools without harming anyone, perhaps also tracking the mass exposed to justified envy. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem. But it does not carry Theorems 1–3 across: it optimizes one allocation, while those theorems compare mechanism outputs across problems. Extending them would require defining continuum versions of DA and EADA, partial consent, and a measure of blocking. In particular, a measure of blocking pairs does not preserve the paper’s exact set-inclusion criterion; choosing one would change the object being compared. The narrower \(I^*\) question remains a fresh optimization problem, not a result whose computational landscape this paper charts.

There is a plausible domain for such work: large cohorts with repeated preference and priority profiles. That keeps the negative case from being a claim that school choice has no meaningful high-multiplicity regime. The case is weaker there: a researcher could reasonably pursue the continuous allocation problem on its own merits. What the paper cannot support is the stronger claim that doing so would be a worthwhile *mirror of this paper’s computational contribution*. There is no such contribution here to mirror.
