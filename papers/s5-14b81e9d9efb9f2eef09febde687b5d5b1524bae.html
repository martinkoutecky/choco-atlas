The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow population-limit version of the paper’s Fractional Ballot evaluation. It is plausible as a computational question, but it is not anchored to a named computational result: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Its numbered equations define the methods; Sections 6–7 report simulation findings. In particular, §7.2’s Fractional Ballot comparisons are not a qualifying anchor. So I cannot honestly supply an anchor-backed mirror under your rules.

A candidate question, if a prospective mirror is useful, is Continuous Fractional-Ballot Evaluation. An instance gives \(K\) rational candidate positions \(x_k\in[0,1]^2\), a rational temperature \(\sigma>0\), and \(\tau\) rational voter ideal points \(v_t\in[0,1]^2\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to one. A type is a complete ideal point; its mass is the population fraction at that point. The regime is a large electorate whose members fall into relatively few questionnaire-response or otherwise identical preference groups: \(n\gg\tau\).

For each type, calculate the paper’s softmax weights
\[ w_{tk}=\frac{\exp(-\|v_t-x_k\|_2/\sigma)} {\sum_j\exp(-\|v_t-x_j\|_2/\sigma)}. \]
The population weights are \(\bar w_k=\sum_t\mu_t w_{tk}\), and the outcome is \(\hat x=\sum_k\bar w_kx_k\). The task is to return additive-\(\varepsilon\) approximations to that outcome and its distance from the population geometric median \(q_\mu\), which minimizes \(\sum_t\mu_t\|v_t-p\|_2\). The expected direction is tractability for approximation: the weights are explicit finite sums, and the geometric median is a fixed-dimensional convex optimization problem. Further questions include error bounds for replacing the population by a finite sample, and whether conclusions comparing electoral systems remain stable under that replacement.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as an exact-population extension of their scenario simulator: their scenarios are parameterized by voter distributions, but their reported runs use finitely many sampled voters. That makes this distinct from merely making the outcome fractional. It also means the proposal is modest: it formalizes evaluation of one mechanism, rather than mirroring a hardness or algorithmic result of the paper.

My weakest point is decisive for the requested grading: without an eligible named result, this cannot satisfy the required anchor-to-question link. And because the paper already specifies distributional scenarios and studies Monte Carlo stability, its authors might regard exact-population evaluation as an implementation refinement rather than a new computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is at the level of what this paper contributes. It does not state a computational problem and prove a complexity or algorithmic result about it. Its findings are simulation comparisons from finite voter samples, and its main contribution is a framework for running those simulations. The proponent is right to concede that they have no qualifying anchor: there is no named result here whose high-multiplicity version could be the mirror.

Their proposed Fractional-Ballot evaluation is a sensible extension, but it looks like routine aggregation rather than a new computational question. Given \(\tau\) types, calculate each type’s \(K\) softmax weights, take their mass-weighted sums, and approximate the geometric median in fixed dimension. That is roughly \(O(\tau K)\) weight calculations plus a small convex optimization. The paper’s Monte Carlo section already motivates replacing samples with a population calculation; doing so would improve the simulator, but it would not mirror a complexity result in the paper.

A stronger candidate would replace the finite sample with the exact Gaussian-mixture population used to generate the scenarios, then compute each rule’s outcome and compare it with the population geometric median. That is a real extension, but its central work is integration over a two-dimensional distribution. Or one could aggregate voters by ballot type and run the rules on masses; the rules mostly use direct sums and familiar finite procedures. Either could become useful research, especially if the input model or dimension were expanded. But that would be a new computational project prompted by the simulator, not a continuization of a computational result the authors establish.

I would not object that high multiplicity is implausible here: large electorates grouped by identical questionnaire responses are a natural regime. Nor would I object that the fractional outcome is already continuous; the population in the paper is still sampled finitely. The negative case is narrower: the paper offers no computational result for ChoCo to mirror, and its clearest exact-population extension appears to be a tractable refinement of evaluation.

That case is strongest under the programme’s stated notion of a mirror. It is weaker if “mirror” includes any new computational question suggested by a paper’s software or experiments. Exact aggregation over richer distributions might be worthwhile on its own; I cannot rule that out universally.
