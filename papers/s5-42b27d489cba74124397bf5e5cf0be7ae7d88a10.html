The continuous mirror question

For \(C\) with \(|C|=4\), let \(T\) be its 24 strict rankings and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) be a society distribution. Can a synthesis procedure produce a margin-invariant rule \(F:\Delta(T)\to 2^C\setminus\{\emptyset\}\) that selects the Condorcet winner uniquely, excludes every Condorcet loser, is neutral, and satisfies strict positive involvement: whenever \(x\in F(\mu)\), \(F((\mu+\lambda\nu)/(1+\lambda))=\{x\}\) for every \(\lambda>0\) and every distribution \(\nu\) supported on rankings with \(x\) uniquely first?

The model it lives in

Use the 24 strict rankings as types, \(\mu\) as their normalized masses, and \(m_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sigma_t(x,y)\) as the margins. Synthesize exclusion predicates over these margins, then select the leximin-maximal candidates among those not excluded.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest result is Theorem 13, proved here: for four candidates, there exists a margin-invariant voting method satisfying the Condorcet winner and loser criteria, strict positive involvement (and therefore positive involvement and resolvability), and neutrality. Theorem 2 is a cited impossibility for five or more candidates; Theorem 6 is a cited impossibility when ordinal margin invariance is added. These are axiomatic possibility and impossibility results, not complexity results. Theorem 10 is cited quantifier elimination, while Lemmas 11–12 support the construction.

There is a plausible population model nearby. In a large four-candidate election, take the 24 strict rankings as types and let \(\mu\) give each type’s population share. The margins are \(m_\mu(x,y)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\sigma_t(x,y)\), and the decision is a nonempty set of winning candidates. A precise existence question would ask for a neutral, margin-invariant rule on all such distributions that satisfies the two Condorcet criteria and strict positive involvement when positive mass of voters ranking \(x\) first is added.

That is the strongest positive lead, but it fails ChoCo’s gate: it is an axiomatic existence question, not a computational one. Moreover, the paper’s construction already works over real-valued margin vectors, and Lemma 11 characterizes transitions generated by real-valued \(x\)-first profiles. So this continuous formulation appears to be covered by the paper’s construction rather than opening a new complexity landscape. The plausible large-electorate regime and the real-mass mathematics do not supply a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive: there is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 13 establishes the existence of a voting method with specified axioms; Theorems 2 and 6 are axiomatic impossibility results. Theorem 10 and Lemmas 11–12 support the construction, but none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper uses computation to synthesize and verify a rule; that does not make its main result a complexity result.

The proposed society of 24 ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity model. But its natural question—whether a rule on population distributions satisfies the Condorcet criteria and involvement axioms—is still axiomatic, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Nor does changing voters into masses add a new mathematical domain to this particular construction: the paper defines its exclusion predicates over real margin vectors, and Lemma 11 characterizes transitions from adding arbitrary real-valued mass of voters who rank a candidate first. The method is margin-invariant, so a distribution over rankings matters only through its six margins. The proposed continuous extension is therefore already present in the construction’s real-valued machinery, while its existence claim remains axiomatic.

A stronger computational proposal would be to ask for the cost of changing a society’s distribution until a target candidate wins under this rule. That is a possible new problem, but it does not mirror Theorem 13. For the paper’s fixed four-candidate rule, there are only 24 ranking types, and the reported exclusion formulas define a finite union of linear regions in six margins. Such a fixed-size intervention model looks like a finite collection of linear optimization problems, not a new complexity landscape established by this paper. Extending the question to variable candidate sets would require a broader rule family the paper does not provide; indeed, its cited impossibility result rules out the same axioms from five candidates onward.

So neither Theorem 13 nor the cited impossibilities yield a qualifying continuous computational mirror. The negative case is weaker only if “mirror” is broadened to include new computational tasks attached to the synthesized rule; those may be worth studying, but they are not computational results of this paper awaiting continuization.
