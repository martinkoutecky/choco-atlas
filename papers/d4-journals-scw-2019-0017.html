The continuous mirror question

Given two fixed families represented by finite supports of valuation types, with rational masses and normalized nonatomic valuations encoded by rational piecewise-constant densities on a common partition of \(C=[0,1]\), find a cut \(x\in[0,1]\) and assign the two resulting intervals to the families. For each family \(j\), require that the mass of its types \(t\) satisfying \(v_t(X_j)\ge 1/2\) be at least \(\rho_j/2\), where \(\rho_j\) is that family’s total mass.

The model it lives in

Each type is a family label and a complete valuation \(v_t\), with population mass \(\mu_{j,t}\). Choose a cut \(x\) and an assignment of \([0,x]\) and \((x,1]\); the objective is feasibility of the condition that at least half the mass within each family values its assigned interval at least \(1/2\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s connected \(1/2\)-democratic fair-share guarantee for two equally entitled families. It leaves the paper’s other fairness criteria, multi-family bounds, unequal entitlements, envy-freeness, and Pareto-efficiency results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: Two-Family Mass-Democratic Fair-Share, my lead mirror. The cake remains \(C=[0,1]\), and there are two fixed families with equal entitlements. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_{j,t}\) for family \(j\)’s valuation types \(t\), with \(\sum_{j,t}\mu_{j,t}=1\). A type includes the family and a complete, normalized, nonatomic additive valuation \(v_t\). Write \(\rho_j=\sum_t\mu_{j,t}\). To make this a finite computational input, represent each valuation by a piecewise-constant rational density over a common partition of the cake.

The question is: find a cut \(x\) and assign \([0,x]\) and \((x,1]\) to different families so that, for each family \(j\),
\[ \sum_{t:\,v_t(X_j)\ge 1/2}\mu_{j,t}\ \ge\ \rho_j/2. \]
Here \(X_j\) is the interval assigned to family \(j\). A solution is the cut and assignment; the condition says that at least half of each family’s population values its family’s share at least \(1/2\). The mass is population share, while every individual still values the whole piece their family receives.

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: with two equally entitled families, a connected democratic fair-share division exists and can be found by an efficient algorithm. The proof uses each agent’s half-value cutpoint and the median of those cutpoints within each family. [The paper’s Theorem 6](https://econ.biu.ac.il/sites/econ/files/shared/efrat/fair_cake-cutting.pdf) is the closest named computational result, though its “efficient algorithm” is in the cake-cutting protocol model, not a stated Turing-machine bound for binary-encoded inputs.

The mirror has a direct Class A algorithm for the finite encoding above. Compute each type’s half-value cutpoint, then take a mass-weighted median of those cutpoints for each family. Give the left interval to the family with the lower median, the right interval to the other, and cut anywhere between the two medians. The weighted-median guarantees give each family at least half its mass on the satisfied side. This takes time polynomial in the number of supported types, the partition size, and the rational input length, independent of the number of citizens. A plausible regime is two states dividing shared land: each state has many residents, but residents may fall into a relatively small number of shared land-valuation profiles. The type assumption is substantive, but it is exactly the high-multiplicity assumption that identical types are interchangeable.

This is a population mirror, not a claim that continuous cake is new: the paper already has a continuous outcome, while its population is a finite list of agents. Here the population becomes a distribution over complete types, and the democratic condition is measured by mass. The weakest point is the computational bridge: Theorem 6 does not itself analyze this finite type-mass encoding or establish bit complexity. The proposed encoding and weighted-median algorithm make that question precise, but the paper supplies an algorithmic lead rather than a hardness result or a ready-made complexity theorem. Further questions include how the type-support size affects fair division when there are more than two families, and whether comparable guarantees hold with unequal entitlements.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong negative case against this anchor. Theorem 6 is an explicit algorithmic result, and its population axis is real: residents within each of two fixed groups can share valuation types while the groups themselves remain fixed. In a land division between two states, for example, the question “what fraction of each state’s population considers its state’s share fair?” is sensible at mass scale. Continuizing the cake alone would miss that question; the proposed mirror continuizes the residents.

The strongest reservation is that this particular mirror appears to collapse immediately to weighted order statistics. With piecewise-constant rational valuations, compute each supported type’s half-value cutpoint, take a weighted median within each family, and cut between the two medians. That is a direct high-multiplicity counterpart to the paper’s proof. It makes this a modest computational result, with little need for the continuous-optimization machinery ChoCo hopes to bring into ComSoC.

But under the programme’s stated standard, that reservation does not defeat the mirror: a well-posed question survives even if its answer is simple, and a direct algorithm is still a computational result. Nor can I rule out more varied population scenarios by saying that valuation types may proliferate; the state-population example gives a plausible regime with many residents per type. So the case against this anchor is weak. I do not see an honest basis for claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
