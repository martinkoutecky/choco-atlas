The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Theorem 1 compares equilibrium welfare under two voting rules; Theorem 2 characterizes strategies in interior equilibria; Proposition 4 constructs an asymmetric equilibrium. The paper gives no algorithmic problem or complexity claim to continuize. Turning any of these into “compute an equilibrium” would introduce a new task rather than mirror a named result.

There is a natural high-multiplicity setting: a large election with many voters sharing one of two signal types, alongside the passive minority. So the objection is not that repeated types are implausible. The problem is that the paper’s strategic incentives come from finite-election pivot probabilities and ties. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot does not change the vote shares or winner. Every ballot is then a best response to the unchanged outcome, and the equilibrium predictions lose their force.

That defeats the three anchors at their shared foundation. The welfare comparison in Theorem 1 no longer selects an equilibrium outcome; aggregate behavior can produce different welfare under either rule. Theorem 2’s mixed strategies rely on voters being indifferent between ballots because of pivotal events, which disappear in the atomless game. Proposition 4’s division between single voters and double voters likewise depends on finite counts and individual pivotality. Splitting a continuum into masses with different ballot strategies does not restore those incentives: infinitesimal members of each mass still cannot affect the outcome.

One could preserve strategic influence by keeping a finite electorate, modeling coalitions that move positive mass, or retaining a large-\(n\) sequence with its pivotality scale and tie probabilities. Each choice changes the object. Finite electorates with compressed type counts give a plausible high-multiplicity computation problem, but not a continuous population. Coalition deviations make cohorts strategic players rather than individual voters. A limit that retains finite-size pivotal effects needs information beyond the limiting type distribution. The paper’s cited large-election analyses are relevant analytic work, but they do not supply the missing computational question.

So the negative case is strongest against a *continuous computational mirror of these results*: the paper has no computational anchor, and the natural atomless mirror removes the mechanism that makes its equilibrium claims interesting. The vulnerable point is that computing equilibria for finite high-multiplicity versions could still be worthwhile. That would be a new computational project, however, rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 1, Theorem 2, or Proposition 4.
