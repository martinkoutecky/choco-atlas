The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s large-legislature interpretation. It explicitly connects qualified-majority rules to two pivotal legislators selected by ideological cutoffs. A plausible high-multiplicity regime is therefore a legislature with many members but relatively few complete preference types, such as types \(t=(w_t,e_t)\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of legislators of each type. That is a credible continuous-population extension; the paper’s distribution \(F\) over shocks is uncertainty, not the population being continuized.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Lemma 1 proves equilibrium existence and shows that equilibrium behavior can be represented by a one-period game with continuation-payoff parameters. Propositions 4 and 5 give conditions for equilibria using the inefficient instrument and for welfare comparisons. These are game-theoretic characterizations, not results that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. They are proved here, but none qualifies as a computational anchor.

So I have no valid anchor and no per-anchor continuous problem or A/B/C expectation to assign. One could formulate a new problem about computing equilibria in a legislature whose preferences have distribution \(\mu\), but moving from the paper’s two pivotal legislators to a mass of strategic legislators requires specifying a new collective decision mechanism. That would be a potentially recognizable extension, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. My weakest point is also the decisive one: the paper makes high multiplicity plausible, but supplies no computational result for the continuization programme to build on.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s large-legislature foothold is plausible: under the paper’s qualified-majority interpretation, a distribution over legislators’ ideological parameters can determine the two pivotal cutoffs. But that population is not what the paper’s game analyzes. Its strategic players are those two veto players; the shock distribution \(F\) describes uncertainty over their shared state of nature across time, not the composition of a society. Replacing the legislature’s list of members with a mass distribution would re-encode the cutoffs. Making a continuum of members themselves bargain would require a new decision protocol, since the paper’s individual veto players have pivotal power.

The named results the proponent mentions do not supply computational anchors. Lemma 1 establishes equilibrium existence and reduces equilibrium behavior to a one-period game with continuation-payoff parameters. Proposition 4 gives conditions for inefficient-instrument equilibria; Proposition 5 characterizes when adding that instrument improves welfare. None defines an algorithmic problem or establishes its complexity. Asking whether an equilibrium has a property, or whether the welfare conditions hold, could become a computational task after specifying a finite input representation for the shock law, recognition probabilities, and legislative types. That would be a newly formulated task. The paper allows general continuous shock distributions and does not give those inputs a computational encoding.

So the strongest negative case is that no result in this paper is eligible for the required computational mirror. The paper does make a high-multiplicity legislative regime imaginable, but that regime alone cannot turn its equilibrium and welfare characterizations into computational results to mirror.

I cannot honestly support the broader claim that no continuous model inspired by this paper could ever merit study. A computational analysis of equilibria in a specified large legislature could be worthwhile on its own. The negative case is strong on mirror eligibility and weaker on universal worthlessness.
