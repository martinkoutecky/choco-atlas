What the mirror covers

The proposal covers Proposition 1’s sharp welfare bound and its midpoint calculation; it leaves the Bayesian, maximin, and minimax-regret conclusions as policy analysis rather than separate computational mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror to. Its sole numbered result is Proposition 1, proved here. It says that, for a population \(J\) with utilities in \([0,1]\), no ties, compulsory utility-maximizing votes, and knowledge of the joint distribution of \(u(A)\) and \(1[u(B)>u(A)]\), the sharp bounds are
\[ E[u(A)\mid u(B)>u(A)]P[u(B)>u(A)] < E[u(B)] < P[u(B)>u(A)] + E[u(A)\mid u(A)>u(B)]P[u(A)>u(B)]. \]
That is a statistical identification result, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class.

The best positive case is therefore a prospective computational problem derived from Proposition 1, not a qualifying computational anchor from the paper. Call it *Finite-Support Sharp Welfare-Bound Computation*. An instance is a finite set of types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, rational status-quo utilities \(a_t\in[0,1]\), and a label \(v_t\in\{A,B\}\) recording which policy type \(t\) prefers. The unknown proposed-policy utility \(b_t\in[0,1]\) must satisfy \(b_t>a_t\) for \(v_t=B\) and \(b_t<a_t\) for \(v_t=A\). The question is to return the exact infimum and supremum of \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t\) over all such assignments, including whether either endpoint is attainable. The solution is the sharp welfare interval; it can be computed directly from the type masses and utilities in polynomial time. These bounds also determine the paper’s midpoint-based Bayesian and minimax-regret choices.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large health-policy evaluation: patients fall into a modest number of standardized health-state and policy-preference groups, each with many members. Here a type is one such group, and mass is its fraction of the patient population. The paper itself says the joint distribution needed for Proposition 1 could be learned by observing status-quo outcomes and voting behavior together. An author could recognize the finite-support version as a computational implementation of that analysis.

But this is a weak positive case. The paper already models its population as a probability space and reasons directly about utility distributions; population continuization is already present. The proposed finite-support problem is a tractable restatement of the paper’s bound calculation, not a continuous mirror of a discrete hardness result. There is also a type-granularity concern: the paper leaves proposed-policy utilities unobserved, so grouping patients by observed utility and vote may not make them identical in every problem-relevant respect.

So the most honest conclusion is: Proposition 1 offers a clear, plausible finite-support computational companion, but it is not a named computational anchor, and this paper supplies no discrete hardness result whose fate in a continuum could be studied. The candidate covers only the sharp-bound analysis of Proposition 1; it does not establish a continuous mirror for the paper’s broader policy-planning discussion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no discrete computational result here to continuize. Proposition 1 is a statistical identification theorem, and the paper already states it for a population that is a probability space with an arbitrary utility distribution. A finite electorate is just an atomic special case of that model. So the proposed mirror starts from a population continuum the paper already has.

Even granting the proponent’s best computational formulation, finite-support sharp-bound computation has no unresolved algorithmic content. Given rational masses \(\mu_t\), status-quo utilities \(a_t\), and the vote label for each type, the lower and upper bounds are weighted sums over the two vote groups:
\[ \sum_{t:\,v_t=B}\mu_t a_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t:\,v_t=B}\mu_t+\sum_{t:\,v_t=A}\mu_t a_t. \]
Their endpoint attainability follows from the same strict inequalities. These quantities, and the paper’s midpoint-based decision threshold, can be computed by direct arithmetic in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length. The paper has already derived the formulas in the continuous-population model. Turning their evaluation into an algorithm would repackage that result, not open a complexity question.

A large health-policy population with many patients in standardized groups is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s probability-space model fits it naturally. That supports the relevance of the analysis, but it does not supply a missing computational problem: the relevant inputs are already the distribution of status-quo utility and the fraction preferring the proposal. If instead one adds an implicit or infinite representation of that distribution, the computational challenge would depend on the new representation or uncertainty assumptions, not on continuizing the paper’s model.

The universal negative is hard to prove: one could invent richer robust-planning problems nearby. But those would be new problems. For this paper’s stated analysis, the best proposed mirror is already continuous and already solved by a closed-form bound; there is no worthwhile continuous computational mirror left to chart.
