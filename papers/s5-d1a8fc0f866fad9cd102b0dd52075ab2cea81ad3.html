The case FOR (proponent)

Proposition 8 is my lead anchor. The paper gives no numbered hardness result for Fisher equilibrium, so I would not manufacture a hardness anchor from its prose about computation being challenging.

Proposition 8 (proved in this paper) says: for a finitely generated invariant domain \(D=\{\Á_1,\ldots,\Á_q\}_{\mathrm{complete}}\), suppose an algorithm finds an \(\epsilon\)-equilibrium price for a one-agent Fisher market with preferences in \(D\) in at most \(T\) operations. Then an \(\epsilon\)-equilibrium price for any \(m\)-agent market with preferences in \(D\) can be found in \(O(mq+T)\) operations. The paper’s proof aggregates the agents’ preference parameters using their relative budgets, then calls the one-agent algorithm.

The continuous mirror I would put to a prover is Finite-Generated Continuous Fisher Equilibrium. Fix \(n\) goods and \(q\) elementary homothetic preferences with expenditure functions \(E_1,\ldots,E_q\). A preference type is \(\theta\in\Delta^{q-1}\), with
\[ \ln E_\theta(p)=\sum_{\ell=1}^q\theta_\ell\ln E_\ell(p). \]
A full consumer type is \((\theta,b)\), including its per-capita budget \(b>0\). The input society is a finite-support distribution
\[ \mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\lambda_j\delta_{(\theta^j,b_j)}, \qquad \sum_j\lambda_j=1, \]
with binary rational masses, parameters, budgets, and a supply vector \(X\). Here \(\lambda_j\) is the fraction of society of type \(j\), and \(N\gg\tau\) is the intended regime: many consumers grouped into relatively few preference-and-budget classes.

The problem is to find positive prices \(p\) and bundles \(x_j\in D_{\theta^j}(p,b_j)\) for the types such that the market clears:
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\lambda_jx_j=X. \]
For the algorithmic version anchored by Proposition 8, given \(\epsilon>0\), a solution is a price vector and demand choices satisfying
\[ \sum_{i=1}^n p_i\left|X_i-\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\lambda_jx_{ji}\right|\le\epsilon B, \qquad B=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\lambda_jb_j. \]
Prices can be normalized to \(\sum_i p_i=1\). This is a market-clearing feasibility problem; the solution variables are prices and type-level demand bundles, and the \(\epsilon\) inequality measures the allowed excess supply or demand.

The expected answer is Class A, conditional on the one-agent problem. The budget-weighted mean parameter is
\[ \bar\theta_\ell=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\lambda_jb_j\theta^j_\ell. \]
The aggregate consumer has budget \(B\) and preference \(E_{\bar\theta}\). Thus the continuous society reduces to one consumer after \(O(\tau q)\) arithmetic operations, giving the mirror of Proposition 8’s bound, \(O(\tau q+T)\). For a non-atomic society, the same formula uses \(\int b\theta_\ell\,d\mu\); the distribution matters to equilibrium through these \(q\) budget-weighted moments. A bit-complexity bound still depends on how the elementary preferences and the one-agent algorithm are encoded.

This is a plausible problem for the authors: it is their Fisher-market equilibrium question with population demand expressed as an integral, and the continuous-agent interpretation fits their population aggregation framework. A scenario would be a large divisible-advertising market where millions of advertisers use a finite generated bidding language and many share the same preference parameters and budgets. The model’s type includes both preference and budget, as required for high multiplicity. If the rational masses are multiplied by a common denominator, the instance becomes a finite market with repeated agents; conversely, a finite market gives its empirical type distribution. That is a direct high-multiplicity dictionary.

The mirror covers the algorithmic Fisher-market discussion in §4.3 and Proposition 8; it does not claim to continue the paper’s welfare, identification, or indecomposability results. It generates questions about unconditional one-agent complexity, exact versus approximate equilibrium and bit complexity, and what happens outside finitely generated invariant domains.

My weakest point is that the continuum step is almost already present: the paper develops continuous aggregation in Theorem 2, and its aggregation formula makes this equilibrium extension straightforward. So I would argue that the mirror is well-posed and faithful, while conceding that it may yield an immediate Class A result rather than a substantial new complexity gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 8 is a genuine algorithmic result, so “there is no computational theorem to mirror” is not a usable objection. The large-market scenario is plausible too: preferences and budgets define complete types, and the input can be a rational-mass distribution over a small support.

The strongest negative case is that the proposed price problem is already the paper’s own continuous-population model combined with its algorithmic reduction. The paper explicitly allows a continuum of consumers, gives the budget-weighted aggregation formula, and characterizes Fisher equilibrium prices by a convex optimization problem. For the proposed types,
\[ \ln E_\theta(p)=\sum_{\ell=1}^q\theta_\ell\ln E_\ell(p), \qquad \bar\theta_\ell= \frac{\sum_j\lambda_j b_j\theta^j_\ell}{\sum_j\lambda_jb_j}. \]
Thus the society’s aggregate preference is exactly \(E_{\bar\theta}\). For finite support, computing \(\bar\theta\) costs \(O(\tau q)\); the remaining price computation is the same one-agent problem with running time \(T\). For a non-atomic society, the same calculation uses integrals and depends only on the corresponding budget-weighted moments. This is precisely the reduction Proposition 8 establishes with \(m\) consumers. The paper’s continuous aggregation results supply the mass formulation; the proposed mirror adds no new population-dependent complexity question.

An arbitrary non-atomic distribution would not rescue the mirror: it needs a finite encoding or an oracle model before it defines a computational problem. In the finitely generated setting, any such representation still feeds the same aggregate moments into the one-agent problem. Adding explicit type-level bundles is a stronger output than Proposition 8’s price result. The paper already formulates the allocation side through the Eisenberg–Gale program, but its stated \(O(mq+T)\) bound does not by itself establish that bundle-recovery bound.

So the case against this *specific price anchor* is a collision with the paper’s own continuous model and reduction, not a complaint about high multiplicity or about the answer being easy. The negative case is still limited: if the intended mirror requires explicit type-level allocations or a different, non-finitely-generated domain, that may support a worthwhile computational question, and the paper does not settle its complexity.
