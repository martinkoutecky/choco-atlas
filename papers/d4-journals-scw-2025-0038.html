The continuous mirror question

Given a binary agenda of \(r\) issues, a finite support \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^r\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a candidate slate \(C\) with preference vectors \(a_c\in\{0,1\}^r\), and an odd committee size \(k\le |C|\), assume no issue is tied in the society: \(\sum_{t:t_i=1}\mu_t\ne 1/2\) for every issue \(i\). Define \(b_i=1\) when \(\sum_{t:t_i=1}\mu_t>1/2\), and \(b_i=0\) otherwise. Find a set \(D\subseteq C\) of exactly \(k\) candidates maximizing \(\sum_{i=1}^r \mathbf{1}[\mathbf{1}[\sum_{c\in D}a_{c,i}>k/2]=b_i]\).

The model it lives in

Voter types are complete binary preference bundles over the agenda, and \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population share. The decision variable is a size-\(k\) committee from the fixed candidate slate; the objective counts issues where its majority vote matches the majority induced by \(\mu\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2’s full-information representative-selection problem; it leaves the election-rule comparisons, empirical results, and delegation analyses unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which restrictions on the agenda or candidate preference vectors make the problem tractable?
  • What approximation or parameterized guarantees are possible?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous-population version of the paper’s full-information representative-selection problem. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: Max \(k\)-Majority Agreement is NP-hard. In that problem, there are \(r\) binary issues and a slate of candidates, each with a binary preference on every issue. Given an odd committee size \(k\), the task is to choose exactly \(k\) candidates to maximize the number of issues on which the committee majority prefers \(1\), with \(1\) taken to be the voter majority’s preference. The paper proves hardness by reduction from its Theorem 1, Max \(k\)-Coverage.

Call the mirror Continuous-Population Max \(k\)-Majority Agreement. An instance has a fixed agenda of \(r\) binary issues; a finite support \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^r\) of voter preference types; positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); an explicit candidate slate \(C\), with each candidate \(c\) having a vector \(a_c\in\{0,1\}^r\); and an odd \(k\le |C|\). Type \(t\) records a voter’s complete preference bundle across the agenda, and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the electorate. Assume no issue is tied. Let \(b_i=1\) when more than half the society has type preference \(1\) on issue \(i\), and \(b_i=0\) otherwise.

The decision variable is a set \(D\subseteq C\) of exactly \(k\) representatives. For each issue, their majority outcome is \(M_D(i)=1\) if \(\sum_{c\in D}a_{c,i}>k/2\), and \(0\) otherwise. The task is to return a committee maximizing \(\sum_{i=1}^r \mathbf{1}[M_D(i)=b_i]\). The input gives the \(\tau=|T|\) supported types and their masses as binary rationals. Its natural complexity parameters are \(r\), \(|C|\), \(k\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a very large electorate can consist of recurring bundles of views on a fixed agenda, with many citizens per type and \(\tau\ll n\). The authors should recognize the question: their Theorem 2 already asks how to select representatives to match the direct-democratic majority across binary issues. The mirror supplies that majority from a distribution over complete voter types.

I expect hardness to transfer. For any Theorem 2 instance, take a society with mass \(3/5\) on type \(1^r\) and \(2/5\) on type \(0^r\). Its strict majority prefers \(1\) on every issue, so the continuous problem’s objective is exactly the paper’s objective. Thus the mirror is NP-hard even with only two voter types. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the agenda and the choice of representatives, not in the number of individual voters. That makes this a clear Class B boundary case: high multiplicity gives a natural continuous model, but does not make this hardness disappear.

This case covers the paper’s full-information problem of choosing representatives to maximize agreement with the voter majority. It does not claim to mirror the empirical comparisons of election rules or the later delegation analyses. Further questions include whether the problem is tractable under restrictions on the agenda or candidate preference vectors, and what approximation or parameterized guarantees hold.

The weakest point is that, for this particular objective, the distribution affects the answer only through the issue-by-issue majority vector \(b\). The mass values and the rest of the type structure are discarded once those majorities are computed. That limits the mirror’s evidence for a *mass-sensitive* continuous theory. But the population itself remains a meaningful high-multiplicity object, and the resulting computational question is recognizably the authors’ representative-selection problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is about its research yield, not its validity. In the proposed version, the population distribution \(\mu\) affects the optimization only through the issue-majority vector \(b\). Computing \(b\) from the \(\tau\) supported types is straightforward; after that, the problem is exactly the paper’s finite committee-selection problem. A two-type society already transfers the hardness, so this would establish a Class B case, but it would not expose computational structure that depends on the population distribution or use the continuous-optimization tools that motivate ChoCo.

A more mass-sensitive objective, such as weighting each issue by the electorate’s majority margin, might address that limitation. But it changes the paper’s objective, which counts matching issues equally. That is a reason to rate this mirror as a modest contribution, not a reason to dismiss it: the authors could reasonably recognize the faithful version as their full-information representative-selection problem, with the majority benchmark derived from a continuous society. The finite agenda and candidate slate do not disqualify it under the programme’s stated scope.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. Its input has a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation: for example, many citizens could share one of two complete patterns of views across the agenda. Existing high-multiplicity election work supports treating repeated voter types as natural, while the nearby work on weighted representative democracy appears to study large-electorate agreement analytically rather than the computational complexity of selecting a committee ([Fitzsimmons and Hemaspaandra](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.08927), [Pivato and Soh](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S030440682030032X)). Those facts weaken a claim that this is a rich or novel continuous-computational case; they do not make the mirror unworthy. The negative case is weak, and theorem 2 survives it.
