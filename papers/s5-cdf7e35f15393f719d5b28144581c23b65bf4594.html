What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror addresses only Theorem 2’s indistinguishability bound; it leaves the performance guarantees in Theorems 1 and 3 and the strategic results in Theorems 4 and 5 aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\varepsilon>0\), every population size \(n>0\), every voting rule \(r\), and either utility model \(v_N\) or \(v_C\), there is a quality-dominant environment \(I\) with \(\mathcal P_I(r,n)<\tfrac12+\varepsilon\). This is a named worst-case performance impossibility, though it is not a standard complexity result such as NP-hardness or a polynomial-time algorithm. The paper gives no such complexity classification.

A plausible regime is municipal participatory budgeting with many residents and a fixed set of projects. Let a voter type be an approval ballot \(S\subseteq A\), and let \(\mu_S\) be the fraction of residents with that ballot. For fixed \(m=|A|\), there are at most \(2^m\) types, so a large electorate can have \(n\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|\{S:\mu_S>0\}|\). Mass is the share of residents casting each ballot; the decision remains an indivisible, budget-feasible project set. This is continuization of the population, not of the projects.

Here is the precise question I would hand to a prover: Continuous Truth-Revealing PB Rule Synthesis. An instance consists of a finite project set \(A\), rational costs \(c_j>0\), a rational budget \(B\), a utility model \(v\in\{v_N,v_C\}\), a rational target \(\rho\), and a finite family \(\Omega\) of possible quality-and-signal scenarios. In scenario \(\omega\), the ground-truth qualities are \(L^\omega\), and the binary signal probabilities are \(Q_j^{L^\omega_j}\). The continuum society has ballot-type masses
\[ \mu_\omega(S)=\prod_{j\in S}Q_j^{L^\omega_j}\prod_{j\notin S}(1-Q_j^{L^\omega_j}). \]
A rule \(F\) sees \((\mu,c,B)\), but not which scenario generated \(\mu\), and returns a feasible set \(W\). Its guarantee is
\[ \rho(F)=\min_{\omega\in\Omega} \frac{v_{L^\omega}(F(\mu_\omega,c,B))} {\max_{W:\,c(W)\le B}v_{L^\omega}(W)}. \]
Given the instance, determine whether some such rule has \(\rho(F)\ge\rho\), and return its choices on the observed mass profiles if one exists. I expect an indistinguishability ceiling: when different truths generate the same \((\mu,c,B)\) but have different optimal sets, no rule can be truth-revealing in both scenarios. Theorem 2’s construction is the natural starting point for proving a bound near \(\tfrac12\) in this continuum version. It also invites follow-up questions about the best guarantee under bounded cost ratios and how rule-synthesis complexity depends on \(m\) and \(\tau\).

I would present this as a narrow, qualified mirror of Theorem 2, not as a complexity result the authors already establish. Its strength is that the type and mass have a natural PB interpretation, and the theorem’s indistinguishability argument survives replacement of sampled ballots by exact population masses. Its weakness is substantial: the paper already studies the \(n\to\infty\) limit, so this may largely re-express its asymptotic analysis. Moreover, asking for a synthesized rule extends the paper’s evaluation of specified rules. The result is an information-based performance ceiling, not evidence that discrete computational hardness dissolves—or persists—in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that this paper offers no native computational problem for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 2 is a named impossibility about worst-case welfare, but the paper’s results concern information aggregation and equilibrium, not the complexity of an algorithmic task. Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor available, and the proponent’s many-resident, fixed-project-set scenario is plausible: approval ballots are natural types, and projects can remain indivisible. The objection is not that such a population cannot be modeled.

The proposed rule-synthesis problem, though, gets its substance from an extra robust-decision formulation. For a finite scenario list \(\Omega\), group scenarios that induce the same mass profile \(\mu\). A rule that sees only \((\mu,c,B)\) must choose one outcome for each group; choices for different groups are independent. So the alleged synthesis is a table of feasible project sets, with a robust subset-selection problem at each table entry. The masses identify which scenarios are indistinguishable, but the proposal gives no representation or efficiency requirement for \(F\). Add one, and the resulting problem is a new restriction-dependent rule-design task, not an algorithmic question supplied by Theorem 2.

That limitation is especially visible here because signals are independent across projects. The exact mass of each approval ballot is determined by the signal probabilities \(p_j\); equivalently, \(\mu\) reveals those marginals. If two scenarios yield the same \(\mu\), they are indistinguishable under the proposed observation model. If they yield different profiles, the exact continuum distinguishes them. The finite-\(n\) paper already studies how sampled approval counts concentrate around those probabilities. Passing to exact masses removes sampling variation; it does not, by itself, create an optimization over a continuous population. Theorem 2’s shared-distribution construction still gives an impossibility, but translating that indistinguishability argument is not a new computational classification.

The most natural alternative anchor is Theorem 1, or its bounded-cost counterpart, Theorem 3. Yet Theorem 1’s central step is precisely concentration as \(n\) grows. With exact masses, approval rates are already their limiting probabilities; under quality dominance, AV’s ordering is immediate. One could ask for the exact performance of other rules on mass profiles, but that is a separate algorithmic question about those rules. The paper does not establish that complexity, and its asymptotic theorem does not supply it.

The strategic results are a worse fit. In an atomless society, one agent has measure zero, so a unilateral deviation cannot change the aggregate ballot distribution or the selected set. Theorem 4’s pivotal incentive comparison therefore disappears. Modeling deviations by a positive mass of agents would instead ask about coordinated manipulation; retaining individual pivotality requires a finite-agent or finite-\(n\) limit model. Neither is a continuous mirror of the paper’s unilateral-equilibrium result.

So I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible, that indivisible projects block continuization, or that a continuous answer matching the discrete limit would be uninteresting. Those objections would be weak. The stronger negative is that the only direct anchor is already an asymptotic information bound, while the proposed “computational” mirror either restates its indistinguishability structure or adds a separate rule-synthesis problem whose continuous population is only a way to label observations. A worthwhile mirror might emerge from a new computational question about positive-mass campaigning or evaluating rules on compressed type distributions. On the evidence here, that would be a new project rather than a mirror of this paper’s results.
