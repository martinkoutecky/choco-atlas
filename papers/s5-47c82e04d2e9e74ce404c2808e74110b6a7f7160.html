What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror restates Theorems 2 and 3 as population non-rationalizability questions; the estimation procedure and empirical model-fit results have no named computational guarantee to anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind ChoCo requires as an anchor. The closest results are Theorem 2, proved here, which says that for a single-winner election with \(J\ge3\) candidates and full rankings, IRV is not rationalizable by any IID statistical model; and Theorem 3, also proved here, which says the same for STV-K when \(J\ge3\), \(1\le K\le J-2\), and votes are full rankings. Both proofs use Lemma 1, cited from earlier work. These are results about statistical rationalizability, not about whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise complex. The paper’s proposed estimation procedure does not supply a numbered complexity result either.

There is a natural population analogue of Theorem 2: let types be complete rankings of a fixed candidate set, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). Ask whether there exists a fixed IID model \(p(v\mid s,\theta)\) such that, for every rational population distribution \(\mu\), maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log p(t\mid s,\theta)\) returns the IRV winner as its population parameter \(s\). A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballots and a fixed number of candidates, so the number of voters greatly exceeds the number of distinct ranking types. Theorem 2’s counterexample already uses equal-sized profiles; normalizing their vote counts gives population distributions, and the same contradiction applies to the population likelihood. A corresponding question can be stated for STV-K under Theorem 3’s conditions.

That is a recognizable continuous-population restatement, but it asks whether a voting rule can be represented as an MLE; it does not ask a computational question about manipulating, controlling, or computing outcomes. Its answer is also a non-rationalizability result, outside ChoCo’s tractability/hardness trichotomy. The paper therefore offers a plausible adjacent population question, but no qualifying anchor for a continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The most decisive problem is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 2 says IRV is not MLE-rationalizable under its stated conditions; Theorem 3 says the same for STV-K. Both use Lemma 1’s profile-combination argument. Neither studies an algorithm or makes a complexity claim. Their continuous versions may be sensible questions in statistical social choice, but they do not supply the computational anchor ChoCo is looking for.

The obvious population formulation makes that mismatch clear. Let ballot rankings be types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and ask whether maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\log p(t\mid s,\theta)\) recovers the IRV or STV-K outcome. The paper’s counterexamples carry over by normalizing the profiles: combining two equal-sized profiles corresponds to averaging their distributions. That preserves the rationalizability contradiction, but it does not create a computational task for continuous optimization. The paper already treats ballots as draws from statistical distributions; replacing observed counts with exact masses removes the sampling uncertainty central to its inference and model-fit analysis.

The strongest alternative is to ask for the minimum mass of ballots that must change to alter the IRV winner. High multiplicity is plainly plausible here: the NYC election had over a million ballots and many repeated rankings. But this would add a new intervention objective and a cost for changing ballots. Neither the theorems nor the paper’s analysis defines or studies such perturbations; its focus is explaining voter preferences and assessing statistical fit. A robustness study could be worthwhile, but it is an extrapolation from the paper, not a continuization of one of its results. On the anchors actually proposed, the negative case is strong; against every possible extrapolation, it is necessarily less conclusive.
