What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for Continuum-SBBA Outcome, a mass version of the paper’s single-market, single-unit double auction.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that SBBA is prior-free, individually rational, strongly budget-balanced, and dominant-strategy truthful; its expected market-gain-from-trade is at least \(1-1/k\) times the optimum, where \(k\) is the maximum number of efficient deals. The expectation is over SBBA’s randomization. Strong budget balance means the mechanism leaves no surplus with the auctioneer, so the traders’ market-gain-from-trade equals the total gain from trade.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. An instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over buyer and seller types. A type records its side and its rational valuation: each buyer wants one indivisible unit, and each seller can supply one. Let the total mass of each side be \(1/2\), and let \(\tau\) be the number of supported types. Each type’s mass is a fraction of the population; it represents a continuum of otherwise identical, individually negligible traders. The question is to give a single direct mechanism that, for every reported profile \(\mu\), computes a common price \(p\) and trade rates \(x_t\in[0,1]\). The rates must yield equal buyer and seller trade mass, respect individual rationality, and make all payments at the same price. The mechanism must be prior-free and dominant-strategy truthful for individual traders, and its allocation must maximize gain from trade:
\[ \sum_{t\in T_B}\mu_t b_t x_t-\sum_{t\in T_S}\mu_t s_t x_t. \]
A solution for an instance is the price and type-level trade rates, together with the uniform rule that produces them and meets those conditions on every input. The rates describe what fraction of each type trades; each person still trades a whole unit or does not trade.

This is a natural large-market regime: for example, a regional exchange for standardized one-unit capacity blocks, where many buyers’ willingness-to-pay and sellers’ reserve prices fall into a modest set of contract-value bands. Then the number of traders \(n\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). A continuous mass is meaningful because the question concerns what fraction of each band trades, rather than whether one named trader does.

The problem looks tractable. Sort buyer types by decreasing value and seller types by increasing value, then match the two sides by mass until the next buyer value falls below the next seller value. At the marginal values, choose a price supporting that efficient allocation and trade just the required fraction of any cutoff type. Buyers above the price trade, those below it do not, and the cutoff mass fills the remaining quantity; sellers follow the corresponding rule. Payments at that price balance exactly, and every trade is individually rational. Since one trader has zero mass, a unilateral report cannot change the aggregate profile or the price. Reporting truthfully is therefore optimal: a buyer below the price cannot gain by trading, and a buyer above it cannot gain by reporting below the price; sellers have the symmetric incentives. With rational input, weighted sorting and the cutoff calculation take polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\), for example \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons plus rational arithmetic.

I expect Class A: an exact, efficient allocation in the continuum, with the theorem’s common-price, budget-balance, and individual-incentive concerns preserved. The paper’s \(1-1/k\) guarantee gives a concrete reason to look at this limit: as the number of efficient deals grows, the finite-market loss from reducing a deal becomes small. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s single-market result only. The spatially distributed extension and the paper’s open question about deterministic strongly budget-balanced mechanisms are further directions, not additional numbered anchors here.

The weak point is also the key modeling step: atomlessness makes an individual trader unable to move the price. That removes the pivotal-agent problem that motivates SBBA’s random trade reduction. A referee could see this as the familiar price-taking competitive market, rather than a continuization of the paper’s strategic mechanism. I think the mirror remains recognizable because it keeps the paper’s agents, single-unit trades, common price, individual truthfulness, strong budget balance, and gain-from-trade objective—and asks directly what happens to its large-market guarantee when the population itself is continuous. The answer should be presented as a limit question, not as a claim that the paper’s finite-agent incentive problem has disappeared from every regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 1 is not a computational-complexity result. It proves economic properties of SBBA—truthfulness, individual rationality, strong budget balance, and an expected welfare guarantee—but asks no complexity question. The spatial section’s minimum-cost-flow computation is inherited from earlier work, not presented as a new complexity result. A question about computing an allocation for rational type masses can certainly be posed, but that would add an algorithmic problem to the paper rather than continuize one of its computational results.

The proposed one-market model is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many traders can share a small set of valuation bands, and type-level mass is meaningful. The problem is what happens to the theorem’s central incentive issue in that regime. In the finite mechanism, an individual can change a rank, affect the critical price, and thereby create the incentive problem that the trade-reduction rule and lottery address. In an atomless population, one trader’s report cannot change aggregate masses or the price. The remaining incentive constraint is essentially to trade when one’s value is above the resulting price. That is a natural price-taking market limit, but it no longer studies the strategic design challenge SBBA was built to solve.

A proposed rescue that gives each deviator positive mass would preserve price impact, but it changes the strategic unit from an individual to a block or coalition. That is a different incentive question, rather than the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s individual-trader guarantee. The same issue arises in the spatial extension: aggregating traders into market-and-value capacities gives a natural continuous flow model, but individual deviations again cannot move the price vector. It may be useful market analysis, yet it does not supply the missing computational-complexity result for SBBA.

This is not an airtight case that no worthwhile continuous question can be asked. The paper’s authors might value the atomless limit as a market-design result. The negative case is narrower: under ChoCo’s stated aim of charting computational questions, the paper supplies no named complexity result to mirror, and the most natural proposed mirror removes the pivotal-agent problem that gives its main theorem its distinctive content.
