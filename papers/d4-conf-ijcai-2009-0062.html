What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort question targets Theorem 3's hiding-or-lying characterization; it leaves Theorems 1, 2, and 4 and the paper's examples unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow one: a continuous version of the paper’s strategy-proofness question, with a type cohort acting as the strategic unit. The paper has no named result about P, NP-hardness, or parameterized complexity. Its strongest usable anchor is a named theorem about a mechanism’s strategic behavior.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that the grounded direct mechanism is strategy-proof for agents with focal arguments, when agents may both hide arguments and report arguments outside their type, if and only if the following holds: for every agent \(i\), every alternative report \(S\subseteq A\), and every report profile \(A_{-i}\) of the other agents, if \(i\)’s focal argument \(\hat\alpha_i\) is absent from the grounded extension under truthful reporting, it must also be absent under report \(S\). In full, the condition is
\[ \hat\alpha_i\notin GE(\langle A_i\cup A_{-i},\rightharpoonup\rangle) \ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\alpha_i\notin GE(\langle S\cup A_{-i},\rightharpoonup\rangle). \]
The paper proves this characterization for its individual-agent mechanism; it does not give a complexity classification for deciding the property.

A plausible regime is a large consultation where many affected people are represented through standardized stakeholder blocs. Members of a bloc share the same available arguments and focal argument; the bloc’s representative makes one disclosure decision on their behalf. There might be hundreds of thousands of represented people but only tens or hundreds of such argument-and-interest types. Let \(A\) be the finite argument set, and let each supported type \(t\) specify an available set \(B_t\subseteq A\) and focal argument \(\hat\alpha_t\in B_t\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types. A declaration plan assigns each type’s mass to reports \(S\subseteq A\); the mechanism accepts the grounded extension of the union of arguments in reports with positive mass.

The resulting question is Continuous Focal-Cohort Strategy-Proofness Recognition. Given the argument framework and supported types with their masses, decide whether truthful reporting by every type cohort weakly dominates every alternative report, for every possible set \(D\subseteq A\) declared by the other cohorts. For a cohort \(t\), truth yields \(GE(\langle D\cup B_t,\rightharpoonup\rangle)\); an alternative \(S\) yields \(GE(\langle D\cup S,\rightharpoonup\rangle)\). The answer is “yes” precisely when, for all supported \(t\), all feasible \(D\), and all \(S\subseteq A\),
\[ \hat\alpha_t\notin GE(\langle D\cup B_t,\rightharpoonup\rangle) \ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\alpha_t\notin GE(\langle D\cup S,\rightharpoonup\rangle). \]
A “no” instance has a concrete witness \((t,D,S)\): a cohort, opponents’ declarations, and a report that changes its focal argument from rejected to accepted. If other cohorts have positive mass, every \(D\subseteq A\) is feasible; if \(t\) is the only cohort, only \(D=\varnothing\) is feasible.

I expect this recognition problem to be tractable when parameterized by \(m=|A|\): enumerate supported types, contexts \(D\), and reports \(S\), then compute grounded extensions. This takes \(O(\tau 4^m\operatorname{poly}(m,L))\) time, so it is fixed-parameter tractable in \(m\) and polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\). That is a forecast for the proposed mirror, not a result claimed by the paper. It also opens further questions: can the dependence on \(m\) be improved, and what changes if incentives are tested for coalitions spanning several types?

The weakest point is that the continuous population does little mathematical work here. Grounded semantics depends on whether an argument is present, so the masses’ magnitudes do not affect the outcome once support is fixed. More seriously, a whole cohort coordinating its report is not the paper’s single-agent deviation. The mirror is credible only in a setting where blocs really do make disclosure decisions collectively. That makes this a defensible, limited positive case, rather than evidence that the paper’s individual-agent result has a strong continuous-population counterpart.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor, but it is a characterization of strategy-proofness, not a computational result: it gives no complexity classification or algorithm. The proposed recognition problem—checking the theorem’s condition over types, contexts, and reports—is a new problem derived from the paper. Its proposed \(O(\tau 4^m\operatorname{poly}(m,L))\) algorithm searches finite argument sets; the rational masses do no work.

That is a structural issue with the mirror. The mechanism takes the union of reported arguments, so duplicating a speaker changes nothing: any two distributions with the same positive support produce the same outcome, however different their masses. More seriously, the theorem asks whether a particular agent can change the outcome by deviating. In an atomless population, an individual has measure zero. If the aggregate uses arguments reported by a positive-mass set, an individual deviation cannot change it, and strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. If even a single measure-zero speaker can introduce an argument, the distribution no longer determines the outcome; named speakers and their reports must be retained.

The best rescue is the proponent’s stakeholder-bloc scenario. A bloc with a representative can make a consequential report, and Theorem 3’s kind of question can be asked about those blocs. But then the strategic unit is a coordinated bloc, not an individual in a high-multiplicity population. The mass records how many people the bloc represents, while the mechanism still responds only to whether its arguments appear. Making mass affect acceptance—for example, by weighting support—would change the grounded mechanism at the center of the paper.

So the proposed bloc question may be worthwhile on its own, but it does not give this paper’s individual-agent strategy-proofness result a nontrivial continuous-population mirror. The negative case is strongest on that mismatch; it is less decisive about whether a future paper on collective argumentation could make blocs the intended agents.
