Why no mirror

The proposed distribution over locations preserves the paper’s outlier objective, and the stated mass-coalition incentive notion makes deviations meaningful for a continuum. But Theorem 9 bounds the approximation of truthful mechanisms; it says nothing about the complexity of computing outcomes or mechanisms. Since distortion bounds and impossibility results about rules fail the computational gate, this anchor cannot support a ChoCo mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed model covers only Theorem 9’s utilitarian randomized lower bound for \(n=4\) and \(z=1\); it does not cover the paper’s other bounds or its prediction results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, though it is a mechanism-approximation mirror rather than a standard \(P\)-versus-\(NP\) result. The paper’s strongest anchor for it is Theorem 9, proved here: for \(n=4\) and \(z=1\), no randomized mechanism that is strategyproof in expectation achieves an expected approximation guarantee better than \(3/2\) for the utilitarian objective.

My lead question is whether that lower bound survives for a large society represented by a distribution. Fix outlier mass \(\rho=1/4\). An instance is a finitely supported probability measure \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{x_j}\) on rational locations, with rational masses summing to one. The location \(x_j\) is a voter type’s preferred facility location, and \(\mu_j\) is that type’s fraction of society. Define the cost at facility location \(y\) by
\[ \operatorname{SC}_{1/4}(y;\mu) =\min_{\substack{0\le\nu\le\mu\\ \nu(\mathbb R)=3/4}} \int_{\mathbb R}|y-x|\,d\nu(x), \qquad \operatorname{OPT}_{1/4}(\mu)=\inf_{y\in\mathbb R}\operatorname{SC}_{1/4}(y;\mu). \]
Here \(\nu\) is the mass retained in the objective; all mass can still use the facility.

A solution is a randomized facility mechanism \(\mathcal M\) on these inputs, together with its worst-case expected approximation guarantee. Require mass-coalition strategyproofness: a coalition may move any submass of true types to reported locations, but there must be no deviation under which every member weakly lowers their expected distance to the facility and a positive mass strictly lowers it. The question is to determine the best possible worst-case ratio
\[ \inf_{\mathcal M}\ \sup_{\mu:\operatorname{OPT}_{1/4}(\mu)>0} \frac{\mathbb E_{Y\sim\mathcal M(\mu)} [\operatorname{SC}_{1/4}(Y;\mu)]} {\operatorname{OPT}_{1/4}(\mu)}, \]
with exact cost required whenever the optimum is zero.

The paper’s four-agent lower-bound instance embeds directly: take mass \(1/4\) at each of \(0,1/3,2/3,1\). Moving one quarter of the mass at an endpoint to the adjacent inner location reproduces the unilateral deviations used in Theorem 9. The continuous costs are the discrete costs scaled by \(1/4\), so the ratios are unchanged. Thus the anchored \(3/2\) lower bound transfers to this continuous mechanism class. That is a meaningful positive case: the mass model preserves the paper’s outlier objective and its incentive issue, and the anchor’s obstruction does not disappear just because each quarter represents many agents. This is an approximation impossibility transferring from the discrete instance, not an NP-hardness result.

The regime is a large municipality choosing a shared service or event location. Residents with the same ideal point have the same preferences and costs; the society may have millions of residents but only a modest number \(\tau\) of distinct preferred locations. A quarter of the population is excluded from the objective according to distance from the chosen facility, just as the paper excludes the \(z\) most costly agents.

The authors would recognize the facility, distance costs, outlier rule, and strategic reports as their problem. There is one necessary co-modification: unilateral deviations by infinitesimal agents do not change \(\mu\), making ordinary individual strategyproofness vacuous. Mass-coalition strategyproofness gives manipulation substantive meaning. The paper says its deterministic mechanisms are group-strategyproof in the strong sense, which supports this extension, though Theorem 9 itself concerns randomized individual strategyproofness. That change in incentive notion is the weakest point: the transferred lower bound is valid, but the continuous question imposes a stronger requirement than the randomized theorem does.

The scope is just Theorem 9’s randomized utilitarian lower bound. I would expect its \(3/2\) obstruction to transfer, as above; the exact optimum over all continuous societies may be larger and needs a new characterization. Natural follow-up questions are how the best guarantee varies with \(\rho\) and support size \(\tau\), and whether randomization helps under mass-coalition strategyproofness.

One qualification matters for ChoCo’s gate: this paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 9 is a named lower bound on truthful mechanism approximation. If “computational result” is restricted to complexity classifications, the paper supplies no eligible anchor; the case above rests on the broader algorithmic-mechanism-design reading of approximation guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the proponent’s sole anchor, Theorem 9, is a distortion lower bound for truthful mechanisms, not a computational result in the programme’s stated sense. It says that no randomized strategyproof rule can beat a \(3/2\) approximation on a particular instance. It says nothing about the time needed to compute a rule’s outcome, or about the complexity of a manipulation, control, bribery, or robustness problem. The mechanisms are treated as arbitrary functions; computational cost never enters the theorem.

The proposed population model itself is credible. Four types with mass \(1/4\) reproduce the instance, and moving a quarter of an atom reproduces the deviation. The \(3/2\) lower bound therefore carries over to mass-coalition strategyproof mechanisms. But that establishes a continuous strategyproof-distortion question, not a continuous computational question. Rephrasing it as the best possible ratio over mass-based mechanisms leaves the same issue: it is a characterization of truthful rules, with no complexity measure or algorithmic bottleneck. Requiring polynomial time would add a new condition absent from the paper; it would not make Theorem 9 a result about computational complexity.

A natural computational variant—calculating a facility location from the finitely supported distribution—would be a different problem from the theorem’s lower bound. It has no hard computational task identified in the paper to continue. So the mirror may be worthwhile for a programme on continuous mechanism design, but the proponent has not established a worthwhile mirror for ChoCo. That is the negative case’s strength; its limit is that it rejects this anchor on programme scope, not on the plausibility of the population model.
