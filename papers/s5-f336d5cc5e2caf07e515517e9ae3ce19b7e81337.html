What the mirror covers

The proposed mass restatement covers Theorems 1 and 2's clone-invariance and clone-resistance characterizations; neither yields a computational result to transfer.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive observation is that the population model fits the paper’s clone operation naturally. Let \(\mu\) be a finite-support rational distribution over rankings. To add a clone \(x'\) of \(x\), split each ranking type \(\rho\) into mass \(p\mu_\rho\) that ranks \(x\) above \(x'\), and mass \((1-p)\mu_\rho\) that ranks \(x'\) above \(x\), keeping the pair adjacent and all original comparisons fixed. For a scoring rule, scores are the mass-weighted sums of rank scores. A reasonable continuous question is to characterize which positional scoring rules preserve the original choice set under every such extension (\(p\)-CR), or preserve the whole choice set including the entrant (\(p\)-CI). A large electorate with relatively few ballot types gives this a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors would likely recognize the mass-splitting operation as their clone question.

But this paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1, proved here using Young’s 1975 result, characterizes Plurality by Anonymity, Neutrality, Consistency, and \(1\)-CI. Theorem 2, also proved here, classifies positional scoring rules satisfying \(p\)-CR: it gives the permitted rules for three alternatives, says only the Trivial rule works for at least four alternatives when \(p\in(0,1)\), and identifies Plurality and inverse Plurality at the endpoints. These are axiomatic characterizations, not results about complexity, algorithms, or a computational formulation.

For rational \(\mu\) and rational \(p\), the proposed mass extension can be scaled to an integer electorate, so the corresponding classification is already determined by those theorems. That gives a clean continuous-population restatement, but not a computational problem with a named result as its anchor. It is also an axiomatic question, which the programme puts out of scope. So the paper offers a plausible population mirror in the broad sense, but no honest anchor for the requested continuous computational case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one genuine anchor, and it is a natural one: represent the society by masses on rankings, then split each ranking type \(\rho\) into \(p\mu_\rho\) mass ranking \(x\) above \(x'\) and \((1-p)\mu_\rho\) ranking \(x'\) above \(x\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity model. The clone operation does not depend on voter identity, and there is no degeneracy in the mass version.

The problem is that neither of the paper’s main results asks a computational question. Theorem 1 characterizes rules satisfying four axioms; Theorem 2 classifies positional scoring rules satisfying \(p\)-CR. A continuous version can ask the same classifications over rational population measures, but that remains an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s scope. Since \(p\) and the masses are rational, scaling recovers an ordinary finite profile with the required clone extension. That correspondence makes the mirror well-defined; it does not give the paper a population-as-input computational problem.

The strongest alternative would be to fix a society and ask whether cloning changes its winners, or to search for a society witnessing a violation. The first is just score calculation for a specified profile and scoring rule. The second is a new counterexample-search problem, not a computational result in the paper: Theorem 2 classifies rules, while its proof supplies small witness profiles. To make that search a ChoCo problem would require adding an input encoding and an objective the paper never studies. Theorem 1 is still further from an algorithmic mirror, since it ranges over general social choice rules without giving them an input representation.

So I would reject the proposed mirror as a ChoCo project, while granting that the population interpretation is plausible and the continuous axiom question is mathematically coherent. The negative case is strong for this paper and programme; it cannot rule out someone inventing a useful computational problem about clone vulnerability, but such a problem would be a new project rather than a mirror of these results.
