The continuous mirror question

Given finite resident types \(T\) and room classes \(J\), with rational population masses \(\mu_t\) and supply masses \(\sigma_j\) summing to one, rational values \(v_{tj}\), and a rational mass assignment \(y_{tj}\) whose row sums are \(\mu_t\) and column sums are \(\sigma_j\), suppose an initial class compensation vector \(p^0\) satisfies \(\sum_j \sigma_j p^0_j=\alpha\) and envy-freeness: whenever \(y_{tj}>0\), \(v_{tj}+p^0_j\ge v_{t\ell}+p^0_\ell\) for every \(\ell\). For an occupied root state \((k,j_0)\), find rational compensations \(p_j\) preserving that budget balance and envy-freeness such that every occupied state reaches \((k,j_0)\) in the directed graph on states with \(y_{tj}>0\), with an edge \((t,j)\to(s,\ell)\) when \(v_{tj}+p_j=v_{t\ell}+p_\ell\).

The model it lives in

A pooled housing market with a continuum of residents and rooms, finite resident types, and room classes identical in value to every resident type. Each resident receives one indivisible room; \(y_{tj}\) records assignment mass, and all copies in class \(j\) share compensation \(p_j\). The task is feasibility: preserve the assignment while finding envy-free, per-capita budget-balanced compensations that make every occupied state linked to a positive-mass root state.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 1’s identification of linked envy-free allocations and its use as a subroutine for the least-manipulable rules; it does not continuize the strategic manipulation measures themselves.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s boundary update be proved for rational type and room-class masses, with each iteration adding an occupied state to the linked set?
  • Does the resulting algorithm have polynomial bit complexity in the number of types, room classes, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a large, standardized housing market: many residents, many room copies, and relatively few distinct valuation types. A type \(t\) is a complete vector of quasi-linear values \(v_{tj}\) for room classes \(j\). Agents of that type are interchangeable for the model. Let \(\mu_t\) be their population share and \(\sigma_j\) the per-capita supply of room class \(j\), with both distributions summing to one. A finite replication with \(q\mu_t\) residents of type \(t\) and \(q\sigma_j\) indivisible rooms of class \(j\) gives the high-multiplicity interpretation. Residents still get one whole room; fractional quantities describe cohort averages. A campus or housing authority with repeated room designs and recurring resident cohorts is a plausible regime where \(q\) is large and the support \(\tau\) is modest. The paper’s small-household example alone would be a weak high-multiplicity case.

My lead is \(k\)-Linked Mass Rent Rebalancing. Its input is rational \(\mu,\sigma\), rational valuations \(v_{tj}\), a per-capita compensation total \(\alpha\), and a fixed type-to-room mass assignment \(y_{tj}\) with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(\sigma_j\). It also includes an initial room-class compensation vector \(p^0\) such that \(\sum_j \sigma_j p^0_j=\alpha\) and the allocation is envy-free: whenever \(y_{tj}>0\), \(v_{tj}+p^0_j\ge v_{t\ell}+p^0_\ell\) for every room class \(\ell\). Finally, a tagged resident of a positive-mass type \(k\) is assigned to a class \(j_0\) with \(y_{kj_0}>0\).

The task is to find new rational compensations \(p_j\), leaving \(y\) fixed, that preserve budget balance and envy-freeness and make the allocation \(k\)-linked. For precision, form a directed graph on occupied states \((t,j)\) with \(y_{tj}>0\). There is an edge from \((t,j)\) to \((s,\ell)\) when \(v_{tj}+p_j=v_{t\ell}+p_\ell\). A solution is \(p\), together with paths from every occupied state to the root state \((k,j_0)\). This is a feasibility problem: the paper’s anchor identifies such allocations; it does not minimize a scalar objective.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for a fixed finite preference profile and agent \(k\), Algorithm 2 finds an agent-\(k\)-linked envy-free allocation in at most \(|N|\) iterations, starting from an arbitrary envy-free allocation. The algorithm holds the assignment fixed, finds the current group linked to \(k\), and, if the group is incomplete, adjusts compensations across its boundary by the smallest cross-group envy gap. These adjustments preserve envy-freeness and budget balance while adding at least one agent to the group. The theorem gives an iteration bound, not an explicit bit-complexity classification; the paper has no named hardness result to anchor instead.

I expect the proposed mass version to be tractable, a Class A candidate. With uniform compensation within each repeated room class, agents assigned that class are mutually indifferent, so a linked group contains whole room classes. If its mass is \(q\), a plausible weighted update raises compensation on its classes by \((1-q)\lambda\) and lowers compensation outside it by \(q\lambda\), where \(\lambda\) is the smallest cross-group envy gap. The changes balance because the inside and outside room supplies are \(q\) and \(1-q\). The graph has at most \(\tau m\) occupied states, and the rational masses permit a compressed implementation without expanding a potentially enormous \(q\)-replica. The concrete open work is to prove this weighted update preserves envy-freeness and grows the linked set, then bound arithmetic bit length in \(m,\tau,L\).

This is a plausible continuation of the authors’ problem because it retains one indivisible room per resident, quasi-linear valuations, envy-freeness, budget balance, and the exact linkedness condition their algorithm computes. It covers Theorem 1’s allocation-identification task and its role as a subroutine for the least-manipulable rules discussed in Section 3; it does not itself continuize the strategic manipulation measures. Further questions include whether the mass version of those measures has a natural definition, and whether tractability survives when copies in a room class may carry different compensations.

The weakest point is the passage from individual room prices to a common compensation for repeated room classes, together with proving that the finite algorithm compresses to the mass representation. A tagged root resident also has zero mass, even though linkedness depends on that resident. These are real modeling and proof obligations. Still, the tag preserves the theorem’s root-agent question, and common compensation is credible for interchangeable rooms. This makes the mirror worth asking, though Theorem 1 does not settle its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the paper’s setting: one finite economy, with one room per agent and rents balanced across those rooms. To make the population large, one must also replicate the rooms. If the copies belong to separate households, keeping each household’s budget balanced leaves a collection of small problems. Pooling them into one large economy makes the mirror work, but changes the motivating story: an authority must allocate rooms across many residents and balance rents across the whole pool.

That pooled-market version also needs a narrow type regime. A type must encode the resident’s full valuation vector over room classes, and the linkedness condition relies on exact indifferences. Repeated room layouts alone do not give repeated types. Where valuations vary across residents, the number of types may grow with the population, weakening the compression that makes a continuous model attractive.

Those objections do not defeat the proponent’s stronger example, though. A large dormitory or housing authority with recurring resident types, repeated room classes, and a pooled budget is plausible. In that model, the algorithm’s core also seems to survive aggregation: linked agents occupy a union of room classes; if that union has supply mass \(q\), raising its compensations by \((1-q)\lambda\) and lowering the others by \(q\lambda\) preserves weighted budget balance. Taking \(\lambda\) as the smallest cross-group envy gap mirrors the paper’s update. So the mass version appears both well posed and amenable to a compact algorithm.

The strategic results are harder to carry over: one resident’s report has zero mass in an atomless society, so changing it cannot affect a rule that depends only on the population distribution. Giving a type positive-mass deviations would turn individual manipulation into coalition manipulation. But that does not dispose of Theorem 1, the proponent’s anchor. The theorem is a named algorithmic result, and its linked-allocation task has a credible high-multiplicity version.

So the negative case is limited: the paper’s main setting does not itself supply a convincing large-population regime, and the proposed mirror needs exact repeated valuations and pooled rent balancing. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The standardized housing-market case remains a viable mirror, even if it is a narrower continuation of the paper than its motivating roommate example.
