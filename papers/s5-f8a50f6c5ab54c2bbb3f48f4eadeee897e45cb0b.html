The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow one: this paper suggests a continuous-population problem for planning adaptive recommendations across a large cohort. But it supplies no numbered computational result of the kind the brief requires as an anchor.

Its only numbered result is Proposition 1, presented as the paper’s own proposition rather than attributed to outside work. It says that an option dominated under preference model \(R\) becomes Pareto-optimal after adding preference \(r_i\) exactly when it is strictly better under \(r_i\) than every option that dominated it under \(R\), and no worse under \(r_i\) than every option equally preferred under \(R\). That is a structural characterization used in the suggestion calculations, not a result that classifies a problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized-tractable. The paper’s computational evidence is in its simulation tables, not in a numbered complexity result. So I cannot honestly present Proposition 1 as the required anchor.

A plausible mirror is Adaptive Suggestion Planning\(_\infty\). Consider a high-volume product-search platform serving many shoppers over a fixed horizon of \(H\) interaction cycles. A type records a shopper’s true preference model, response behavior, and current interaction state; shoppers of one type are indistinguishable for this problem. The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). The product catalog and preference domains are finite and discretized, as in the paper’s simulations.

At each cycle, the system chooses a slate \(S\) of at most \(K\) suggestions for each observable interaction state. It cannot condition on a shopper’s hidden true preferences. Shoppers react according to the supplied response model; mass then splits among the resulting observable states, and the system updates its beliefs as in the paper. The input includes the catalog, the finite type and response data, the initial masses, \(K\), and \(H\). The question is whether there is a policy whose expected share of shoppers with their full preference model elicited by cycle \(H\) is at least a given rational threshold \(q\). A solution is such a policy, together with its achieved value.

The regime could be a large platform running millions of searches for apartments or computers, where the same catalog and normalized attributes recur and interaction logs support a relatively small set of preference and response patterns. The continuous object here is the population share in each type, not the product options or the probabilities already used inside the paper’s user model. The paper’s use of prior preference distributions and observed reactions makes this a recognizable extension of its adaptive-suggestion problem. Its simulations of hit rate and full discovery give the most direct motivation, although they are not eligible named anchors.

For an explicitly listed finite state-and-action graph, I would expect this planning problem to be tractable by dynamic programming. With a compact representation of attributes, beliefs, and histories, the reachable state space may grow sharply; the paper does not establish where that boundary lies. The useful follow-up questions are whether the compact version admits an efficient algorithm, how the result changes when the policy must serve all shoppers with one shared slate, and how sensitive planning is to response probabilities estimated from logs.

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies adaptation for an individual recommender session, not a cohort objective, and it gives no named computational result for that problem. The cohort policy is a plausible scale-out that authors might recognize, but it is not a clean high-multiplicity relaxation of a complexity result proved in this paper. Under the brief’s anchor rule, there is no qualifying anchor and therefore no compliant per-anchor continuous problem to report.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Its only numbered result, Proposition 1, characterizes when a dominated option becomes Pareto-optimal after a preference is added. It does not classify an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. The paper’s computational contributions are a suggestion strategy, a Bayesian update procedure, and simulation results. None supplies the discrete complexity question that the programme’s mirror is meant to continuize.

Adaptive Suggestion Planning\(_\infty\) is the proponent’s strongest candidate, but it is a new cohort-planning problem, not a mirror of a result proved here. The paper adapts suggestions within an individual session; it does not optimize a policy over a population or maximize the share of users whose preferences are fully elicited by a deadline. Its prior distribution describes uncertainty about one user’s preferences. A distribution over actual shoppers is a different object, even if both can be written using probabilities.

A better cohort formulation is possible: represent shoppers by complete preference and response types, then choose suggestions to maximize expected preference discovery across a large platform cohort. That is a sensible scenario, so I would not argue that high multiplicity is inherently implausible here. But without extra coupling, the cohort objective is just a weighted sum of individual outcomes. To make it a genuinely joint planning problem, one would need to add constraints such as a shared suggestion budget or fairness requirements. Those additions may yield worthwhile research, but they make the new question depend on assumptions absent from the paper. They do not rescue a paper result that can be mirrored.

So the negative case is strong on the narrow point the anchor rule tests: the proponent identifies no named computational result, and the proposed planning problem cannot substitute for one. It is weaker as a universal claim that no scenario could justify a continuous study. A large recommender platform provides a plausible high-multiplicity setting; what this paper does not establish is that studying it would be a continuization of the paper’s computational contribution.
