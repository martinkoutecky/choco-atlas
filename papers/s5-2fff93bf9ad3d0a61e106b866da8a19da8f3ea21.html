The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 5.15

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5.15 — survived: A finite ordered stream of type cohorts preserves the online problem while replacing repeated buyers with mass; the opponent acknowledges this is coherent, though the proponent did not specify a finite stream encoding.
  • Theorem 4.2 — survived: The same finite-cohort repair preserves the arrival-sensitive question, provided the arbitrary cost curves have an evaluable representation.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) items, a finite valuation-type support \(T\), population proportions \(\mu_t\), total mass \(N\), and a bound \(K\), an adversary reveals at most \(K\) ordered cohorts of total mass \(N\), with type totals \(N\mu_t\). Each infinitesimal buyer chooses a utility-maximizing integral bundle at the causal posted prices; the seller's marginal costs are \(c_i(q)=q^d\), with cumulative costs \(C_i(x)=\int_0^x q^d\,dq\). For \(0<\epsilon<1\), \(Z\in(U_{\max},U_{\max}/\epsilon]\), ℓ_i=\min\{Z^{1/d},N\}, and \(B_i=\lceil12\log(4n\ell_i/\epsilon)\rceil\), under the fluid analogues of Theorem 5.15's threshold assumptions, does a causal pricing policy achieve \(W_{\mathrm{on}}\ge\frac{W^*-18\sum_i B_i c_i(B_i)}{18(d+1)(27/16)^{d+1}}\) for every admissible cohort order? Here \(W^*\) is the offline welfare optimum over fractional cohort-to-bundle assignments.

The model it lives in

A society with finite type support and masses μ, together with an online arrival process represented by at most \(K\) ordered type-mass cohorts. Individuals receive integral bundles; cohort mass can flow across bundles as prices change. The online policy chooses prices from the observed prefix and cumulative sales \(q_i\); welfare subtracts \(\sum_i C_i(q_i)\). The offline benchmark maximizes value over assignments of cohort mass to bundles.

The objection that survived

The proponent preserves arrival order but does not encode it finitely; the mirror needs an explicit ordered cohort representation and a parameter for its size.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the welfare guarantees in Theorems 5.15 and 4.2. It leaves the profit guarantee in Theorem 6.1 and the paper's other pricing analyses unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the smoothing schedule and its welfare bound extend to real-valued quantities under the stated finite cohort model?
  • Which finite representation of arbitrary cost curves makes the Theorem 4.2 analogue computationally meaningful?
  • How do the fluid guarantees compare with large finite buyer streams as \(N\) grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here because the paper’s central object is already an online stream of buyers choosing bundles. The change is to make the buyer population nonatomic while keeping bundles indivisible for each buyer and preserving the online order.

A plausible regime is a large cloud resource market: \(N\) requests arrive during an operating period, but many share one of \(\tau\) workload and valuation types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Items are resource entitlements such as compute or storage units; adding capacity has increasing marginal cost. Each request still gets an integral bundle. The aggregate mass of requests of type \(t\), however, may be assigned fractionally across bundles. That is a natural consequence of representing repeated requests by mass.

Formally, let \(I\) be the \(n\) items, and let \(T\) be a finite support of complete valuation types \(v_t:2^I\to\mathbb R\). The society has fractions \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and scale \(N\), so type \(t\) has mass \(N\mu_t\). A play consists of an ordered stream of type-labeled infinitesimal cohorts. The seller learns the stream only as it arrives. At cumulative sales \(q_i\), a causal posted-price policy sets the price for the next infinitesimal amount of item \(i\); an arriving type chooses a bundle maximizing \(v_t(S)-\sum_{i\in S}p_i(q_i)\). Let \(c_i(q)\) be the nondecreasing marginal cost at quantity \(q\), and \(C_i(x)=\int_0^x c_i(q)\,dq\) the cost of supplying \(x\) units. The offline benchmark chooses masses \(y_{t,S}\ge0\) with \(\sum_S y_{t,S}=N\mu_t\), and maximizes \(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}v_t(S)-\sum_i C_i(q_i)\), where \(q_i=\sum_{t,S:i\in S}y_{t,S}\). The stream order remains part of the online problem; the population mass is what becomes continuous.

My lead is the continuous analogue of Theorem 5.15, proved in this paper. The theorem concerns the smoothing pricing algorithm for polynomial marginal costs \(c_i(k)=(k-1)^d\), \(d\ge1\), with sufficiently many buyers relative to the threshold \(c_i^{-1}(Z)\), and with \(B_i\ge18(2d+1)\) and \(\ell_i\ge2(B_i+1)\). Here \(B_i=\lceil12\log(4n\ell_i/\epsilon)\rceil\), \(\ell_i=\min\{c_i^{-1}(Z),m\}\), and \(Z\in(U_{\max},U_{\max}/\epsilon]\). Its guarantee is \(W(\mathrm{alg})\ge\bigl(W(\mathrm{opt})-18\sum_i B_i c_i(B_i)\bigr)/\bigl(18(d+1)(27/16)^{d+1}\bigr)\).

I would hand a prover this problem: Polynomial-Cost Nonatomic Online Welfare Pricing. Given \(n\) items, a finite type support and an ordered nonatomic stream with aggregate masses \(N\mu_t\), polynomial cost curves \(c_i(q)=q^d\), and the corresponding \(Z,\epsilon\) parameters, find a causal posted-price policy whose induced welfare satisfies \(W_{\mathrm{on}}\ge\bigl(W^*-18\sum_i B_i c_i(B_i)\bigr)/\bigl(18(d+1)(27/16)^{d+1}\bigr)\), under the theorem’s analogous threshold assumptions with \(m\) replaced by \(N\). The coordinate shift from \((k-1)^d\) to \(q^d\) makes the first infinitesimal unit have zero marginal cost. The solution is the price policy and its induced measurable allocation, with the guarantee holding for every stream order and utility-maximizing response. I expect a tractable, positive answer: the smoothing schedule is already built around marginal quantities and should have a real-mass counterpart.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 4.2, also proved here. For arbitrary nondecreasing marginal-cost functions and \(Z\in(U_{\max},U_{\max}/\epsilon]\), its Section 4 algorithm guarantees \(W(\mathrm{alg})\ge W(\mathrm{opt})/2-\sum_i(c_i(\tau_i)-c_i(1))\tau_i/(4\max_i\tau_i)\), where \(\ell_i=\min\{c_i^{-1}(Z),m\}\) and \(\tau_i=\lceil\log(4n\ell_i/\epsilon)\rceil\).

Its mirror is General-Cost Nonatomic Online Welfare Pricing: for the same continuous buyer and bundle model, replace \(m\) by total mass \(N\), and ask for a causal policy for arbitrary nondecreasing \(c_i(q)\) that achieves \(W_{\mathrm{on}}\ge W^*/2-\sum_i(c_i(\tau_i)-c_i(0))\tau_i/(4\max_i\tau_i)\), with \(\ell_i=\min\{c_i^{-1}(Z),N\}\) and the corresponding \(\tau_i\). This is a logarithmic-guarantee question for a wider cost class; I expect the real-mass version of the paper’s chunked pricing method to work.

Both questions preserve the paper’s substantive challenge: buyers have arbitrary combinatorial valuations, the seller posts item prices, production costs rise with sales, and the policy must react without knowing future demand. They are not outcome-space continuity or a noise model. Fractional type-level bundle totals are the aggregation of integral bundles received by individual requests.

The main soft spot is that the paper’s proofs use discrete copy indices and a finite buyer sequence. A continuous stream needs a well-defined price-and-demand path, especially when many identical agents are indifferent between bundles. The schedule may extend cleanly, but that limit argument is part of the proposed work. Also, these are approximation-algorithm anchors, not NP-hardness results: the paper supplies no named complexity-hardness anchor whose hardness could be shown to disappear. What this mirror tests is whether its online welfare guarantees persist for a nonatomic population. The two theorems are complementary rather than filler: one gives a constant-factor target for polynomial costs; the other gives a broader logarithmic target for arbitrary increasing costs.

Further questions follow naturally: can the continuous schedules achieve the same constants, what are the best possible online guarantees, and how do the results translate between a nonatomic stream and large finite populations with \(\tau\ll N\)?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is about the paper’s input, not about whether repeated buyers are plausible. Theorem 5.15 and Theorem 4.2 are genuine algorithmic results, and a market with many repeated request types is credible. I would not argue that the paper lacks results to mirror or that high multiplicity is inherently unreasonable.

Both anchors, however, depend on the order of buyers. The same type masses can produce different online outcomes under different arrival orders. A static society \(\mu\) therefore cannot carry the information needed for either guarantee. The proponent preserves order by proposing an ordered stream of infinitesimal cohorts, but that makes the operative input a type-by-time arrival process. If that process has \(K\) encoded segments, \(K\) becomes an important input parameter; if arbitrary measurable paths are allowed, the stream has no finite input representation of the kind the programme’s complexity comparisons use. The population masses alone do not compress the online problem.

That problem affects Theorem 5.15 even in its stronger, polynomial-cost version. A careful mirror could specify a finite, piecewise-constant arrival path, scale marginal costs per capita, and ask for the same welfare guarantee against every admissible path. That is a coherent online question, and the authors might recognize it. But it is a limit theorem about a time-indexed demand process, rather than a computational question on a continuous society described by its type masses. The cost scaling also has to be chosen: without it, fixed per-buyer values and \(c_i(q)=q^d\) can make the economically relevant quantity stay bounded as population mass grows; with per-capita scaling, one has specified a particular fluid regime.

Theorem 4.2 has the same problem, so it does not supply an independent anchor. Its chunking schedule depends on the amount of supply the stream can reach, while the welfare guarantee still depends on which types arrive when. Replacing the number of buyers by total mass does not determine that history. A finite-segment arrival model could make the question precise, but it would need to account for temporal complexity as well as type support and mass.

This is a serious limitation on the proposed mirrors, but it does not establish the universal negative the assignment asks for. An adversarial arrival process can be specified as part of a continuous model, and repeated cloud or resource-market requests can plausibly have shared valuations. The more accurate conclusion is that the anchors do not straightforwardly yield the programme’s usual static continuous-society problem; a time-indexed version remains defensible. I cannot honestly say that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
