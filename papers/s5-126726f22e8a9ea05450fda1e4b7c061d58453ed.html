The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Propositions 1–5 are analytical comparisons of optimal consumption: Proposition 1 compares ex ante prioritarian and utilitarian consumption under DARA; Proposition 2 compares ex post with ex ante prioritarian consumption when \(g'''\ge 0\); Propositions 3 and 4 compare ex post prioritarian with utilitarian consumption under condition (5), respectively without and with learning; Proposition 5 compares ex ante prioritarian with utilitarian consumption under learning and DARA. None asserts a complexity classification, an algorithm, or a computational formulation the paper solves.

There is a conceivable population extension: treat households with the same preferences and risk distribution as a type, let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population share, and choose type-level consumption allocations to maximize a prioritarian welfare objective. But that would be a new problem, not a mirror supported by any of this paper’s named computational results. The paper’s uncertainty over the cake is a risk distribution, not a distribution over a continuous population of agents; its central comparison concerns consumption across a small number of periods. Its social welfare functions also raise an axiomatic question, which is outside the programme’s scope.

So I cannot honestly name a lead continuous problem per anchor here. The soft spot is decisive: the paper may offer useful economic motivation for studying welfare under risk, but it supplies no computational result whose discrete hardness or tractability a population continuization could test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “CASE FOR” offers no continuous mirror to rebut; it argues that none is anchored in the paper. That is the strongest case against a mirror here. Propositions 1–5 compare optimal consumption under different welfare criteria. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem, give an algorithm, or specify a finite input whose computational treatment could be mirrored. Although the paper writes down optimization problems for \(c\), it does not define how utility functions or risk distributions are encoded as inputs. The propositions are analytic comparative statics, not computational results.

The most natural rescue is to treat generations as a population: let each generation be a type, assign it a share of society, and optimize prioritarian welfare over those shares. But the paper’s central model has two periods, or three in its learning extension. Making many identical people within each period leaves the per-person consumption choice unchanged; their mass simply scales welfare. Making the number of generations large instead continuizes time, while the first-period choice, sequence of residual cake, and learning structure depend on temporal order. Neither move supplies the missing population-complexity problem.

A stronger extension would give households different utility or risk types and choose consumption by type. That could be a worthwhile continuous welfare-optimization problem, but it changes the model’s central object: the paper studies a scalar allocation across periods under a common uncertain cake, not allocation across a distribution of contemporaneous household types. Its results do not anchor that new problem as a discrete-to-continuous mirror. This does not rule out useful research inspired by the paper; it does leave the case for a *mirror of this paper’s results* without an anchor.
