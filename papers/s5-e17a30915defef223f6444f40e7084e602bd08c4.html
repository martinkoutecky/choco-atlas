The continuous mirror question

Given item categories \(V\), a finite support \(T\) of out-star preference graphs \(G_t=(V_t,A_t)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\ge 0\), and an integer threshold \(D\), does there exist \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) for integral bundles \(S\subseteq V_t\) such that \(\sum_{S\subseteq V_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v}x_{t,S}\le q_v\) for each \(v\), and \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(\delta_t(S)>D\)? The continuous min-max value is the least feasible \(D\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete out-star preference graphs with masses \(\mu_t\); each person receives an integral bundle, while \(x_{t,S}\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\). Per-capita category supplies \(q_v\) constrain total use, and the objective minimizes the largest dissatisfaction among assigned mass.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 1's \(\mathrm{MINMAX}\) hardness for out-star preferences under type masses and per-capita category supplies; it does not mirror the paper's other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the pricing method for out-star types be extended to general DAG preference types?
  • What is the continuous complexity of min-sum dissatisfaction under the same supply model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The affirmative case I believe is narrow: Lead — Continuous Out-Star Min-Max Dissatisfaction.

A plausible regime is a large school district distributing a standard gift catalog to many children. A child’s type is their complete preference graph over gift categories; children with identical graphs are one type. For example, an out-star records one gift preferred to several fallback gifts. The district has many children but relatively few distinct preference types, and stocks each category in proportion to enrollment. Each child still receives an integral bundle; only the share of a type assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper by reduction from 3X3C: \(\mathrm{MINMAX}\) is NP-complete even when every preference graph is an out-star. In full, the decision problem takes a set \(K\) of agents, a set \(V\) of items, a DAG \(G_i=(V_i,A_i)\) for each agent with \(V_i\subseteq V\), and an integer \(d\). It asks whether there is an allocation \(\pi\) giving each agent a set of items, with no item assigned to more than one agent, such that every agent has dissatisfaction at most \(d\). Agent \(i\)’s dissatisfaction is the number of items in \(V_i\) not dominated by any item in \(\pi(i)\), where an item dominates itself and every item reachable from it in \(G_i\).

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. An instance has item categories \(V\), a finite support \(T\) of out-star preference graphs over those categories, rational type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\geq 0\), and an integer threshold \(D\). For a bundle \(S\subseteq V_t\), let \(\delta_t(S)\) be the number of vertices of \(G_t\) not dominated by any item in \(S\). Does there exist a continuous allocation \(x_{t,S}\geq 0\) such that
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq V_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v}x_{t,S}\leq q_v\quad\text{for every }v, \]
and \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(\delta_t(S)>D\)? A solution is the mass \(x_{t,S}\) of each type receiving each integral bundle \(S\). The maximum-dissatisfaction objective is the least \(D\) for which such an allocation exists.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For fixed \(D\), these constraints form a linear feasibility problem with one column for each acceptable type–bundle pair. Although there may be exponentially many bundles, the pricing question for each type is manageable for an out-star. If its root is allocated, dissatisfaction is zero; otherwise, dissatisfaction is the root plus every unselected leaf, so meeting the threshold requires selecting at least \(|V_t|-D\) leaves. Given arbitrary dual prices on items, the cheapest acceptable bundle can be found by choosing negative-price leaves and then, if needed, the cheapest remaining leaves to meet that quota. This is polynomial-time pricing. Separation and the ellipsoid method therefore give a credible polynomial-time route for fixed \(D\); testing the possible integer thresholds gives the min-max optimum. The expected running time is polynomial in the number of categories, the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

This is a high-multiplicity version in the programme’s sense: for a population of size \(N\), type \(t\) has \(N\mu_t\) children and category \(v\) has \(Nq_v\) copies. After scaling rational masses and a rational solution by a common denominator, the mass assignment is realizable with integral bundles in a finite replicated population. The paper’s preference graphs and dissatisfaction measure remain central to the question; scaling stock with population is the co-modification that makes a many-person allocation regime coherent.

My weakest point is that scaling category supplies changes the paper’s one-shot inventory model, even though it preserves indivisible items for each recipient and is a natural high-multiplicity allocation setting. Also, Theorem 1’s reduction has a number of distinct preference graphs that grows with its number of agents. Its NP-hardness is therefore an anchor for asking what happens under continuization, not evidence that this continuous problem remains hard. The fractional assignment of a type across bundles may remove the exact-cover obstruction that drives the reduction.

The scope here is only Theorem 1’s min-max hardness for out-star preferences; I would not stretch this mirror to cover the paper’s other results. It also raises useful follow-up questions: does the LP-and-pricing approach extend to general DAG types, and what is the continuous complexity of min-sum dissatisfaction under the same supply model?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the paper’s literal inventory. It has a fixed set of \(n\) indivisible items, each assignable once. If the number of agents grows while that inventory stays fixed, only \(O(n)\) agents can receive anything. Every positive-mass type therefore has unserved agents, whose dissatisfaction is \(|V_t|\). For sufficiently large populations, the minimum possible maximum dissatisfaction is simply \(\max_t |V_t|\). That limit is real, but degenerate.

The proposed district model avoids the degeneracy by scaling each category’s supply with enrollment. That is the serious objection: it changes a one-shot allocation of named items into allocation from per-capita stocks of repeatable categories, and the paper’s Theorem 1 reduction gives no evidence that this regime is present in its applications. Its set-agents have distinct preference graphs, so the hardness construction grows its type support rather than exhibiting a large population concentrated on a small number of types. This makes the district scenario a new modeling choice, not a limit directly supplied by the theorem.

That objection does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s stated scope. Per-capita supplies are an allowed co-modification, and a district allocating many copies of catalog categories is plausible. The preference graphs are complete type descriptions; the objective still constrains every positive mass to receive an integral bundle whose dissatisfaction is at most \(D\). The out-star pricing argument also appears sound: for each type, the cheapest acceptable bundle can be found by considering whether to take the root, or by selecting the required number of leaves. The resulting LP may settle the continuous question quickly, but tractability is not a reason the question lacks value.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The literal fixed-inventory limit is uninteresting, and the scaled-inventory mirror is a worthwhile, recognizable high-multiplicity allocation problem. The negative case is weak precisely where the proponent’s model is strongest: the programme permits the supply scaling, and the paper’s own objective carries over cleanly.
