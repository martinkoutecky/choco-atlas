The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its main result, Theorem 2, says that for \(u_1,u_2,v_1,v_2\in V\), \((u_1,u_2)<_L(v_1,v_2)\) if and only if \((u_1\gg u_2)<(v_1\gg v_2)\). It establishes a semantic property of the connective, not the complexity of a computational problem. The proceedings paper omits the proof and points to an extended version. Theorem 1 is a cited folk theorem about the impossibility of representing lexicographic order on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with a real-valued function; it is neither a computational result nor a result proved by these authors. The paper itself lists a future complexity-theoretic study as further work.

A continuous population of users ranking query results is a plausible follow-on: types could be users’ preference formulas, masses their population shares, and a result’s score its average satisfaction across those types. But that population model and aggregation objective are absent from the paper. Theorem 2 does not establish anything about their computational complexity. Presenting this as a mirror of a named result would invent the anchor the task requires. So the strongest honest positive case is only that the paper’s application suggests a continuous-population research direction; there is no defensible per-anchor continuous problem to state for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 says the connective preserves lexicographic order pointwise: for \(u_1,u_2,v_1,v_2\in V\), \((u_1,u_2)<_L(v_1,v_2)\) exactly when \((u_1\gg u_2)<(v_1\gg v_2)\). It does not study an algorithm, a complexity problem, or a society. Theorem 1 is a cited folk theorem, not a result of the paper. The authors themselves leave inference and complexity-theoretic questions for future work.

The best attempt at a mirror is a population of users whose types are their preference formulas, with query answers as alternatives. One could rank answers by average satisfaction, or apply a rank-based social choice rule to the users’ induced rankings. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: users sharing a formula are indistinguishable, and the paper explicitly motivates ranking query results. But either version adds a social aggregation rule and a computational task that the paper does not define. Average satisfaction also requires treating the paper’s rational encoding as cardinal utility; a rank-based alternative avoids that assumption, but is still a new problem. If answers are explicit, the population calculation is just aggregation over the supported types. If answers are implicit and hard to search, the complexity depends on the query and outcome representation, which this paper does not specify.

So the proponent’s query-ranking idea is a credible adjacent research direction, but it does not supply a continuous computational question anchored in a result of this paper. That makes the case against a *paper mirror* strong. I would not claim, however, that no scenario could make the direction worthwhile: the paper’s application gives it a natural foothold, and ruling out every useful population model would go beyond what the missing computational results establish.
