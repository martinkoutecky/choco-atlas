Why no mirror

Theorems 2 and 4 are computational results, but both already pose private mechanism learning over a population distribution of valuation types and evaluate performance under that distribution. The proposed questions restate those results, so they fail the programme’s explicit already-continuous collision rule.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorems 2 and 4, the private profit-learning impossibility and its smooth-distribution algorithm; it leaves the gain-from-trade results and the uniform-convergence results unanchored.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is for a distributional population of bilateral trade opportunities. Take a marketplace with many seller–buyer encounters. A type is a valuation pair \(v=(v_s,v_b)\in[0,1]^2\), and \(\mu(B)\) is the fraction of encounters whose pair lies in \(B\). A broker learns one DSIC, individually rational trade rule for this population. Its allocation region must be monotone, with payments determined by the Myerson payment rule, and its objective is expected broker profit,
\[ P_\mu(M)=\int \operatorname{profit}(M,v)\,d\mu(v). \]
The learner sees \(n\) sampled encounters; replacing one encounter in the sample corresponds to moving \(1/n\) mass between valuation types. Its decision is a randomized choice of trade mechanism, and its objective is high expected profit while keeping that choice differentially private.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved here. It says that for every learning algorithm, every \(\alpha<1/64\), every \(\varepsilon>0\), and every sample count \(N\), there is a valuation distribution \(D\) on which the algorithm cannot be both \(\varepsilon\)-differentially private under replacement of one sampled valuation pair and \(\alpha\)-optimal for profit. The optimality condition is in expectation over the training sample, the fresh valuation pair, and the learner’s randomness:
\[ \sup_{M\in\mathcal M}\mathbb E[\operatorname{profit}(M,v)] \le \mathbb E[\operatorname{profit}(A(S),v)]+\alpha. \]
The corresponding continuous question is Valuation-Population Private Profit Learnability: given sampling access to \(\mu\), \(N\), \(\alpha<1/64\), and \(\varepsilon\), does there exist a learner mapping \(N\) sampled encounters to a DSIC/IR mechanism that satisfies that expected-profit guarantee and is \(\varepsilon\)-DP for every neighboring sample? A solution is such a learner; the answer for unrestricted populations is no. This is an information-theoretic privacy impossibility, not an NP-hardness result. It is my strongest anchor because the obstruction holds for every finite sample count.

A companion anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here. It says that if \(D\) is \(\sigma\)-smooth—its density is bounded by \(1/\sigma\)—then a polynomial-time randomized learner is \((\alpha,\beta)\)-optimal for profit and \(\varepsilon\)-DP when
\[ n\ge \frac{405\log^2(50/\alpha\sigma)}{\alpha^2\sigma} +\frac{128\log(2/\beta)}{\alpha^2} +\frac{256\log(2/\beta)}{\alpha^2\sigma\varepsilon}. \]
Here \((\alpha,\beta)\)-optimal means that with probability at least \(1-\beta\), the expected-profit gap on a fresh encounter is at most \(\alpha\). The mirror question, Smooth Valuation-Population Private Profit Learnability, is the same learning problem with the promise that \(\mu\) has such a bounded density. Theorem 4 answers it positively, with the stated sample bound and polynomial-time implementation. I expect this version to be tractable; the general-distribution version is impossible under the paper’s privacy and accuracy requirements.

The marketplace scenario is plausible: many encounters may be drawn from a stable population law, and the rule is meant for that aggregate market rather than for any named pair. The authors should recognize the learning objective, the privacy condition, and the two distribution regimes as their problem. But this mirror requires extending ChoCo’s usual finite type set to a continuous valuation space. Under the strict complete-type definition, distinct valuation pairs are distinct types, and the paper’s smooth distributions generally have infinitely many types with positive density. The hard distributions for Theorem 2 are continuous measures on lines, while the smoothness promise in Theorem 4 rules out finite atomic populations. So these results do not establish a high-multiplicity regime with moderate support \(\tau\), nor do they show that a discrete hardness result dissolves in a continuum.

That is the weak point—and it also raises the main follow-up questions. Do the impossibility and positive results persist for finite-support populations with \(\tau\ll n\)? Can the smooth-population result be expressed from an explicit mass representation rather than sampling access? And, once the population is supplied as masses, does optimizing profit over monotone trade regions admit a direct continuous-optimization formulation? Those would be genuinely new ChoCo questions. The present mirror is strongest as a faithful population-distribution reading of the paper, but weakest as evidence of a new finite-support high-multiplicity complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both anchors already have the population distribution at their center. The paper takes \(D\), a distribution over valuation pairs, as the society being studied: samples are drawn from \(D\), and mechanism quality is measured by expected profit under \(D\). That is already a continuous-population computational model. The paper then proves algorithmic results about it—sample-complexity bounds and a polynomial-time learner—so this is not merely continuous analytic work of the kind ChoCo distinguishes from its target. Relabeling \(D\) as \(\mu\) does not create a new mirror.

That makes the proposed Theorem 2 question an exact restatement of the paper’s private-profit learning problem: learn a DSIC/IR mechanism from samples, evaluate it by expected profit on a fresh draw, and require differential privacy for neighboring samples. The no-go result is not an objection because its answer is negative; it is a collision because the computational question is already posed over distributions and answered in the paper. Theorem 4 is the same collision on the \(\sigma\)-smooth subclass, with the paper giving the polynomial-time learner and sample bound directly.

The proponent’s finite-support caveat identifies a real limit, but it does not rescue either anchor. A bounded-density distribution on \([0,1]^2\) is not a society with moderate support \(\tau\), and Theorem 2’s proof does not establish impossibility for every restricted finite-support regime. If ChoCo instead asks for bounds parameterized by \(\tau\), or changes the input from sample access to an explicit list of rational masses, that may be a worthwhile extension. But it is a new input regime and question; these theorems would no longer be its results. The paper’s general model already permits distributions with finite support, though that alone does not settle the restricted regime’s complexity.

There is also a mismatch between a mass-transfer problem and the privacy objective. Differential privacy compares datasets that differ in one sampled encounter. The true population distribution \(D\) is not that dataset. Replacing a sample changes its empirical distribution by \(1/n\), but modeling privacy against such changes simply reconstructs the paper’s sample-based problem. Giving the learner the exact population masses instead changes what data are private and what adjacency means.

So the best negative case is narrow but strong: every anchor raised is already a computational result about a distributional population, and the proposed formulation reproduces it. A finite-support, explicit-mass variant could be new; the paper does not show that it is uninteresting. The negative case is weakest there, but that variant is not a continuous mirror of Theorems 2 or 4.
