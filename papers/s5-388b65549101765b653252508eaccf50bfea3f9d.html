The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a mirror is a plausible new high-multiplicity question inspired by the paper. It does not pass your anchor rule: the paper has no named computational result. Its numbered results are existence theorems, with no algorithm, running-time bound, or complexity classification.

The closest substrate is Theorem 1.4, proved here: for prime-power \(n\), a reference measure and \(d-1\) groups of \(n\) absolutely continuous probability measures admit a convex partition into \(n\) equal-reference-measure pieces that is envy-free for every group. The construction can omit one measure from each group. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says that for any \(\Delta\)-space of partitions into \(n\) pieces and any \(n\) absolutely continuous probability measures, an envy-free partition exists in that space, even without advance access to one measure. The latter builds on a cited KKM theorem. “Can be found” in these statements describes the existence guarantee; it supplies no efficient-finding guarantee.

A credible population regime would be large cohorts of residents with repeated, standardized spatial valuation profiles: \(N\) agents but only \(\tau\ll N\) distinct valuation types. A type is the agent’s complete valuation measure \(\nu_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s population fraction. The paper’s use of measures as preferences makes this a natural extension, though identical full valuation profiles are a substantive assumption.

One possible unanchored research problem is mass-weighted envy-free partition feasibility. Given \(d\), prime-power \(n\), a reference measure \(\lambda\), and \(d-1\) cohorts with finite type supports \(T_r\), rational masses \(\mu^r_t\) summing to \(1\), and absolutely continuous valuations \(\nu^r_t\), ask whether there is an equal-\(\lambda\) partition \(C_1,\ldots,C_n\) and, for each cohort, a flow \(x^r_{t,j}\geq 0\) such that

\[ \sum_j x^r_{t,j}=\mu^r_t,\qquad \sum_t x^r_{t,j}=\frac1n,\qquad x^r_{t,j}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \nu^r_t(C_j)\geq\nu^r_t(C_\ell)\quad\text{for every }\ell. \]

For a concrete finite encoding, the measures could have rational piecewise-constant densities and the partition could be restricted to power diagrams. The solution is the partition and the flows: each unit of population mass gets a whole piece, while a type’s mass may split across pieces only when they are tied among its favorites. If the masses are multiples of \(1/n\), this recovers the discrete matching interpretation. The population is the continuous object; the geometric pieces remain the bundles.

I would leave this problem’s class open. The paper establishes finite-agent existence, not feasibility for arbitrary type masses or a way to compute the partition. The central questions would be whether the mass-flow version is always feasible and, if so, whether it is tractable in \(d,n,\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

That is a reasonable positive research direction, and the authors could recognize it as a high-multiplicity extension of their envy-free partition problem. Its weak point is also decisive for this task: the quota-constrained mass flows introduce a question their theorems do not answer, and the paper gives no computational result to mirror. I would present it as a new problem suggested by the paper, not as a continuous mirror of one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1.4 is a topological existence theorem; “can be found” means a partition exists even when one measure is withheld, not that there is an algorithm with a stated input model or running time. Theorem 3.2 is an even broader existence statement for partitions in a \(\Delta\)-space. Neither gives a complexity question whose high-multiplicity version this paper leaves open.

The stronger versions of those anchors do not fix that gap. One could define a search problem by encoding measures as rational densities and partitions as power diagrams, but choices about the geometric domain, representation, precision, and fairness test would create a new computational problem. To make the population continuous, one could either scale the number of pieces with the number of agents—leaving an output whose size grows with the population—or keep a fixed number of pieces and introduce the proponent’s mass-flow quotas. The latter is a sensible high-multiplicity extension, but its capacity constraints are absent from the paper’s one-agent-per-piece matching. The paper’s existence proof does not establish that those constraints can be met.

That applies to both proposed anchors: Theorem 1.4’s simultaneous fairness for several groups, and Theorem 3.2’s result for arbitrary \(\Delta\)-spaces. In each case, replacing finite lists of observers with masses and quota-constrained flows changes the mathematical question; it does not reveal a computational result hidden in the original theorem. The mass-weighted proposal may be worth studying on its own, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

I would therefore reject this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror. The broader claim that *no* scenario could yield worthwhile work is less secure: repeated, standardized valuation profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting geometric feasibility problem could be interesting. The negative case is strongest on the paper’s lack of computational anchors; it cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile follow-on question.
