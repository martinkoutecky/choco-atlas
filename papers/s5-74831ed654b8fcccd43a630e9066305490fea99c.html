What the mirror covers

Theorem 2 and the other named characterizations are axiomatic; no computational result is covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: a solution is a two-stage solution rationalizable by Pareto-consistent relations \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) if and only if it satisfies PAR, EXP, and WIIA. That is an axiomatic characterization; it asserts no algorithm, tractability, or complexity bound. The other named results are also characterizations. So the paper has no named computational result to anchor the requested continuous problem on.

The strongest plausible population extension would model many bargaining claimants by a finite set of claimant types \(T\), with mass distribution \(\mu\). A feasible outcome would be a measurable welfare profile \(u(t)\) over types, with a disagreement profile \(d(t)\); the arbitrator would first filter profiles using a relation \(P_1\), then select among the survivors using \(P_2\). A population of many claimants sharing a small number of payoff-relevant types is plausible, and this extension preserves the paper’s two-stage choice idea.

But the resulting question—whether a continuum version of Theorem 2’s characterization holds—is axiomatic, not computational. The paper supplies neither a computational problem nor a representation of \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) from which to formulate one. That is the positive case’s decisive weakness under ChoCo’s scope: it is a recognizable population extension of the bargaining framework, but it cannot honestly be presented as a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. Theorem 2 characterizes which axioms are equivalent to a two-stage representation; Theorems 3–5 and Corollary 6 are characterizations too. The proof’s construction of \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) defines relations from a solution and a domain of compact feasible sets. It gives no algorithm for finding a selected outcome, and the paper supplies no finite encoding of those sets or relations from which to ask for a running time.

The strongest population model is still easy to imagine: many claimants drawn from finitely many types, with a feasible welfare profile over types, a Pareto filter, and then a fairness criterion. That makes mass a sensible quantity, and the profile is a natural co-modification of the outcome. But it does not supply the missing computational problem. The paper permits arbitrary relations and admissible domains. To ask for an algorithm, one must choose how feasible profiles and both relations are represented and evaluated. Those choices determine the computational question; they are new structure, not a consequence of the paper’s characterization.

One could build a useful continuous-computation project around a particular bargaining rule and a compact representation of feasible allocations. Nash or equality criteria might be starting points. But that would be a new algorithmic study of population bargaining, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The negative case is strong on that boundary; it does not establish that no adjacent project could be worthwhile.
