The anchor — hardness

Theorem 5

  • Theorem 5 — survived: The opponent engaged it, but the cohort-level mass assignment remains a recognizable incremental stable matching question despite changing the theorem's narrow regime.

The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and vacancy type sets \(U,W\), rational masses \(a_u,b_w\) summing to \(1/2\) on each side, mutually acceptable type pairs, and old and new weak rankings, decide whether there is a new assignment within distance \(\varepsilon\) of a given old stable assignment \(x^1\). Require that ties occur only in vacancy-type rankings and have size at most two, and that the profiles differ by one adjacent swap in the ranking of one positive-mass vacancy type. An assignment \(x\) has \(x_{uw}\geq 0\), with row and column sums at most \(a_u,b_w\); it is stable in profile \(r\) if, for every acceptable \((u,w)\), at least one of \(a_u-\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_u^r w}x_{uw'}\) and \(b_w-\sum_{u':\,u'\succeq_w^r u}x_{u'w}\) is zero. The distance condition is \(\sum_{(u,w)\text{ acceptable}}|x^2_{uw}-x^1_{uw}|\leq\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

A bipartite population of interchangeable applicant and vacancy types with rational masses; \(x^1\) records the old matched mass by type pair, and \(x^2\) records the new assignment. Minimize the \(L_1\) change in type-pair mass, subject to capacities and no positive mass blocking pair. This is a deterministic mass assignment, and the objective is the minimum per-capita symmetric-difference churn over individual realizations.

The objection that survived

The type-pair \(L_1\) distance captures the minimum named-pair churn over realizations, not the churn of every realization; the mirror therefore treats agents within a type as interchangeable.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 5's incremental stable-marriage hardness regime through a cohort analogue of its one-swap restriction and its women-only ties of size at most two. It leaves the paper's Incremental Stable Roommates results, XP algorithms, and master-list and outlier results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this restricted problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of types and the input bit length, or is it NP-hard in the number of types?
  • Does the continuous problem remain hard when one positive-mass type undergoes the single adjacent swap, or does fractional assignment dissolve the tie-based gadget structure?
  • Is the strict-preference version polynomial-time solvable?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Incremental Type-Stable Marriage with Ties. The anchor is Theorem 5 in the published MFCS version, corresponding to the supplied paper’s \(\ISMWties\) result. It is proved here: \(\ISMT\) is W[1]-hard parameterized by the reassignment budget \(k\) plus the total number of ties in the two profiles, even when the profiles differ by one swap and only women have ties. The paper also states that its reduction gives W[1]-hardness parameterized by \(k\) alone when every tie has size at most two, under the same one-swap and women-only restrictions. The proof reduces from the paper’s forced-edge variant, whose hardness is established using results of Marx and Schlotter. [Theorem 5 and its surrounding discussion](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol241-mfcs2022/LIPIcs.MFCS.2022.21/LIPIcs.MFCS.2022.21.pdf)

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large annual market of applicants and individual vacancies. Applicants can share a type when they have the same credentials and rank vacancy categories the same way; vacancies can share a type when they rank applicant categories the same way. A type records the side of the market, its acceptable counterpart types, and its complete preference descriptions before and after the update. Its members are interchangeable in every respect used by the model. For example, a single positive-mass cohort could undergo the same adjacent swap in its ranking. Hundreds of thousands of applicants and vacancies could then be represented by tens or hundreds of types. The paper itself discusses agent types and preference-list similarity, so the cohort structure is a natural extension of its subject. [The paper’s discussion of agent types](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol241-mfcs2022/LIPIcs.MFCS.2022.21/LIPIcs.MFCS.2022.21.pdf)

Here is the mirror problem. Let \(U\) and \(W\) be finite sets of applicant and vacancy types, with rational masses \(a_u,b_w\) satisfying \(\sum_{u\in U}a_u=\sum_{w\in W}b_w=\tfrac12\). Masses are fractions of the whole society. Acceptability is mutual and fixed across the two profiles; each type has a weak ranking of acceptable counterpart types in each profile. An assignment \(x\) has entries \(x_{uw}\geq 0\), with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding type masses; any unused mass is unmatched. The input also includes an old assignment \(x^1\), stable under the old preferences, and a rational threshold \(\varepsilon\). The question is whether there is a new assignment \(x^2\), stable under the updated preferences, such that

\[ \sum_{(u,w)\text{ acceptable}} |x^2_{uw}-x^1_{uw}| \leq \varepsilon . \]

For precision, under weak stability an acceptable pair \((u,w)\) blocks \(x\) in profile \(r\) exactly when both quantities below are positive:

\[ a_u-\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_u^r w}x_{uw'}, \qquad b_w-\sum_{u':\,u'\succeq_w^r u}x_{u'w}. \]

They measure the mass on each side that is unmatched or assigned strictly worse than the proposed partner. In an atomless cohort, any positive mass on both sides can form a blocking mass. A solution is an assignment matrix \(x^2\) satisfying the capacity and stability conditions. It is a deterministic partition of cohorts across partner types, not a lottery over outcomes. In a finite market of \(N\) agents on each side, with masses normalized to the whole society, the distance equals \(|M_1\triangle M_2|/(2N)\); thus \(\varepsilon=k/(2N)\) recovers the reassignment budget’s scale.

I expect continuum-specific hardness (Class C) for the general problem, though I would not claim that Theorem 5’s W[1]-hardness transfers. Its one-swap restriction concerns one discrete agent; in the mirror, a positive-mass cohort makes that same update, and assignments can split mass. Those are material changes to the model. The reason to suspect hardness is that mass stability still imposes a choice for every acceptable pair: one side must have zero mass assigned strictly worse than that pair. Fixing those choices leaves a linear feasibility or optimization problem, but the choices interact across pairs. Enumerating them gives an immediate \(2^{|E|}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) algorithm, where \(E\) is the acceptable type-pair set and \(L\) is the rational-input bit length. The central question is whether that can be improved to polynomial time in the support size \(\tau\) and \(L\), or whether the coupled stability choices make the problem NP-hard in \(\tau\). This is an expectation, not a result.

The mirror covers the paper’s incremental stable-marriage-with-ties hardness result only. Its weakest point is that type-level stability permits fractional mass assignments that the paper’s individual matchings do not. That could dissolve the very tie-based gadget structure behind Theorem 5. So the credible claim is that this is a plausible continuous analogue of the authors’ underlying problem—not that the discrete hardness survives. The next questions are whether hardness persists when only one positive-mass type changes by one adjacent swap and all ties have size at most two, and whether the problem becomes polynomial with strict preferences.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a real computational anchor: it proves W[1]-hardness for incremental stable marriage with ties under a reassignment budget, even when the profiles differ by one swap. I would not argue that the paper lacks a result to mirror, or that its high-multiplicity neighbors count against it. Large cohorts in a matching market are plausible.

The strongest mirror is a type-based market with rational masses, old and new assignments, and stability defined by whether positive mass on both sides would prefer a type-pair to its current assignments. One could ask whether a stable new assignment exists within an \(\varepsilon\) distance of the old one. That is a coherent computational question. The difficulty is that the natural type-level distance no longer measures the paper’s objective. The paper counts changes to named pairs. An aggregate assignment matrix records how much mass is matched between types, but not whether Alice still has Bob: an assignment can keep every type-pair count unchanged while replacing nearly every individual partnership. If the mirror charges only for mass moving between type-pairs, it studies changes in relationship categories, not the paper’s minimum symmetric difference. If it charges for changed individual pairs, it must retain enough identity-level information to distinguish those pairs, undermining the type compression.

The one-swap restriction sharpens the mismatch. In a market of \(N\) agents, one person’s swap affects mass \(1/N\), which vanishes in the continuum limit. Making a positive-mass cohort perform that swap gives a meaningful limit, but turns the theorem’s single-agent change into a bulk preference shock. The forced-pair and tie gadgets also rely on particular agents and their particular partners. A mass model can ask a related question, but it does not preserve the specific hardness regime that makes Theorem 5 distinctive.

That is a substantial case against this anchor as a *mirror* of the paper’s result. It is not a proof that the aggregate stable-assignment problem lacks value: that problem could be worth studying on its own, and the authors might recognize it as a useful extension. The negative case is weakest there; what I can defend is that its value cannot simply be claimed on the basis of Theorem 5 without changing the objective or losing the one-swap setting.
