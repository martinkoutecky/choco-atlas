The continuous mirror question

Given a finite non-bipartite graph \(H=(T,E)\) of types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), shared weak rankings, quotas \(q_t=\mu_t\), and rational partnership-mass capacities \(b_e\leq\min(\mu_t,\mu_u)\), find an exact rational mass assignment \(x\) satisfying the quotas and capacities that is stable: no residual-capacity edge \(e=tu\) admits an arbitrarily small \(\varepsilon>0\) that both endpoint choice functions accept on \(e\).

The model it lives in

An atomless cohort market on a finite type graph: type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), a shared weak ranking, and total quota \(\mu_t\); edge \(e\) has partnership-mass capacity \(b_e\); decision variables \(x_e\) are matched mass.

The objection that survived

The case leaves unformalized whether blocking in a finite replicated cohort market converges to its infinitesimal-mass blocking rule; this leaves the discrete-to-continuous dictionary open.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 6.2 for the non-bipartite relaxed stable allocation problem with weak orders, quotas, and real capacities. It leaves the integer choice-function results, including Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 6.1, without a continuous extension.

Open questions for a prover

  • What finite replicated-market conditions make individual blocking correspond to the mirror’s infinitesimal-mass blocking?
  • What bit-complexity bound applies to exact rational output for rational \(\mu\) and \(b\)?
  • Can a stable mass assignment be rounded to a stable or approximately stable finite matching?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: the paper’s real-valued, weak-order partnership model can be read as a market among large cohorts of identical agents. The paper contains no named hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor to prefer. My lead anchor is Theorem 6.2, stated and proved here using earlier rotational-poset machinery: for the non-bipartite relaxed stable allocation problem with real edge capacities and quotas, where preferences come from weak linear orders, a stable assignment always exists and can be found in strongly polynomial time. It concerns the underlying partnership problem, not just an encoding.

Here is a population version I think the authors would recognize. Call it Continuous Cohort Stable Partnership. An instance has a finite, possibly non-bipartite graph \(H=(T,E)\) of eligible partnerships between agent types, and a rational population distribution \(\mu\) with \(\mu_t>0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Cohort \(t\) consists of an atomless mass \(\mu_t\) of agents. Its members share a weak ranking of eligible partner types and a per-agent quota; for the one-to-one case, the total quota for the cohort is \(q_t=\mu_t\). Each edge \(e=tu\) has a rational capacity \(b_e\), measured as partnership mass, with \(b_e\leq\min(\mu_t,\mu_u)\) in the one-to-one case. A type’s choice function \(C_t\) applies its ranking and quota to offered partnership mass, filling higher-ranked options first and splitting mass at a tie as in the paper’s mixed-type model.

A solution is a mass vector \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{E}\) with \(x_e\leq b_e\) and \(C_t(x_t)=x_t\) for every type. It must also be stable: there is no edge \(e=tu\) with residual capacity for which both \(t\) and \(u\) would accept some arbitrarily small additional mass on \(e\), according to \(C_t\) and \(C_u\). The question is: given \((H,\mu,b,(C_t)_{t\in T})\), find an exact stable partnership mass vector \(x\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large, unpartitioned partnership market of professionals might have millions of participants but only dozens or hundreds of relevant types, defined by specialization, eligibility, quota, and ranking. The graph can contain triangles, so neither side is permanently the “worker” side or the “firm” side. For a population of size \(N\), \(\mu_t\) records the fraction of participants of type \(t\), while \(x_e\) records the fraction of the population’s partnership units on type-pair \(e\). Normalizing quotas and edge capacities by population size is a necessary co-modification: the model’s resources must grow with the cohort. Idiosyncratic preferences or quotas become separate types, as high multiplicity requires.

The expected answer is Class A: tractable. The input graph has \(\tau=|T|\) vertices, regardless of the number \(N\) of agents. Theorem 6.2 supplies existence and a strongly polynomial construction on that type graph; with rational data, the computational question is exact mass output, with bit complexity also accounted for in the input and output encoding. This is a direct mirror of the paper’s weak-order, quota-filling partnership problem, not a softened question that drops stability. The paper’s more general integer choice-function setting is outside this case.

The mirror generates useful follow-ups: how closely can a stable mass vector be rounded to a stable or approximately stable matching in a finite \(N\)-agent market, especially when the integer model has indivisibility obstructions? Can the full set of stable mass vectors be described or computed efficiently, rather than just finding one? These are separate questions; Theorem 6.2 does not settle the finite-population rounding issue.

The weak point is that the paper already allows real-valued assignments, so this mirror could be mistaken for outcome-space continuity dressed up as population continuity. The population interpretation needs to do real work: \(\mu\) is the society’s input, and quotas and capacities are cohort-level quantities scaled with it. The theorem’s finite graph is then the support of that distribution, not a list of named people. I think that is a credible high-multiplicity reading, but a formal dictionary from finite replicated populations to these cohort choice functions would make the case much stronger.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cohort model is credible: let each vertex \(t\) represent a cohort, set its quota to \(q(t)=\mu_t\), and let \(x_{tu}\) be the mass matched between cohorts \(t\) and \(u\). Give each cohort its shared weak ranking of eligible partner types and set edge capacities in partnership-mass units. This is a genuine high-multiplicity interpretation, with no individual identity needed in the stability question.

That is also exactly the model computationally solved by Theorem 6.2. Its graph can be the type graph; its real-valued assignments are partnership masses; its quotas and edge capacities encode cohort sizes and feasible matches. For quota-filling choices from weak rankings, the paper’s stability condition becomes the cohort condition that neither endpoint accepts an additional positive amount on a residual edge. The theorem already finds such an assignment in strongly polynomial time in the graph size. This objection rests on an exact match to a continuous-population computational model, not merely on the fact that the paper permits fractional outcomes.

Making the population distribution \(\mu\) explicit adds no new question when \(q(t)=\mu_t\). Scaling a finite cohort population by \(N\) gives quotas \(N\mu_t\) and correspondingly scaled capacities; the proposed exact mass problem remains the same stable-assignment problem. A request to round the mass solution to a stable finite matching, or to characterize all stable assignments, could be worthwhile, but it asks for additional results beyond the anchor.

This defeats the proponent’s sole anchor as a *new* continuous mirror. It does not establish that no useful mirror exists for the paper’s broader integer choice-function model: the paper supplies no canonical real-valued aggregate choice rule for that generality, and a principled one could lead to a new problem. I cannot rule that avenue out.
