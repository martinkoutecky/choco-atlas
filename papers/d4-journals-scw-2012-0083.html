What the mirror covers

The characterization theorems and Section 4’s counting formulas concern functions over finite electorates; neither supplies a named computational result with a continuous-population analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive observation is that this paper has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large electorate could be represented by masses on a small set of weak-ranking types. The closest named result is Theorem 3.8, proved here: an SWF satisfies IIA if and only if it is clerical-dictatorial. But that is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. The same is true of Theorems 3.6 and 3.7. Section 4 gives formulas for counting finite-electorate SWFs, but it states no named result about computational complexity.

So there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous problem under your rubric. One could define a rule on distributions of voter types and ask whether it satisfies continuous versions of IIA or WPP. That would make the population continuous, but the question would still be axiomatic—the stated scope excludes it—and the paper does not establish a computational result for it. The finite counting formulas also have no natural mass-based counterpart: they count functions whose domains depend on a finite number of voters.

My weakest point is decisive: although the electorate can sensibly be large with few types, turning that regime into a computational question anchored in this paper would require adding a new problem the authors did not study. I cannot give a defensible continuous prover question per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case has no computational anchor to preserve. The electorate can certainly be large and concentrated on a few weak-ranking types, but that only makes a continuous population plausible; it does not turn the paper’s results into computational questions.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 3.8, with Theorems 3.6 and 3.7 close behind. They characterize which social welfare functions satisfy axioms. A continuous version would classify functionals on distributions of voter types under continuous versions of IIA, WPP, or citizens’ sovereignty. That may be an interesting question, but it remains axiomatic, which is outside this programme’s scope. Turning the characterization into a decision or evaluation algorithm would require adding an encoding for an arbitrary social welfare function; the resulting computational problem would depend on that new encoding and would not mirror a problem studied here.

The counting formulas in Section 4 are the closest alternative, but they count maps from profiles of *labelled* voters to rankings. A distribution over types forgets which voter supplied each preference, while the characterized functions can depend on a particular voter’s role, including the dictator’s. One could preserve a distinguished voter as an atom, or enrich types with organizational roles, but then the result would still be a count or classification of functions, not a computational problem from the paper. Making it one requires inventing a new target.

So the case against is strong at the paper level: the paper offers no named computational result for a continuous mirror to inherit. The plausible high-multiplicity electorate does not fill that gap. This does not prove that no computational question inspired by the paper could ever be worthwhile; it means such a question would be a new research direction, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
