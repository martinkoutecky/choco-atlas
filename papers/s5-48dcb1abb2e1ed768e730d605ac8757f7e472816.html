The anchor — hardness

Theorem 5.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5.1 — survived: A category-ranked mass version is a recognizable balanced-matching extension, although the theorem's reduction does not establish hardness for it.

The continuous mirror question

Given finite candidate and vacancy type sets \(I,J\), positive rational masses \(a_i,b_j\) summing to one on each side, strict rankings of acceptable opposite-side types, and a rational threshold \(K\), decide whether there is a stable mass assignment \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq a_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq b_j\), and \(\max\{D_I(x),D_J(x)\}\leq K\), where \(D_I(x)=\sum_{ij}r_i(j)x_{ij}\) and \(D_J(x)=\sum_{ij}s_j(i)x_{ij}\). For every acceptable pair \((i,j)\), stability requires \(U_{ij}=0\) or \(V_{ij}=0\), where \(U_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) unmatched or assigned below \(j\), and \(V_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(j\) unmatched or assigned below \(i\). Let \(\Omega_I,\Omega_J\) be the minimum respective dissatisfaction over stable mass assignments and define the rational excess \(\theta=K-\max\{\Omega_I,\Omega_J\}\); determine the decision problem's complexity and whether a meaningful parameterization by this excess requires a specified population scale.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-sided placement market with candidate and vacancy types, each type's mass and preference ranking over opposite-side types, and a mass matrix as the matching decision. Stability rules out any acceptable pair for which positive mass on both sides would prefer to match; the objective minimizes the larger side's total rank-weighted dissatisfaction.

The objection that survived

The paper's strict rankings over named individuals do not aggregate exactly to rankings over partner types, and its reduction gives no protection against fractional splitting; the rational excess also needs a chosen mass scale before parameterized hardness can be claimed.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

This mirrors only Theorem 5.1's \(\maxBSM\) threshold question; the Above-Min kernel result and Theorem 4.1's \(\mathcal{O}^*(8^t)\) algorithm remain unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the threshold problem admit an efficient algorithm, or is it hard as the number of types grows?
  • How do fractional stable assignments affect the optimum compared with assignments restricted to a chosen finite population granularity?
  • What population scale, if any, makes the excess \(\theta\) a useful parameter for a parameterized-complexity result?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Above-Max Balanced Stable Mass Matching. Consider a large graduate-placement market with one candidate per vacancy. Candidate types are defined by credentials and a strict ranking of acceptable vacancy types; vacancy types are defined by role and location and rank acceptable candidate types. A type includes every attribute the matching problem uses, so members of a type are interchangeable. The market might have hundreds of thousands of candidates and vacancies but only dozens or hundreds of preference types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: mass records the fraction of candidates or vacancies of each type.

My anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper: \(\maxBSM\) is W[1]-hard parameterized by \(t=K-\max\{\Omega_M,\Omega_W\}\). Here the input is a stable-marriage instance and target \(K\); \(\Omega_M\) and \(\Omega_W\) are the least total dissatisfaction attainable by the men and women, respectively, over stable matchings; and the question is whether some stable matching has balance at most \(K\), where balance is the larger of the two sides’ total dissatisfaction. The paper proves the theorem via Lemma 5.1: it reduces from Clique in fixed-parameter time, producing an instance with \(t=6(q+\binom q2)\) for a Clique target of \(q\). Clique’s W[1]-hardness is cited from elsewhere; this paper proves the reduction.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. Give two finite sets of types \(I,J\), with positive rational masses \(a_i,b_j\) summing to one on each side. Each type has a strict ranking of its acceptable types on the other side. A candidate solution is a nonnegative mass matrix \(x=(x_{ij})\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le a_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le b_j\); unmatched mass is allowed. For an acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(u_{ij}\) be type \(i\)’s mass unmatched or assigned to types it ranks below \(j\), and let \(v_{ij}\) be type \(j\)’s mass unmatched or assigned to types it ranks below \(i\). The assignment is stable exactly when, for every acceptable pair, \(u_{ij}=0\) or \(v_{ij}=0\): otherwise positive amounts on both sides could match and improve.

Write \(D_M(x)=\sum_{ij}x_{ij}r_i(j)\) and \(D_W(x)=\sum_{ij}x_{ij}s_j(i)\), using the rank of each partner type. Let \(\Omega_M\) and \(\Omega_W\) be the minimum of the respective side’s dissatisfaction over stable mass assignments, and let \(\operatorname{Bal}_\infty=\min_{x\text{ stable}}\max\{D_M(x),D_W(x)\}\). Given a rational threshold \(K\), Above-Max Balanced Stable Mass Matching asks whether \(\operatorname{Bal}_\infty\le K\). Its natural excess is \(\theta=K-\max\{\Omega_M,\Omega_W\}\). A witness is the mass matrix \(x\). These are precisely the paper’s stability and balance objectives applied to type-level masses; the co-modification is that each person’s rank is now a rank of partner types.

I expect the threshold problem to remain hard as the number of types grows. The Clique choices in the paper’s reduction live in the preference structure, and distinct preference types can preserve that combinatorial structure even when each type represents mass. This is a Class B prediction: population size can dissolve combinatorics that depend only on how many named people there are, but it does not automatically remove structure encoded in the types. The question for a prover is whether the reduction can be made to survive fractional mass transfers, or whether a rigidity result forces the relevant stable mass assignments to retain the discrete choices.

The weakest point is that the theorem does not establish this transfer. Its reduction uses detailed rankings of named individuals, and its population is not shown to consist of a small number of repeated types. In the mirror, a type may split its mass among partners, and that can create stable assignments unavailable in the discrete instance. There is also a parameter-scaling issue: the paper’s \(t\) is an integer total cost, while normalized mass makes \(\theta\) rational. Any W[1]-hardness claim for the parameterized mass problem needs a stated unit of population mass; the theorem alone cannot supply one. This mirror covers Theorem 5.1’s Above-Max result only, not the paper’s Above-Min kernel and FPT algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.1 is a genuine computational result, but the proposed mass model does not preserve its problem. In the paper, each person ranks named partners, and those individual ranks determine both blocking and dissatisfaction. A type-to-type mass matrix forgets which members of a partner type receive the mass. If they occupy different rank positions, that choice changes the objective; it can also change which pairs block. Preserving those details requires refining types by their rank signatures. Under strict rankings, those signatures can make the types essentially individual.

The proposed repair—rank partner *types* instead—gives a coherent model, but changes the preference structure to tiers over categories. Its stability condition asks whether positive masses on both sides can improve, and its costs use ranks of categories. That is a sensible tied, capacity-like matching problem. It is not automatically the paper’s Above-Max Balanced Stable Marriage problem. The distinction matters for this anchor: Theorem 5.1 proves hardness for strict rankings over individuals, and its Clique gadgets encode choices through identity-specific acceptable pairs and ranks. The reduction does not supply a high-multiplicity instance of the category-ranked model.

There is also no canonical continuation of the theorem’s parameter. In the paper, \(t\) is an integer excess, and a person’s worse rank raises dissatisfaction by at least one. With normalized masses, moving mass \(\epsilon\) can raise the excess by only \(\epsilon\), so the mirror’s \(\theta\) is rational and depends on the chosen per-capita scale. Turning it into an integer parameter requires a population granularity that the distribution \(\mu\) does not contain. That leaves a well-posed threshold question, but not yet a well-defined analogue of the paper’s parameterized hardness claim.

This is the strongest objection I can make to the anchor: the paper’s theorem does not transfer to the proposed model, and an exact aggregation of its strict-rank semantics has no evident high-multiplicity regime. It does not establish that the category-ranked mass problem is unworthy. If the authors would recognize that tied, type-level variant as their problem, it remains a plausible continuous mirror; the negative case is weak precisely there.
