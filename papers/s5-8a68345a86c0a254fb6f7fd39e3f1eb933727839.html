Proposition 2 bounds an expected welfare statistic under equilibrium selection; Proposition 3 bounds the expected average rank at the two stable-matching extremes. Proposition 1 is a prior asymptotic result about truthful play. These are claims about market performance, not computational problems with an input, output, and running-time question.

The strongest mirror of Proposition 2 would take a distribution over student preference types and school priority types, then ask for the mass of students assigned their top choice in a stable mass assignment. Proposition 3 suggests asking for the minimum and maximum average rank across such assignments. Type masses, fractional aggregate placements, and category-based priorities could make those sensible matching models; indivisible assignments to individual students do not rule them out. But the paper’s propositions do not ask how to compute these quantities. Proposition 2 quantifies over selections without requiring that a selection be computed, and Proposition 3 identifies its endpoints through the student-optimal and school-optimal stable matchings. Turning either into an algorithmic problem requires specifying a new type law, priority model, and input encoding. Any resulting complexity question would be a research direction of its own, not a computational result mirrored from this paper.

There is also a mismatch between the paper’s random-market object and the natural high-multiplicity input. In its balanced random markets, school priorities rank named students; retaining all those priorities in a student’s complete type generally leaves each realized type with negligible mass. Grouping students by preference alone discards priority information, while adding priority scores or categories changes the model. That does not make a mass matching formulation invalid, but it reinforces that the authors’ asymptotic claims do not supply its computational problem.

So the negative case is strong under the paper-specific anchor requirement: none of the proposed mirrors is anchored in a named computational result. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous matching problem could arise in any scenario is harder to defend.
