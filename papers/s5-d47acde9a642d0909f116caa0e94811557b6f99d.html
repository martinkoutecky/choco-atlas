Why no mirror

The manuscript contains no named result about computing a solution, an algorithmic guarantee, or the complexity of a computational task. Theorem 1–3 are structural incentive results, so the computational-result requirement fails. A target-set rule can be extended to a population distribution, but evaluating that rule does not mirror a computational result in the paper.

What the mirror covers

The target-set rule has a natural distributional extension based on the extreme supported peaks. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3, and the benchmark claims concern axioms or impossibility, and none supplies a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is a large population of residents choosing a public facility location, with many residents sharing each of a relatively small number of complete single-peaked preference types. A distribution \(\mu\) over those types would represent population shares. For a fixed target interval \([c,d]\), the natural extension of the paper’s target rule would choose \([c,d]\cap[p_-,p_+]\) when that intersection is nonempty, where \(p_-\) and \(p_+\) are the smallest and largest supported peaks; otherwise it chooses the nearest extreme peak. That gives a clear continuous-population object, but it does not provide a computational result to mirror.

The paper has no named computational result asserting membership in P, hardness, fixed-parameter tractability, or a comparable complexity claim. The closest author-proved results are Theorem 1, which says that pairwise strategy-proofness forces anonymity among minimally flexible generalized median correspondences; Theorem 2, which characterizes minimally flexible, strategy-proof, voter-sovereign, pairwise strategy-proof correspondences as target set correspondences; and Theorem 3, which says no minimally flexible generalized median correspondence can satisfy both voter sovereignty and pairwise strategy-proofness under the pessimistic extension. These are axiomatic and structural results, not computational anchors.

The main obstacle to turning Theorem 2 into a computational mirror is its pairwise incentive condition. In an atomless society, two agents have zero mass, so their reports do not change \(\mu\). Requiring deviations by positive-mass groups instead would change “pairwise” strategy-proofness and thus the theorem’s question. The target-set rule is a plausible continuous analogue of the outcome, but the paper’s central result is about axioms on a fixed finite electorate. Under the programme’s scope, I would not offer it as a qualifying mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The residents choosing a public-facility location are a credible high-multiplicity population. A distribution over peak types is sensible here, and the target-set rule has a natural extension: intersect \([c,d]\) with the essential range between the society’s lowest and highest peaks, using the nearest extreme when that intersection is empty. So the objection is not that this setting lacks meaningful masses.

The decisive gap is that none of the paper’s results gives a computational problem or complexity claim to mirror. Theorem 1 derives anonymity from pairwise strategy-proofness; Theorem 2 characterizes target-set correspondences through incentive axioms; Theorem 3 gives an impossibility under the pessimistic extension. Their continuous versions would still be axiomatic questions. Evaluating the target rule on a finite-support distribution takes a scan for its extreme peaks, but that is a new, routine evaluation task, not a computational counterpart to any theorem in the paper.

The strongest attempt to make Theorem 1 survive illustrates a deeper mismatch. In an atomless society, two agents have zero mass, so their deviation leaves the distribution unchanged. Requiring deviations by positive-mass groups could yield a meaningful population-level incentive condition, but it replaces “at most two voters” with a different coalition concept. The same issue affects Theorem 3. Theorem 2’s target-set structure survives more naturally as a rule on distributions, but turning its characterization into a computational question requires adding a task—such as finding a manipulation of bounded mass—along with a cost model, input representation, and objective that the paper does not specify.

That manipulation problem may be worth studying. Its interest would come from those added choices, not from a computational result in this paper; support-based rules also make the effects of tiny masses depend sharply on whether they change the support’s extremes. The strongest defensible negative is therefore that this manuscript has no qualifying computational mirror under ChoCo’s stated aim. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile population-level computational question could grow from it is weaker: the location model supplies a sound population object, and a carefully designed new problem could still be useful.
