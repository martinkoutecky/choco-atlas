The continuous mirror question

Given finite seller and buyer type sets \(S\) and \(B\), binary-rational type masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), and the paper's valuation functions and integer price bounds for each type pair, compute a stable mass matching. Represent it by \(\lambda_{ijp}\ge 0\), the mass of type-\(i\) sellers matched to type-\(j\) buyers at integer price \(p\), with \(\sum_{j,p}\lambda_{ijp}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_{i,p}\lambda_{ijp}\le\nu_j\); require individual rationality and no positive-mass seller and buyer subgroups that can both strictly improve by trading at any feasible price.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete seller and buyer types, with each agent still able to make at most one indivisible trade. The aggregate decision is a mass allocation \(\lambda_{ijp}\) over type pairs and integer prices; unmatched mass receives the outside option, and stability rules out mutually profitable positive-mass subgroups.

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational-complexity anchor in this paper. The closest named results are Theorem 6.5, proved here, which says the proposed algorithm terminates after finitely many iterations, and Theorem 6.6, also proved here, which says that when it terminates, its output is individually rational and has no blocking buyer–seller pair. Together they give a constructive existence result for the paper’s finite market with bounded integer prices. They do not establish that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class.

The paper itself marks that gap: its conclusion says the algorithm’s complexity may depend on the price-interval length and calls a polynomial-time algorithm an open problem. So treating Theorem 6.5 as a tractability result would overstate what is proved.

A plausible population mirror would use a fixed set of seller and buyer types, each with a complete valuation and price-bound description, and assign each type a mass. A solution would be a one-to-one matching measure with integer transfers, individually rational and with no positive-mass group able to block. Large markets with many agents but few recurring types could make that a sensible question. But it would mirror the paper’s stable-outcome existence result, not a named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. I can’t honestly present it as the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.5 and Theorem 6.6 do not establish a computational complexity result. Theorem 6.5 proves that the proposed algorithm eventually stops; it gives no bound on its running time in the input size. The paper’s conclusion says that the runtime may depend on the price interval and leaves a polynomial-time algorithm as an open problem. Theorem 6.6 proves that a terminating run produces a stable outcome. Together, they give constructive existence, not a complexity classification or an efficiency guarantee. Treating finite termination as tractability would blur exactly the distinction this programme is meant to study.

The strongest proposed mirror—a large market with a few recurring buyer and seller types, represented by a mass matching and a stability condition—could be a sensible model. Exact repetition of types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime here. But asking for the computational complexity of finding a stable mass matching would require defining and studying a new computational problem; this paper supplies no complexity result for the finite problem to mirror. Asking only whether such an outcome exists would mirror the paper’s existence question, but would not be the computational inquiry ChoCo prioritizes.

So the negative case is strong against using this paper as a qualifying computational anchor. It is weak as a claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the paper’s model does admit a plausible population continuization. I would reject the case for this paper under the programme’s anchor criterion, while leaving open whether that new mass-matching problem is worth studying on its own.
