What the mirror covers

Theorem 4 and the paper’s other numbered results concern existence and welfare properties; none supplies a computational result for a continuous analogue. Money is divisible, but the paper’s society remains finite.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible population regime is a large employer allocating repeated task categories and salary among employees of the same rank. A type would include an employee’s task valuations and externality parameters—the full information the allocation problem uses. Mass would be the fraction of employees of each type; task supplies and the salary budget would scale per employee. Each employee would still receive one indivisible task. That looks like a credible high-multiplicity version of a problem Velez discusses, and the paper’s linear-externality setting makes it especially plausible.

But there is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 4 is the closest result: with linear externalities strictly bounded by \(1/(n-1)\), noncontestable allocations exist when the budget is large enough, and every such allocation is efficient. That is an existence and welfare result, not a result about computational complexity. The other numbered theorems and corollaries likewise establish existence or efficiency; none classifies a problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized-tractable.

So I cannot honestly provide the required continuous problem *per anchor* or predict how an anchor’s complexity behaves under continuization. The paper does offer a credible setting for a future continuous-population question about finding noncontestable task-and-salary allocations, but that would be a new computational question inspired by its economics—not a mirror of a named computational result. The paper’s divisible money is outcome-space continuity; its population remains finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is not a computational anchor. It says that, under a bound of \(1/(n-1)\) on linear externalities, noncontestable allocations exist when the budget is large enough, and that those allocations are efficient. The other numbered results likewise establish existence or welfare properties. None states a complexity result for a computational problem. So the proposed task of *finding* a noncontestable allocation adds an algorithmic question the paper did not ask; it does not mirror a named computational result.

The stronger employee scenario may be a sensible high-multiplicity model: group employees by their full preference and externality type, use repeated task categories, and scale the budget per employee. But to make “find an allocation” a complexity question, one must specify a finite input for the type and task data and an output and precision standard. The paper supplies no algorithmic benchmark for that task. Even a compelling formalization would be a new computational project inspired by Velez’s economics, rather than a continuous mirror of one of his computational results.

That is a strong reason to reject this paper as a ChoCo mirror. It does not establish the universal claim that no scenario could make the resulting allocation problem worth studying: the repeated employee setting is plausible, and the honest negative case is weaker on that broader claim. Its decisive point is narrower: none of the proponent’s discussion identifies a computational result in this paper for the continuous mirror to preserve.
