The continuous mirror question

Given a binary-rational type distribution \(\mu\) over per-node weight levels \(w_t>0\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t w_t=1\), population scale \(N\), and \(k\) queries per node, sort types by weight to form the non-increasing rank profile \(a_\mu(u)\), \(u\in[0,1]\). Choose a cutoff \(q\in[0,1]\), allowing a boundary type’s mass to be split, so that the top mass \(q\) gossips and the rest answers individual queries. Find a cutoff minimizing \(C(q)=\max\{Nq,\;k\,\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{u>q}a_\mu(u)\}\), taking the second term as zero if no mass remains.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over node types carrying per-node weights \(w_t\) and identical query-response behavior within each type. The decision variable is a mass cutoff \(q\) in descending weight order; the objective balances the gossip burden \(Nq\) against the largest expected query load \(k\,\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{u>q}a_\mu(u)\) among non-gossiping nodes.

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Lemma 2’s Zipf-profile cutoff result to finite-support type distributions. It leaves Theorem 1’s fairness characterization, Lemma 1’s rank-specific load asymptotics, and the Byzantine simulations and consensus-performance analysis outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a finite-support input \((\mu,w)\), characterize the exact optimal cutoff and its cost, including when the cutoff splits the mass of a type.
  • Which assumptions on the type-weight profile preserve an \(O(\sqrt{N})\) worst-node message burden?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: the paper’s fair sampling rule has a direct population mirror, and its gossip threshold gives a second, more tentative mirror about communication cost. Neither is a hardness result. The paper has no named NP-hardness, FPT, or similar classification; its relevant named results concern fairness and message complexity.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: when votes within a sampled quorum are unweighted (\(g\equiv 1\)), the scheme is fair—robust to both splitting and merging—if and only if the node-selection function is the identity, \(f(m)=m\). The theorem applies across quorum sizes and weight distributions.

A natural high-multiplicity regime is a large ledger network with many devices in a small number \(\tau\) of complete types: the same mana level, current binary opinion, and response behavior. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population fraction and \(m_t\) its per-node normalized weight. Write \(\bar m=\sum_t\mu_t m_t\). In the continuous society, the mass of a group \(A\subseteq T\) is \(\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t\), and its share of total mana is \(\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t m_t/\bar m\).

The corresponding design question, Mass-Fair Sampling\(_\infty\), is: given the finite set of possible rational weight levels, choose a sampling score \(F(m)\) so that, for every distribution of mass over those types, every quorum size \(k\), and every group \(A\), the group’s expected share of the unweighted sampled opinions equals its share of total mana. With score \(F\), the group’s expected sampling share is
\[ \frac{\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t F(m_t)}{\sum_t\mu_t F(m_t)}. \]
A solution is a score function satisfying the equality for all such societies. The universal solution is \(F(m)=m\), up to an irrelevant positive scale factor; normalizing the score fixes it to the identity. For a particular finite support, the resulting type-level sampling probabilities are computed with \(O(\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations.

This is a population mirror of the paper’s problem, not a change to fractional outcomes: each type’s mass is a fraction of the network, and the queried opinions remain binary. The authors would likely recognize the question, since it preserves their definition of voting power and their splitting-and-merging criterion. The expected direction is tractable: proportional weighting gives the rule directly. Further questions include whether weighted ballots (\(g\ne1\)) can be characterized over type masses, and how first-round voting power relates to final consensus outcomes under Byzantine behavior. This mirror covers the fairness design result, not the paper’s full dynamic or adversarial analysis.

A second anchor is Lemma 2, proved in the paper: assuming Zipf-distributed weights with parameters \(s\) and \(N\), there is a gossip threshold that makes every node process the same order of messages, with per-node message complexity \(O(\sqrt N)\) for every \(s\).

Its continuous counterpart is Type-Gossip Cutoff\(_\infty\). The input is a population of total scale \(N\), represented by a finite-support mass distribution over types with normalized per-person weight profile \(a(u)\), where \(u\in[0,1]\) is population rank and \(\int_0^1 a(u)\,du=1\). Each node makes \(k\) queries, with targets sampled proportional to weight. Choose a cutoff \(q\): the top fraction \(q\) gossips its opinions, while the rest answers individual queries. The question is to find \(q\) minimizing the worst per-node message burden
\[ C(q)=\max\left\{Nq,\; k\,\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{u>q}a(u)\right\}. \]
Here \(Nq\) is the number of gossiped opinions a node must process, and the second term is the largest expected query load among the remaining types. A solution is an optimal cutoff and its cost. For a finite type profile, sorting the types and checking the possible cutoffs solves this in polynomial time in \(\tau\).

The paper’s Zipf profile is a special, fine-grained case: for rank \(h\), \(a_h=Nh^{-s}/H_{N,s}\), where \(H_{N,s}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}j^{-s}\). Gossipping the top \(h\) ranks gives a gossip burden of order \(h\); the query load at the boundary has the order used in the lemma’s balancing argument. The mirror predicts a tractable cutoff problem. It also suggests a finite-support extension: for any non-increasing profile of mean \(1\), taking \(q=N^{-1/2}\) gives gossip burden \(\sqrt N\), while the remaining query load is at most \(k\sqrt N\). That bound follows from monotonicity and the mean constraint; it is an inference for the mirror, not a claim proved in the paper.

The main weakness is that the paper’s exact Zipf analysis gives distinct ranks, so its support is effectively \(\tau=N\). A fixed-\(\tau\) population of repeated weight types is a plausible ledger regime, but Lemma 2 does not directly establish its sharper Zipf exponents there. The fairness mirror is stronger on that point: it fits finite type support exactly, though Theorem 1 is a structural protocol result rather than a standard complexity-class theorem. The case for a continuous mirror rests on these specific fairness and communication questions, not on a claim that the paper already supplies a complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result in the programme’s sense. Theorem 1 characterizes a fairness axiom; Lemmas 1 and 2 derive message-load bounds under a Zipf model. None defines an algorithmic problem whose complexity the paper classifies. A continuous computational problem could be built around these topics, but it would need a new objective and input model; the named results do not supply one.

The strongest mirror of Theorem 1 is just its aggregation over types. Suppose type \(t\) has \(r_t\) nodes, each with weight \(m_t\). Its share of queries under sampling score \(f\) is
\[ \frac{r_t f(m_t)}{\sum_u r_u f(m_u)}. \]
Writing \(\mu_t=r_t/N\) gives the proposed mass expression. Every binary-rational \(\mu_t\) can be represented exactly by choosing a common denominator and replicating the types. The theorem also already considers arbitrary real split ratios. So the proposed Mass-Fair Sampling question adds no computational input beyond grouping the theorem’s nodes. Treating the score as an unknown asks for a universal functional characterization; fixing a finite support asks for arithmetic on that support. Neither yields the computational landscape the programme targets.

Theorem 1’s result is not made uninteresting because the same rule works in both models. The obstacle is that the mirror has no per-instance algorithmic task to classify. A more substantial task—such as choosing a sampling rule under capacity constraints—would introduce constraints absent from the paper and would need its own justification.

Lemma 2’s best mirror has a similar problem. For a finite-support, non-increasing weight profile, the proposed objective
\[ C(q)=\max\left\{Nq,\;k\,\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{u>q}a(u)\right\} \]
balances one increasing term against one decreasing term. In a type profile, the relevant cutoffs occur at support breakpoints or at a crossing of the two terms. Sorting the types and checking those candidates is a small one-dimensional optimization. The Zipf-specific asymptotics are already the paper’s result; extending the cutoff calculation to arbitrary profiles could help tune a protocol, but it does not produce a difficult complexity question. Adding realistic routing, bandwidth, or neighborhood constraints could make the problem richer, while also changing the cost model and making network position part of the type. That would be a new network-design problem, not a mirror of Lemma 2.

There is a stronger direction nearby: model a large, fully mixed population by weight, opinion, and termination-counter types, then ask how much Byzantine mass is needed to cause a specified failure. A population limit for that process may be sensible. But the paper does not state that computational problem as a result: its adversary is specified and its findings are simulations. Turning the idea into a complexity question requires adding an optimization target or a choice of adversarial strategies. That might be a worthwhile project, but it does not rescue either proposed anchor.

So the case against these anchors is strong: one is an aggregation of an existing axiom characterization, and the other leads to a simple cutoff calculation. The broader claim that no continuous FPC question could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend. A new Byzantine-control problem is the negative case’s weakest point; I would not rule it out categorically, but it is a separate proposal rather than evidence that these named results support a continuous computational mirror.
