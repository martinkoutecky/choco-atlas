What the mirror covers

The proposed query covers Proposition 3’s mixed-strategy equilibrium and its Bayes-consistent signal beliefs for \(\rho>F(x_{n/2})\); it leaves the other equilibrium regimes, the informed-voter extension, and comparative-statics results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity electorate of voters grouped by ideal point. Each voter’s complete type is their ideal point \(x_i\), together with the paper’s shared utility, information, and tie-breaking rules. The mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of voters with that type. Two candidates choose platforms, possibly using mixed strategies. After voters observe the relative-position signal, they vote according to their posterior expected utility; the candidates’ payoff is their share of the electorate’s votes.

There is a close fit to the paper: its footnote 3 explicitly gives the alternative interpretation of infinitely many voters with ideal points distributed according to \(F\), where candidates maximize vote share. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated ideal-point types, so the number of voters \(N\) greatly exceeds the number of types \(\tau\). For the result below, the types are the \(n\) points in the paper’s policy grid, each with positive rational mass. This is population continuization; the candidates’ platform choices and the finite policy grid stay as they are.

One important limitation: the paper has no named result in the stated computational-complexity sense—no theorem or proposition classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results characterize equilibria analytically. The nearest usable result is Proposition 3, proved in this paper, rather than a qualifying complexity anchor. For even \(n\), under the paper’s surrounding assumption \(F(x_{n/2})>1/2\), it gives a unique sequential equilibrium in undominated strategies when \(\rho>F(x_{n/2})\). Writing \(H^*=(\rho-F(x_{n/2}))/(\rho-1+F(x_{n/2}))\), the strategies are
\[ \sigma^{*A}_k=\frac{(H^*)^{k-1}(1-H^*)}{1-(H^*)^n}, \qquad \sigma^{*B}_k=\frac{(H^*)^{n-k}(1-H^*)}{1-(H^*)^n}, \quad k=1,\ldots,n. \]
The proposition also specifies the voter’s Bayes-consistent beliefs after each signal. In equilibrium, a voter votes for \(A\) in signal \(L\) if \(x_m<1/2\), for \(A\) in signal \(R\) if \(x_m>1/2\), and for \(A\) with probability \(\rho\) after a tie signal \(I\); otherwise the voter votes for \(B\). The mixed strategies and beliefs are the result’s content, not an encoding of some other problem.

A precise candidate mirror is High-Multiplicity Platform Equilibrium Computation. Its instance is an even-sized grid \(X_n\), rational full-support voter masses \(\mu_i\) summing to one, the paper’s common voter utility and information rules, and a rational advantage parameter \(\rho\). The question is to compute the candidates’ sequential equilibrium in undominated strategies, including the strategies, voter responses, and signal-contingent beliefs; a solution must satisfy best responses, sequential rationality, and belief consistency. In the regime of Proposition 3, the expected direction is tractable: compute \(F(x_{n/2})\), evaluate the displayed formulas, and obtain beliefs by Bayes’ rule. This takes polynomial time in \(n\) and the input bit length \(L\); explicitly listing all beliefs takes \(O(n^2)\) output. The scope is just this large-advantage equilibrium regime, not every result in the paper. Natural further questions are how computation changes with type-dependent information or utilities, and what survives with a continuous policy space.

The soft spot is also decisive for novelty: the paper itself already presents the infinite-electorate, vote-share interpretation. So this is a strong example of how its equilibrium result reads as a population model, and a clear tractable equilibrium-computation question, but the mirror largely restates an interpretation the authors already give. It should not be presented as a new computational result of the paper or as a fresh continuous-population contribution without some additional question beyond that interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed type model is well formed: ideal points are voter types, their masses determine vote share, and the finite policy grid can stay fixed. There is no sound objection based on voters being individually named or on the equilibrium tracking voter identities.

The problem for this mirror is that the paper already gives the continuous-population interpretation. For any signal \(S\), the continuum share of voters who vote for \(A\) is the mass of ideal-point types whose response is to vote for \(A\). That is exactly the paper’s \(p_S(F,b_S)\), the expected probability that its randomly drawn voter votes for \(A\). Thus the candidates have the same payoffs at every platform pair—and under mixed strategies—in both descriptions. Footnote 3 states this vote-share interpretation explicitly; Section 5.4 also considers a proportion of informed voters. This is a collision with a model already in the paper, not an objection to high multiplicity as a regime.

So Proposition 3 does not supply a fresh continuous mirror. It characterizes equilibrium in a game whose payoffs already represent an infinite electorate. Recasting its input as rational type masses and asking for the equilibrium, including beliefs, adds a computational wrapper, but the paper gives the equilibrium strategies and Bayes-consistent beliefs for that same setting. And Proposition 3 is an analytical equilibrium result, not a complexity classification of a computational social choice problem of the kind ChoCo is charting.

A richer version could add type-specific information or preferences, or make platforms continuous. That might pose worthwhile new questions, but it would change the strategic model rather than uncover a population-continuous mirror missing from this paper. Continuous platforms in particular are not required for the population interpretation; the paper even discusses them separately. My negative case is therefore strongest for the proposed anchor and weaker as a universal claim: the paper itself leaves room for interesting extensions, but the proponent has not identified one that is both a mirror of a result here and unaddressed here.
