The continuous mirror question

For fixed finite player-type and arm-type sets, rational player masses μ summing to \(1\), per-capita arm capacities ν, strict type preferences and priorities, and \(1\)-sub-Gaussian rewards with distinct means, does there exist a decentralized learning policy whose type-weighted regret against a player-pessimal stable flow is at most \(C\log^2 T\), with \(C\) independent of the number of workers in finite replications of these type masses? Workers have private reward histories and independent random seeds, observe aggregate service by type and arm each round, and arms serve proposed mass in priority order up to capacity.

The model it lives in

A continuum of workers distributed over fixed types with mass μ; each worker learns arm rewards from private observations, while arm types with per-capita capacity ν serve worker mass according to cohort priorities. The objective is expected aggregate regret against a player-pessimal stable flow, uniformly over finite replications of the fixed type data.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has named algorithmic results, but none classifies a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or FPT. My strongest available anchor is Corollary 7, proved in this paper. Under arbitrary strict preferences, when every player runs CA-UCB with delay probability \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), player \(p_k\)’s regret against their player-pessimal stable arm satisfies
\[ R_k(T)\le 24\max_{a_\ell\in K}\Delta_{k,\ell}\, \frac{N^5L^2}{\varepsilon^{N^4+1}}\log T \left(\frac{\log T}{\Delta^2}+3\right), \]
where \(\varepsilon=(1-\lambda)\lambda^{N-1}\), \(\Delta=\min_{i,j,\ell}|\mu_j^{(i)}-\mu_\ell^{(i)}|\), and
\(\Delta_{k,\ell}=\max\{\mu_{m(k)}^{(k)}-\mu_\ell^{(k)},\mu_{m(k)}^{(k)}\}\); \(m(k)\) is \(p_k\)’s player-pessimal stable arm. Thus the paper proves an \(O((\log T)^2)\) regret guarantee for each fixed finite market. The constant has a severe dependence on market size.

A credible population regime is a large online labor or service market: many workers repeatedly choose among many job or service opportunities, learning their fit from successful matches. Workers fall into a fixed number \(\tau\) of cohorts with the same reward distributions and preferences; opportunity types have the same priorities over those cohorts. The number of workers and available slots grows while \(\tau\) stays fixed. Cohort membership must include every feature used by the model. If an opportunity ranks workers individually, those rankings distinguish them and the proposed high-multiplicity regime disappears. I would therefore model priority by cohort, with exchangeable tie-breaking within a cohort. Slot capacity scales with the population, which is a natural co-modification for a large market.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Fluid CA-UCB Regret. An instance consists of finite player-type and arm-type sets \(\mathcal P,\mathcal A\); rational player masses \(\mu_p\) summing to \(1\); rational per-capita arm capacities \(\nu_a\) with \(\sum_a\nu_a\ge1\); and a horizon \(T\). Each player type \(p\) has a strict preference ordering over arm types, induced by distinct mean rewards \(r_{pa}\), and a reward distribution for each arm type that is \(1\)-sub-Gaussian with that mean. Each arm type has a strict priority ordering over player types. Every individual has private reward observations and an independent random seed. All individuals observe the aggregate mass of each type successfully served by each arm in the previous round, plus their own success and reward.

At each round, individuals apply the CA-UCB rule to their own histories: a plausible set excludes an arm type if, in the previous round, that arm served positive mass from a higher-priority type; otherwise the individual chooses the plausible arm with greatest UCB, except when the delay coin tells them to repeat their previous attempt. Each arm type accepts proposed mass in priority order up to its capacity, allocating a partial acceptance within a type exchangeably. This defines a mass flow \(y_{pa}(t)\) of successful matches. A stable benchmark is a specified player-pessimal stable flow \(y^{\mathrm{pess}}\). The objective is type-weighted stable regret,
\[ R_{\mathrm{fluid}}(T) = T\sum_p\mu_p\bar r_p^{\mathrm{pess}} - \sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{p,a}\mathbb E[y_{pa}(t)]r_{pa}, \qquad \bar r_p^{\mathrm{pess}} = \frac{1}{\mu_p}\sum_a y^{\mathrm{pess}}_{pa}r_{pa}. \]
The question is whether decentralized policies in this model can achieve a bound \(R_{\mathrm{fluid}}(T)\le C\log^2 T\), with \(C\) depending on the fixed type data, gaps, capacities, and \(\lambda\), but not on the number of workers in a finite replication. A solution is a policy and a proved bound, or a fixed-support counterexample and a lower bound that rules out the target rate.

This is a population version of the paper’s learning problem: agents still learn privately from stochastic rewards, avoid conflicts using public observations, and measure performance against stable matches. Existing mean-field work cited by the paper is relevant background for the aggregate market, but it studies analytic market behavior rather than this regret guarantee. I expect the arbitrary-priority version to be continuum-specifically hard, in the sense that proving a population-size-independent rate must handle a distribution of private learning histories and aggregate capacity rationing. This is a Class C-like expectation, not a complexity classification supplied by the paper. A globally ranked version may be more tractable, but I would keep that separate from the lead mirror.

My weakest point is the change needed to make the population genuinely high-multiplicity. The paper gives every arm a strict ranking over named players and resolves a conflict with one winner; the mirror groups priorities by type and scales slot capacity. Those changes are sensible for cohort markets, but a referee could view them as a new capacitated model rather than the paper’s exact problem. The finite theorem also gives no uniform limit by itself: its constant grows rapidly with \(N\), and the proof’s randomized coordination step depends on the number of players. So the authors could recognize this as a natural extension of their problem, while still judging it a weak fit for a programme focused narrowly on computational complexity. My case covers Corollary 7’s arbitrary-preference regret result; it does not claim a continuous mirror for the paper’s incentive-compatibility results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 7 is a genuine anchor, and the strongest version of the mirror is a fluid market with cohort priorities, per-capita capacities, private reward histories, and regret measured against a stable flow. That is a coherent population model. The difficulty is that its central question—whether a decentralized learning policy has population-size-independent regret—is a statistical learning and dynamical-systems question. Corollary 7 gives no running-time or computational-complexity result, and the proposed mirror asks for no bound on the computation needed to choose an action. It may be worthwhile research, but its main deliverable does not look like the complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

There is also a sharp gap between the theorem and the proposed limit. The proof for arbitrary preferences relies on a positive probability \(\varepsilon=(1-\lambda)\lambda^{N-1}\) of one player resolving a blocking pair while the other \(N-1\) players hold still; its convergence bound then uses paths of up to \(N^4\) such resolutions. The resulting constants deteriorate extremely rapidly with \(N\). In a fluid population, changing one individual’s action moves zero mass. Independent delay coins instead move positive fractions at once, so the proof’s single-player coordination event has no direct fluid counterpart. The proposed model would need a new analysis of simultaneous flows and a distribution of agents’ private learning histories. Static cohort masses alone do not determine those histories or the resulting UCB choices.

One could design a fluid policy that moves a positive fraction of a cohort at a time, or introduce finite packets so individual moves affect the aggregate. Either choice could lead to an interesting result. The first changes the coordination dynamics that Corollary 7 analyzes; the second makes packet size a consequential granularity parameter. In both cases, the research question becomes a new learning-policy problem, without a clear computational problem whose complexity is being classified.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope objection, not as a claim that no sensible continuous model exists. The cohort market is plausible, and high multiplicity would support its modeling premise. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror; I can argue that this anchor’s best mirror sits at the boundary of ChoCo’s stated computational focus.
