What the mirror covers

The proposal targets equilibrium existence in Theorem 4.1, also summarized as item (V) of Theorem 4.2; it leaves the voting-measure properties in Sections 3 and 4.2 aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest affirmative mirror I can defend is the paper’s positive-penalty delegation game with a continuous population and finitely many voter types. My single anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: with finitely many agents, each choosing a fractional delegation weighting and having a preference vector over who consumes their vote, the game using \(V^\epsilon\) has at least one Nash equilibrium for every \(0<\epsilon<1\). The proof uses Kakutani’s theorem, relying on continuous utilities and convex best-response sets. This is an existence result, not a running-time bound or an algorithm for finding an equilibrium.

Here is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large civic platform whose members delegate votes to people in a small number of recognized roles or expertise cohorts. A voter cares which cohort ultimately uses their vote, not which named member of that cohort receives it. The type of a voter therefore includes their full satisfaction vector over recipient cohorts and the null outcome, along with their delegation options and any other relevant parameters. If the platform has \(n\) members but only \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\tau\ll n\), its society is \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t>0\) is the rational fraction of members of type \(t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

I would call the mirror Type-Continuum \(\epsilon\)-Delegation Equilibrium. An instance gives \(T\), \(\mu\), preference vectors \(w_t\in\mathbb{Q}^{\tau+1}\), and rational \(0<\epsilon<1\). A type-\(t\) voter chooses \(x_t=(q_t,d_{t1},\ldots,d_{t\tau})\in\Delta^\tau\): they retain and cast fraction \(q_t\), and delegate fraction \(d_{tu}\) uniformly among members of type \(u\). Delegation to one’s own type goes to other members of that type; in the continuum, no individual is a positive-mass target.

For a policy profile \(x\), let \(R_u(x)\) be the terminal distribution of one unit of vote weight originating at a type-\(u\) voter who follows \(x_u\). Its coordinates are the recipient types and a null outcome. With \(e_u\) and \(e_\bot\) the corresponding unit vectors, define it by

\[ R_u(x)=\epsilon e_\bot+(1-\epsilon)q_u e_u +(1-\epsilon)\sum_{v\in T}d_{uv}R_v(x). \]

The positive penalty makes this system a contraction, so \(R(x)\) is uniquely defined. The aggregate terminal vote mass at destination \(s\) is \(\nu_s=\sum_t\mu_t[R_t(x)]_s\).

A type-\(t\) voter who deviates to allocation \(a=(q,d)\in\Delta^\tau\) has utility

\[ \Pi_t(a;x)=\epsilon w_{t,\bot} +(1-\epsilon)\left(qw_{t,t} +\sum_{u\in T}d_u\,\langle w_t,R_u(x)\rangle\right). \]

The question is whether there is a type-symmetric policy profile \(x\) such that \(\Pi_t(x_t;x)\geq \Pi_t(a;x)\) for every type \(t\) and every allowed deviation \(a\). A solution is such an equilibrium profile. The model preserves the paper’s key ingredients—fractional delegation, preferences over where one’s own vote ends up, and a penalty on long delegation chains—while making the *population* the continuous object.

I expect the existence answer to be yes for every \(\epsilon>0\). In this type-symmetric model, the policy space is a finite product of simplices; the terminal distributions vary continuously with the policies; and each type’s best-response set is convex because utility is linear in its own allocation when the population policy is held fixed. That gives a Kakutani-style existence argument. This prediction is my inference for the mirror, not a result stated in the paper. Whether an equilibrium can be found efficiently as a function of \(\tau\), input bit length, and desired accuracy is a separate question.

The scope is deliberately narrow: I am mirroring Theorem 4.1’s equilibrium-existence result, not claiming that the paper’s voting-weight properties or all of Theorem 4.2 have continuous-population counterparts. The paper’s fractional actions and vote weights alone do not make its population continuous; the mirror does that by replacing named agents with positive-mass types.

The weakest point is that the mirror’s type-level targeting and type-symmetric equilibrium are real restrictions. In the finite game, a named agent’s action can affect votes that return to that same agent; in an atomless population, a single deviator has zero effect on the environment, and an exact return to that individual has zero probability. The paper’s finite-agent nonexistence example at \(\epsilon=0\) therefore need not survive this limit. That also makes the positive-\(\epsilon\) existence result easier to recover, and raises the risk that the mirror has simplified away part of the authors’ strategic interaction. Still, a large delegation platform with role-based recipients is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and a symmetric type equilibrium is a recognizable version of their question.

One qualification matters for the programme’s grading: the paper has no named result classifying computational complexity—no theorem that an equilibrium problem is in P, NP-hard, or similar. Theorem 4.1 is its strongest computationally adjacent anchor, but it establishes existence, not computational complexity. If “computational result” is required to mean a complexity classification, then this paper has no qualifying anchor; the case above is the strongest positive analogy around its named equilibrium theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Theorem 4.1, and it does not establish a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. It proves that an equilibrium exists, using Kakutani’s theorem; the corresponding decision question—“does an equilibrium exist?”—is answered yes for every instance. Asking how to *find* an equilibrium could be a computational problem, but that would be a new question, not a mirror of a complexity result in this paper.

The proposed type model also makes the population distribution largely ornamental. Its equilibrium conditions depend on the type policies through \(R_u(x)\), but not on the masses \(\mu_t\). The masses appear in aggregate vote totals, yet those totals do not enter the paper’s utility, which evaluates where an individual’s own vote ends up. Changing the population proportions therefore leaves the equilibrium problem unchanged. With one policy per type, the model is a finite representative-agent game with fractional strategies—the action-space continuity the paper already has—not a computational problem whose society is continuous.

There are ways to make \(\mu\) matter, such as adding congestion or making utility depend on aggregate vote mass. But those change the payoff that Theorem 4.1 studies. Conversely, preserving the paper’s preferences over named recipients can require distinguishing individuals in the type description; in that regime, the number of types may grow with the population, removing the high-multiplicity compression. The role-based version is the best response to that concern, but it is precisely the version in which population proportions drop out.

So this anchor does not support a worthwhile continuous mirror *of the paper’s result* under the programme’s computational remit. The negative case is weaker against a broader project: equilibrium computation in a new, aggregate-sensitive continuum delegation game could be interesting. But that would extend the paper’s model and ask a question its theorem does not answer.
