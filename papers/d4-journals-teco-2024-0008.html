The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of disutility types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(r\) chore classes with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j>0\), and type costs \(d_{tj}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_j q_jd_{tj}=1\), let \(\delta_t=\max_j d_{tj}\) and \(W(\delta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}N V_N(\delta/N)\), where \(V_N\) is the guarantee in Theorem 2 for \(N\) agents. For integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\), assign mass \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) so that \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\) for every type and chore class. Minimize \(\rho=\sup_{t,b:x_{t,b}>0}(\sum_jd_{tj}b_j)/W(\delta_t)\); in particular, determine whether an allocation with \(\rho\le1\) can be constructed in time polynomial in the compressed input size, including for fixed \(r\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of people partitioned into finitely many complete disutility types, with mass \(\mu_t\) per type and per-capita supply \(q_j\) of each indivisible chore class. The decision variables \(x_{t,b}\) assign mass to integral bundle vectors while meeting type-mass and aggregate-supply constraints; the objective minimizes the worst type-normalized disutility.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's per-agent guarantee and constructive allocation result. It leaves aside the exact characterization of Hill's share in Theorem 1, the experiments, and whether the limiting guarantee is tight for this restricted population model.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(r\), can the guarantee threshold be decided and a feasible mass allocation constructed in time polynomial in the bit length of the rational type and supply data?
  • Is \(W\) tight for this restricted continuous model, or can the mass allocation achieve a stronger guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the per-capita Hill-guarantee allocation. The strongest numbered anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n\) agents with additive nonnegative disutilities over \(m\) indivisible bads, if agent \(i\)’s largest single-bad disutility is at most \(\alpha_i\), there is an allocation giving every agent disutility at most \(V_n(\alpha_i)\), and such an allocation can be computed in polynomial time. It also proves tightness over the larger domain \(U(\alpha)\): for each \(\alpha\), there is a profile with every agent in \(U(\alpha)\) such that every allocation gives some agent disutility at least \(V_n(\alpha)\). The paper’s prose also says computing MinMaxShare is NP-hard, citing earlier work, but that is not a numbered result proved here, so I do not use it as an anchor.

Here is a continuous-population version that keeps each person’s bundle integral. Consider a large cooperative assigning recurring indivisible chores from \(r\) standardized classes. There is a finite set \(T\) of complete disutility types, with type \(t\) occupying mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each class \(j\) has per-capita supply \(q_j>0\). A type \(t\) has disutility \(d_{tj}\) per chore of class \(j\), normalized so that \(\sum_j q_jd_{tj}=1\); let \(\delta_t=\max_jd_{tj}\). This regime has many people but relatively few distinct types, and every person of a type has the same costs across all chore classes.

A bundle is an integral count vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\), with rescaled cost \(c_t(b)=\sum_jd_{tj}b_j\). The allocation variable \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned that whole bundle. Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\quad\text{for each }t, \qquad \sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\quad\text{for each }j. \]
Thus the mass assignment can mix people across bundles, but nobody receives a fractional chore.

The finite guarantee suggests the limiting benchmark
\[ W(\delta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}N V_N(\delta/N). \]
Reading this limit from the paper’s formula for \(V_n\), for \(k\ge0\),
\[ W(\delta)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{k+2}{k+1}, &\dfrac1{k+1}<\delta<\dfrac{k+2}{(k+1)^2},\\[6pt] (k+1)\delta, &\dfrac{k+2}{(k+1)^2}\le\delta\le\dfrac1k . \end{cases} \]
For \(k=0\), take \(1/k=\infty\). The proposed problem is to find a feasible allocation minimizing
\[ \rho(x)=\sup_{t,b:x_{t,b}>0}\frac{c_t(b)}{W(\delta_t)}. \]
The direct mirror of Theorem 2 asks for an allocation with \(\rho(x)\le1\), as well as an algorithm to construct one from the compressed type and per-capita data.

The scaling explains the connection to the paper. In an \(N\)-person approximation, give each class \(j\) exactly \(Nq_j\) copies, and charge type \(t\) disutility \(d_{tj}/N\) for each copy. Each person’s total disutility over the whole pile is \(1\), and their largest single-chore disutility is \(\delta_t/N\). Theorem 2 then bounds their disutility by \(V_N(\delta_t/N)\). Multiplying by \(N\) gives the rescaled bound whose limit is \(W(\delta_t)\). The authors should recognize the question: it retains additive disutilities, indivisible bundles, a guarantee for each person, and the maximum single-item cost. Repeated standardized chores and stable disutility types are the high-multiplicity regime; scaling the chore supply with the population is part of making that regime meaningful.

I expect Class A when \(r\) is fixed. The allocation can be represented as a configuration LP over integral bundles, with a pricing problem over count vectors in \(r\) dimensions. Fixed-dimensional integer optimization gives a plausible route to an exact separation algorithm and then to the LP machinery emphasized by ChoCo. The paper’s polynomial algorithm in the explicit number of agents does not establish polynomial time in this compressed input: the population size \(N\) is implicit, and its dependence could be large relative to the bit length \(L\). If \(r\) grows with the input, the pricing problem may become knapsack-like, so I would leave that case open rather than claim tractability.

This scope mirrors Theorem 2’s guarantee and allocation result; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s full characterization of Hill’s share or its experiments. The main weakness is the repeated-class regime: chores with agent-specific costs or many genuinely distinct identities do not fit these types. Also, Theorem 2’s tightness over \(U(\alpha)\) does not establish that \(W\) is tight for this restricted continuous regime. Atomless mass may allow stronger guarantees, and proving either tightness or a better bound is a natural next question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed continuum question adds little beyond the finite guarantee’s existence statement. For rational type masses and per-capita supplies, take finite populations along a sequence of common denominators. Theorem 2 gives allocations meeting \(V_N(\delta_t/N)\); after rescaling, their costs approach \(W(\delta_t)\). With finitely many chore classes and bounded average supply per type, the bundle distributions have a convergent subsequence. So the threshold question \(\rho\leq1\) appears to follow from the theorem and a limit argument. The paper’s theorem does not, by itself, make the new question’s compressed-input algorithm a substantial research problem.

That objection does not defeat the anchor. The proposed regime keeps bundles integral, and repeated standardized chores with stable disutility types are a credible high-multiplicity setting. Per-capita supply is an appropriate co-modification, and the type description preserves the features that determine each person’s burden. The finite guarantee’s worst-case tightness need not survive this restricted regime for the mirror to be worth asking.

The only remaining case against is therefore about priority: the broad guarantee may be inherited, while the genuinely new task is finding a running time in \(\tau\), \(r\), and the input bit length. For fixed \(r\), configuration pricing over integer bundle vectors looks plausibly tractable. But that is a likely Class A result, which this programme counts as a success. I can make a case that this anchor is narrower than the proponent suggests; I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
