What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers Safe Implementation; the paper's structural conditions, application results, and impossibilities are not computational results and remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: this is a natural high-multiplicity extension of Safe Implementation, but the paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 gives a necessary structural condition; Theorem 3 says that, for \(n\ge3\), strong comonotonicity and Safe No-Veto suffice for \((A,k)\)-Safe Nash Implementation whenever \(1\le k<n/2\). Both are proved here, but neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. Proposition 2’s “continuous” setting concerns the allocation space, with a finite population. Those results support the relevance of implementation and safety; they do not meet your anchor rule. So there are zero anchors, and no per-anchor mirror I can honestly claim the paper establishes.

The positive case I would still defend is a follow-on question: Uniform \(\kappa\)-Mass Safe Implementation. An instance gives a finite set \(T\) of complete preference types, a finite state set \(\Theta\), a finite outcome set \(X\), utilities \(u_t(x,\theta)\), rational population distributions \(\mu^\theta\in\Delta(T)\), a target correspondence \(F(\theta)\subseteq X\), an acceptability correspondence \(A(\theta)\supseteq F(\theta)\), and a safety rate \(0<\kappa<1/2\). Type \(t\) includes every payoff-relevant feature, and \(\mu_t^\theta\) is its share of the population.

For each sufficiently large admissible population size \(n\), the society has \(n\mu_t^\theta\) agents of type \(t\). The question is whether there is a uniformly finitely described family of mechanisms \(M_n\) such that, at every state, the set of Nash-equilibrium outcomes is exactly \(F(\theta)\), and changing the messages of at most \(\lfloor\kappa n\rfloor\) agents from any equilibrium always produces an outcome in \(A(\theta)\). A solution is a mechanism schema for all sufficiently large \(n\), or a proof that none exists. The population is represented by type masses; outcomes remain in \(X\).

This is plausible as the authors’ problem because it preserves their target, their Nash implementation requirement, and their safety requirement. The co-modification is to scale the allowed number of deviations with population size. Theorem 3 gives real supporting evidence: on instances satisfying its conditions, \(k=\lfloor\kappa n\rfloor<n/2\), so its implementation result applies across the large finite replications. With finitely listed types, checking those conditions is a promising tractable subproblem. The complexity of deciding implementation in general remains open; Theorem 3 is not an algorithmic classification, and Proposition 4’s impossibility under rich preferences is not a hardness result.

The weakest point is that this uses a sequence of finite populations as the meaning of the mass model. In a directly atomless mechanism, one agent has zero mass, so a unilateral Nash deviation may not change the outcome at all. A direct continuum formulation would need a careful equilibrium and deviation concept, and could change the authors’ question. The further questions are whether the finite replications admit an exact mass-level characterization, and which parts of the implementation decision become tractable when the number of types is small.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The zero-anchor point is sound. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are structural necessity and sufficiency results, and Propositions 4 and 5 are implementation impossibilities. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 2’s continuum is the allocation space; the population remains finite. So the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror.

The proposed Uniform \(\kappa\)-Mass question is the strongest candidate, but it does not yet define a computational problem: “uniformly finitely described” leaves unspecified how mechanisms are encoded, how they may depend on \(n\), and what size or running time is measured. More fundamentally, each \(M_n\) still implements a finite game. Its Nash condition turns on unilateral deviations by individual agents, while safety allows deviations by a positive fraction. The type distribution compresses the input, but the question asks for an eventual property of a sequence of finite games; it does not state a problem directly over the mass society. I would not reject it just for using finite replications—the programme explicitly treats those as a bridge—but here the \(1/n\)-scale individual incentives remain central.

A direct atomless version faces a real fork. If outcomes depend only on aggregate message masses, one person’s deviation leaves the outcome unchanged, so every message profile is Nash. Exact implementation then becomes severely constrained. Personalized allocations can preserve individual incentives, but require a mechanism and outcome representation that tracks each person’s report and assignment; the population distribution alone no longer describes the relevant strategic object. Requiring positive-mass coalitions to have no profitable deviation restores strategic bite, but changes the equilibrium concept. That could be a worthwhile research question, yet it is a new coalition-implementation problem, not a straightforward continuization of the paper’s Nash implementation results.

So the strongest negative case is that the paper offers no computational anchor, and the proponent’s best follow-up has not resolved whether its object is an asymptotic family of finite games or a genuinely continuous implementation problem. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight: with a carefully specified anonymous mechanism class, personalized outcomes, or mass-coalition deviations, a worthwhile mirror may survive. The negative case is strongest against the proposed formulation’s claim to be a continuous computational problem; it is weak as a claim that no such mirror is worth pursuing in any scenario.
