The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass population with supported ranking types τ of masses μ_τ, school capacities \(s_n\) per unit population, and proposed assignment shares \(Q_{τ n}\), is there a measurable assignment realizing mass μ_τ Q_{τ n} of type τ at school \(n\), with each individual receiving at most one school and each school respecting capacity?

The model it lives in

Use an atomless population with finitely many complete ranking types and per-capita school capacities. The decision variables are assigned masses \(x_{τ n}=μ_τ Q_{τ n}\); feasibility requires \(∑_n x_{τ n}≤μ_τ\) for each type and \(∑_τ x_{τ n}≤ s_n\) for each school. A feasible flow is a witness, while individual assignments remain integral.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It concerns interim realizability under one-to-one matching constraints. For a set \(A\) of agent-type-item triples, define \(S(t,A)\) as the agent-item pairs represented in \(A\) at type profile \(t\), and let \(C(X)\) be the maximum amount assignable on pairs in \(X\) under the ex-post matching constraints. The theorem says that if \(Q\) is realizable, then for every \(A\),
\[ \sum_{(i,\tau,n)\in A}\mu_i^\bullet(\tau)Q(i,\tau,n) \leq \sum_t\mu(t)C(S(t,A)). \]
Conversely, if these inequalities hold, then \(\tfrac12 Q\) is realizable. More specifically, for every order \(R\) on the type-item coordinates, \(\tfrac12 Q\) is \(R\)-FOSD dominated by the greedy matching interim allocation \(Q^R\), and \(\tfrac12 Q\) lies in the convex hull of interim allocations induced by truncated greedy matchings. The halving is essential: the theorem does not claim that \(Q\) itself is realizable.

This is an approximation and realizability result, not a P-versus-NP classification or a running-time bound. The paper has no numbered theorem asserting such a complexity classification. It cites Gopalan et al. for the difficulty of exact finite matching characterizations, but that cited result is not my anchor.

My proposed mirror is Atomless School-Matching Interim Realizability. There is a unit mass of students, a finite set \(T\) of complete school rankings, and mass \(\mu_\tau\) of students of each type \(\tau\). School \(n\) has capacity \(s_n\) per unit mass of students. The input also gives proposed type-level assignment shares \(Q_{\tau n}\): the fraction of type-\(\tau\) students to receive school \(n\). The question is whether there is a feasible assignment of the population realizing those shares. A solution can be given by the assigned masses \(x_{\tau n}=\mu_\tau Q_{\tau n}\), with
\[ \sum_n x_{\tau n}\leq\mu_\tau \quad\forall\tau, \qquad \sum_\tau x_{\tau n}\leq s_n \quad\forall n. \]
These conditions are sufficient: split each atomless type cohort into measurable pieces of the specified sizes and assign each piece a whole school. Thus individuals still receive integral assignments; only the cohort averages are fractional. Recognition reduces to a transportation-flow feasibility problem, polynomial in the number of supported types, schools, and input bit length \(L\). There is no welfare objective in this anchor’s underlying problem: the task is to decide realizability and return an assignment witness.

A plausible regime is a large school district in which student numbers greatly exceed the number of distinct ranking types, while school seats scale with the population. For example, with \(N\) applicants, the count of type \(\tau\) can approach \(N\mu_\tau\), and school capacity can approach \(Ns_n\). The number of supported rankings \(\tau\) can be moderate relative to \(N\). This mirrors the paper’s technological-feasibility question directly: which type-conditioned assignment marginals can be induced under matching constraints? The paper’s own move from indivisible matching to an ex-post polytope also makes it plausible that its authors would recognize the mass-assignment version.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The finite theorem loses its factor-\(\tfrac12\) guarantee in the atomless fixed-composition model because cohorts can be split exactly and feasibility becomes a fractional matching problem. That prediction is a claim about the continuous model, not a result proved in the paper. The scope here is only Theorem 3’s matching realizability question; it does not mirror the paper’s deferred-acceptance payoff guarantee, incentive constraints, or cardinal-preference applications.

The weakest point is that the paper’s interim allocations average over other agents’ potentially random types, whereas this mirror fixes the aggregate type masses. It removes finite-profile uncertainty and its correlations. That is a meaningful simplification, and the authors could object that it changes the source of difficulty. I still think it is a credible high-multiplicity mirror: it asks the same implementability question for a large population whose composition is stable.

The next questions are whether finite-market realizability approaches these flow constraints as population grows, and what changes when aggregate type masses remain uncertain because of common shocks or correlation. A further extension would put incentive constraints or deferred acceptance back into the atomless model and ask whether the flow-based tractability survives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s strongest anchor: it gives a named, approximate characterization of interim realizability for matching. It is not a complexity classification or a running-time result, but that alone does not defeat it; asking for an algorithm or a sharper characterization could still be worthwhile.

The sharper objection is that the proposed flow problem changes the object being characterized. In the paper, \(Q(i,\tau,n)\) is an interim probability: it averages \(q(i,n,t)\) over the other agents’ types, conditional on \(i\)’s type being \(\tau\). The proposed \(Q_{\tau n}\) instead describes shares assigned within one fixed aggregate population. Its row and column constraints characterize a feasible assignment of that society, but do not ask whether a rule defined across the paper’s possible type profiles induces the specified interim probabilities. The atomless split is valid for the fixed-composition problem; it does not by itself mirror Theorem 3.

A stronger mirror could take large finite markets with repeated complete types, scale school capacities with population, retain the paper’s prior over profiles, and study the limit of type-symmetric interim allocations. With independent types, the aggregate composition concentrates, so the limiting feasibility conditions may reduce to flow constraints. To preserve persistent correlation, one could instead make the aggregate composition random and average allocations across those population states. But then the former version asks for a finite-to-continuum convergence result, while the latter adds a distribution over societies whose representation and computational role the paper does not specify. That makes the proposed model a plausible starting point, but not yet a clear continuation of the theorem’s computational question.

This is still a weak negative case. The high-multiplicity regime is sensible here, and showing precisely how the interim-feasibility polytope approaches—or fails to approach—the flow polytope could be a worthwhile bridge. I can challenge the proposed formulation’s claim to mirror Theorem 3 directly; I cannot honestly establish that every stronger, well-posed version would be unworthy of the programme.
