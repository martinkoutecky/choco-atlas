The continuous mirror question

Given finite supported agent types \(I\) with rational masses \(\alpha_i>0\), room types \(J\) with rational supplies \(\beta_j>0\), and rational valuations \(v_{ij}\), rent bounds \(\ell_j,u_j\), and per-capita total rent \(\rho\), where \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\), decide whether there are prices \(p_j\) and assignments of mass \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\alpha_i\), \(\sum_ix_{ij}=\beta_j\), \(\ell_j\leq p_j\leq u_j\), and \(\sum_j\beta_jp_j=\rho\), with \(v_{ij}-p_j=\max_{k\in J}(v_{ik}-p_k)\) whenever \(x_{ij}>0\). Return a feasible mass assignment and prices, or correctly report infeasibility.

The model it lives in

Each agent type is a complete valuation vector over room types; each room type groups interchangeable rooms with common bounds and a shared price. The variables are assignment masses \(x_{ij}\) and prices \(p_j\); the task is feasibility, with one room per unit of agent mass and total rent \(\rho\) per capita. When the rational masses scale to integer counts, transportation-flow integrality yields whole-room assignments in this homogeneous regime.

The aggregate feasibility problem appears to reduce to a routine transportation primal-dual LP, so the contribution may lie mainly in documenting the high-multiplicity correspondence.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a focused mirror of the paper’s room-rent-bounds feasibility result. It preserves the rent-division question and changes the scale at which the population and room supply are described.

The anchor is Theorem 3†, proved by the authors in Appendix B. In the room-bounds setting, Algorithm 7 starts from a welfare-maximizing assignment and an envy-free rent vector; it returns an envy-free rent vector satisfying the given lower and upper bounds if one exists, and otherwise correctly reports “No solution.” The paper’s Remark 8 says these event-driven rent-adjustment algorithms can be made strongly polynomial. This is an algorithmic anchor; the paper gives no comparable hardness result.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite set \(I\) of agent types with rational masses \(\alpha_i>0\), and a finite set \(J\) of room types with rational supplies \(\beta_j>0\), where \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\). An agent type \(i\) is its complete valuation vector \(v_{ij}\) over room types. A room type \(j\) groups interchangeable rooms: every supported agent type values them identically, and they share rent bounds \(\ell_j,u_j\). The input also gives a rational per-capita total rent \(\rho\).

The question is whether there are prices \(p_j\) and an aggregate assignment \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) such that
\(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\beta_j\), \(\ell_j\leq p_j\leq u_j\), and \(\sum_j\beta_jp_j=\rho\), with envy-freeness requiring \(v_{ij}-p_j=\max_k(v_{ik}-p_k)\) whenever \(x_{ij}>0\). A solution is the mass assignment \(x\) and rent vector \(p\), or a correct infeasibility answer. The mass \(x_{ij}\) means that this much of type \(i\) receives an actual room of type \(j\); it does not give anyone a fractional room.

This is a plausible large-market regime in university or employer-sponsored housing: many students or employees, repeated standardized room categories, and a relatively small catalogue of exact reported valuation profiles. For illustration, the population might be in the tens of thousands while the number of distinct agent and room types is in the hundreds. Types here mean identical valuation vectors, not merely similar preferences. The room supply must scale with the population so that each agent still gets one room; that co-modification is part of the mirror.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). Its mass assignment is a transportation problem. A polynomial-size LP can choose \(x\), prices \(p\), and utilities \(y_i\), impose \(y_i+p_j\geq v_{ij}\), the rent bounds and total rent, and equate the transportation welfare \(\sum_{ij}v_{ij}x_{ij}\) with the dual value \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i+\sum_j\beta_jp_j\). Primal–dual equality enforces that positive assignment mass goes only to rooms that type regards as utility-maximizing. The underlying question is still fair rent division with one room per agent; the LP is the route to solving it. Its size depends on the numbers of supported types and the rational input length, not on the number of represented agents.

There is also a clean finite bridge. If the masses are counts divided by \(N\), a feasible continuous solution gives a flow on the tight agent-room-type pairs. Multiplying by \(N\) gives integer row and column totals, and bipartite-flow integrality yields an assignment of whole rooms to whole agents. Conversely, a grouped finite solution normalizes to a mass solution. So in this homogeneous regime the continuous formulation is not merely suggestive: it captures the grouped finite problem exactly.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps envy-freeness, welfare-compatible assignment, fixed total rent, and room-specific rent bounds, while aggregating agents and interchangeable rooms. The paper’s own motivation includes university housing contracts, which makes the regime especially natural. This mirror covers Theorem 3’s bounded-rent feasibility, not the paper’s room-specific agent-budget results or its maximin, leximin, and spread objectives.

The soft spot is that the paper’s most distinctive extension is the budget model, which this case leaves aside. Also, aggregation only helps when identical valuation profiles and interchangeable room categories are genuinely common; if nearly every agent or room is distinct, the support is large and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. Those limits narrow the claim, but they do not undermine this specific mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong case against Theorem 3’s mirror. Its mass assignment preserves the one-agent/one-room structure: each unit of agent mass receives one room, and the flow constraints balance agent mass against room supply. Identical valuation vectors and interchangeable rooms are legitimate types under the programme’s definition. When masses have denominator \(N\), flow integrality recovers a whole-room allocation of \(N\) agents; the continuum has not discarded individual assignments.

The best skeptical point is about research value, not whether the mirror makes sense. In this homogeneous regime, envy-free feasibility follows from a transportation LP and its dual, so the resulting tractability may be immediate rather than technically revealing. But an easy Class A result is still a computational mirror under the programme’s stated standard. The paper itself notes that the bounded-rent problem can be written as an LP once the assignment is fixed. The proponent’s type-level flow formulation extends that idea while handling assignment and aggregation.

The existing continuous-utility rent-division work cited by the paper does not defeat this anchor: it concerns continuity of utility functions, not a continuous population. [Arunachaleswaran, Barman, and Rathi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.04163). Nor can I turn the need to scale room supply with the population into an objection; the programme explicitly allows that co-modification.

So the negative case rests on a narrow contribution concern: this mirror may yield a straightforward application of standard optimization, with value concentrated in documenting the high-multiplicity bridge. That does not defeat the anchor. Under the programme’s criteria, this is a credible mirror, and I do not see an honest basis for the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
