What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns equilibrium-agenda recognition under quota voting; it does not computationally mirror the arbitrary-agenda existence results or the amendment-procedure results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it proves no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational class. Theorems 4 and 5 are the closest candidates, but they are structural possibility results, not computational ones.

Theorem 4, proved in this paper, says that under voting by quota, for any quota and any set \(F\) of issues, there is an additively separable preference profile with all voters as agenda setters such that \(F\) is exactly the set of issues left free at every consistent equilibrium agenda. With betweenness also required, the construction works except when \(|F|=1\), and when there are an odd number of voters using simple majority, also when \(|F|=2\). Theorem 5, also proved here, gives the analogous arbitrary-\(F\) result for the amendment procedure with an odd number of voters and suitable alternative orderings; separability and betweenness can be retained except in the stated small cases. Neither theorem analyzes the complexity of finding or recognizing those agendas.

A plausible *new* question would be Continuous Consistent Agenda Recognition. Its instance would be a finite support of voter types—complete rankings over \(X=\{0,1,-\}^K\)—with rational masses summing to one, a voting quota expressed as a population fraction, and a target ordered agenda. Take all members as agenda setters. For any agenda, the vote is determined by the mass of types whose best available alternative selects position \(1\) on each issue. Ask whether the target agenda belongs to some consistent equilibrium collection, using the paper’s continuation and consistency conditions, with agenda-setter comparisons ranging over the types in the support. A solution is a yes/no answer; the complexity could be studied in \(K\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\).

That is a credible high-multiplicity research direction: large legislative bodies or membership societies can have many members but relatively few preference types, and Theorems 4 and 5 show that agenda outcomes can vary widely even under restricted preferences. But it is not anchored in a computational result of this paper, so it cannot satisfy the requested anchor-led case. Its weakest point is the extension of the agenda-setting game: a single member can add an issue in the paper, while a continuous mass model must explain how that individual action remains meaningful when the individual has zero mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 4 and Theorem 5 establish which equilibrium agendas can arise for suitable preference profiles; they do not give complexity bounds or algorithms for a computational problem. Their quantifiers run over profiles chosen to produce a desired agenda, rather than asking what agenda results from a given society. A continuous restatement of either theorem would therefore be a structural possibility result, not the kind of computational mirror ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed “Continuous Consistent Agenda Recognition” is a more serious follow-on, but it changes the task: it asks for the complexity of an equilibrium-computation problem the paper does not analyze. It may be worth studying on its own. As a mirror of Theorems 4 and 5, though, it is weakly anchored: their arbitrary-agenda constructions do not identify recognition, or any other algorithmic task, as a bottleneck.

There is also a modeling tension in that proposal. The voting outcome can be computed from type masses, but agenda formation turns on whether *some individual* setter prefers one continuation to another. With all voters as setters, any positive-mass type supplies such individuals, regardless of how small its mass is. Thus a type can retain full proposal power as its population share approaches zero, then lose it abruptly when its mass reaches zero. A continuous model can stipulate that every individual keeps the right to propose, but then mass is not carrying the strategic part of the problem; it only determines the voting thresholds. If instead the agenda setters are a small committee, their individual preferences and rights must remain explicit alongside the continuous electorate.

That tension is a reason to doubt that continuizing the population yields a useful new complexity landscape for this paper. It is not a proof that no sensible hybrid or high-multiplicity question exists: the authors’ interest in computing equilibrium agendas makes such a question plausible. So the negative case is strong on the absence of a computational result to anchor a mirror, but weak on the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous agenda-computation problem could be formulated.
