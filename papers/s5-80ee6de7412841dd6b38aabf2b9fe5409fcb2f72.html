The continuous mirror question

Given finite supported type sets on two sides, rational masses of total \(1\) per side, an acceptable type-pair graph with strict rankings, a stable allocation \(\sigma\), and rational thresholds \(\kappa,q,t\), decide whether there is an allocation \(x\) with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_A(a)\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\mu_B(b)\), \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\ge|\sigma|+t\), \(\sum_{a,b}|x_{ab}-\sigma_{ab}|\le q\), and \(\mathcal B(x)\le\kappa\). For each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let \(R_{ab}(x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) assigned below \(b\), including unmatched mass, and define \(S_{ab}(x)\) symmetrically; set \(\mathcal B(x)=\sum_{(a,b)}R_{ab}(x)S_{ab}(x)\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-sided matching market with finite supported preference types and rational masses. The decision variables are type-to-type allocation masses \(x_{ab}\); the constraints bound the gain in matched mass, the \(L_1\) change from a supplied stable allocation, and the normalized mass of mutually willing pairs. The computational question is feasibility for given rational thresholds.

The objection that survived

The paper’s \(q\) measures changes to named matching edges, while the proposed distance cannot distinguish allocations with the same type flows but very different individual rewiring; the case acknowledges this gap but cannot establish that the authors would accept aggregate churn as their local-search measure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers only Theorem 3, via a type-flow analogue of proximity to a stable matching; it leaves Theorems 1, 2, and 4 and ordinary ASM unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact decision complexity in the number \(\tau\) of supported types and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does the problem remain hard or become tractable under restrictions on the acceptable type-pair graph or on the number of allocation cells changed from \(\sigma\)?
  • How do optima relate to finite cohort instances when the blocking-edge budget scales with the number of possible cross-side pairs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s local search problem. A large two-sided labor market gives it a credible high-multiplicity regime: think of a national hiring round with many applicants and positions, but relatively few applicant types and position types. A type records everything the matching question uses—qualifications, eligibility, and a strict ranking of acceptable types on the other side. Acceptability is class-based: if two types can match, every pair of individuals in those classes can. Millions of agents can then be represented by type masses, with \(\tau\) types far smaller than the population. The fractional allocation describes how much mass is matched between each pair of types; it does not make any individual hire fractional.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper by a parameter-preserving reduction from Multicolored Clique. It states that Local Search-ASM is W[1]-hard parameterized by \(k+q+t\). In the paper’s problem, the input is a bipartite graph with strict preference lists, a stable matching \(\mu\), and nonnegative integers \(k,q,t\). The question is whether there is a matching \(\eta\) with at least \(|\mu|+t\) edges, at most \(k\) blocking edges, and \(|\mu\triangle\eta|\le q\). The authors note that this reduction does not establish hardness for constant maximum degree, or even degree bounded by a function of the parameter.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Mass Local Search-ASM. Its input is two finite supported distributions, \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\), each of total mass \(1\); an acceptability graph between their types and strict rankings over acceptable opposite-side types; a stable allocation \(\sigma\); and rational thresholds \(\kappa,q,t\). A solution is a real nonnegative matrix \(x=(x_{ab})\), with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_A(a)\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\mu_B(b)\). Its matched mass is \(|x|=\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), and it must satisfy \(|x|\ge|\sigma|+t\).

To define its blocking-pair mass, let \(R_{ab}(x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) assigned to a type it ranks below \(b\), including unmatched agents; define \(S_{ab}(x)\) symmetrically as the mass of type \(b\) assigned below \(a\). Set \(\mathcal B(x)=\sum_{(a,b)}R_{ab}(x)S_{ab}(x)\), summing over acceptable type pairs. This is the measure of mutually willing cross-side pairs, normalized by the total number of possible cross-side pairs in a finite population. Define \(\mathcal D(x,\sigma)=\sum_{a,b}|x_{ab}-\sigma_{ab}|\), the aggregate mass analogue of symmetric difference. The question is whether some \(x\) has \(\mathcal B(x)\le\kappa\), \(\mathcal D(x,\sigma)\le q\), and the required gain in matched mass. The input allocation \(\sigma\) is promised stable, meaning \(\mathcal B(\sigma)=0\).

This is a direct mirror of the size-versus-stability tradeoff near a supplied stable outcome: \(t\) asks for more matched population, \(\kappa\) limits instability, and \(q\) limits how much of the allocation changes. The main complexity question is its exact decision complexity in the number of supported types \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\). My tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness: even after individuals are aggregated, the blocking-mass constraint couples allocation variables through products. Theorem 3 motivates the question, but its W[1]-hardness does not by itself transfer to this mass model.

The weakest point is that this is a genuine coarsening of the paper’s strict individual preferences. Within a type, agents are interchangeable and indifferent; moreover, \(\mathcal B\) measures the density of blocking pairs, while the paper counts individual blocking edges. The aggregate distance likewise ignores identity-level rewiring that leaves type-to-type flows unchanged. I think the mirror remains worth asking because those choices are natural in a large market whose operative decisions are made between preference classes, and because the paper’s local-search formulation already treats proximity to a stable outcome as part of the problem. This case covers Theorem 3’s local-search result; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s other hardness or algorithmic results.

The model also raises a rounding question: as finite cohort sizes grow, how closely do discrete optima and blocking-edge counts track this mass problem? A separate question is whether the continuous problem is tractable under restrictions on the type graph or on the number of allocation cells changed from \(\sigma\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational result, so the “no result to mirror” objection does not apply. Nor is a large matching market implausible. The problem is that the feature Theorem 3 studies is specifically a small change to a *particular matching*: \(q\) bounds the number of individual edges changed from the supplied stable matching \(\mu\). A type-level flow \(\sigma\) does not record which individuals are paired. The same flow can be realized while retaining most named pairs or while changing nearly all of them. The proposed \(\mathcal D(x,\sigma)\) measures changed *shares* between types; it cannot recover the paper’s symmetric-difference distance. To preserve that distance, type descriptions must retain the relevant identities, which removes the compression to a small support.

The blocking budget has a related scaling problem. With homogeneous types and complete compatibility between type classes, a type-level blocking interaction represents a rectangle of individual pairs. If the two sides have \(N_A\) and \(N_B\) agents, then \(\mathcal B(x)\) corresponds to a number of blocking edges on the order of \(N_A N_B\mathcal B(x)\). Keeping the paper’s fixed budget \(k\) therefore sends the allowed blocking mass to zero as the market grows. A fixed positive \(\kappa\), by contrast, permits a quadratic number of blocking edges. A bounded or linear number of exceptional blocking edges disappears in the limit. Recovering those sparse defects requires finite-population or identity-level information.

Mass distance and blocking density do define a coherent computational problem. But that problem replaces both defining quantities of the local-search result: individual churn becomes movement between type cells, and a small count of blocking edges becomes a bound on their density. Theorem 3 motivates that new problem without showing that it is the authors’ problem. I would not count this as a strong mirror unless the authors affirm that aggregate reallocation and macroscopic instability are what they want local search to measure.

This is a limited negative case, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. Its weak point is exactly that an author may endorse the aggregate interpretation: then the flow problem is a sensible computational question, even though it no longer preserves the paper’s identity-level notion of closeness.
