The continuous mirror question

Given finite driver and request type sets, rational offline masses, rational per-period arrival masses, edge data, group memberships, and a finite horizon, characterize the competitive-ratio vectors achievable by causal fluid matching policies. Each policy may split mass among allowed probe sequences, tracking patience states and splitting probed mass into successful and failed fractions according to edge success probabilities; compare its operator-profit and group-fairness values with the separate fluid optima. Determine whether every achievable three-objective vector satisfies \(r_O+r_U+r_V\leq 1\), for group fairness and for the corresponding per-unit and per-arrival-cohort individual-fairness objectives.

The model it lives in

A finite-type online fluid matching model: offline mass \(a_u\) and arriving mass \(b_{v,t}\) carry the paper’s utilities, groups, edge success probabilities, and patience data. At each period, a causal policy allocates submass to probe sequences; successes and failures update the mass in each patience state. Define each fluid benchmark as the separate optimum for operator profit or the relevant Rawlsian fairness objective, and measure a policy by its ratios to those benchmarks.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity fleet-and-request regime: many drivers and riders, but relatively few complete types. A driver type records everything the model uses—service area, vehicle class, group membership, utilities, acceptance probability, and patience. A request type records its route or service category, group, utilities, and patience. Those details matter: agents can share a type only when the matching problem treats them identically.

A continuous instance has finite type sets \(U,V\), rational offline masses \(a_u\), and rational online arrival masses \(b_{v,t}\) over the paper’s finite horizon \(t=1,\ldots,T\). In KIID, \(b_{v,t}=b_v\); in KAD, the masses may vary with time. Each edge \((u,v)\) retains its success probability \(p_{uv}\), three utilities, and patience constraints. At each time, a causal policy sends arriving mass through allowed probe sequences, respecting the available offline mass and patience. If it probes mass \(x\) on an edge, \(p_{uv}x\) becomes successful matches and \((1-p_{uv})x\) becomes failures that update the relevant patience states. Each person still gets at most one match; fractional quantities describe averages across a large cohort.

For example, the operator’s utility is \(\sum_{u,v,t} w^O_{uv}s_{uvt}\), where \(s_{uvt}\) is successful mass. Offline group utility is the minimum, over groups \(g\), of
\[ \frac{\sum_{u\in U(g)}\sum_{v,t} w^U_{uv}s_{uvt}}{\sum_{u\in U(g)}a_u}, \]
and online group utility divides the corresponding total for a group by its arriving mass. Individual fairness can likewise be defined per unit of each offline type and per unit of each arrival-period cohort. Compare a policy with separate fluid optima for operator utility and each fairness objective, just as the paper compares its algorithm with separate benchmarks.

This is plausible in a large platform serving repeated demand across a city or region: perhaps hundreds of thousands of requests and drivers per horizon, represented by hundreds or thousands of zone, route, service, and demographic types. The paper’s NYC experiment already bins locations into request and driver types, which gives a useful starting point. The high-multiplicity regime would require enough repeated agents per complete type; it would not fit a small committee, or a market where every driver has a genuinely different price, utility, or patience. That is a limitation of the scenario, not of the research area.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that in the KIID setting, \(TSGFKIID(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\) achieves simultaneous competitive ratios \((\alpha/(2e),\beta/(2e),\gamma/(2e))\) for operator profit, offline group fairness, and online group fairness, respectively, for positive \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) with \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma\leq 1\).

The corresponding question is Fluid-KIID Three-Objective Fair Matching: given the type masses, edge data, groups, horizon, and rational \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\), construct a polynomial-size description of a causal fluid policy that achieves at least those three ratios against the separate fluid benchmarks—or prove that no such policy exists. The question is about the underlying matching policy, not an LP encoding. I expect this mirror to be tractable: the paper’s benchmark is already a linear program over typed edges, and aggregate mass and patience-state conservation give a natural route to a time-expanded flow formulation. The question worth pursuing is whether the fluid setting admits better ratios, and how quickly finite populations approach its guarantees.

A second useful anchor is Theorem 4.4, also proved here, with its technical proof in Appendix B.4. It says that for all arrival models, no algorithm can simultaneously guarantee competitive ratios \((\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\) for operator profit, offline-side group fairness and online-side group fairness when \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma>1\). The same impossibility applies when both fairness objectives are individual fairness.

Its mirror is Fluid Three-Benchmark Ceiling: over finite-type fluid instances with the paper’s KIID or KAD arrival data, characterize the competitive-ratio vectors achievable by causal type-mass policies, separately for the group-fairness and individual-fairness variants. In particular, prove or refute that every achievable vector satisfies \(r_O+r_U+r_V\leq 1\). I expect the ceiling to persist: the paper’s obstruction comes from competing uses of matching capacity, and its proof uses only a small number of type classes, so scaling those classes to large cohorts does not remove the conflict. In ChoCo’s trichotomy this is closest to hardness transferring, though it is a structural impossibility, not a standard NP-hardness result. It invites a follow-up question about the exact fluid Pareto frontier.

There is an important qualification about the anchors: the paper has no named NP-hardness, \(P\), W[1]-hardness, or FPT theorem. These are named computational results in the form of an algorithmic competitive guarantee and an impossibility bound, not conventional complexity-class results. That still makes a mirror legitimate: the central target is a computational matching problem, and Theorem 4.1’s LP-based algorithm gives the mirror a concrete algorithmic anchor. Theorem 4.4 supplies a meaningful boundary rather than a fabricated hardness claim.

The paper’s online-type distributions are supporting prior art, not a collision. In its model, \(p_{v,t}\) is the probability of one indivisible vertex of type \(v\) arriving at time \(t\); offline vertices are still represented individually, and assignments remain discrete. The mirror makes both sides of the market population measures. The original algorithm and fairness objectives remain recognizable, while mass becomes the quantity used to allocate and measure them. This covers the paper’s group-fair KIID algorithm and its three-objective performance boundary, with corresponding individual-fairness questions. It does not cover the empirical evaluation or continuize time: the horizon and decision order remain part of the problem.

My weakest point is the online dynamics. In a fluid limit, random arrival fluctuations and random probe outcomes can wash out, which may improve the achievable ratios and change the paper’s \(1/(2e)\) guarantee. Conversely, if real driver-specific attributes split every cohort into its own type, the support grows until the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. So the defensible claim is that the mirror is a natural, author-recognizable problem for large homogeneous market cohorts—not that the paper’s quantitative bounds automatically transfer unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is a scope objection. Both anchors concern online competitive guarantees, not the running time or complexity of a computational problem over a continuous society. The paper gives algorithms and bounds, but its theorems do not classify how hard it is to compute an optimal policy. A fluid mirror could be a sensible online-control problem while still sitting outside ChoCo’s stated complexity agenda.

That objection is sharpest for Theorem 4.1. Its guarantee depends on indivisible arrivals and assignments, random probe outcomes, and contention for offline vertices. Keep those features and the model still has atomic agents; replace them with mass flows and deterministic success and failure fractions, and the policy class and benchmark change. In particular, the fluid question compares against fluid optima, while the paper’s competitive ratio compares against optima on realized stochastic instances. A convergence theorem connecting the two would be useful, but it is a different target from mirroring Theorem 4.1. Fluid approximations already guide competitive algorithms in large-inventory online allocation, including matching, so a mass model here would extend established online-optimization machinery rather than open a new complexity landscape. [Goyal, Iyengar, and Udwani](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03983)

Theorem 4.4 has the same scope problem. It rules out certain competitive-ratio vectors; it does not prove computational hardness. Its proposed fluid counterpart—a characterization of achievable ratios—is well-defined, but it asks for a performance frontier under a new benchmark and policy model. To make it a complexity question, one would need to specify a decision or optimization problem, such as deciding whether a given vector is achievable and measuring that problem’s complexity. The fluid frontier could be worthwhile in online matching, but the theorem itself supplies no hardness anchor for ChoCo’s tractability trichotomy.

I cannot make multiplicity or fairness degeneration carry this negative case. A large fleet with repeated, fully specified driver and request types is plausible; when agents of a type have identical data, type-level averages preserve the relevant fairness quantities. The exact type signature may be demanding in a real platform, but the paper’s binned rideshare setting offers a credible starting point.

So the strongest honest negative is limited: these are plausible fluid mirrors, but their value to ChoCo depends on whether it includes competitive online algorithm design and fluid-control analysis within its computational remit. I cannot honestly defeat the anchors under the broader reading. Theorem 4.4 in particular survives as a natural fluid performance question, even though it is not a complexity result.
