What the mirror covers

The proposed LP recasts the welfare allocation in Theorem 1; it does not cover the CMRA’s strategic results, including Theorems 2–4, or the paper’s revenue and empirical analyses.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible positive lead is the paper’s Theorem 1, proved here: CMRA-truthful bidding leads to an efficient allocation. In the paper’s general model, there may be any number of bidders and goods; goods and feasible bundles may be divisible or indivisible; bidders have monotone, quasilinear utilities. A truthful headline demand maximizes utility at the current clock prices, and an additional bid on a bundle is the bidder’s utility for that bundle minus the surplus from its headline demand, when this amount is nonnegative. The theorem says the auction ends at a welfare-maximizing allocation without further restrictions on preferences or on the permitted price-adjustment process.

That is a result about allocative efficiency, not computational efficiency: the paper gives no running-time claim, hardness result, or complexity formulation. So, under your anchor rule, the paper contains no qualifying named computational result. Theorem 1 is the nearest possible anchor, but it does not qualify. Theorem 4’s risk-free-collusion result is also a game-theoretic equilibrium characterization, not a complexity result.

If Theorem 1 were accepted as a near-anchor, a concrete population question would be Mass-CMRA Welfare Allocation. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete bidder types—each type includes its feasible packages and utility function—and let rational masses \(\mu_t\) sum to \(1\). For \(m\) goods, give each type a feasible set \(X_t\subseteq\{0,1\}^m\), a utility function \(U_t\), and let \(s\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\) be supply per capita. Find nonnegative rational masses \(y_{t,S}\), where \(y_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned integral package \(S\), maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\in X_t} y_{t,S}U_t(S) \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{S\in X_t}y_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\in X_t}y_{t,S}\mathbf{1}_S\le s. \]
This makes the population continuous while each individual still receives an integral package. A plausible regime is a large market of enterprise customers buying complementary bundles of standardized network or computing capacity: many customers, relatively few complete valuation-and-capacity types, and supply scaled with market size. The paper’s general bidder model makes this a recognizable extension of its efficiency question, though it covers only Theorem 1’s allocation result—not the strategic equilibria or collusion analysis.

With an explicit package list, this allocation problem is an LP. With succinct package and valuation descriptions, its pricing step can be hard; I would expect general complementarity to retain hardness driven by the package dimension \(m\), while structured valuations may permit efficient optimization. Those are research expectations, not results of this paper. The weakest point is that this turns the paper’s economic efficiency theorem into a new winner-determination problem; the authors do not study its computational complexity. The practical spectrum-auction setting also has few substantial bidders, so the high-multiplicity customer scenario is an extension rather than the paper’s demonstrated regime.

The paper already allows divisible goods, but that continuizes the outcome space, not the population. It does not supply the computational anchor ChoCo requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the case for concedes: the paper has no named computational result. Theorem 1 proves that a specified bidding strategy yields an efficient allocation; Propositions 1–2 and Theorems 2–4 concern revenue and strategic incentives. None analyzes the complexity of finding an allocation, equilibrium, or profitable deviation. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, there is no computational claim here to mirror.

The proposed Mass-CMRA Welfare Allocation is a coherent high-multiplicity problem. It also changes the question: it gives a planner the type distribution and asks for the welfare-maximizing assignment, leaving out the CMRA’s bids, price clock, closing rule, and strategic behavior. With an explicit package list it is an LP; with succinct package descriptions, the hard step is pricing over packages. That may be a worthwhile problem in its own right, but it is not a computational result of this paper or a complexity question about its truthful-auction theorem.

A closer version would give each complete bidder type a mass and let those types submit mass-level CMRA bids. That preserves the population axis, and fractional type-level allocations can still assign integral packages to each unit of mass. But the strategic results become difficult to carry over: in an atomless population an individual deviation has no effect on the aggregate auction, while treating a whole type’s mass as a strategic bidder makes the type a coalition. A finite, encoded version might still be worth studying, but the paper does not identify that computational problem.

So the proposed welfare-allocation problem does not survive as an anchor under the programme’s criterion. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists is less secure: a carefully specified mass-market auction could be interesting. The negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational result in this paper, and weak as a universal claim against every plausible reformulation.
