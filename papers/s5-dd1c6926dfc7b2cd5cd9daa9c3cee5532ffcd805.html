The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) items, \(\tau\) types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational linear valuations \(v_t\), per-agent budgets \(B_t\), per-capita supplies \(s_j\), and feasible linear constraints on aggregate allocations, compute an optimal constrained-EG allocation and its supporting prices and type-specific interventions. For an approximation version, can this be done with feasibility residuals and primal-dual gap at most \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\), where \(K\) is the number of constraints and \(L\) is the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A Fisher market with a continuum of agents partitioned into \(\tau\) types, where type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), common valuation vector \(v_t\), and common budget \(B_t\). Each agent of type \(t\) receives a bundle \(y_t\) and leftover budget \(\delta_t\); aggregate allocation is \(z_{tj}=\mu_t y_{tj}\), with per-capita supply constraints \(\sum_t z_{tj}\le s_j\). The designer maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t[B_t\log(v_t\cdot y_t+\delta_t)-\delta_t]\) subject to linear constraints on \(z\), then uses the dual multipliers to obtain prices and interventions.

The objection that survived

The typed program compresses identical buyers into a weighted Eisenberg–Gale program, so it may introduce little new algorithmic structure; this limits novelty but does not make the population-mass question ill-posed.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1's constrained-EG implementation and Proposition 2's OPIC convergence under type masses; it leaves Propositions 3–12, the welfare and incentive claims, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational linear utilities, budgets, supplies, and constraints, does the typed constrained-EG problem admit an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • What exact bit-complexity guarantees are available for prices and intervention multipliers, and what finite-round bounds hold for OPIC on type-mass inputs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s large advertising market: millions of buyers, but many sharing the same valuation vector, budget, and fairness-group membership. A type is that complete tuple. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers of that type. The number of buyers can be enormous while the support size \(\tau\) stays moderate. This is plausible in the paper’s setting of advertisers buying recurring impression categories, and the paper itself treats repeated reports as representative buyers. That existing aggregation supports the regime; it does not settle the complexity questions about binary-encoded masses and support size.

The population is the object being continuized. Items remain a finite set, and individual allocations remain fractional, as in the paper’s Fisher model. For a finite market with \(n_t\) buyers of type \(t\), the normalized mass is \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); supplies scale per buyer, as they do in the paper’s definition of a large market.

Lead mirror — Continuous Constrained-Fisher Intervention. Proposition 1, proved in this paper (Appendix B), says that for a constrained Eisenberg–Gale optimum \(x^*\), the Lagrange multipliers on the linear allocation constraints define personalized price interventions: \(\bar p^*_{ij}=\sum_k A_{kij}\lambda_k^*\). Together with prices from the supply constraints, these interventions make \(x^*\) a tax-subsidy equilibrium satisfying the constraints. This is a constructive implementability result, not a P-time theorem.

Here is a typed version of that problem. An instance has \(m\) items; \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to one; rational linear valuations \(v_{tj}\) and per-buyer budgets \(B_t\); per-capita item supplies \(s_j\); and feasible linear constraints on aggregate allocations. A type-\(t\) buyer receives \(y_{tj}\) units of item \(j\) and keeps \(\delta_t\) of their budget, with utility \(u_t=v_t\cdot y_t+\delta_t\). Aggregate allocation is \(z_{tj}=\mu_t y_{tj}\), so supply requires \(\sum_t z_{tj}\le s_j\). The designer’s objective is the typed constrained-EG objective \(\sum_t\mu_t[B_t\log(u_t)-\delta_t]\), subject to supply and the given fairness constraints on \(z\).

The question is to find an optimal allocation and its constraint and supply multipliers, then return the resulting tax-subsidy schedule and equilibrium prices. A solution must attain the constrained objective, satisfy the aggregate constraints and supply limits, and make each type’s chosen bundle demand-optimal at prices \(p_j+\bar p_{tj}\). For an \(\varepsilon\)-computational version, ask for an allocation and dual solution with feasibility residuals and primal-dual gap at most \(\varepsilon\), with running time measured in \(m,\tau,K,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\), where \(K\) is the number of constraints and \(L\) is the input bit length. An aggregate exposure floor, \(\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t\sum_{j\in P}y_{tj}\ge \ell\), is one direct instance; the mirror also covers the paper’s other linear constraints.

I expect this mirror to be Class A for approximation, under standard convex-optimization assumptions: with finite typed support, the program has \(O(\tau m)\) allocation variables and linear constraints, and its objective is concave. Proposition 1 supplies the equilibrium interpretation of its dual variables. The paper does not establish a bit-complexity bound, so that tractability claim is a new question for a prover, not a result to attribute to the authors. Exact computation and the representation of exact prices or multipliers need separate treatment.

Second mirror — Continuous OPIC. Proposition 2, also proved here, is the paper’s convergence result for Online Price Intervention Computation. Given observed equilibrium allocations, the algorithm updates inequality multipliers by projected subgradient steps and equality multipliers by ordinary subgradient steps. With no redundant constraints and a learning-rate sequence satisfying \(\sum_r\gamma_r=\infty\) and \(\sum_r\gamma_r^2<\infty\), the induced intervention prices converge to the set of optimal intervention prices.

In the typed version, each round has the same mass distribution and supplies. Valuations and budgets are not disclosed to the designer; they are fixed within each type, and the designer observes the resulting per-type allocations \(y^r\). Let \(z^r_{tj}=\mu_t y^r_{tj}\). For inequality constraints \(A_1z\le b_1\) and equalities \(A_2z=b_2\), update \(\lambda_1^{r+1}=[\lambda_1^r+\gamma_r(A_1z^r-b_1)]_+\) and \(\lambda_2^{r+1}=\lambda_2^r+\gamma_r(A_2z^r-b_2)\); set each type’s intervention from those multipliers. The question is whether these observed-residual updates converge to optimal interventions that implement the constraints in the continuous market. A solution is a proof of that convergence, together with the induced limiting intervention prices. I expect convergence under the paper’s assumptions, hence a Class A iterative result; finite-time rates and the number of rounds needed for an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate outcome are further questions. The paper’s proposition guarantees asymptotic convergence, not a polynomial iteration bound.

Neither anchor is a named NP-hardness or P-classification result; the paper gives no such complexity classification. They are the paper’s two named computational results: Proposition 1’s constructive implementation and Proposition 2’s convergence guarantee. I would not present either as already proving polynomial-time solvability.

This mirror covers the implementation and online-computation results of Propositions 1 and 2. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s welfare counterexamples or its buyer-level Pareto, envy, and incentive claims. Those raise additional questions, but are not needed for a strong case on the central computational contribution.

The case’s weakest point is how close the paper already comes to this model. It defines large markets with supply growing with the buyer count, discusses finite type distributions, and explicitly aggregates repeated reports in its mechanism. A referee could reasonably view the mass formulation as a high-multiplicity reparameterization of their finite model. The positive case is that the paper does not classify computation in the parameters \(\tau\) and \(L\), or ask how much of the intervention and convergence story survives when rational type masses are the input. The authors are therefore likely to recognize the model and the questions, even if they regard the formulation as a natural next step rather than a different market theory.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor, as stated, isolates a new population-sensitive computation. Proposition 1 is a KKT argument for constrained Eisenberg–Gale: its multipliers become price interventions. In the proposed typed model, replacing each group of identical buyers by a type of mass \(\mu_t\) simply rewrites that same program using \(z_{tj}=\mu_t y_{tj}\). The paper even says to treat identical reports as a representative buyer with the group’s total budget. So the proposed typed implementation theorem largely repeats the paper’s construction in aggregate coordinates.

That objection does not dispose of a sharper question about bit complexity in \((m,\tau,L)\). The paper does not answer that question, and its representative-buyer discussion is not a complexity classification. But for explicit rational linear valuations and constraints, the typed formulation is a finite-dimensional weighted EG program. The population limit adds no new computational primitive to the construction. That makes this a weak claim of “no worthwhile mirror,” not a collision that settles it.

Proposition 2 is more vulnerable as presented. Its continuous version is the same dual subgradient update with per-type masses in the observed constraint residuals. The proposed mirror asks whether those updates converge, and the paper already proves convergence for its finite market. For rational masses, the typed equations follow by aggregation; there is no new convergence phenomenon in the stated question.

A stronger OPIC mirror could ask for finite-time error bounds and running time in the support size and input bit length, with a precise model for the equilibrium oracle. That question is not answered by Proposition 2. Yet the paper’s update already operates on aggregate constraint violations, and the continuous population does not itself supply a new oracle or algorithmic bottleneck. Whether this is worth pursuing depends on what complexity guarantees the programme wants; the paper alone does not rule it out.

I cannot honestly defeat the best version of both mirrors. The paper’s market has plausible repeated buyer types, its central constraints depend on aggregate allocations, and individual identity is not essential to either anchor. High multiplicity therefore remains a sensible regime here. My negative case can discount the mirrors as close reformulations and argue that their current statements add little; it cannot support the universal conclusion that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The unresolved input-sensitive complexity question for the typed constrained market is precisely where this case is weakest.
