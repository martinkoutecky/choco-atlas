The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(T_L,T_R\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to \(1\) on each side, positive rational valuations \(v_{ab},w_{ba}\), a rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and a welfare threshold \(\Omega\), does there exist a mass matching \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\), such that \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}(v_{ab}+w_{ba})\ge\Omega\) and, for every pair \((a,b)\), at least one of \(\mu_a-\sum_{b':v_{ab'}\ge\alpha v_{ab}}x_{ab'}\) and \(\nu_b-\sum_{a':w_{ba'}\ge\alpha w_{ba}}x_{a'b}\) is zero?

The model it lives in

A two-sided population with finitely many types, where each type carries mass and valuations over opposite-side types; the decision variable \(x_{ab}\) is matched mass, welfare is \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}(v_{ab}+w_{ba})\), and a type pair blocks when both sides have positive mass assigned below the \(\alpha\)-threshold.

The objection that survived

The reduction’s condition \(\alpha>(N-1)/N\) depends on rank gaps that shrink with population size; with a fixed finite type set and fixed valuation levels, it does not supply hardness as \(N\) grows.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the maximum-welfare \(\alpha\)-stable matching decision question anchored by Theorem 2. It leaves Theorem 1’s \(\alpha\)-Boosting guarantee and Propositions 1–2’s stability–efficiency tradeoff uncontinuized, and does not claim the NP-completeness reduction survives.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the decision problem remain hard when every type has large mass and the number of types is modest?
  • Can it be solved in polynomial time for fixed support or under bounded valuation asymmetry?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It establishes that, for \(\alpha\in((n-1)/n,1)\), MAX-WEIGHT-\(\alpha\)-SMCT is NP-complete: given a balanced instance with complete cardinal preference lists and ties, decide whether it has an \(\alpha\)-stable matching with social welfare at least a specified threshold. The paper proves this by reducing from MIN-COST-SMT, whose hardness is cited to Manlove et al. (2002). In the reduction, rank costs become cardinal values \(n+1-c\); the chosen \(\alpha\) makes \(\alpha\)-blocking correspond to strict blocking in the source instance, and maximizing welfare corresponds to minimizing rank cost.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large placement market: many graduates and positions, grouped by credential and by their full valuation vectors over the other side’s position or candidate types. The type counts might be modest compared with the cohort size. A type records everything relevant to the matching, including its valuations of every opposite-side type; people with different valuation vectors belong to different types.

Here is the continuous decision problem I would put to a prover. Call it Maximum-Welfare \(\alpha\)-Stable Mass Matching. Its input is finite type sets \(T_L,T_R\); rational type masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b>0\), each side summing to \(1\); positive rational valuations \(v_{ab},w_{ba}\) for every pair of types; a rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\); and a welfare threshold \(\Omega\). Seek a matrix of matched masses \(x_{ab}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\). Any leftover mass is unmatched. A unit of mass receives one partner type, while a cohort can be divided across partner types.

For each pair \((a,b)\), let \(B^L_{ab}(x)=\mu_a-\sum_{b':\,v_{ab'}\ge\alpha v_{ab}}x_{ab'}\), and define \(B^R_{ab}(x)=\nu_b-\sum_{a':\,w_{ba'}\ge\alpha w_{ba}}x_{a'b}\). These are the masses on each side whose current assignments are worth strictly less than an \(\alpha\)-blocking partner. Call \(x\) mass-\(\alpha\)-stable if, for every pair, \(B^L_{ab}(x)=0\) or \(B^R_{ab}(x)=0\). This captures the absence of a positive mass of mutually blocking agents. The question is whether such an \(x\) exists with \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}(v_{ab}+w_{ba})\ge\Omega\); the matrix \(x\) is the solution witness.

The anchor-faithful slice can take masses of the form \(\mu_a=p_a/N\), \(\nu_b=q_b/N\), with counts summing to a population scale \(N\), and \(\alpha\in((N-1)/N,1)\). The mass flows \(x_{ab}\) remain divisible. That distinction is central: the proposed problem keeps the paper’s cardinal welfare objective and multiplicative blocking rule, while changing the population representation to type masses. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their maximum-welfare \(\alpha\)-stable matching question for an anonymous cohort market. The paper’s cited work on stable fractional matchings is a relevant neighbor, but it studies fractional outcomes for specified agents; it does not by itself settle this type-population question.

For this mirror, I would provisionally expect continuum-specific hardness in the unrestricted number of types. Each type pair creates a choice about which side must have zero vulnerable mass. Fixing one such choice for every pair leaves a linear program, so direct enumeration gives an \(2^{|T_L||T_R|}\operatorname{poly}(|T_L||T_R|,L)\) algorithm. That makes the problem fixed-parameter tractable in the number of type pairs, while leaving room for hardness as support grows. This is a prediction about the mass problem, not a consequence of Theorem 2.

The weakness is substantial: the paper’s reduction uses indivisible matchings. In the mass problem, a type’s supply can split across partner types, potentially changing both stability and achievable welfare. The theorem therefore does not establish hardness for this mirror, especially when \(N\) is much larger than the type support. That gap is also the main research question: does the hardness persist when every type has large mass, or does divisible population make the problem tractable in \(|T_L|+|T_R|\)? Other natural questions are whether hardness holds under a fixed bound on valuation asymmetry, and whether the type-pair enumeration can be improved to a polynomial-time algorithm. This mirror covers the paper’s optimality-hardness result in Theorem 2; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s approximation algorithm or its full stability–efficiency tradeoff.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a weak anchor for a population mirror. Its reduction gives each named agent a valuation \(n+1-c_{ij}\) for each named counterpart, encoding that agent’s position in a particular preference list. In a high-multiplicity model, a type must share its valuations across counterpart types. Preserving the reduction’s arbitrary preference structure may therefore require \(\tau=\Theta(n)\) distinct types: the population count grows, but the instances gain little from treating the population as a distribution. And even then, the reduction does not establish hardness for divisible mass transfers; the indivisible matching choices it relies on have changed.

The proponent’s sharper scaling argument does not fix this. In the reduction, \(\alpha>(n-1)/n\) makes every strict rank improvement count as blocking. If instead the market has a fixed finite type set and fixed valuations while its population \(N\) grows, the ratios between distinct valuation levels have a maximum \(q<1\). Eventually \((N-1)/N>q\), so \(\alpha\)-stability on that slice coincides with ordinary stability. The reduction’s near-one threshold then no longer supplies the argument. Keeping the threshold tied to increasingly fine valuation levels requires the type or valuation structure to grow with the population.

There is a credible alternative: a large placement market in which graduates and positions fall into a modest number of classes, with values determined by class, and a positive mass of mutually willing agents counts as a block. That is a coherent continuous-population problem, and the authors might recognize it. But it is a new anonymous-flow question, not a continuous version of the reduction’s demonstrated hardness. The paper on stable fractional matchings is a close computational neighbor: it studies welfare optimization under cardinal preferences and approximate stability, but for named agents whose fractional assignments give them average utilities. That does not settle the cohort model, where different members of a type can receive different partner types and stability depends on vulnerable mass. It does mean the contribution would have to be specifically about population aggregation, not merely fractional matching ([Caragiannis et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2020.103416)).

So the strongest negative is that Theorem 2 does not carry its computational content into the useful high-multiplicity regime; faithfully retaining its individualized structure tends to make the type support grow with the population, while a compact type model changes the problem the reduction encodes. But the cohort-placement mirror remains plausible and distinct from outcome-space fractional matching. I cannot honestly turn that objection into the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
