What the mirror covers

The proposed inspiration concerns the stability bounds in Theorems 1.3 and 1.5; it does not mirror a computational result, and the paper's other analytic results and open problems remain outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive opening is a limit intuition: with fixed \(k\) and many voters, the ballot proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) form a natural continuous description of the electorate. The paper’s quantitative bounds might motivate asking whether paradox probability is controlled by distance from a transitive rule in such a limit.

But I cannot give that as a valid ChoCo mirror under your anchor requirement. The closest named results are Theorem 1.3, proved here, which says that for an IIA GSWF on \(k\) alternatives, nontransitivity probability at most \(C(\epsilon/k^2)^3\) implies \(D_1(F)\le\epsilon\); and Theorem 1.5, also proved here, which gives a different bound implying \(D_2(F)\le\epsilon\). Both are quantitative stability theorems. Neither asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm for a computational problem.

The limit intuition also does not supply a faithful mass-transfer question. The paper studies functions on named voters’ full profiles, with probability taken over a product distribution on those profiles. Its distances to dictatorships depend on voter identity. Replacing a profile by its ballot-mass vector would generally make the rule anonymous and lose that identity-dependent information; the paper’s theorems would not automatically carry over.

So the honest positive case is only that the results may inspire a new continuum-limit stability question. There is no named computational anchor here, and no precise continuous computational problem I can defend as mirroring one of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.5 are quantitative analytic bounds: they relate the probability of a non-transitive outcome to distances between social welfare functions. They give no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational optimization problem for a continuous-population model. A continuous question inspired by either theorem could be mathematically interesting, but it would not mirror a computational result of this paper.

Theorem 1.3 is the stronger candidate to rescue. One might ask for a stability bound for a social welfare functional defined on a continuum of voter types. But the theorem’s \(D_1\) and paradox probability are measured over the uniform distribution on named-voter profiles \((S_k)^n\). A continuous society, as ChoCo defines it, is a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types. Its mass vector does not specify how an arbitrary GSWF acts on the individual coordinates of every possible profile. If the rule is anonymous, masses can summarize a profile, but that restricts the rules being studied; the theorem covers general IIA GSWFs. Moreover, applying a rule directly to exact masses makes the outcome deterministic. Recovering the theorem’s probability requires adding finite-sample randomness or noise, which makes uncertainty the continuized object. Replacing dictatorship distance with distance to some continuum analogue would be a reasonable new stability question, but it would need its own object, metric, and computational task.

Theorem 1.5 has an even weaker route to a population mirror. Its \(D_2\) measures the distance of a pairwise choice function on the voters’ Boolean preference coordinates from a constant or a single-voter dictatorship. Retaining that coordinate-based distance in a continuum leaves the individual voter as the relevant unit; an atomless population has no positive-mass individual. A positive-mass bloc could stand in for a dictator, but that changes the benchmark and asks a different stability question. Either version still lacks a computational problem for which an algorithm or complexity classification is being sought.

Including voter identity or role in the type description does not fix the gap. A type can record everything relevant to the problem, as ChoCo requires. Here, however, preserving arbitrary identity-dependent rules can require a distinct type for each voter, so the type distribution offers no useful aggregation of the rule’s input. Restricting to repeated roles or anonymous rules may yield a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but it changes the class of rules and does not create a computational anchor in this paper.

The best case for the paper is therefore that it could inspire new work on stability for aggregate or anonymous social choice. That is a real possibility, but it is a newly formulated analytic question; the proposed mirrors do not turn either named theorem into a continuous computational problem. The negative case is strongest on that scope mismatch. It cannot show that no related question could ever be worthwhile, but the paper gives no solid basis for spending ChoCo’s effort on a continuous mirror.
