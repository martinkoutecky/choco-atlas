The continuous mirror question

Given worker types \(A\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\), job types \(Q\) with rational per-worker supplies \(\sigma_q\), and additive valuations \(v_a(q)\), let \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{Q}\) range over bundles schedulable by earliest-deadline-first. Define \(\mathrm{MMS}_a(\sigma)=\max_{\lambda}\min_{B:\lambda_B>0}v_a(B)\), where \(\lambda\) is a distribution over feasible integral bundles with mean supply \(\sum_B\lambda_B B_q=\sigma_q\) for every \(q\). Find the minimum \(\delta\ge 0\) and masses \(x_{a,B}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_B x_{a,B}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}B_q=\sigma_q\) for every \(q\), and every bundle with \(x_{a,B}>0\) satisfies \(v_a(B)\ge\mathrm{MMS}_a(\sigma)-\delta\).

The model it lives in

A continuous workforce with society mass \(\mu\) over complete worker valuation types and per-worker job supplies \(\sigma\). Decision variables \(x_{a,B}\) assign worker mass to integral, schedule-feasible bundles; minimize the worst additive shortfall \(\delta\) from each type’s maximin-share benchmark.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the optimal additive-MMS objective across worker types. It leaves the paper’s multiplicative and welfare objectives, its other parameterized algorithms, and its remaining hardness results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the problem FPT in \(\tau_Q+\#d+p_{\max}+v_{\max}\), with polynomial dependence on \(\tau_A\) and the input bit length?
  • For rational inputs, when does the continuous optimum coincide with a finite representative instance, and how can a continuous allocation be rounded to finite workforce sizes?
  • Where does the problem become hard as job-type support grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the paper’s optimal additive MMS scheduling problem, with the workers—not the jobs—as the continuous population.

The regime is a large platform workforce: many workers perform shifts under the same scheduling rules, and workers with the same complete valuation vector for the recurring job types are one type. Let \(A\) be these worker types, with rational population masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\). Let \(Q\) be the finite set of job types. A job type specifies its processing time and deadline; \(\sigma_q\) is the rational supply of type \(q\) per worker. A finite representative instance has \(N\mu_a\) workers of type \(a\) and \(N\sigma_q\) jobs of type \(q\), for a common denominator \(N\). Thus \(N\) can be very large while the support sizes \(\tau_A=|A|\) and \(\tau_Q=|Q|\) stay modest. This is a workforce continuum with the per-capita job supply scaled along with it.

Here is the lead question, Continuous Additive-MMS Scheduling. A worker bundle is a vector \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{Q}\): each worker still receives an integral bundle of jobs, and \(B\) must be schedulable before its deadlines by earliest-deadline-first. Write \(v_a(B)=\sum_{q\in Q}v_a(q)B_q\). A continuous allocation is a nonnegative mass \(x_{a,B}\) of type-\(a\) workers assigned bundle \(B\), satisfying
\[ \sum_B x_{a,B}=\mu_a\quad\forall a,\qquad \sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}B_q=\sigma_q\quad\forall q. \]
The first constraint allocates every worker; the second uses every job in the per-capita supply. Although \(x\) and the type-level averages may be fractional, every bundle assigned to a worker is integral and schedule-feasible.

Define type \(a\)’s continuous maximin share against the per-capita supply by
\[ \mathrm{MMS}_a(\sigma)= \max_{\lambda}\ \min_{B:\lambda_B>0} v_a(B), \quad \sum_B\lambda_B=1,\quad \sum_B\lambda_B B_q=\sigma_q\ \ \forall q, \]
where \(\lambda\) ranges over distributions on feasible integral bundles. This is the continuum version of partitioning the jobs among many identical copies of one agent: in a finite representative instance, \(\lambda\) is the fraction of workers receiving each bundle. Given these benchmarks, the problem is to find an allocation \(x\) minimizing \(\delta\ge0\) such that every bundle assigned positive mass to type \(a\) satisfies \(v_a(B)\ge \mathrm{MMS}_a(\sigma)-\delta\). The output is the optimal \(\delta\) and a witnessing allocation.

My anchor is Meta-Theorem 2, proved in this paper. Its full relevant claim is that, for MMS, multiplicative MMS, additive MMS, and welfare MMS scheduling with deadlines, assuming \(P\ne NP\), there is no FPT algorithm under any strict subset of the parameter sets \(\{n\}\), \(\{m,v_{\max},g_{\max}\}\), or \(\{m,\#d,p_{\max},v_{\max}\}\); the paper’s reductions establish NP-hardness even when the parameters in the smaller sets are constant. I rely here on its additive-MMS case. The paper proves the hardness through reductions in its hardness section; this is not a result merely cited from elsewhere.

I expect this mirror to be Class A when the number of job types and relevant scheduling parameters are bounded: plausibly FPT in \(\tau_Q+\#d+p_{\max}+v_{\max}\), with polynomial dependence on \(\tau_A\) and the input bit length. For a fixed target value, both the benchmark calculation and allocation feasibility can be written as configuration LPs over the feasible bundles. Their pricing problem optimizes over integer bundle vectors subject to the deadline constraints and a valuation threshold. With few job types, that is fixed-dimensional integer optimization; binary search over integral valuation thresholds then gives the MMS values and the additive optimum. This is a credible use of the paper’s computational focus: the problem remains exact fair scheduling, while type-level mass makes aggregate assignments amenable to continuous optimization.

The result I am mirroring is the optimal additive guarantee across workers, not the paper’s entire classification or every MMS objective. The reduction from finite high-multiplicity instances is direct when the rational masses have a common denominator. Further questions are whether this continuous optimum is the limit of finite-instance optima, how accurately one can round its allocation back to a finite workforce, and where the configuration pricing problem becomes hard as job-type support grows. I would expect hardness to persist in some unrestricted regimes; continuization should not be presumed to erase hardness encoded in many distinct job types.

The weak point is that this supplies a careful continuum extension of MMS rather than one already defined by the authors: a referee could dispute whether the clone-type benchmark is the right continuous meaning of MMS. Also, the paper’s hardest reductions often use many jobs while holding the number of machines small. My mirror makes the machine-side agent population large, so those reductions do not themselves show that hardness disappears. I think the question survives that limitation because it keeps the paper’s indivisible worker bundles, deadline feasibility, and MMS objective intact while changing the population to a high-multiplicity regime. The job supply per capita is a necessary co-modification; the objects being continuized are the workers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the additive-MMS anchor is that the proposed model scales job supply along with the worker population and defines each worker’s benchmark against that per-worker supply. That makes it a continuum allocation market, rather than the literal limit of a scheduling instance with a fixed set of jobs.

But the paper’s deadline scheduling model allows precisely this co-modification, and the connection to a finite instance is direct. Take \(N\mu_a\) workers of each type and \(N\sigma_q\) jobs of each type. When allocation masses are multiples of \(1/N\), each worker still receives an integral, feasible bundle, and the allocation is an ordinary schedule. Likewise, a distribution \(\lambda\) whose masses are multiples of \(1/N\) is exactly a partition of the jobs into \(N\) feasible bundles for computing one worker’s MMS. Thus the proposed benchmark is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s own MMS definition, not an unrelated fairness notion.

The better version of the mirror is plausible too: take recurring worker cohorts with the same valuation vector over a finite set of job types, and recurring jobs whose processing times and deadlines match. The setting fits the paper’s gig-work motivation, and defining a worker type by the full valuation vector handles the identity objection. With bounded job-type support, feasibility and value thresholds give configuration LPs; pricing over integral bundle counts appears to be a fixed-dimensional integer optimization problem. That is a credible route into the continuous-optimization tools the programme is meant to examine. Whether it proves tractable is not a reason to reject the question.

The remaining negative case is that many real platforms have highly varied workers or jobs, so support may be large and the algorithmic gain may disappear. That identifies a boundary to investigate; it does not defeat the mirror in a plausible repeated-cohort regime. I can’t honestly make a strong case that this anchor has no worthwhile continuous mirror. The defensible negative point is narrower: the proposed model changes job supply as well as population, so its applicability depends on whether the authors recognize per-capita supply and clone-based MMS as the right scheduling question. Given the paper’s stated applications and the finite-instance correspondence, that objection is weak.
