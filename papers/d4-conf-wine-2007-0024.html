The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: for every class of set systems closed under subdivision, mechanism MP is false-name-proof when each agent owns one element, and its frugality ratio is \(O(2^n)\), where \(n=|E|\). Theorem 2, also proved here, gives a matching \(\Omega(2^n)\) frugality lower bound for truthful, false-name-proof mechanisms on the stated classes. Theorem 3 proves AP false-name-proof even when agents own multiple elements; Theorem 4 bounds its total payments by the reserve cost \(r\). These are named mechanism-design guarantees, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified computationally. The path-auction polynomial-time implementation and the general-case NP-hardness mentioned in §3.1 are not numbered results, so they cannot serve as anchors under your rules.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is repeated routing or service procurement: many identical tasks are assigned to feasible routes, and providers are grouped by their full capability and cost type. A continuous version could choose masses of tasks assigned to each feasible path, subject to provider-capacity constraints, while minimizing procurement cost. That is a sensible continuous-population problem, but it changes the paper’s single-team mechanism question into a repeated-task allocation problem. In particular, it does not mirror Theorem 1’s false-name guarantee or its dependence on the finite number of elements \(n\).

So the positive lead is only that team procurement has plausible high-multiplicity settings. The weak point is decisive for this assignment: I cannot tie that lead to a qualifying named computational result, and I would not present it as a mirror of one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent supplies no qualifying anchor. Theorems 1–4 establish false-name-proofness, frugality bounds, and a payment bound. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s polynomial-time path implementation and general set-system hardness appear only in passing. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The best attempt to build a mirror from the theorems runs into a deeper problem: their central quantities count identities. Theorem 2’s \(n=|E|\) grows when one agent self-divides an element into more pseudo-agents, even though the underlying work need not grow. Theorem 1’s penalty responds to that same act. Theorem 3’s false-name guarantee concerns how an agent’s elements are split among identities, while Theorem 4’s payment bound uses the number of agents in the winning set. A distribution of provider types records mass and attributes; it does not by itself record how many distinct names claim a bundle, or which claims share an owner. Preserving those facts requires retaining the discrete identity structure that the proposed population aggregate was meant to replace.

A better setting might have many repeated service tasks, interchangeable providers, and capacity constraints, with provider types represented by masses. That could yield a sensible continuous allocation problem. But it changes the paper’s single-team procurement question into repeated allocation. More fundamentally, in an atomless model an individual has zero mass, so unilateral false-name manipulation—the paper’s subject—can become vacuous. Retaining positive-mass strategic agents preserves the incentive question but restores an atomic model. That makes the repeated-task setting a possible adjacent research direction, not a continuous mirror of these results.

So the case against mirroring this paper is strong: its named results are outside the programme’s computational target, and the most tempting repurposings rely on identity-sensitive, atomic incentives. I would not claim to have proved that no worthwhile continuous procurement model exists in any scenario. Repeated-task procurement may be worth studying; it simply does not supply the missing mirror of this paper’s theorems.
