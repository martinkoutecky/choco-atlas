The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational result in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Definitions 1–3 define voting concepts and methods; they are not computational anchors. The paper does say, in an unnumbered aside, that finding a Chamberlin–Courant winning committee is NP-hard, citing Lu and Boutilier [25]. That is prior work, not a named result proved here. So I cannot honestly supply an anchor of the kind your instructions require.

The strongest plausible mirror I see is a continuous version of the paper’s IAC spoiler-frequency analysis. Let \(C\) be the candidates, \(k\) the committee size, and \(T\) the finite set of ballot types, including partial ballots interpreted as in the paper. A society is a mass distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Fix Scottish-style STV with fractional surplus transfers and a deterministic tie-break; in the continuum its Droop quota is \(1/(k+1)\). For a candidate \(s\), form \(\mu^{-s}\) by deleting \(s\) from each ballot and preserving the remaining rankings and masses. Call \(s\) a spoiler if it is not in the original winning committee and the winning committee changes under \(\mu^{-s}\).

One concrete problem would be IAC-Limit Spoiler Volume: given \(C\), \(k\), the ballot-type convention, and a rational accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), estimate within \(\varepsilon\) the normalized volume of societies \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) for which at least one candidate is a spoiler under STV. This corresponds to the paper’s question about spoiler likelihood under IAC: as the electorate grows, the uniform distribution over anonymous finite profiles approaches uniform volume over the simplex of population shares. The many agents are voters in a large election; types are their complete ballot descriptions, and mass is the share holding each ballot. The continuum concerns the population itself. Fractional transfers are part of the STV counting rule, not the continuization.

I would expect additive approximation of this volume to be tractable by sampling societies and running the STV count; computing the exact volume may be harder, and the paper gives no basis for classifying it. This mirror covers the IAC simulation question only. It does not mirror the Scottish-data findings, which concern particular finite electorates. The paper’s Table 13, for example, reports simulated IAC spoiler rates for \(m=4,k=2\), but that is an empirical simulation result, not a qualifying named computational anchor.

The case’s weakest point is therefore decisive for the requested format: the paper studies spoiler frequency, not the complexity of computing it, and supplies no named computational result for the continuous question to carry forward. The IAC-limit problem is a plausible research question the authors could recognize, but it cannot be presented as a mirror of a qualifying anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject the proponent’s IAC-Limit Spoiler Volume as a surviving anchor. The paper has no named computational result to mirror: Definitions 1–3 define voting concepts and methods, while the paper’s results are simulation estimates and empirical measurements. Its NP-hardness statement for Chamberlin–Courant cites prior work; it is not a result established by this paper.

The IAC proposal is coherent, and I would not object that ranked ballots lack a meaningful high-multiplicity regime. For fixed \(m\), anonymous profiles with many voters do converge to a uniform distribution over the simplex of ballot shares. But the proposed volume calculation is a new computational question about that limit, not a computational question the paper formulates. For a given society, the spoiler predicate can be checked by running weighted STV and then checking the \(m\) candidate-deletion cases. The paper instead estimates how often that predicate holds under selected finite models and in a database.

The stronger exact-volume version does not repair the connection. With an explicitly listed type set \(T\), generic sampling estimates the volume using \(O(\varepsilon^{-2})\) samples, each evaluated by weighted STV. That may be a useful estimate, but it largely restates the paper’s simulation exercise. If the goal is complexity in \(m\), the natural IAC simplex includes at least \(m!\) complete-ranking types. Efficiently sampling or integrating over that succinctly represented space becomes a new problem; the fixed-\(m\) limit does not supply the needed algorithmic formulation. Exact volume could be technically interesting, but its value as a complexity problem is not anchored in a computational result of this paper.

A robustness variant—such as the minimum population mass that must change before a spoiler appears—also asks for more than the paper studies. Its stability analysis varies which candidate is removed; it does not optimize changes to voters’ ballots. That could motivate a separate project, but it is not a stronger mirror of a result here.

So the negative case is strongest on the programme’s formal standard: there is no qualifying result to anchor a mirror to, and the proposed volume problem either recasts the paper’s Monte Carlo statistic or adds a new exact-integration problem. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous study of spoiler effects is possible. High-multiplicity ballot societies are sensible, and a carefully chosen study might be valuable; the paper itself does not make the case for one.
