The continuous mirror question

Given a depot, τ sponsor types at shared hubs with rational masses μ_t summing to 1, a rational directed cost matrix K satisfying the triangle inequalities, and a minimum-cost route σ through the depot and all types, find rational allocation rates a_t such that ∑_t μ_t a_t = c_{K,σ}(T) and ∑_t z_t a_t ≤ c_{K,σ}(supp(z)) for every coalition mass vector z with 0 ≤ z_t ≤ μ_t. Here c_{K,σ}(supp(z)) is the cost of shortcutting σ through the types with positive coalition mass.

The model it lives in

A unit mass of sponsors is distributed across τ hub types with identical relevant travel costs within each type. The decision is a per-unit allocation rate a_t; a coalition choosing mass z_t of type t pays z_t a_t, while its cost is the fixed-route cost determined by the coalition’s support.

The objection that survived

The opponent shows that arbitrary partial coalitions add no constraints beyond the ordinary core on the τ types, so the continuous formulation brings no additional algorithmic challenge.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s fixed-route core-existence result; it leaves Theorems 2, 3, and 5 and the unrestricted traveling-salesman game aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow one around Theorem 1, the fixed-route traveling-salesman game. The paper has no theorem stating an NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification. I count Theorem 1 as an algorithmic anchor only because its proof constructs an LP and uses an optimal dual solution to obtain a core allocation. The theorem itself asserts existence, not a running-time bound.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that if \(K_{ii}=0\), \(K\) satisfies the directed triangle inequalities, and the cyclic route \(\sigma\) is a minimum-cost tour, then the fixed-route game \(c_{K,\sigma}\) has a core allocation. Here a coalition’s cost is the cost of following \(\sigma\) while skipping cities outside that coalition. The proof introduces a linear production game, shows its core is contained in the fixed-route game’s core, and uses integrality of the relevant LP to establish equality of the grand coalition’s costs. It invokes Owen’s result on linear production games and Schrijver’s integrality machinery. In the rational-input setting, the proof’s polynomial-size LP and dual witness give a polynomial-time search algorithm in the number of cities; that complexity consequence is inferred from the proof rather than stated as the theorem’s result.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a speaker invited by many departments, member organizations, or sponsors located at a relatively small number of campuses or transport hubs. A type is a hub together with its complete relevant directed travel-cost profile. Units of the same type have identical costs and are served by the same stop. The number \(N\) of sponsoring units can be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is small. Their mass is their fraction of the sponsoring population. This is a sensible instance regime for the paper’s sponsor interpretation: one visit to a campus can serve many co-located sponsors.

My lead mirror is Fixed-Route Traveling-Salesman Core Search\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a depot \(0\), a finite type set \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational directed cost matrix \(K\) on \(\{0\}\cup T\), and a minimum-cost cyclic route \(\sigma\) through the depot and all types. Assume \(K_{ii}=0\) and the directed triangle inequalities hold. For any coalition mass vector \(z\) with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), let \(S(z)=\{t:z_t>0\}\); its cost is the cost \(c_{K,\sigma}(S(z))\) of shortcutting \(\sigma\) through the types in that support. A solution is a rational per-unit allocation vector \(a\in\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}\) satisfying

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t=c_{K,\sigma}(T) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}z_ta_t\le c_{K,\sigma}(S(z)) \quad\text{for every }z\in[0,\mu]. \]

The allocation rate \(a_t\) means that a coalition containing mass \(z_t\) of type \(t\) pays \(z_ta_t\). At population size \(N\), this corresponds to each type-\(t\) unit paying \(a_t/N\). The question is to output such an allocation, or report that none exists. Symmetric allocation within a type is natural: when the members of a type are interchangeable, averaging any core allocation over their permutations preserves the core constraints.

I expect this problem to be Class A, though Theorem 1 does not establish that for the continuous instance. Its fixed route and LP-based proof give a plausible starting point for a compact type-level algorithm. The paper’s LP has size polynomial in the number of individual cities; finding a formulation or pricing method polynomial in \(\tau\) and the binary input length \(L\) is the continuous computational question. For rational masses, one useful bridge is to approximate the population by \(N\mu_t\) identical copies of each type and study limits of symmetric core allocations in those finite games.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s fixed-route result only. Theorems 2, 3, and 5 also concern core existence, but they do not offer a stronger computational anchor here, and I would not stretch the case to the unrestricted traveling-salesman game.

The weakest point is that the paper proves a finite-player existence theorem, while the mirror’s partial coalitions create new constraints. Also, under the natural “one stop serves every sponsor at a hub” interpretation, any positive mass at a type requires the stop regardless of how small that mass is. That support-based discontinuity is meaningful for co-located sponsors, but it makes the continuous core a limit question rather than a smooth relaxation. The LP proof makes the mirror worth asking; it does not settle it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorems 1–5 establish core existence, nonexistence, or structural conditions. Theorem 1’s proof does give a constructive LP route to a core allocation, but it does not open a complexity question: the natural search task is already solved by a polynomial size LP.

The proposed mass version adds no new coalition constraints. Write \(x_t=\mu_t a_t\) for each type’s total allocation. For any type subset \(S\), choosing \(z_t=\mu_t\) on \(S\) gives \(x(S)\le c_{K,\sigma}(S)\), so a feasible mass allocation yields a core allocation of the finite type game. Conversely, triangle inequalities make the fixed route game monotone. Thus any core allocation satisfies
\[ x_t=c_{K,\sigma}(T)-x(T\setminus\{t\}) \ge c_{K,\sigma}(T)-c_{K,\sigma}(T\setminus\{t\})\ge 0. \]
Setting \(a_t=x_t/\mu_t\), every partial coalition satisfies
\[ \sum_t z_t a_t\le \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t a_t=x(S)\le c_{K,\sigma}(S). \]
So the proposed continuous core search is exactly the finite core search on the \(\tau\) city types, with allocations rescaled by mass.

The best population story is many sponsors at a small number of hubs. That is plausible, but the route cost of a hub is incurred whenever any positive mass of its sponsors joins a coalition; it does not vary with that mass. The cost matrix and LP therefore live on the hubs, and applying the paper’s construction to those \(\tau\) types already finds the allocation. If sponsors instead have distinct locations, their travel profiles distinguish them and the multiplicity disappears. The more ambitious alternative—a spatial density of locations with a rescaled route cost—would change the location space and the cost model, and would need a new core definition. That may be interesting work, but it is not a population-only mirror of this paper’s fixed-matrix game.

My negative case is strongest against treating Theorem 1 as a ChoCo computational anchor: its natural mass formulation reduces directly to the theorem’s finite type game, and its proof already supplies the search method. It is not airtight as a universal claim. Co-located sponsors make the high-multiplicity scenario credible, and a programme that values documenting exact finite-to-continuous identities could still regard that mirror as useful.
