What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Proposition 1's three-axiom incompatibility; it does not computationally address the paper's discussion of stronger efficiency notions or related literature.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper has a natural continuous-population assignment model. Many applicants can be grouped by a small number of preference types when they share a public ordering of jobs or slots and differ only in where they are indifferent. The paper itself points to unit-length jobs as a setting with a public ranking. In a large market, the meaningful quantities could be shares of applicants with each type and per-capita supplies of the ordered jobs.

For \(m\ge4\) objects \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) in a common public order, let each type be a ranking made by placing ties between adjacent objects, such as \(a_1\succ a_2\sim a_3\succ a_4\). A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Agents have total mass \(1\), and each object has supply \(1/m\). An assignment is a type-contingent lottery \(p_{t,a}(\mu)\): each type receives a lottery over objects, and feasibility requires
\[ \sum_a p_{t,a}(\mu)=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t \mu_t p_{t,a}(\mu)=1/m. \]
This preserves the paper’s random-assignment question while scaling supplies with population mass.

The relevant anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: for \(n\ge4\), there is no strategyproof mechanism satisfying ordinal efficiency and the equal division lower bound on the uniform-preference domain. In full, strategyproofness means truthful reporting stochastically dominates any misreport; ordinal efficiency means no feasible random assignment stochastically dominates the outcome; and the equal division lower bound requires every agent’s lottery to stochastically dominate the uniform lottery over objects. The paper’s following remark says its proof also gives incompatibility with the weaker upper-contour strategyproofness notion for more than four agents and objects.

A precise candidate mirror is the Uniform-Preference Continuum Assignment Impossibility Problem: given \(m\), the common object order, and the type space above, does there exist a direct mechanism \(F\) mapping every finite-support society \(\mu\) to a feasible assignment \(p(\mu)\), such that it is ordinally efficient, satisfies the equal division lower bound, and is strategyproof? For the continuum strategyproofness condition, a mass-zero individual’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so a type \(t\)’s lottery \(p_t(\mu)\) must stochastically dominate the lottery \(p_r(\mu)\) from any alternative report \(r\). A solution would be such a mechanism; a negative answer would require an impossibility proof.

That is a plausible population-level question, and it mirrors Proposition 1 alone. But it is not a valid ChoCo computational anchor. Proposition 1 is an axiomatic mechanism-existence impossibility, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified. The paper contains no named computational-complexity result of that kind. The proposed continuum question is also an axiomatic existence question, so it remains outside the programme’s stated scope.

The weakest point is the bridge from the finite proposition to the continuum mechanism. In the paper, a unilateral report changes a finite profile. In the nonatomic model, it does not change the population distribution. The pointwise incentive condition above is a reasonable continuum definition, but the paper’s impossibility proof does not establish that the same incompatibility holds under it. A formal limit argument would need assumptions connecting finite mechanisms to distribution-based mechanisms. So this is a credible adjacent continuous-assignment question, but not a continuous computational mirror of the paper’s result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 is named, but it is an axiomatic mechanism-existence impossibility: no rule can satisfy strategyproofness, ordinal efficiency, and the equal division lower bound across the stated profiles. The paper gives no algorithmic task, input-output problem, or complexity classification.

The proposed population model is plausible: large cohorts of applicants can be grouped by preference type, with supplies scaled per capita. But its central question—whether a type-based mechanism satisfies those axioms—is still an axiomatic existence question. Turning it into a computational problem requires changing what is asked. Finding an efficient assignment for one society drops strategyproofness, which compares outcomes across reports and profiles. Asking an algorithm to produce a mechanism across societies requires a representation and computational task not supplied by the paper. Treating a positive-mass cohort’s deviation as the strategic move makes the question about coalition manipulation, a new axiom rather than a mirror of Proposition 1.

The nonatomic version also does not inherit the proposition’s incentive comparison unchanged. If an individual’s report leaves the society distribution fixed, the mechanism’s response is a different object from the response to a unilateral report in the finite model. Adding an atom to restore that effect moves back toward a finite-agent model; allowing mass deviations changes the strategic question. Either could motivate new research, but neither turns this paper’s result into a computational anchor.

I would not claim that random assignment has no sensible continuous-population setting: the job example and the high-multiplicity literature make that claim hard to defend. The narrower negative case is stronger: this paper offers no result from which to formulate a worthwhile ChoCo computational mirror without replacing its central question. An independent computational study of continuous assignment might be worthwhile, but it would not be a mirror of the paper’s result.
