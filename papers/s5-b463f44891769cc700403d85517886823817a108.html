The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), feature incidences \(a_{ft}\in\{0,1\}\), target panel mass \(\kappa\), and quota bounds \(\ell_f,u_f\), decide whether there exists a measurable selected subpopulation with masses \(y_t\) satisfying \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_t=\kappa\), and \(\ell_f\le\sum_t a_{ft}y_t\le u_f\) for every feature \(f\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity sortition pool is represented by type masses \(\mu_t\); variables \(y_t\) select mass from each type subject to capacity, total panel mass, and feature-quota inequalities. Feasibility is an LP, and leximin fairness can be added using per-capita probabilities \(\pi_t=y_t/\mu_t\) for \(\mu_t>0\).

The objection that survived

The strongest remaining objection is institutional and asymptotic: many actual assemblies keep a finite panel size while the pool grows, whereas the mirror scales panel mass and quotas, potentially describing a standing proportional chamber rather than the paper's usual lottery.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the quota-panel feasibility hardness of Theorem 1 and the related selection and leximin formulation of Corollary 2. It does not directly cover the full fairness-measure framework, empirical comparisons, implementation details, or identity-sensitive constraints such as household restrictions.

Open questions for a prover

  • What normalization of panel mass and quotas gives a faithful discrete-to-continuous dictionary, and what additive rounding guarantees recover finite panels?
  • For which type representations and additional constraints does the LP remain polynomial, and when do integrality or type proliferation restore hardness?
  • Can optimal mass vectors be implemented by distributions over measurable quota-compliant panels while preserving the desired individual marginals?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s core panel-selection problem, not a claim that the paper’s observed pools of \(70\)–\(1{,}727\) volunteers are already continuum populations.

The lead anchor is Supplementary Information §6, Theorem 1, proved in this paper by reduction from Exact Cover by 3-Sets. It states: given agents, a panel size, features, and lower and upper quotas for each feature, deciding whether there exists a quota-compliant panel is NP-hard. The reduction represents each 3-set by an agent, each universe element by a feature, sets the panel size to the number of sets in an exact cover, and imposes quota exactly \(1\) on every feature. A feasible panel is therefore exactly an exact cover. The theorem is the paper’s clearest computational-hardness anchor. [The theorem and reduction appear in the supplementary paper](https://procaccia.info/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/assemblies.pdf).

My mirror is Continuous Quota-Panel Feasibility.

A type \(t\) is a complete feature vector \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^r\): two citizens have the same type exactly when every attribute used by the selection rule is the same. The society is a distribution \(\mu=(\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\). This represents a large pool in which many individuals share the same demographic and eligibility profile, while \(\tau=|T|\) remains small compared with the population size.

Let \(\kappa\) be the desired panel mass. The decision variable is \(y_t\), the mass of type \(t\) selected for the panel. It must satisfy \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_t=\kappa\), and, for every feature \(f\), \(\ell_f\le\sum_t a_{ft}y_t\le u_f\). The exact optimization version minimizes total quota violation, \(V(y)=|\sum_t y_t-\kappa|+\sum_f((\ell_f-\sum_ta_{ft}y_t)_++(\sum_ta_{ft}y_t-u_f)_+)\); the decision question is whether the optimum is \(0\).

This is genuinely a population continuization. A solution \(y_t\) is not a fractional citizen: in an atomless type block, one selects a measurable subset of type-\(t\) citizens of mass \(y_t\). Every individual is still selected or not selected, and every realized panel satisfies the quotas. The fractional object is only the aggregate mass, just as the programme’s continuous society requires.

The expected result is Class A. The problem is a linear program with \(\tau\) variables and \(r\) quota constraints, hence solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), and the encoding length. The paper’s NP-hardness disappears because the X3C reduction relies on choosing indivisible \(0\)-\(1\) agents. In a high-multiplicity regime, repeated citizens of the same type can be selected in arbitrary mass. The reduction’s exact-cover choice becomes a linear mass-allocation problem.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ problem because it preserves the objects they actually compute with: feature membership, panel size, quota compliance, and the selection of a panel. It changes only the granularity of the population. The paper itself defines selection probabilities as the marginals of an output distribution over quota-compliant panels, and its algorithmic framework treats those marginals as the relevant fairness object. [The paper’s model and optimization framework are described here](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03788-6).

A secondary anchor is Supplementary Information §6, Corollary 2, also proved here. It states that, unless \(\mathrm{RP}=\mathrm{NP}\), no randomized selection algorithm can find a quota-compliant panel in polynomial time with constant success probability, even if fairness is ignored. This is a consequence of Theorem 1, so it is not independent evidence, but it directly anchors the paper’s algorithmic selection task.

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Egalitarian Sortition. The instance is the same, now guaranteed feasible. Define the per-capita selection probability of type \(t\) by \(\pi_t=y_t/\mu_t\). The objective is to maximize \(\min_{t:\mu_t>0}\pi_t\), with the paper’s leximin refinement available by repeatedly fixing the currently bottleneck types and resolving the resulting LP. The solution consists of the optimal \(y\), together with a random measurable-panel rule having those marginals. For example, within each type block, a random measure-preserving shift can select mass \(y_t\), giving every type-\(t\) citizen probability \(\pi_t\) while preserving the quotas in every realization.

This problem is also expected to be Class A: its first stage is the quota LP, and each max-min or leximin stage is another LP. The paper’s discrete impossibility result therefore becomes a precise research question: which parts of the difficulty were caused by individual-level integrality, and which survive after population continuization?

The appropriate regime is a national or regional recurring civic lottery with a very large volunteer pool and a panel whose size scales with the pool—for example, a standing deliberative chamber selecting \(1{,}000\) citizens from a pool of \(100{,}000\). The relevant feature taxonomy might have \(r\) demographic or eligibility attributes and \(\tau\) observed type combinations, with \(N\gg\tau\). Citizens sharing a type are interchangeable for quotas, fairness, and eligibility. The paper’s actual small panels are not themselves a convincing high-multiplicity regime; the mirror relies on this larger-pool scenario.

The weakest point is that the paper’s normative language concerns named individuals, whereas my mirror treats citizens with the same recorded features as interchangeable. If individual identity, rare intersectional characteristics, or idiosyncratic eligibility conditions matter, then \(\tau\) may approach \(N\), and the continuum advantage disappears. Likewise, exact finite-\(N\) rounding is a further problem: one must determine when a fractional solution can be rounded to an integral panel while preserving quotas or incurring only controlled additive error.

So the positive claim should be modest but firm: this paper supplies a natural hardness anchor whose high-multiplicity population version is a well-defined continuous optimization problem, and the most direct mirror predicts that its discrete NP-hardness dissolves into LP tractability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is weak, because the first anchor survives the strongest objections.

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational result, not merely an impossibility or axiomatic statement. Its X3C reduction makes every agent’s feature-incidence vector effectively a distinct type, so the paper’s hardness depends on choosing indivisible individuals. That gives a legitimate continuization question: replicate each feature-vector type many times, scale the panel size and quotas proportionally, and ask whether a measurable panel of the required mass exists. The resulting problem is exactly the proposed LP

\[ 0\le y_t\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t y_t=\kappa,\qquad \ell_f\le \sum_t a_{ft}y_t\le u_f. \]

This is not a fractional-citizen misunderstanding. In an atomless block of citizens of type \(t\), a measurable subset of mass \(y_t\) can be selected, and every realized panel can have the same type masses. Thus the LP captures a real population-continuum version of the paper’s panel problem.

The best objection is that the paper’s actual assemblies use finite panels, often with \(k\) fixed while the pool grows. Then \(k/N\to0\), individual selection probabilities vanish, and quotas expressed as finite counts do not have a meaningful limit. Moreover, the X3C hardness disappears precisely because the continuum permits fractional use of each replicated type. One might therefore say that the proposed model changes the institution from a citizens’ assembly into a positive-mass representative chamber, and changes the computational object from finding a discrete panel to allocating mass.

That objection does not suffice. A standing assembly selecting, say, \(1{,}000\) representatives from a \(100{,}000\)-person high-multiplicity pool is a plausible sortition institution, and scaling panel sizes and quotas is the natural normalization of its population limit. The programme explicitly permits such co-modification when it is forced by continuization. The disappearance of the X3C integrality barrier is also not a defect: identifying exactly that boundary is the point of the mirror.

Corollary 2 does not provide an independent escape. It is a consequence of Theorem 1, and its continuous counterpart is the same LP feasibility problem, now embedded in a max-min or leximin objective. The paper’s concern with named individuals does not defeat this version either. Within each type block, a random measure-preserving selection rule can give every individual selection probability

\[ \pi_t=\frac{y_t}{\mu_t}, \]

while preserving the quota constraints in every realization. Identity-sensitive extensions, such as household restrictions, may cause the type space to become large, but they do not invalidate the core quota-panel mirror.

Nor can the mirror be rejected as already continuous. The paper uses finite agents, finite panels, and optimization over distributions on discrete panels. Its use of fair-division language and column generation is supporting technology, not a continuous-population treatment.

Thus the honest negative conclusion is limited: the mirror may be institutionally less faithful for small, fixed-size citizens’ assemblies, and its relevance depends on whether large repeated type blocks and proportional panel sizes are credible. But the stronger high-multiplicity model is mathematically coherent, computationally nontrivial as a relaxation of the paper’s hardness result, and naturally connected to the paper’s fairness objective. I cannot honestly defeat it universally.
