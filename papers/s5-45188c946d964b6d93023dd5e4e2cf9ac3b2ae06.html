The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), integer weight vectors \(a_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^k\), and a feasible rational aggregate \(z\), let \(N_M(z)\) count labelled coalitions with aggregate weight \(Mz\) in replications having \(M\mu_t\) members of each type. Compute \(\Phi(z)=\limsup_{M\to\infty,\,N_M(z)>0} M^{-1}\log N_M(z)\) and an approximately maximizing coalition-mass profile.

The model it lives in

A continuum has mass \(\mu_t\) of each complete weight type; a coalition selects mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), with \(\sum_t x_ta_t=z\). Its asymptotic log-count is the maximum of \(\sum_t\mu_t h(x_t/\mu_t)\) over those choices, where \(h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)\).

The objection that survived

The paper uses coalition counts to derive voting power, whereas \(\Phi(z)\) discards subexponential and individual-swing information; the proponent acknowledges that loss but does not establish that the authors would treat \(\Phi\) itself as an output of interest.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s aggregate coalition-weight counts through their high-multiplicity growth rate; it leaves the exact finite counts and the swing-based and minimal-winning-coalition results, including Propositions 3–10, uncovered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s coalition-weight counting engine. It covers one named result and its aggregate-weight counts; it does not claim to mirror every voting-power index.

My anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. For \(n\) players with \(k\) nonnegative weight vectors, let \(T(i,s_1,\ldots,s_k)\) count the subsets of the first \(i\) players whose total weights are \(s_1,\ldots,s_k\), and let \(\omega^{(j)}\) be the total weight in coordinate \(j\). Proposition 1 gives the recurrence \(T(i,s)=T(i-1,s)+T(i-1,s-w_i)\), with out-of-range states zero, \(T(0,0,\ldots,0)=1\), and \(T(i,0,\ldots,0)=1\). It proves that dynamic programming computes the table in \(O(n\prod_j\omega^{(j)})\) time. This is a pseudo-polynomial bound in the weight totals. The paper has no numbered hardness result of its own; its cited NP-hardness result about game dimension is external, so I would not use it as an anchor.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Replicated Coalition-Weight Entropy. An instance has \(\tau\) types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a nonnegative integer weight vector \(a_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^k\) for each type, and a rational target aggregate \(z\) that is feasible. A type is a complete weight vector: its members are interchangeable in every parameter the game uses. For a replication by \(M\), there are \(M\mu_t\) members of type \(t\). Let \(N_M(z)\) count the coalitions with aggregate weight \(Mz\); equivalently, \(N_M(z)=\sum_{r:\,0\le r_t\le M\mu_t,\ \sum_t r_ta_t=Mz}\prod_t {M\mu_t\choose r_t}\), over replications for which these quantities are integral. The task is to compute the exponential growth rate \(\Phi(z)=\limsup_{M\to\infty,\,N_M(z)>0}M^{-1}\log N_M(z)\), and return a maximizing type-mass profile.

The continuous society has mass \(\mu_t\) of each type; the coalition decision variable is \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) in the coalition. The expected characterization is \(\Phi(z)=\max\{\sum_t\mu_t h(x_t/\mu_t):\sum_t x_ta_t=z,\ 0\le x_t\le\mu_t\}\), where \(h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)\). This is the high-multiplicity limit of the table entry: a type profile \(r_t\) contributes \(\prod_t {M\mu_t\choose r_t}\) discrete coalitions, and its log count per member tends to the displayed entropy. A solution could be an additive-\(\epsilon\) approximation to \(\Phi(z)\) and a profile achieving that accuracy.

The regime is a large membership body whose members fall into a modest number of genuinely identical multi-parameter classes—for example, a large cooperative whose members share one of a few standard share packages and committee roles. Here \(n\) may be in the millions while \(\tau\) is in the tens or hundreds. Mass is the fraction of each class in a coalition. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s weighted voting model.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: its stated optimization maximizes a concave entropy function over a box with linear equalities, so additive approximation is a natural convex-optimization question in \(k,\tau\), input bit length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\). The entropy profile also gives a follow-on question: maximize \(\Phi(z)\) over winning aggregates \(z\), obtaining the exponential growth rate of winning coalitions. For a hyperplane winning rule, that adds a linear inequality and remains a concave optimization problem. A more general decision function may change that complexity.

The weakest point is that this mirrors the growth rate of coalition-weight counts, not the paper’s exact finite counts or its individual swing-based power indices. Taking the limit loses subexponential information, and infinitesimal type members do not retain individual pivotality. The case survives because Proposition 1’s aggregate count table is the paper’s central computational substrate, and the proposed question is its high-multiplicity limit with each type defined by precisely the weights the game uses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Proposition 1, and I would not dismiss it: it gives an algorithmic bound for computing the coalition-weight table. There is no honest “no computational result” objection here.

The strongest objection is that the table counts *labelled coalitions*, while the paper uses those counts to compute swing-based power indices. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass. Adding or removing one member changes a type’s mass by \(1/M\); in the limit, that change has no effect except at the decision boundary. So a literal continuum version of a swing count loses the event the index is meant to measure. Recovering it requires boundary sensitivity or finite-size lattice corrections. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they retain microscopic arithmetic that the mass distribution alone does not encode.

The proposed entropy mirror repairs the count table by keeping its leading exponential growth rate. That is mathematically coherent, and a large body with many members in a few identical share-and-committee classes is plausible. But \(\Phi(z)\) measures the exponential number of coalitions with aggregate \(z\); it is not a voting-power index. Maximizing it over winning aggregates gives an asymptotic count of winning coalitions, while the paper’s power measures depend on which individual additions cross the boundary, often at a smaller scale than the entropy rate records. A more refined limit could try to preserve those crossings, but then the continuation needs extra fluctuation and lattice data, and the case that the continuous society itself is doing the computational work becomes less clear.

That is the best negative case, but it does not defeat Proposition 1’s mirror. The entropy rate is a direct high-multiplicity asymptotic of the table the authors compute. If they regard asymptotic coalition counts as part of their problem, it survives the objection; the stronger complaint is that its connection to their central power-index questions remains unshown. So the negative case is weak, and I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
