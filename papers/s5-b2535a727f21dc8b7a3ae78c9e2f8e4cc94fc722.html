What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered: Theorem 1 and the other tradeoff results concern axioms, while the mechanism propositions establish rule guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive route is a continuous population version of the paper’s Condorcet tradeoff. Let \(T=L(A)\) be the rankings of the alternatives, and let a society be a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction holding ranking \(t\). A mechanism \(F\) maps \(\mu\) to a lottery over \(A\). Define a \(q\)-neighbor by moving mass \(q\) from one ranking to another; taking \(q=1/n\) recovers the change made by replacing one voter in an \(n\)-voter profile. Require \(F\) to satisfy \(\varepsilon\)-DP for these neighbors, and define Condorcet winners and losers using the mass-weighted pairwise margins.

A precise candidate problem is: given \(m,\varepsilon,q,\alpha,\eta\), determine whether there is a neutral mechanism \(F\) on all such societies that is \(\varepsilon\)-DP, satisfies \(\alpha\)-Condorcet criterion whenever a Condorcet winner exists, and satisfies \(\eta\)-Condorcet loser criterion whenever a Condorcet loser exists. A solution is an explicit mechanism or an impossibility proof. The optimization version asks for the largest achievable \(\alpha\eta\). This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ question: it makes the society continuous while retaining the same private randomized choice and the same axioms.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: no voting rule satisfies \(\varepsilon\)-DP, \(\alpha\)-Condorcet criterion, and \(\eta\)-Condorcet loser criterion when \(\alpha\eta>e^\varepsilon\). Its continuous analogue is a natural target; the paper’s CWRR/CLRR mixture suggests the same bound is achievable. But Theorem 1 is an axiomatic tradeoff theorem, not a computational-complexity result. Under the requested anchor standard, it is not a qualifying anchor.

That is the central limitation: this paper has no named result classifying a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized-tractable. Its numbered propositions and theorems establish privacy and axiom guarantees or impossibility bounds; the proposed mechanisms are not accompanied by complexity results. So the candidate above is a defensible continuous-population extension of the paper’s subject, but it is not an anchor-driven computational mirror for the continuization programme.

The weakest point in the candidate itself is the privacy unit. A literal individual has zero mass in a true continuum, so individual-replacement DP needs a positive mass \(q\) to remain meaningful. Setting \(q=1/n\) preserves the finite voter’s privacy change, but carries a granularity parameter into the continuum model. This is a plausible co-modification, yet it keeps the proposed mirror closer to a high-multiplicity extension than to a privacy notion native to an uncountable population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Propositions 1–8 bound how well randomized rules can satisfy voting axioms under differential privacy; Theorems 2–6 bound tradeoffs among those axioms. None classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity or supplies an algorithm whose tractability depends on a computational bottleneck. The mechanisms are explicit randomized rules, not solutions to computational tasks.

The proposed continuous version of Theorem 1 is coherent as an axiomatic extension: represent rankings by masses, define pairwise margins from those masses, and study privacy under mass transfers. But that asks whether a rule can satisfy certain properties over a continuous domain. It does not turn the paper’s result into a computational question. For a finite-support input, the relevant scores and pairwise margins are weighted sums over the support; computing them does not expose the kind of algorithmic landscape the programme is meant to chart.

There is also a real modeling strain in the proposed privacy unit. Replacing one voter in an \(n\)-voter profile changes mass by \(1/n\). A fixed transfer \(q\) instead protects a positive-mass cohort; letting \(q\) shrink with \(n\) preserves the finite privacy unit but carries the population-size scale into the model. A metric privacy notion for arbitrarily small transfers is another possible reformulation, but it changes the question. These are defensible extensions, yet they do not supply the missing computational anchor.

A mechanism-synthesis problem—optimizing over all rules on the continuous domain—could be posed, but it would be a new computational project layered onto this paper’s axiomatic agenda. The negative case is strongest for the requested programme fit, not as a claim that no interesting continuous follow-up exists: the paper simply gives no named computational result whose continuous mirror would chart the complexity landscape.
