The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(m\) items, rational nonnegative intrinsic values \(b^i_t\) and block interaction weights \(K^i_{tu}\), and nonnegative-coefficient polynomial utilities \(f^i_t\) of degree less than \(d\), can a randomized algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,d\) return feasible type fractions \(z_{ti}\) with \(\mathbb{E}[W(z)]\geq \mathrm{OPT}/d\), where \(\sum_i z_{ti}\leq 1\) and \(W(z)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_i z_{ti}f^i_t\!\left(b^i_t+\sum_u\mu_uK^i_{tu}z_{ui}\right)\)?

The model it lives in

A dense block-interaction allocation model: types carry masses \(\mu_t\), each type shares intrinsic values and its interactions with every other type, and \(z_{ti}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) receiving indivisible item \(i\). Welfare is \(W(z)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_i z_{ti}f^i_t\!\left(b^i_t+\sum_u\mu_uK^i_{tu}z_{ui}\right)\); per-agent interaction weights scale as \(K^i_{tu}/N\) in a finite population of size \(N\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 2’s finite-agent guarantee does not itself establish a \(d\)-approximation running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,d\) after block aggregation; that compression and guarantee still need proof.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror centers on Theorem 2’s positive polynomial externalities and also gives a continuous question corresponding to Theorem 1’s negative concave case. It leaves the paper’s bounded-curvature and positive concave results, numerical experiment, and other formulations unmirrored; the introductory hardness statement supplies no separate numbered hardness anchor here.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the positive polynomial type-mass problem admit a \(d\)-approximation in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,d\), and can the finite-agent relaxation and rounding be compressed to establish it?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a block-structured continuum of the paper’s own allocation problem. A type must include everything the model uses: its utility function and intrinsic value, and its influence on every other type. People within a type are interchangeable; people in different types may still interact differently. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that the paper’s arbitrary network matrices are all compressible this way.

Take a nonatomic society with finitely many types \(T\), support size \(\tau\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. There are \(m\) indivisible items. Let \(b^i_t\) be type \(t\)’s intrinsic value for item \(i\), and let \(K^i_{tu}\) be the influence a unit of type \(u\) assigned item \(i\) has on a type \(t\) user’s utility. If \(z_{ti}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) assigned item \(i\), feasibility is \(\sum_i z_{ti}\leq 1\). The resulting per-capita welfare is

\(\displaystyle W(z)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{i=1}^m z_{ti}\, f^i_t\!\left(b^i_t+\sum_{u\in T}\mu_u K^i_{tu}z_{ui}\right).\)

Each person still receives at most one item; \(z_{ti}\) just says what fraction of a type receives each item. The society’s size has disappeared from the instance, while \(m,\tau\), and the binary description length \(L\) remain. This is a natural scaling of the paper’s model: in a finite approximation with \(N\) people, cross-person influences can be \(K^i_{tu}/N\), keeping each person’s exposure finite as \(N\) grows.

This regime has concrete examples in the paper’s own application areas. A large population of drivers can be grouped by origin, destination, time, and vehicle class when each cohort has the same route utilities and route-specific congestion effects. A large networked-goods market can be grouped into a small number of user segments with shared valuations and segment-to-segment influence. In either case, \(N\) may be enormous while \(\tau\) stays modest. The paper’s Lovász and multilinear extensions do not already make its society continuous: they relax a finite allocation vector. Here the population itself is a measure.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that when externality weights are positive and every externality function is a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients of degree less than \(d\), Algorithm 1 is a \(d\)-approximation for the paper’s finite-agent MSW problem. The paper also records the positive-linear special case as a 2-approximation in Corollary 1. I would mirror Theorem 2 with \(\mathrm{CMSW}^{+}_d\): the input is \((T,\mu,m,K,b,f)\), with rational nonnegative \(K^i_{tu}, b^i_t\), and \(f^i_t\) given by nonnegative-coefficient polynomials of degree less than \(d\). The task is to return feasible type fractions \(z\) with \(\mathbb{E}[W(z)]\geq \mathrm{OPT}/d\), using time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,d\). A vector of fractions is a solution: an atomless type population can be partitioned to realize those fractions exactly.

I expect this approximation problem to be Class A, tractable. There is a particularly direct route to a proof. In finite block-symmetric approximations, symmetry lets the concave Lovász relaxation be represented by one allocation vector per type rather than per person. The iterative KT thresholds can then act on whole type blocks. Also, after expanding the positive polynomial welfare, each term is a nonnegative coefficient times a product of type-allocation fractions; the corresponding Lovász term uses the minimum of those fractions, which is at least their product. That makes the relaxation a plausible upper bound on the continuum optimum, to which the paper’s \(d\)-approximation analysis can apply. That transfer still needs to be proved, but it is a concrete algorithmic question in the paper’s exact objective.

A second anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here. Under the preceding nonnegativity condition on welfare, it gives a randomized \(e\)-approximation for MSW when the externality weights between distinct agents are nonpositive and the externality functions are nondecreasing and concave. Its continuous counterpart, \(\mathrm{CMSW}^{-}_{\mathrm{conc}}\), uses the same input and objective, with \(K^i_{tu}\leq 0\) for interactions between distinct people and nondecreasing concave \(f^i_t\). For a finite computational representation, one can restrict this mirror to rational piecewise-linear concave functions. The task is to return feasible \(z\) with expected welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/e\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) and the function-description size. I also expect this approximation task to be Class A, through a type-compressed version of the theorem’s submodular relaxation and rounding. The compression and limiting argument are new obligations; Theorem 1 itself only proves the guarantee for the paper’s finite-agent instances.

The two questions generate useful follow-ups: do these factors survive with running time polynomial in support size rather than population size? Does the continuous fractional allocation permit better factors or exact optimization? And can any hardness be shown once the interaction matrix is specified over types, even when each type contains a large mass? This paper has no numbered hardness theorem to anchor a hardness-transfer claim. Its introductory statement that the problem is “typically NP-hard” is too broad to serve as one.

My weakest point is also the boundary of the proposed mirror. The paper allows arbitrary non-anonymous interaction matrices; if each person’s exact network position matters, that full position belongs in their type and \(\tau\) may grow with \(N\). The positive result has a concrete symmetry-and-relaxation path. For the negative result, getting an efficient type-compressed algorithm is less automatic. So I am arguing for these continuous questions in a plausible, explicitly block-structured high-multiplicity regime—not for a continuum mirror of every instance covered by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a scope argument. The paper’s agents are connected by an arbitrary matrix of pairwise influences, so in many instances an agent’s network position is part of what makes that agent distinct. If two agents differ in whom they influence, they are different types for this problem. In a sparse network with persistent local effects, a faithful type description may therefore need to grow with the population. Scaling every interaction by \(1/N\) would also erase those local effects as \(N\) grows.

That limits how much of the paper a small-support mirror could cover. But it does not defeat either anchor: the question is whether there is a worthwhile instance regime, and dense block interaction models are a plausible one. In the proponent’s model, agents in a type share an intrinsic value and their effects on every other type. Giving each of \(N\) agents an interaction weight \(K^i_{tu}/N\) with agents of type \(u\) yields the stated per-capita welfare in the large-population limit. Large cohorts of drivers facing shared route congestion, or user groups whose adoption affects other groups’ product values, fit that model. The paper itself studies those application areas.

For Theorem 2, the tempting objection is that its \(d\)-approximation rounds binary variables for individual agents, while \(z_{ti}\) is a fraction of a type. The proof’s guarantee therefore does not automatically establish a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L,d\). But that is a proof obligation, not a defect in the question. The continuum has a well-defined objective over type fractions, and the paper’s positive polynomial externalities give that objective a natural restricted case. One can ask whether the theorem’s relaxation and rounding extend with running time polynomial in the number of types. The paper’s Lovász extension does not already answer this: it extends functions on a finite set of agents, while this problem takes the population itself to be a distribution. Nor does a difference in the eventual approximation guarantee make the question unworthy.

For Theorem 1, the same objection applies to its randomized rounding of individual agents. It proves an \(e\)-approximation for finite populations with nonpositive cross-agent influences and concave utilities; it does not settle the type-distribution version. Yet a dense negative interaction model with weights scaled by \(1/N\) has a nonvanishing aggregate effect, and its limiting welfare is well-defined. Restricting to instances with nonnegative welfare gives a recognizable continuous question. The finite theorem might help prove a matching guarantee, or the fractional mass allocation might lead to a different algorithm. Neither possibility is a reason to reject the mirror.

So the honest negative case is that these are narrow mirrors of dense, type-homogeneous network models, not continuizations of the paper’s arbitrary networks. In sparse networks, many types may be needed, and the proposed mirrors may then offer little compression. That is a real limit on their reach, but it does not dispose of either anchor: the dense regime is plausible, the paper’s own applications admit it, and neither result has already been solved for a continuous population. I cannot make a convincing case that no scenario is worth the programme’s time. The case against is weakest against Theorem 2, whose positive polynomial welfare supplies the clearest route to a type-compressed computational question.
