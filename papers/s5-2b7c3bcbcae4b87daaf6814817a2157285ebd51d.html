What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered. The paper reports simulations and regressions about equally weighted policy positions under fixed party-system draws and coalition rules; population-weighted prevalence and any model deriving seats or pivotality from voter mass are not results of the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The required anchor is absent. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its equations define a decision model; its numbered coalition scenarios define simulation conditions. Table 3 and Table 4 report simulation and regression findings, not results such as hardness or polynomial-time solvability. So there are no anchor numbers to quote and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The strongest plausible positive lead is an unanchored follow-on question. In a large PR electorate with a small party set, let a voter type be an ideal policy point \(z\), and let \(\mu_z\) be the fraction of voters of that type. Given the parties’ positions and seat distribution, a coalition-formation rule, and the paper’s one-seat-pivotality approximation, each type chooses the party that maximizes its expected utility over policy outcomes. One could ask for that choice for each supported type and the total mass
\[ \sum_z \mu_z\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[a(z)\ne \arg\min_k |z-y_k|\right], \]
where \(a(z)\) is the utility-maximizing vote and \(y_k\) is party \(k\)’s position. This gives mass a clear meaning and preserves the paper’s voting action and policy-utility objective. Millions of voters grouped by a modest number of shared or similar ideal points would be a credible high-multiplicity regime.

The weakness is central: the paper measures the share of *policy positions* with strategic incentives, not the share of an electorate. It fixes small simulated parliaments and approximates an individual voter’s pivotal effect; it does not model a distribution of voter types whose mass determines election outcomes. A continuous-population version would therefore be a plausible research question suggested by the paper, but whether the authors would recognize it as their problem is uncertain, and its complexity class would be new work. It cannot serve as an honest anchored case for this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to anchor a mirror: its claims come from simulations and regressions over party systems, not from theorems about an algorithmic problem. Its reported strategic share is a statistic over policy positions, with each position weighted equally. That leaves the proponent’s proposed population question as a follow-on, not a continuous version of a stated complexity result.

The proposed statistic is coherent. Write \(g(z)=1\) when a voter with ideal point \(z\) chooses strategically under the paper’s calculation. Then a society with type distribution \(\mu\) has strategic-voter mass \(\int g(z)\,d\mu(z)\). But in the paper, \(g\) is computed from an assumed seat distribution, coalition rule, and pivotality approximation; the distribution of voter types determines none of them. With those inputs fixed, \(\mu\) only reweights an existing map. That could answer an applied question about which voters are affected under a chosen electorate distribution, but it does not put the paper’s strategic-voting problem into a population-driven computational model.

The stronger version would derive party sizes and seat outcomes from \(\mu\), then compute each type’s expected utility from changing its vote. That runs into a substantive modeling fork. In an atomless society, one voter’s deviation has zero mass and does not change the aggregate vote shares; the individual pivotal effect central to the paper disappears. Giving a voter positive mass instead turns the decision into a coordinated bloc deviation. Keeping a one-voter effect requires retaining a finite electorate scale, seat rounding, and a model of uncertainty around the result. Those may support worthwhile research, but the uncertainty and pivotality assumptions would supply the central question; the continuous population would not.

So the negative case is strongest against calling this paper a continuization target: there is no computational result to mirror, and the most natural population extension either just weights existing outputs or changes individual strategic voting into a finite-size or collective-action problem. It is not airtight. A distribution over voters’ ideal points is sensible for large electorates, and the paper itself asks how prevalent strategic incentives are. Its authors might well recognize the distribution-weighted statistic as their question. That possibility makes a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists harder to defend than the narrower claim that the paper supplies no strong computational anchor.
