The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a party partition, a distinguished party \(P^\star\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported complete rankings of \(C\), decide whether there is a nomination containing exactly one candidate from each party such that the nominee from \(P^\star\) is a nonunique Schulze winner. Pairwise margins are \(M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t\); path strengths use positive margins as arc weights.

The model it lives in

Ranking types \(t\) carry rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. The decision variable is an integral nomination \(N\), with exactly one candidate per party; the objective is feasibility of the Schulze-winner inequalities for the distinguished nominee under the pairwise margins induced by \(\mu\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the possible-president and necessary-president hardness results; it leaves the two-voter algorithm and the rest of the paper’s parameterized landscape unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does complexity vary with support size \(\tau\), especially between the easy one- or two-type cases and the hardness already witnessed by at most three ranking types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best lead is a continuous version of Schulze Possible President. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of the candidate set \(C\). Its support contains \(\tau\) rankings, each with positive binary-rational mass, and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is one complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. This fits a large party-based association or civic organization whose factions each nominate one candidate for a common membership-wide election, where many members share one of a relatively small number of preference orders.

For a nominated set \(N\), define the pairwise margin
\[ M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t . \]
There is an arc \(x\to y\) of weight \(M_\mu(x,y)\) when that margin is positive. The strength of a path is its weakest arc, and \(\operatorname{str}_\mu(x,y)\) is the greatest path strength from \(x\) to \(y\), or zero if there is no path. Candidate \(x\) is a nonunique Schulze winner when \(\operatorname{str}_\mu(x,y)\geq\operatorname{str}_\mu(y,x)\) for every other nominee \(y\).

Continuous Schulze Possible President takes \(C\), its party partition, a distinguished party \(P^\star\), and the supported ranking distribution \(\mu\). It asks whether there is a nomination \(N\)—exactly one candidate from every party—whose nominee from \(P^\star\) is a Schulze winner. The decision variable is the nomination; the question is feasibility, with no optimization objective. The nomination remains integral, just as in the paper.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: Schulze Possible President is NP-complete even when each party has at most two candidates and the number of voters is any fixed odd constant of at least three. The authors prove the hardness by reduction from \(2\)-Balanced \(3\)-SAT. The theorem number and statement are from the [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11933). Its reduction’s combinatorics lie in the candidate and party choices, so they remain present when the electorate is represented by mass.

A second, independently worthwhile mirror is Continuous Schulze Necessary President, on the same input. It asks whether there is some \(p\in P^\star\) who wins for every valid nomination in which \(p\) is nominated. A solution is such a candidate \(p\). Its anchor is Theorem 4.12, also proved in this paper: Schulze Necessary President is coNP-complete even when every party has at most two candidates and the voter count is any fixed even constant of at least four. The proof reduces from \(2\)-Balanced \(3\)-SAT. This mirrors the paper’s universal guarantee over opponents’ nominations, rather than weakening it to a single favorable nomination.

Both mirrors have a direct finite-to-continuous dictionary. Given a finite profile with \(n_t\) voters of ranking \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Then \(M_\mu(x,y)\) is the paper’s majority margin divided by \(n\). Every beatpath strength is scaled by the same positive factor, so the winner comparisons and the nomination answers are unchanged. Thus Theorem 4.1’s three-voter instances and Theorem 4.12’s four-voter instances embed as societies with at most three and four ranking types, respectively. For the continuous inputs, rational margins and Schulze winners can be computed with exact polynomial-time arithmetic; a nomination witnesses a yes-answer for Possible President, and counterexample nominations for each candidate in \(P^\star\) certify a no-answer for Necessary President. The expected classifications are therefore NP-complete and coNP-complete, respectively: both are Class B, with hardness transferring from the discrete problems. In particular, bounded support size alone cannot make these mirrors tractable.

The mirror covers these two classical nomination questions and their corresponding hardness results, not the paper’s entire parameterized landscape or its two-voter algorithm. Natural follow-up questions concern which restrictions on party structure or candidate interactions yield tractability, and how the parameterized results behave when society is encoded by rational type masses.

The soft spot is that the hardness proofs use only three or four voters. They establish a rigorous high-multiplicity mirror, but they do not show that moving to large populations itself creates a new tractability phenomenon. I would present this as a convincing Class B boundary case, not as an algorithmic payoff: the society becomes a continuous object, while the hardness survives because its source is the nomination structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that these nomination problems are driven by the candidate and party choices, while the electorate enters only through pairwise margins. For a nominated set, the Schulze comparison uses the margins induced by the population; it never chooses or reallocates voter mass. So the continuous versions are weighted-electorate versions of the same finite nomination questions, and the paper’s main combinatorics remain in the nominees.

That limits the likely payoff, but it does not defeat either anchor. Theorem 4.1 gives an NP-complete problem to mirror, and Theorem 4.12 gives a coNP-complete one. In both cases rankings are complete voter types, and the continuous questions are clear: choose nominations that make the distinguished nominee a winner, or guarantee a winner against every other party’s nomination. A large electorate in which many members share each of a small number of rankings is a plausible regime for either question. The hardness reductions’ use of only three or four voter types makes them stylized examples of that regime, but does not make the regime meaningless.

The stronger version of the mirrors also survives: allow an arbitrary supported distribution over rankings, retain integral nominations, and analyze complexity in the number of candidates, support size, and mass bit length. Clearing denominators maps each such rational distribution to a finite profile, while encoding masses directly can represent very large electorates compactly. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity question even if the answer is that the nomination hardness persists. Calling it only a change of input encoding would not be enough here: compressed multiplicities are precisely part of the programme’s bridge.

I can make a case that these are less compelling mirrors than problems where a population-level intervention or optimization becomes continuous. But I cannot honestly argue that no scenario makes either worthwhile. The negative case is weak against both anchors; defeating one by silence would leave the other standing.
