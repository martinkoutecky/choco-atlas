What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror covers the §7 goodness method for approval-disapproval-preferential ballots; the paper’s other rule families are outside that proposed scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest candidate is a narrow one: Continuous Goodness-Winner Computation. Its only possible anchor is Proposition A.7, proved in this paper. It says the goodness doctrine’s clauses (102) are disjoint-resolvable, hence equivalent to the doctrine’s Blake canonical form, and that the doctrine is unquestionable for every \(g_x\) and \(\neg g_x\). The paper uses this characterization to give the exact revised values in (106)–(108): \(v^*(g_x)\) is the maximum, over simple paths starting at \(x\), of the minimum of the path’s pairwise-preference values and its endpoint’s initial goodness value; \(v^*(\neg g_x)\) is the corresponding maximum over paths ending at \(x\), starting from an initial badness value; and \(v^*(p_{xy})=\max\{v(p_{xy}),\min(v^*(g_x),v^*(g_y))\}\). This is a computation-ready characterization the paper uses, though it is not a theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time or hard.

The mirror question would be: given a finite option set \(A\), a support \(T\) of complete approval-disapproval-preferential ballot types, and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), compute the set
\[ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in A}\bigl(v_\mu^*(g_x)-v_\mu^*(\neg g_x)\bigr). \]
Each type specifies the ranking and approval information that determines its contribution to every \(p_{xy}\), \(g_x\), and \(\neg g_x\); aggregate those contributions with \(\mu\), then apply the goodness method. The decision is the winning option set, chosen by maximum revised acceptability. This mirrors §7’s underlying rule, not its logical encoding. By the path formulas, I expect this problem to be tractable: aggregate the type contributions and compute the relevant max-min path values. The natural parameters are \(m=|A|\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a large professional association or cooperative election with many members but relatively few recurring ballot types: members sharing a type have the same ranking and approval information, and \(\tau\ll n\). The paper’s rule is well suited to such a population, and this is not a weakened version of its question. The scope is just the goodness method in §7, including the approval-disapproval-preferential setting; it does not extend to the paper’s other rules.

The weak point is substantial: the paper already aggregates individual opinions using relative frequencies or weights in equation (15). So a distribution over ballot types may be a restatement of its existing input model, rather than a new continuization. Also, Proposition A.7 supplies a computational characterization, not a hardness or complexity result. If the anchor requirement is read to require a named complexity classification, then this paper has no qualifying anchor, and I would not claim otherwise. The positive case is that there is a concrete computational problem on a high-multiplicity regime and an exact route to solving it; the case for it as a *new continuous mirror* is weaker because the population is already represented fractionally.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is not a computational result: Proposition A.7 establishes properties of the goodness doctrine’s logical clauses. The actual computation is given by (106)–(108), which calculate the revised goodness values from aggregate preferences and approvals.

Those formulas already operate on a continuous population description. The paper defines collective beliefs as weighted sums of individual opinions in (15), and explicitly uses fractions of voters as degrees of belief. Given ballot types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), set \(\mu_t\) to the sum of the weights of individuals who cast each type. Then the paper’s \(v(p_{xy})\), \(v(g_x)\), and \(v(\neg g_x)\) are exactly the corresponding weighted averages. Identity has no further role. A large association with recurring ballot types is therefore an instance of the paper’s existing input model, not a new population regime.

The stronger version of the proposed mirror—computing the winner from a list of ballot types and rational masses—also appears answered by the paper. Its path formulas apply directly to those fractional inputs. Aggregation takes \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations, and the max–min path values can be computed by a standard \(O(m^3)\) closure; rational bit lengths can be tracked in the same representation. The paper does not state that parameterized complexity bound, but the missing work is formal accounting for an algorithm its formulas already specify. There is no hidden pricing problem or new continuous optimization question here.

A different question, such as the least mass that must change ballot type to alter the winner, could be a worthwhile follow-on. But it requires adding an intervention and a cost model absent from the anchored result; it is not a mirror of Proposition A.7 or of the paper’s winner computation. I would not rule out such new questions in general. The negative case is strongest for the anchor actually offered: the paper already aggregates voter types into fractions and computes its rule on those fractions, so its continuous mirror restates the paper’s own model and solution.
