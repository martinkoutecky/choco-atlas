The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern existence and properties of stable, strategy-proof mechanisms; none classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class. The closest computational statement is an unnumbered sentence after Proposition 2 saying that \(\Psi\) has the same computational complexity as \(DA_M\). That is still about the finite market, so it does not supply the required anchor.

The strongest conceptual foothold is Proposition 2, proved in the paper. For a market with \(|M|\ge |W|\ge3\), it defines \(\Psi\) to use \(DA_W\) when the agents in \(W\) have distinct top choices, and \(DA_M\) otherwise. The proposition proves that \(\Psi\) is stable, strategy-proof for \(M\), and less manipulable by \(M\)-coalitions than \(DA_M\). This is a result about strategic behavior, not computational complexity, so it cannot honestly serve as a qualifying anchor.

A plausible *prospective* mirror is a mass version of that result in a large hiring or task-assignment market. There are many workers and positions, but relatively few complete preference types: each type ranks the types on the other side, and everyone in a type is identical for the purposes of the market. For example, there might be hundreds of preference types among hundreds of thousands of workers and positions.

For a precise continuous question, let \(T_M,T_W\) be the finite type supports, with rational masses \(\alpha_a,\beta_b\), and assume \(\sum_{a\in T_M}\alpha_a\ge\sum_{b\in T_W}\beta_b\). A matching is a flow \(x_{ab}\ge0\), where \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(b\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\beta_b\). Require stability: no pair of types has positive mass on both sides who would each prefer the other type to their current assignment or being unmatched.

Define \(\Psi_\infty\) to use the \(W\)-proposing mass version of deferred acceptance when each type \(a\) has enough capacity for all \(W\)-mass that ranks \(a\) first, and the \(M\)-proposing version otherwise. The associated computational problem, Mass-\(\Psi\) Matching, is: given the rankings and rational masses, compute the exact stable flow selected by \(\Psi_\infty\). A solution is that rational flow; its size and running time should be measured in \(|T_M|,|T_W|\), and the mass bit length \(L\). I would expect this output problem to be tractable, through deferred acceptance on type capacities. That would be a Class A result for the continuous matching computation, though it would be a new result rather than one established by this paper. A separate theorem question is whether \(\Psi_\infty\) is less manipulable than \(DA_M^\infty\) by measurable coalitions of positive mass.

This is a plausible extension of Proposition 2 because mass is the honest quantity in a large cohort market, while the flow records assignments without giving any individual a fractional partner. It covers that strategic-improvement result alone; it does not mirror the paper’s classifications of individual strategy-proof mechanisms or its optimality theorem.

The soft spot is substantial: in an atomless population, one person’s report has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. The mirror needs positive-mass coalition deviations, and its capacity-based “uncontested first choices” condition is a reasoned adaptation of the paper’s distinct-top-choices condition, not a result the authors prove. Combined with the absence of a qualifying computational anchor, this supports a credible research direction, but not a formal anchor-based case under the stated rubric.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper supplies no named computational result to continue. Propositions 1 and 2 and Theorems 1–4 concern which stable, strategy-proof mechanisms exist and how they compare. The sentence that \(\Psi\) has the same computational complexity as \(DA_M\) is an aside about the finite-market mechanisms, not a complexity result. So the proponent has identified a strategic theorem that might inspire a new computational question, not a computational anchor in this paper.

Proposition 2 is the best candidate, and the hiring-market story is plausible. A mass flow can represent assignments among preference types, and replacing distinct first-choice partners with a capacity condition is a sensible adaptation. But that adaptation changes the object being studied. Proposition 2 compares the incentive properties of two mechanisms; the proposed Mass-\(\Psi\) Matching problem computes the output of one selected mechanism. An algorithm for that output would not establish the theorem’s strategic comparison.

A more faithful lift exposes a deeper problem. In a society represented only by its type masses, an individual report has zero mass and cannot change the input distribution. Individual strategy-proofness therefore has no force in the aggregate model. The paper’s proof of Proposition 2 uses finite agents and finite coalitions; its three-agent manipulation witness does not itself survive as a positive-mass deviation. One can study deviations by positive-mass groups, but that makes group incentive compatibility the target. It may be an interesting target, but it is a new one whose relation to the paper’s result needs to be established.

The stronger-looking alternatives face the same gap. A mass version of Theorems 1 and 2 could classify when multiple strategy-proof stable mechanisms exist; a version of Theorem 4 could seek the welfare-best stable mechanism subject to incentive constraints. Those are coherent questions, but their objects are rules over an entire domain of preference profiles. A single distribution of type masses specifies one market instance, not those global rules or their incentive constraints. The paper provides no computational formulation for finding or comparing such rules.

This is a strong case against treating this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: large matching markets with repeated preference types are a credible setting, and mass-stable matching is a coherent model. The most defensible conclusion is that a positive-mass coalition study could be worthwhile as new research, while the paper itself does not establish a computational problem whose continuous mirror the programme should pursue.
