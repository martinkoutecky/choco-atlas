The continuous mirror question

Given finite \(X\), a domain \(D\), a choice correspondence \(c\), and a partial order \(\triangleleft\), does there exist a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(X\), supported on at most two rankings, such that every supported ranking extends \(\triangleleft\) and \(c(A) \subseteq M(\succ_\mu,A)\) for every \(A \in D\), where \(a \succ_\mu b\) exactly when \(\mu(\{t : a \succ_t b\})=1\)?

The model it lives in

The input is \((X,D,c,\triangleleft)\); the witness is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference rankings, with support size at most two. There is no optimization objective: accept exactly when every chosen alternative is Pareto-maximal under the unanimity relation induced by the supported types.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover fixed two-agent \(2\)-WPRAT and \(2\)-PRAT; they leave the paper’s fixed \(K \ge 3\) results, unrestricted-coalition question, and other variants unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-completeness persist when \(\triangleleft\) is restricted to a specified domain-relevant dominance order and the admissible rankings are also constrained?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a society with two preference blocs and a very large population. The paper’s relevant anchor is its Theorem in §3.3, proved in §4; the supplied text prints no theorem number, so I cannot quote one without inventing it. The theorem says that 2-PRAT is NP-complete: given alternatives \(X\), a domain \(D\), and a choice function \(c\), decide whether two complete, transitive, asymmetric preference relations make \(c(A)\) exactly the set of Pareto-maximal alternatives for every \(A\in D\). It also says that 2-WPRAT is NP-complete: given \(X,D,c\) and a partial order \(\triangleleft\), decide whether two such preferences both extend \(\triangleleft\) and every chosen alternative is Pareto-maximal. Both results are proved in this paper: the first by reduction from 3-SAT, the second from monotone not-all-equal 3-SAT.

Consider, for example, a large cooperative or resident association choosing among policy packages. A type is a member’s complete ranking of those packages; members of a type are identical in every respect relevant to the choice problem. There may be millions of members but only two occupied types, such as two stable preference blocs. Let \(T\) be the set of rankings and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of members with ranking \(t\). The society’s Pareto relation is
\[ a \succ_\mu b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu\bigl(\{t\in T:a\succ_t b\}\bigr)=1. \]
Thus an alternative Pareto-dominates another precisely when every occupied type prefers it. The population is continuous, while its type support has size at most two.

My lead question is Two-Bloc Continuous Weak Pareto Rationalizability. An instance consists of finite \(X\), a domain \(D\) of nonempty subsets of \(X\), a nonempty choice correspondence \(c(A)\subseteq A\) for each \(A\in D\), and a partial order \(\triangleleft\) of known unanimous improvements. Does there exist a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(X\), with support size at most two, such that every ranking in its support extends \(\triangleleft\), and
\[ c(A)\subseteq M(\succ_\mu,A) \qquad\text{for every }A\in D? \]
A solution is the distribution itself: its one or two rankings and their positive rational masses summing to one. This is a feasibility question, so it has no optimization objective. The partial order can represent improvements that institutional rules or the setting certify as preferred by every member.

This mirror inherits the theorem’s NP-completeness exactly. A two-person preference profile yields a society with those two types at mass \(1/2\) each; if the preferences coincide, use a single supported type. Conversely, any society with at most two occupied types has the same unanimous Pareto relation as a two-person profile using those rankings. The masses can be any positive rational proportions: under Pareto unanimity, their sizes do not change the relation. The expected direction is therefore hardness transfers (Class B), through a direct two-way correspondence. The paper’s 2-PRAT result gives a cleaner companion question: omit \(\triangleleft\) and require equality \(c(A)=M(\succ_\mu,A)\) for every menu. That version is also NP-complete by the same exact correspondence, though its assumption that all Pareto-efficient alternatives are observed is less behaviorally appealing.

The population regime is concrete, and the paper’s subject—the rationalizability of collective choices by individual preferences under Pareto efficiency—survives intact. This covers the two-agent results, not every coalition-size result or a continuous model with unrestricted type support. Useful next questions are whether hardness persists when the allowed support grows beyond two types, and whether it persists for particular substantively motivated dominance orders.

The weak point is also the mirror’s boundary: with only two occupied types, it lifts the paper’s two-agent problem almost exactly. Mass makes the population genuinely large, but does not affect the Pareto test. So this is a credible two-bloc high-multiplicity regime and a clear hardness-transfer result; it does not establish that continuity changes the complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has strong computational anchors: §3.3 proves \(2\)-PRAT and \(2\)-WPRAT NP-complete, and proves the corresponding results for every fixed \(K\ge 3\). The obstacle is that Pareto rationalizability uses a population’s preferences only through unanimity. For a distribution \(\mu\) over the finite set of rankings,

\[ a\succ_\mu b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\succ_t b\ \text{for every }t\text{ with }\mu_t>0. \]

So the continuous society affects the test only through its support. A tiny positive mass of dissenters blocks a Pareto comparison just as fully as half the population; changing positive masses while keeping the support fixed changes nothing.

If the society is supplied as input, the resulting check is direct: compute unanimous comparisons from the support, then check the listed choice sets against their Pareto maxima. The weak version with a known dominance relation works the same way. This gives a population-based question, but its mass coordinates do no computational work.

The stronger rescue is the analyst’s existential model: ask whether *some* distribution over rankings rationalizes the observed choices. That keeps the paper’s inference question, but its witness is still just a set of rankings. With support at most two, it is exactly the two-preference profile in \(2\)-PRAT or \(2\)-WPRAT, regardless of the positive rational masses. Allowing larger support does not restore a continuum dimension: there are only \(|X|!\) possible rankings, so every distribution reduces to a finite support set. That version is the paper’s unrestricted-profile question, which it explicitly leaves open. These observations concern what the input and witness encode, not whether the continuous problem would turn out to be easy or hard.

A majority or threshold rule could make the amounts of mass matter, but it would replace Pareto unanimity with a different social criterion. I would therefore argue that neither anchor yields a substantial continuous-population computational problem in ChoCo’s sense: the known-society formulation is a direct support check, and the existential formulation is finite-profile rationalizability written with masses.

The case against is still limited. A large association with repeated preference blocs is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the proposed two-bloc model is an exact representation of it. If ChoCo counts a support-only interpretation and a hardness transfer as worthwhile, that mirror survives; I can’t honestly claim a universal obstruction to it.
