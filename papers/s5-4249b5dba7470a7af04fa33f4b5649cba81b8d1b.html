The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper does not provide a compliant anchor under your rules. The closest positive case is therefore conditional on accepting an unnumbered cited result as an anchor. My lead would be the Section 3.5 claim that Kemeny Score remains NP-hard with only four voters, cited to [50, 51, 19]. This is not proved in the paper.

A natural continuous mirror is:

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support of rankings \(T\subseteq S_C\), rational masses \(\mu_\sigma\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(B\), find a ranking \(\rho\in S_C\) minimizing

\[ K_\mu(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma\in T}\mu_\sigma\,\operatorname{KT}(\rho,\sigma), \]

where \(\operatorname{KT}\) is Kendall–Tau inversion distance; decide whether \(K_\mu(\rho)\le B\). Here a type is a complete ranking \(\sigma\), \(\mu_\sigma\) is the fraction of a large population reporting that ranking, and the action is to publish one consensus ranking. The objective is average disagreement with the population.

This is a credible high-multiplicity version of Kemeny rank aggregation: a platform may aggregate a very large population whose rankings fall into a relatively small number of repeated preference types. A discrete election with \(n\) voters maps directly to \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/n\), and a Kemeny threshold \(k\) maps to \(B=k/n\). Thus the cited hardness transfers, making this a Class B mirror rather than a continuum-specific tractability result. In fact, the paper’s four-voter hardness claim suggests hardness even with support size bounded by four, while fixed \(m\) still gives the usual finite-action algorithm.

The main further questions would concern the complexity as a function of \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the encoding length of the rational masses, together with whether compressed repeated rankings permit useful approximation or preprocessing.

The weak point is substantial: this mirror gives no obvious computational gain from continuization; it is essentially weighted Kemeny aggregation, and the paper itself does not formulate populations as distributions. Nevertheless, it is a genuine population mirror rather than an outcome-space relaxation, and the paper is not already studying that continuous-population model. So the mirror is defensible—but the absence of any eligible numbered result means the paper cannot support the requested anchor-based case strictly as written.

But the stronger substantive claim—that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario—is not honestly defensible here. The proposed Kemeny mirror survives the natural objections. Let ranking types be \(T\subseteq S_C\), let \(\mu_\sigma\) be rational population masses, and minimize

\[ \sum_{\sigma\in T}\mu_\sigma\,\operatorname{KT}(\rho,\sigma) \]

over consensus rankings \(\rho\). This is a genuine high-multiplicity model: a large population can contain many agents with identical rankings, and the objective is average disagreement with that population. It does not continuize the outcome space, erase identity needed by the objective, or rely on a vanishing individual’s influence. Replacing the paper’s four voters by four ranking types with masses \(1/4\) is perfectly meaningful, and allowing arbitrary rational masses gives the stronger weighted version.

The best criticism is that this mirror is essentially weighted Kemeny aggregation, so continuization may add little beyond replacing multiplicities by rational weights. It also does not engage the programme’s most distinctive LP-and-pricing agenda. However, “the answer may be unchanged” and “continuization may not help” are expressly unavailable objections: those are legitimate results, not failures of formulation.
