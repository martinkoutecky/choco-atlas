Why no mirror

Theorem 5.1 gives the value \((n-1)/n\) for a probabilistic extremum, but does not assert an algorithm, computational guarantee, or hardness result. The proposed population-share problem is statable, yet it changes the event from one trial’s vote-share comparison to an individual’s preference, so it is not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper.

What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue targets only Theorem 5.1; the Arrow conditions, descriptions of voting rules, and other discussion are not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper has no named theorem classifying a social-choice problem as P, NP-hard, or FPT. But Theorem 5.1 (Arbitrary Random Variables) is an exact optimization result and can serve as a tractability anchor. It is reported from Usiskin [Usi64], not proved in this paper: for \(n\) arbitrary real-valued random variables, maximizing over their joint distributions, the largest possible minimum of the cyclic comparison probabilities
\[ P(X_1>X_2),\ldots,P(X_{n-1}>X_n),P(X_n>X_1) \]
is exactly \((n-1)/n\).

A corresponding continuous-population problem is Cycle-Share Maximization\(_\infty\). An instance is \(n\ge 3\) policies \(c_1,\ldots,c_n\) arranged in a cycle, with \(c_{n+1}=c_1\). A type is a complete weak ranking of those policies; a society is a distribution \(\mu\) over types, with rational masses given in binary. The task is to return the maximum value and a society attaining it:
\[ \max_{\mu\in\Delta(T)} \min_{i\in[n]} \sum_{t:\,c_i\succ_t c_{i+1}}\mu_t . \]
Thus the objective is the smallest population share that strictly prefers one policy to the next edge in the cycle. An answer consists of the exact optimum and a finite-support rational distribution achieving it.

This is a direct mathematical bridge to Theorem 5.1: drawing a type from \(\mu\) gives \(P(X_i>X_j)=\mu(\{t:c_i\succ_t c_j\})\). Conversely, a joint distribution over real-valued scores induces a distribution over weak rankings, preserving those strict-comparison probabilities. The theorem therefore gives the optimum for this population problem. It is tractable: no ranking can strictly endorse every edge of a cycle, so the sum of the \(n\) edge shares is at most \(n-1\), and the minimum is at most \((n-1)/n\). Attain that bound by assigning mass \(1/n\) to each of \(n\) strict rankings, one for each path formed by deleting a different cycle edge. Every edge then receives support from exactly \(n-1\) of the rankings. The output uses only \(n\) types, despite there being \(n!\) strict rankings; it can be constructed in polynomial time. A threshold version—whether some society gives every edge at least rational share \(q\)—is yes exactly when \(q\le(n-1)/n\). I would expect a tractable, Class A answer.

A plausible regime is a large collaborative platform or enterprise making a choice among a small fixed agenda. For five policies, the paper counts 541 possible weak orderings, so millions of participants could fall into at most 541 preference types; the extremal society above uses only five. Within this question, two agents with the same ranking are indistinguishable. This mirrors only Theorem 5.1’s cycle-probability result, not the paper’s Arrow discussion or its other voting material.

The weakest point is the change in what the distribution represents. In the paper, \(X_i\) represents a vote share and the probability is over possible outcomes; here, \(\mu\) is a fixed population composition and the probability is the fraction of members with a given preference. The pairwise quantities and optimization are mathematically equivalent, but the shift from uncertainty across trials to heterogeneity across people is substantive. I think the authors could recognize it as their question about how strongly a cycle can persist, expressed in population shares. They might still reject it if they regard trial-level uncertainty as essential to Theorem 5.1’s meaning.

Further questions include whether the same optimization remains tractable when only some rankings are admissible, and what happens when types must arise from independent latent scores for each policy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.1 is the only plausible anchor, but it is a bound on probability distributions, not a computational result about a society. It is attributed to Usiskin and asks how large the minimum of several pairwise event probabilities can be over a joint distribution of random variables. The paper gives no input society, intervention, or algorithmic problem whose complexity it analyzes.

The proposed mirror also changes what those probabilities mean. In the paper, \(X_i\) is a policy’s share of the vote in a trial, and \(P(X_i>X_j)\) is the probability across trials that policy \(i\) receives a larger share than policy \(j\). In Cycle-Share Maximization, the corresponding quantity is the fraction of people who prefer \(c_i\) to \(c_j\). Those are different events at different levels: in a fixed society, vote shares are aggregates, while preference shares describe individuals. Turning the former into a distribution over whole elections would restore the paper’s meaning, but then the uncertainty is over outcomes or trials, not the population—the scope the programme sets aside.

The proposed optimization is a sensible population question in its own right, but it chooses \(\mu\) from all possible societies rather than taking a society as input. If \(\mu\) is fixed, the pairwise preference shares are direct sums over its support. If the domain is restricted or other constraints are added to make the optimization instance-specific, those constraints supply a new problem absent from the paper. Such a problem might be worth studying, but it would not be a computational mirror of Theorem 5.1.

The tempting stronger variants have the same fork: keep the paper’s trial-level probabilities, and population continuization is not doing the work; make the probabilities population shares, and the object being optimized is no longer the theorem’s one. The other results offer no alternative computational anchor: the Arrow discussion is axiomatic, and the paper’s rule descriptions and other mathematical observations do not classify algorithms or complexity.

This negative case is not airtight. The population-share formulation is a clean question about how strongly a preference cycle can be supported, and the authors might recognize it as relevant to their discussion of policy cycles. But that makes it a plausible new social-choice question, not a strong mirror of this paper’s computational results.
