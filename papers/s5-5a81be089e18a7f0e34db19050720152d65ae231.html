The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result, and no complexity classification. It does contain computations: for example, Table 2 reports Council Shapley–Shubik power shares under the Nice and Lisbon rules. Germany’s share rises from \(8.74\%\) to \(16.29\%\), while Poland’s falls from \(7.99\%\) to \(6.92\%\). Those are relevant findings, but they are not the kind of named result your anchor requirement calls for. I won’t present them as one.

The strongest candidate mirror I can offer, therefore, is explicitly unanchored: an asymptotic version of the paper’s intra-Council question about how voting rules distribute power.

Call it Asymptotic Type Power in a Council. An instance gives a finite set \(T\) of jurisdiction types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. A type \(t\) specifies each jurisdiction’s vote weight \(a_t\) and population contribution \(p_t\). Think of many local jurisdictions in a federation, with many sharing the same institutional class and population band; then \(N\) jurisdictions may have only \(\tau=|T|\) distinct types. For \(N=kD\), where \(D\) clears the denominators of \(\mu\), there are \(N\mu_t\) jurisdictions of each type. Mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction of jurisdictions of type \(t\); a coalition’s yes-voting mass is \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\).

Apply Nice-like and Lisbon-like rules to each finite council. The Nice-like rule requires at least \(255/345\) of total vote weight, \(14/27\) of jurisdictions, and \(62\%\) of population. The Lisbon-like rule requires at least \(55\%\) of jurisdictions and \(65\%\) of population. For rule \(r\), let \(\phi_i^{r,N}\) be the probability that jurisdiction \(i\) is pivotal in a uniformly random ordering, as in the paper’s SSI definition. Let \(P_{t}^{r,N}=\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}\phi_i^{r,N}\) be the type’s aggregate power share.

The question is: for each rule, do the limits \(P_t^r(\mu)=\lim_{k\to\infty}P_t^{r,kD}\) exist, and how can they be computed or approximated from the type distribution and voting attributes? The solution is the vector of type power shares under each rule and their difference. It asks how the paper’s measure of power redistribution behaves when the council population is represented by type masses. I would tentatively expect a tractable answer when \(\tau\) is fixed, through finite-dimensional asymptotic analysis; I would not claim tractability when \(\tau\) is part of the input without a result. Natural follow-up questions are the convergence rate, sensitivity to quota changes, and whether finite high-multiplicity councils approximate the limit accurately.

This covers only the paper’s intra-Council analysis of voting rules and power distribution. It does not mirror the SMP analysis of Council–Parliament power. The model is plausibly recognizable to the authors as their power-distribution question extended to many repeated jurisdictions, but the weakness is substantial: the paper studies 27 distinct states, not a population regime. The proposed scenario changes the institutional setting, and individual pivotality disappears in the limit, so the mirror must track aggregate type power. That is a defensible co-modification, but whether the authors would accept it is uncertain. The mirror’s positive case rests on the paper’s actual computations in Table 2—not on a qualifying named anchor—so it falls short of the requested anchor-based standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper offers no qualifying computational result to mirror. It has numerical comparisons of Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf power under specified Council rules, plus an SMP analysis; it does not state a complexity theorem or classify an algorithmic problem. The case for acknowledges this. Those tables can motivate a new question, but they cannot supply the missing anchor.

The strongest version of its fallback is coherent: replicate a finite set of jurisdiction types, take a limit of the resulting finite councils, and ask for each type’s aggregate power under SSI. Individual pivotality tending to zero would not defeat it, since aggregate type power could remain nonzero. But getting that limit requires treating copies as interchangeable in every relevant respect. Keep each state’s identity and particular preferences, and there is no small-support replication regime; drop them to obtain one, and the question becomes asymptotic power allocation in a different kind of council. It no longer answers the paper’s specific question about which named states gain or lose under Nice and Lisbon, or its EU codecision comparison.

A continuous model of many MEP-like legislators could likewise be constructed. But a distribution over their policy positions is already part of the paper’s preference randomization; that alone does not continuize the population. Making the legislators themselves a replicated population would again introduce a new model. Either route might support a separate study, but neither rescues an anchor in this paper, and neither gives the computational complexity result the programme is meant to chart.

So the negative case is strong on the paper-anchored question: the proposed candidate is not a qualifying mirror of a named computational result. The broader claim that *no* plausible replicated council could yield worthwhile continuous computation is weaker. The aggregate-SSI limit is a legitimate research question; the case against it is that it belongs to a new asymptotic voting-power project, not that it is incoherent or guaranteed to be trivial.
