The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in this paper. The closest results are mathematical comparisons, all proved here: Theorem 2.1 says that with three candidates, for every \(d>1\), the unique Borda winner’s divergence from first place is at most the unique plurality winner’s, with equality exactly when the winners coincide. Theorem 2.3 gives a four-candidate profile for every \(d>1\) where the winners differ and the plurality winner’s divergence is at most the Borda winner’s. Theorem 2.5 extends that construction to larger candidate sets and gives a *strictly* lower divergence for the plurality winner. None classifies a problem as being in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise. Theorem 2.5 is the strongest result to build on, but it does not meet your anchor rule.

The strongest plausible continuous computational question is Population \(d\)-Compromise Winner. Fix an integer \(d\ge2\). An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of complete rankings \(t\in S_m\), each with a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). The mass is the fraction of society with that ranking. For each candidate \(c\), compute
\[ \beta^d_1(c;\mu)=\sum_t \mu_t\bigl(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)-1\bigr)^d. \]
Return all candidates minimizing this quantity. The decision variable is the selected candidate; the objective is exactly the paper’s measure of divergence from first place. With fixed integer \(d\), direct evaluation takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations on the supported ranking types.

There is a credible regime for this model: a large electorate divided among a few stable blocs with shared rankings. The displayed construction behind Theorem 2.5 uses three ranking types, so it can represent a large population with \(\tau=3\). The authors would likely recognize the population version of their compromise measure: replacing voter counts by fractions preserves its meaning, and for a profile of \(n\) voters with type counts \(n_t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives \(\beta^d_1(c;\mu)=\beta^d_1(c;\alpha)/n\). The candidate comparisons are unchanged.

Theorem 2.5’s continuous counterpart is therefore immediate: for every \(d>1\), there is a rational distribution over rankings with distinct unique Borda and plurality winners, where the plurality winner has strictly lower first-place divergence. That is a faithful mirror of its result, and it supports the need to compute the paper’s compromise winner. It does not establish a complexity result for that computation: evaluating the measure appears straightforward, and the theorem is about winner comparisons, not computational hardness or an algorithm.

So the positive case is that the paper’s central measure naturally defines a population-level winner problem, with a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Its weakest point is that this mirror adds little to the programme’s complexity landscape: the discrete profile already records type multiplicities, and the proposed computation is direct. I would treat this as a coherent but modest mirror, not a qualifying anchor-backed case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with a mismatch to ChoCo’s target: this paper has no theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2.1 compares Borda and plurality winners for three candidates; Theorem 2.3 constructs a four-candidate counterexample; Theorem 2.5 extends that construction to larger candidate sets. These are mathematical claims about profiles and a divergence measure. They do not study an algorithmic task such as finding an optimal intervention or classifying its complexity.

Each comparison has a faithful population translation. If type \(t\) has count \(n_t\) in a profile of size \(n\), then setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives
\[ \beta^d_1(c;\mu)=\frac{\beta^d_1(c;\alpha)}{n}. \]
So the comparisons are preserved. In particular, the construction behind Theorem 2.5 yields a rational distribution with the same winner and divergence ordering. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario—large blocs of voters with shared rankings—and I would not object to it on population-model grounds. It still mirrors an extensional theorem, not a computational result.

The proposed Population \(d\)-Compromise Winner makes a reasonable next step, but it is a step beyond the paper. The paper defines a divergence measure and compares two existing rules; it does not define the rule that selects a candidate minimizing that measure. Its conclusion suggests such a rule as further research. For fixed integer \(d\), computing the proposed winner is just evaluating the explicit sums for each candidate, in \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations. Computing the whole candidate-position matrix takes \(O(m^2\tau)\). A polynomial answer could itself be useful; the problem here is that this model supplies no substantial algorithmic question tied to one of the paper’s results.

A stronger algorithmic proposal would be minimum mass transfer, at a stated cost, to make a target candidate minimize the \(d\)-measure. That could lead to an LP with many possible output rankings and a nontrivial pricing problem. It also adds strategic intervention and a cost model that the paper never studies. It is a plausible new project built around the paper’s measure, rather than a continuous mirror of its theorems. If any related computational extension counts as a mirror, this is the negative case’s main vulnerability.

Thus the case against is strong under ChoCo’s stated scope: all three proposed anchors are comparison theorems, and the most faithful computational formulation chooses a new rule and asks for a routine calculation. The universal claim is weaker. A researcher could reasonably take the measure as the basis for a new intervention problem; I would call that a worthwhile adjacent question, but not a computational result of this paper continuized.
