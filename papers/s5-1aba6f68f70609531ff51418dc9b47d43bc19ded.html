What the mirror covers

The paper's axiomatic characterization and comparison of divisor methods with UDP allocations are not computational results; the proposed cohort problem is a separate extension.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a plausible high-multiplicity *follow-on*, but this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its only displayed theorem is “Theorem [Balinski, Young 1982]”: an allocation is unbiased, pairwise consistent, homogeneous, exact, and weakly monotone if and only if it is a divisor method. That theorem is unnumbered in this paper, concerns an axiomatic characterization rather than computational complexity, and is attributed to prior work. The paper’s other claims about UDP allocations are prose analysis, not named computational results.

A natural research extension would group a large population of claimants into \(\tau\) entitlement types, represent each type by its mass, and study computing allocations of indivisible goods that satisfy a specified UDP rule and the total supply constraint. That regime is imaginable—for example, a large benefits cohort with standardized entitlement classes—and type-level average allocations could be fractional even when each person’s allocation is integral. But the paper neither poses nor analyzes that computational problem. It would be a new question inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its named results; so there is no anchor here for a per-result mirror or a tractability prediction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive point, but then offers a follow-on rather than a mirror. The paper’s displayed theorem is Balinski and Young’s axiomatic characterization of divisor methods. It says which allocation rules satisfy five axioms; it does not state the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper presents it as prior work. The paper’s own contribution is also axiomatic: it compares which of those properties UDP allocations satisfy. Even its observation that choosing among UDP allocations can be difficult is motivation, not a formulated algorithmic problem. There is no input encoding, computational task, or complexity result here to continuize.

A large cohort with standardized entitlement classes could be a sensible high-multiplicity apportionment setting. But making it a computational study requires new choices: how masses of claimants determine entitlements, how indivisible seats are assigned across types, and how boundary constraints and multiple feasible allocations are handled. One could, for example, let a type’s mass receive a distribution of integer allotments and optimize over those distributions. That may produce a worthwhile problem, but it supplies the problem and its objective; neither comes from a computational result in this paper. Allowing fractional type-level averages does not change that.

So the case against a *mirror of this paper* is strong: none of the anchors is a computational result to mirror, and the cohort scenario does not create one retroactively. The honest limit is that this paper might inspire a separate computational project in apportionment. I cannot rule out that project being worthwhile; it would be new work, not a continuous mirror of the results presented here.
