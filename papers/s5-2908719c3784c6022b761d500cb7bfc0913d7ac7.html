What the mirror covers

Proposition 6 covers the exponential-turnout welfare optimum; Propositions 1–5 and the corollaries characterize equilibria and comparative statics in the same continuous-population model.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a limited positive case, but the paper does not contain a named result classifying a problem as in P, NP-hard, or similar. Its closest computational foothold is Proposition 6, proved here: with \(B,\beta,\alpha_1,\alpha_2>0\) and turnout functions \(\rho_i(x)=1-e^{-\alpha_i x}\), it gives the welfare-maximizing majority threshold
\[ M^*= \frac{B\beta^2\alpha_1\alpha_2+(\beta+1)^2\alpha_1} {B\beta(\beta+1)\alpha_1\alpha_2+(\beta+1)^2(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)} \]
and the corresponding positive equilibrium spending levels, determined by
\[ e^{-\alpha_1x_r^*}=\frac{(\beta+1)^2}{B\beta\alpha_1+(\beta+1)^2}, \qquad e^{-\alpha_2x_s^*}=\frac{(\beta+1)^2}{B\beta^2\alpha_2+(\beta+1)^2}. \]
That is an explicit solution to an optimization problem, though it is an analytical result rather than a complexity classification.

The precise computation problem behind it is: given rational \(B,\beta,\alpha_1,\alpha_2>0\), find the majority threshold and equilibrium spending that maximize expected voter welfare in the paper’s exponential-turnout model. Its solution is \((M^*,x_r^*,x_s^*)\) as above; the spending levels can be output as logarithmic expressions or approximated to a requested precision. This restricted problem is tractable by the proposition itself. It covers the paper’s exponential example, not its more general equilibrium analysis.

The regime is plausible: a large referendum electorate can have two broad camps, with agents in each camp sharing preferences, preference intensity, and mobilization response. There may be millions of eligible voters and only two types. But this is also the central weakness: the paper already models a unit-mass continuum of voters and a continuously uncertain share supporting reform. The proposed problem restates its own continuous-population model and result; it does not newly continuize a discrete problem.

A natural follow-up would allow finitely many mobilization types within each camp, represented by their masses and turnout responses, and ask for the welfare-maximizing threshold and equilibrium spending. That is a plausible computational extension, but Proposition 6 does not establish its complexity, and the paper offers no discrete hardness result for comparison. So the honest positive case is that the article demonstrates a natural, tractable continuous referendum problem—not that it supplies a new continuous mirror under the programme’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 6 is the strongest anchor, and it already lives in the continuous population model a mirror would propose. The paper has a unit-mass electorate, with a random mass \(e_r\) supporting reform and \(1-e_r\) supporting the status quo. It then solves for the welfare-maximizing threshold and equilibrium spending. Given \(B,\beta,\alpha_1,\alpha_2\), the threshold is the explicit expression in the proposition; the spending levels follow from logarithms of explicit expressions. Casting this as “compute the optimum for a continuous society” would restate the paper’s own model and result. That is a collision because the paper itself makes the population continuous, not because an outside answer happens to be known.

A high-multiplicity discrete referendum is also plausible: many voters can share one of two positions and common mobilization responses. But in this paper’s setup, the only strategic choices are two spending levels, and all population detail enters through the two aggregate turnout functions \(\rho_r\) and \(\rho_s\). Splitting either camp into more types with different costs or response curves can be absorbed into those aggregate functions, so it does not create the type-indexed computational problem the programme targets. A richer model with type-specific campaign allocation or additional welfare consequences could prevent that compression. It would also introduce a new strategic problem, rather than continuize Proposition 6.

The negative case is weaker at that boundary: referenda are an excellent high-multiplicity setting, and a richer model could be worthwhile. But this paper supplies no unsolved computational result for that extension. Its other equilibrium and welfare results likewise concern a small, already-continuous model; they do not identify a complexity question awaiting a continuous mirror.
