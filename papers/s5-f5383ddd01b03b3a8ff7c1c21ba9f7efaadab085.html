The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete firm types and a rational circuit describing a strictly superadditive worth function \(v(x,\pi)\), where each cartel is a type-mass vector \(x\leq\mu\) and \(\pi\) is a finite partition of \(\mu\) into such vectors, decide whether there is a finite-description type-symmetric normal-form game whose composite game for every \(\pi\) has a Nash equilibrium giving each cartel \(x\) payoff \(v(x,\pi)\); construct such a game when one exists. The same question can be asked for weakly superadditive characteristic-function worth functions, matching Theorem 2.

The model it lives in

A continuum of firms is represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete types. Cartels are finite blocks of the continuum, described by their type-mass vectors; each block acts as one strategic player with a joint action, preserving Nash equilibrium at the composite-game level. Encode the worth function and candidate type-symmetric game with finite rational circuits, and ask whether the game reproduces the worth of every cartel in every finite mass partition.

The objection that survived

The finite construction relies on a finite set of strategies and deviations, and the proposed mass-based worth functions and implementing games lack a specified encoding; the paper does not show that its construction extends to them.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a high-multiplicity industry: many firms share a small number \(\tau\) of complete types, such as the same production cost, technology, and strategic parameters. A type’s mass is its share of firms. A coalition is described by the mass of each type it contains, and a partition describes how those masses are divided among cartels.

One possible new problem is Continuous Implementation Synthesis. Given a rational type distribution \(\mu\) and a succinct worth function \(v\) over coalition-mass vectors and finite partitions of that mass, decide whether there is a finite-description, type-symmetric normal-form game whose Nash equilibria reproduce \(v\) for every coalition in every such partition; if so, construct the game. This extends the paper’s notion of implementation to a continuous population and makes construction a computational question.

But this is a research lead, not a qualifying anchored mirror. The closest named result, Theorem 1, says that every strictly superadditive partition-function-form game \((N,v)\) is implementable under Nash equilibrium. Theorem 2 gives the same conclusion for weakly superadditive characteristic-function-form games; Theorem 3 covers strictly superadditive partition-function-form games under rationalizability; and Theorem 4 covers weakly superadditive partition-function-form games under trembling-hand perfect equilibrium. All four are existence results for finite player sets. None states a complexity bound or an algorithmic result. The propositions concern externalities and cooperative solution properties, not computational complexity. The construction in Theorem 1 does not supply a runtime bound.

There is also a real modeling gap: the paper’s worth function can distinguish named players and depends on finite coalition partitions. A continuous mirror needs an anonymous worth function based on type masses, plus a precise account of what partitions and equilibria mean for a continuum of agents. I would not assign the proposed synthesis problem to A, B, or C on this paper’s results.

So, under the required anchor rule, the answer is that the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror on. The synthesis problem is a plausible direction suggested by its subject, but it is not a case supported by an eligible result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 say that certain cooperative games *exist* as implementations under specified solution concepts. They give no decision problem, algorithm, running time, or complexity classification. The proof of Theorem 1 chooses \(\theta\) by taking a maximum over finitely many deviations; that establishes existence, not an algorithmic bound. The propositions about Cournot and Bertrand competition are also economic and solution-theoretic results, not complexity results.

The proponent’s best candidate is a high-multiplicity industry: firms fall into finitely many complete types, and cartel composition is represented by type masses. That is a plausible population regime, even though the paper’s strictly ordered costs do not themselves provide repeated types. But it does not rescue the proposed Continuous Implementation Synthesis as a mirror. For arbitrary worth functions on coalition-mass vectors and partitions, there is no finite input until one specifies how those functions are represented. “Succinct” does not specify an encoding, and the task also needs a representation for the game to be constructed. Restricting to, say, rational parametric worth functions could make a new computational problem precise, but its substance would come from those new restrictions and encodings. The paper’s theorems do not identify or analyze that problem.

So the high-multiplicity scenario makes an aggregate version conceivable; it does not supply a computational anchor. The honest limit of this negative case is that implementation synthesis under a carefully chosen finite-type encoding might become a worthwhile project in its own right. On the material here, though, that would be a new problem suggested by the paper’s subject, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
