The continuous mirror question

Given firm and worker type sets \(A,B\), rational type masses \(r_a,s_b\), and nonnegative rational match surpluses \(\alpha_{ab}\), compute a maximum-surplus mass matching \(x\) and supporting core payoffs \(u,v\) satisfying the type-mass capacity and core conditions; measure complexity in \(|A|,|B|\), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

An atomless assignment market has mass \(r_a\) of each complete firm valuation type \(a\) and mass \(s_b\) of each complete worker valuation type \(b\). Choose matching flows \(x_{ab}\ge0\) and type payoffs \(u_a,v_b\ge0\), maximizing \(\sum_{a,b}\alpha_{ab}x_{ab}\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le r_a\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le s_b\), \(u_a+v_b\ge\alpha_{ab}\), and complementary slackness: positive flow uses tight type-payoff constraints, and unused type mass has zero payoff.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s core-outcome construction target in a type-mass assignment market. It leaves aside the decentralized aspiration dynamics and the least-core and stochastic-stability selection results of Theorem 3 and Corollary 4.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is core construction in a high-multiplicity assignment market. But strictly speaking, the paper has no named result about a computational complexity class: none says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or similar. Its theorems concern learning dynamics and stochastic stability.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: from any initial state, the paper’s process is absorbed into the core in finite time with probability \(1\); its absorbing states are exactly the core states in which singles have aspiration level zero; and from zero initial aspirations, any such absorbing state is attainable. The paper also says the proof gives an algorithm for finding core outcomes. That makes core construction the best computational foothold, though Theorem 1 gives no running-time bound.

A plausible mirror is High-Multiplicity Assignment-Core Computation. Consider a large labor market with many firms and workers but relatively few complete valuation types. A firm type records its willingness to pay against every worker type; a worker type records its willingness to accept against every firm type. Agents within a type share the relevant market parameters, and each type has a rational mass. Let \(\alpha_{ab}\) be the surplus from matching firm type \(a\) with worker type \(b\). The instance is these type sets, their masses \(r_a,s_b\), and the surplus matrix. The task is to return a maximum-surplus matching flow \(x_{ab}\) and supporting type payoffs \(u_a,v_b\), satisfying
\[ x_{ab}\ge 0,\quad \sum_b x_{ab}\le r_a,\quad \sum_a x_{ab}\le s_b, \]
\[ u_a,v_b\ge 0,\quad u_a+v_b\ge \alpha_{ab}, \]
with \(x_{ab}>0\) only when \(u_a+v_b=\alpha_{ab}\), and unused type mass receiving zero payoff. These conditions certify an efficient matching and a core allocation. Fractional \(x_{ab}\) represents the aggregate mass of individually matched agents; it does not require any individual to receive a fractional match.

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: a platform might serve millions of participants whose complete valuation profiles fall into tens or hundreds of types. The expected answer is tractable: the type-level matching and payoff certificates form a linear program of size polynomial in the listed types and input bit length. This question concerns the population itself as a distribution over types.

I would limit the mirror to the core-construction target of Theorem 1. Theorem 3 and Corollary 4 give important least-core selection results, but they are not computational-complexity anchors, so I would not use them to inflate the case.

The weak point is that the paper’s distinctive contribution is the decentralized learning process: agents reach the core without a central authority. The proposed LP computes a core outcome and drops that mechanism. The paper’s constructive proof gives some reason the authors might recognize core construction as their problem, but this is a mirror of the result’s computational target, not of its central dynamics. Whether an analogous decentralized process works for continuous masses, and whether its selection still yields the least core, are natural follow-up questions; they would be additional analytic questions rather than complexity results established by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor: Theorem 1 is a theorem about a specified stochastic process, not a computational-complexity result. It proves almost-sure finite absorption and reachability, but gives no running-time bound in the input size. The paper says its proof can be used to find a core outcome; that supports a connection to computation, but the construction is a positive-probability path in the decentralized process, not an efficient algorithm for a central authority. So the proposed core-computation mirror changes what the paper is studying.

The best static mirror is still sensible: give complete valuation types rational masses and ask for a matching flow and supporting core payoffs. But that formulation separates the answer from the paper’s distinctive claim about decentralized learning and equitable selection. Core computation is already the primal-dual assignment problem: Shapley and Shubik characterize assignment-game core payoffs through the dual of the optimal-assignment LP ([their core paper](https://www.math.utoronto.ca/mccann/assignments/477/ShapleyShubik72.pdf)). Continuous assignment economies are also an established object, though the cited work studies competition and equilibrium analytically rather than computational complexity ([Gretsky, Ostroy, and Zame](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022053199925401)). That prior work is not a computational collision under ChoCo’s rules; it does underline that the proposed mirror is an elementary static optimization problem, not a computational question emerging from this paper’s main result.

A stronger candidate would preserve the equity claim and ask for a least-core outcome in the type-mass market. That is more faithful to Theorem 3’s selection result, but it still computes a static selected allocation rather than explaining the stochastic stability that selects it. Preserving the dynamics instead requires a new limit model: an individual activation changes zero mass in an atomless population, while a population-level flow must track aspirations and matching states. The paper’s finite-state resistance-tree analysis then has no automatic continuum counterpart; choosing how rare shocks scale with population size would be part of the new question. That could be worthwhile work, but it is a different analytic dynamics project, not a computational mirror supplied by this paper.

I would not claim the universal negative is airtight. The type-mass core problem is well posed, and the paper itself gives some reason its authors might recognize core construction as relevant. The honest negative is narrower: the paper offers no named complexity result, and its most defensible computational mirror discards the learning and selection mechanism that motivates the paper. That makes the case against a ChoCo mirror plausible, but not decisive.
