The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), target \(p\), protocol \(P\in\{\mathrm{Plurality},\mathrm{Borda},\mathrm{Maximin},\mathrm{STV}\}\), fixed announced pairing \(\mathcal M\), rational finite-support distribution \(\nu\) over complete rankings with \(\sum_t\nu_t=1-\alpha\), and a controlled \(p\)-top type of mass \(0<\alpha<1\), does some common bloc report \(r\) make \(p\) win under DPRE + \(P\) on \(\mu^r=\nu+\alpha e_r\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity electorate is a rational mass distribution over ranking types; the controlled type has mass \(\alpha\), all its copies use one complete report \(r\) or contingent policy, and the objective is whether aggregate mass makes \(p\) win.

The objection that survived

The common-report positive-mass bloc changes the original one-voter action space and is not forced by high multiplicity, even though the replicated-software-module scenario makes it plausible.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It covers Theorems 6, 13, and 15—the NP-, \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-, and PSPACE-hardness results for DPRE, RPRE, and IPRE manipulation—and leaves the general reduction templates, construction lemmas, and untweaked baseline results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the DPRE problem remain hard when \(\alpha\) is fixed, minimized, or allowed to split across reports?
  • What exact or approximation complexity does RPRE winning-probability computation have for rational mass profiles?
  • Does IPRE PSPACE-hardness survive fractional bloc reports or bounded support in the contingent policy?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a Class B mirror: the paper’s hardness is driven by candidate-level combinatorics, so it should survive continuization of the population. The paper gives three unusually strong anchors, all proved in the paper. My lead is Theorem 6 because its continuous analogue is the cleanest.

Take \(C\) to be the candidate set and \(\mathcal R(C)\) the complete rankings of \(C\). A continuous electorate is a rational distribution \(\nu\) over \(\mathcal R(C)\), with total mass \(1-\alpha\). A controlled bloc of mass \(\alpha>0\) consists of many identical agents whose common true preference has \(p\) as its preferred candidate. The bloc chooses one reported ranking \(r\in\mathcal R(C)\), so the reported society is

\[ \mu^{r}=\nu+\alpha e_r. \]

For candidates \(a,b\), the pairwise comparison uses the total mass ranking \(a\) above \(b\); plurality, Borda, Maximin, and STV likewise use mass-weighted scores. Ties use the same fixed tie-breaking rule as in the paper. Every infinitesimal voter still casts an integral ranking; only the population measure is continuous.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s own motivating setting of software agents: a large deployment contains many copies of a small number of voter modules, and a controlled cohort shares one strategic reporting policy. If a finite instance has \(N\) nonmanipulators and one manipulator, set \(\alpha=1/(N+1)\) and \(\nu_t=n_t/(N+1)\). Repeating every voter type \(R\) times leaves the mass instance unchanged while making the actual electorate arbitrarily large relative to its number of distinct types.

Lead: Continuous DPRE Bloc Manipulation. The instance consists of \(C\), \(p\), a rational finite-support distribution \(\nu\), a bloc mass \(\alpha\), a fixed announced pairing \(\mathcal M\) of candidates, and \(P\in\{\mathrm{Plurality},\mathrm{Borda},\mathrm{Maximin},\mathrm{STV}\}\). The preround eliminates the loser of every pair in \(\mathcal M\); \(P\) is then run on the surviving candidates using the restricted rankings. The question is whether there exists a single ranking \(r\) for the controlled bloc such that \(p\) wins. A solution is the ranking \(r\).

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: in each of \(\mathrm{DPRE}+\mathrm{Plurality}\), \(\mathrm{DPRE}+\mathrm{Borda}\), \(\mathrm{DPRE}+\mathrm{Maximin}\), and \(\mathrm{DPRE}+\mathrm{STV}\), CONSTRUCTIVE-MANIPULATION is NP-complete. This is the deterministic-preround setting in which the schedule is known before voting, with unweighted voters, one manipulator, and all other votes given.

The reduction transfers exactly to rational masses. In the paper’s construction, each pair \(c_v^+,c_v^-\) is tied before the manipulator votes, so the manipulator’s ballot chooses which literal survives. After normalization, the controlled mass \(\alpha=1/(N+1)\) breaks precisely those ties, and the surviving literal candidates encode a satisfying assignment. Thus the continuous problem is expected to be NP-complete, with hardness in Class B: the combinatorics live in the candidate set and preround schedule, not in the number of named voters.

The relevant follow-up questions are whether the problem becomes FPT in the number of voter types, whether one can minimize the required controlling mass \(\alpha\), and what happens if the controlled mass may split across several reported rankings rather than submitting one common ballot.

A second anchor is Continuous RPRE Probability Manipulation. The instance is the same except that the preround schedule is drawn uniformly at random after the population’s rankings have been collected. For a reported ranking \(r\), let

\[ W(r)=\Pr[p\text{ wins after the random preround and }P]. \]

The optimization problem is to compute \(W^\star=\max_r W(r)\); a threshold version asks whether \(W^\star\ge q\) for a given rational \(q\).

The anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper: in each of \(\mathrm{RPRE}+\mathrm{Plurality}\), \(\mathrm{RPRE}+\mathrm{Borda}\), \(\mathrm{RPRE}+\mathrm{Maximin}\), and \(\mathrm{RPRE}+\mathrm{STV}\), CONSTRUCTIVE-MANIPULATION is \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard. The preceding reduction encodes a bipartite graph in pairwise candidate victories. The target wins exactly when the random candidate pairing represents a matching, so its winning probability is the number of matchings divided by the total number of possible pairings. The construction is robust to the manipulator’s additional vote, so exact probability evaluation remains \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard.

The same argument works with masses: the pairwise margins are simply normalized by the total population, while the random pairing distribution is unchanged. This is again Class B. The natural further questions concern exact versus approximate probability computation, possible FPRAS results, and whether nonuniform pairing distributions alter the complexity.

The third anchor is Continuous IPRE Contingent Manipulation. Here the paper’s interleaved preround and elicitation process is retained exactly. At each random scheduling step, the controlled bloc observes the revealed scheduling history and answers the next preference query. A strategy is a nonanticipating map from observed histories to answers that remain consistent with a complete reported ranking. The objective is to maximize the probability that \(p\) wins under \(P\); the decision question asks whether some such strategy achieves probability at least \(q\).

The anchor is Theorem 15, proved in this paper: for each of \(\mathrm{Plurality}\), \(\mathrm{Borda}\), \(\mathrm{Maximin}\), and \(\mathrm{STV}\), there is a reduction satisfying the required properties, and therefore CONSTRUCTIVE-MANIPULATION in each of \(\mathrm{IPRE}+\mathrm{Plurality}\), \(\mathrm{IPRE}+\mathrm{Borda}\), \(\mathrm{IPRE}+\mathrm{Maximin}\), and \(\mathrm{IPRE}+\mathrm{STV}\) is PSPACE-complete. The proof reduces from STOCHASTIC-SAT: random scheduling assigns the \(Y\)-variables, while the manipulator’s adaptive answers assign the \(X\)-variables.

Normalizing the votes to masses preserves every tie and every strict pairwise margin in the construction. Hence the continuous problem should also be PSPACE-complete. The hardness is not caused by population multiplicity: it lies in alternating random events and adaptive strategic responses. Further questions include strategy compression by type support, approximate winning probability, and whether allowing the controlled mass to divide among reports changes PSPACE-hardness.

These mirrors cover the paper’s central computational contribution: the same preround tweak yields NP-, \(\#\mathrm P\)-, and PSPACE-level manipulation problems under three timing regimes. They do not continuize the outcome space, the candidates, or the random schedule. The society itself is the continuous object.

The weakest point is that the paper’s literal problem has one manipulator, whereas the atomless model needs a positive-mass controlled bloc. If the programme insists that a continuous manipulator must be allowed to split mass fractionally among different reports, the reductions above may no longer survive: fractional reporting could convexify the literal choices. I would therefore present the common-report bloc as the primary mirror and make fractional coalition manipulation an explicit follow-up problem. It is still a defensible continuization because the paper’s setting explicitly concerns replicated software agents, and the normalized finite instances embed exactly into the mass model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has not actually continuized the paper’s manipulator. The paper’s actor is one named voter with a unit ballot and a private preference. In an atomless population, that voter has mass zero and cannot change any pairwise margin, plurality score, Borda score, Maximin score, or STV elimination. Literal continuous versions of all three problems therefore become trivial.

The proposed repair is to replace the manipulator by a positive-mass bloc \(\alpha\) whose members must submit one common ranking. That is not forced by high multiplicity. It changes the quantifier from “can one voter manipulate?” to “can a coordinated coalition of mass \(\alpha\) manipulate?” The choice of \(\alpha\) is exogenous, and common reporting is an additional coordination assumption. Repeating every voter \(R\) times does not solve this: it either repeats the manipulator too, turning one manipulator into \(R\) coordinated manipulators, or leaves the manipulator with vanishing mass.

This objection is strongest against the proponent’s claim that the reductions transfer “exactly.” If the controlled mass may split among rankings, the feasible reports become a distribution \(z\) over rankings with \(\sum_r z_r=\alpha\), not one ranking \(r\). That is a different coalition-control problem and can convexify the literal choices on which the reductions rely. If splitting is forbidden, the model has imposed a nontrivial agency assumption absent from the paper.

For Theorem 6, the issue is especially clear. The NP-hardness construction uses one manipulator to break each tied pair \(c_v^+,c_v^-\). A mass \(\alpha=1/(N+1)\) can reproduce that effect, but only because it is deliberately assigned exactly the weight of one finite ballot. The resulting object is a normalized finite election with a marked coalition, not a limiting population in which the original manipulator survives. If the coalition is allowed to act independently, the reduction no longer has the paper’s action space; if it is required to act identically, that requirement must be supplied by a software-module or coordination story external to the theorem.

Theorem 13 is weaker still as a population mirror. Its reduction is explicitly robust to any additional single vote: the manipulator cannot affect \(p\)’s winning probability. In the literal continuous model the manipulator has zero influence; in the normalized construction a bloc of exactly one-vote mass likewise has no influence. The \(\#\mathrm P\)-hardness comes entirely from counting random pairings of candidates. The society is merely a passive, normalized histogram encoding the pairwise candidate graph. A larger controlled bloc would produce a meaningful coalition problem, but that is no longer the paper’s manipulation problem and requires a new analysis of how mass is allowed to report.

The same problem is most pronounced for Theorem 15. The PSPACE reduction relies on one manipulator maintaining a single coherent ranking while answering successive queries. A positive-mass continuum has no canonical analogue of that information state. Requiring the whole bloc to follow one contingent ranking policy creates a coordinated strategic coalition. Allowing different infinitesimal agents to answer differently produces aggregate pairwise responses that need not correspond to any complete ranking, so it requires a new fractional elicitation model. Either choice changes the strategic object whose PSPACE-completeness the paper proves.

These objections do not rely on the invalid claim that the continuous answer is easy or unchanged. They concern the semantics of agency and identity: preserving the paper’s individual manipulator makes its influence disappear, while preserving influence requires adding a controlled mass and a coordination rule. The candidate-level hardness and the random schedule are not themselves objections.

Unfortunately, this does not establish the requested universal negative. The paper’s software-agent motivation gives a plausible rescue: a large deployment may contain many copies of one voter module, all executing the same contingent policy. Under that interpretation, a positive-mass controlled type is a legitimate high-multiplicity object, and the normalized reductions really do embed into it. The programme explicitly permits such population-level co-modifications. Thus the negative case can reject the proponent’s claim that these are canonical continuizations of the paper’s one-voter problems, but it cannot honestly show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any plausible scenario. At least the DPRE mirror remains a defensible Class B candidate.
