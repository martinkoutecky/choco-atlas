The continuous mirror question

For each \(N\), let a society be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many locations \(x\in[0,1]\), with \(N\mu_x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\). Among deterministic rule families \(F_N\) that choose one facility location and remain strategyproof when a resident reports \(r\) instead of \(x\), changing the society to \(\mu+N^{-1}(\delta_r-\delta_x)\), determine the limiting Pareto frontier of worst-case approximation ratios for maximum distance and minimum utility as \(N\to\infty\). Does the pair \((2,3/2)\) remain optimal?

The model it lives in

The input is \((N,\mu)\), where \(\mu\) gives the shares of residents at each supported location and \(N\mu_x\) is integral. A rule \(F_N\) selects a facility \(y\in[0,1]\); each resident’s utility is \(1-|x-y|\), and strategyproofness is tested against a unilateral mass change of \(1/N\). The task is to characterize the limiting best approximation pairs for maximum distance and minimum utility.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s deterministic single-facility guarantees for maximum distance and minimum utility. It leaves the happiness result, randomized mechanisms, and results for multiple facilities aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the optimal deterministic strategyproof approximation ratios converge to \((2,3/2)\), or can they improve as the individual mass \(1/N\) vanishes?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Theorem 1, proved in this paper, gives a useful named anchor. It says the deterministic MIDORNEAREST mechanism \(3/2\)-approximates the optimal minimum utility and \(2\)-approximates the optimal maximum distance; no deterministic strategyproof mechanism has a smaller approximation ratio for either objective. There is an important qualification: the paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or otherwise. Theorem 1 is an approximation-mechanism result, not a complexity bound. If only complexity-class results qualify as anchors, this paper has none.

A plausible mirror is High-Multiplicity Deterministic Facility Mechanism Design on the Line. Consider a town with many residents concentrated in \(\tau\) residential locations. A type is a resident’s ideal location \(x\in[0,1]\); residents at the same location have identical distance costs and utilities. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many such locations, with \(\mu_x\) the share of residents at \(x\). For population size \(N\), require \(N\mu_x\) to be integral, so one resident has mass \(1/N\).

The problem is to design, for each \(N\), a deterministic rule \(F_N\) that takes \(\mu\) and returns a facility location \(y\in[0,1]\). Preserve the paper’s individual strategyproofness: if a resident at \(x\) reports \(r\), the input changes to \(\mu'=\mu+\frac1N(\delta_r-\delta_x)\), and that resident’s distance to \(F_N(\mu)\) must be no greater than their distance to \(F_N(\mu')\). For each society, let \(a\) and \(b\) be its extreme supported locations. The optimum maximum distance is \((b-a)/2\); the optimum minimum utility is \(1-(b-a)/2\). Ask for the Pareto frontier of worst-case approximation pairs for such strategyproof rule families as \(N\) grows. A solution is a rule family and a proof of its limiting guarantees.

The continuous rule suggested by MIDORNEAREST has a simple description: return \(1/2\) if \(a\le 1/2\le b\); return \(b\) if everyone is left of \(1/2\); and return \(a\) if everyone is right of \(1/2\). It can be computed by scanning the \(\tau\) supported types. This gives a concrete Class A direction for the constructive side, with the candidate pair \((2,3/2)\). The paper’s theorem supports that candidate in finite electorates, but does not establish that the pair is optimal in the large-population limit.

The weakest point is the strategic limit. The definition above preserves incentives by checking each resident’s \(1/N\)-mass report change before taking \(N\) large. If we instead use a literal atomless population, an individual has zero influence on \(\mu\), and strategyproofness may become vacuous. The main further question is whether the finite-population lower bounds survive the high-multiplicity limit, where an individual’s share vanishes. This case covers only Theorem 1’s deterministic, single-facility results; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s randomized or multiple-facility results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no numbered result about the computational complexity of a problem. Theorem 1 compares approximation guarantees over the class of deterministic strategyproof mechanisms. It does not analyze the time needed to solve a computational task on a society. A mechanism is an algorithm in the broad sense, but here the result is about which rule is truthful and how well it serves agents—not whether a problem admits an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm.

The proponent’s town example is plausible: many residents can share an ideal location, and the society can be represented by masses over locations. The proposed Pareto-frontier question is also coherent. But it asks which maps \(F_N\) satisfy incentive and approximation conditions, with individual deviations changing the distribution by \(1/N\). That is a characterization of strategyproof rules. If individuals instead have zero mass in a literal continuum, their reports do not change the distribution and individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Keeping \(N\)-sized deviations avoids that problem, but makes the question a limit study of finite-agent mechanisms, not a new computational problem on a continuous society.

Nor does the paper’s model suggest a harder computational mirror. For its single-facility result, the guarantee depends on the extreme locations; the MIDORNEAREST rule can be evaluated from those extremes. A weighted input over \(\tau\) supported locations does not create the pricing problem or algorithmic bottleneck that motivates the ChoCo programme. One could introduce a new task—such as computing a facility after bribery or designing mechanisms on more complex metrics—but that would be an added problem, not a continuous version of Theorem 1.

So the negative case is not that continuous populations are implausible, or that a weighted extension cannot be studied. It is that this paper’s named results concern the quality and truthfulness of rules, while the programme is charting computational complexity. The proposed mirror may be worthwhile mechanism-design work; the paper gives no strong anchor for computational continuization.
