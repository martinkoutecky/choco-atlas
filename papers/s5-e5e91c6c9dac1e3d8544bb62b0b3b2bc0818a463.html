The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), community labels \(g(t)\), nonnegative rational type-to-type rates \(\beta_{tu}\), a rational per-capita seed budget \(\kappa\), and rational \(0<\alpha<1\), choose seed masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\). Define \(a_t^0=y_t^0=x_t\); for each round \(r\), let \(y_u^{r+1}=(\mu_u-a_u^r)(1-\exp(-\sum_t\beta_{tu}y_t^r))\) and \(a_u^{r+1}=a_u^r+y_u^{r+1}\), and let \(a_t^\infty=\lim_{r\to\infty}a_t^r\). For each community \(c\), set \(\mu_c=\sum_{t:g(t)=c}\mu_t\) and \(W_\alpha(x)=\sum_c\mu_c(\sum_{t:g(t)=c}a_t^\infty/\mu_c)^\alpha\). Is there an algorithm, polynomial in the input size and \(1/\epsilon\) and independent of population size, that returns a rational feasible \(x\) with \(W_\alpha(x)\ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\sup_{x'}W_\alpha(x')\)?

The model it lives in

An exchangeable typed IC population: type masses \(\mu_t\), community labels \(g(t)\), and rates \(\beta_{tu}\) define the limiting diffusion; decision variables \(x_t\) allocate seed mass under per-capita budget \(\kappa\), and the objective is community-weighted welfare \(W_\alpha(x)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror asks for a continuous analogue of Theorem 1's approximation result; it does not claim that the paper's RR-set estimator, sampling analysis, or experiments already extend to the population limit.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(W_\alpha\) retain a continuous diminishing-returns property that supports a \(1-1/e\) approximation?
  • Can the limiting diffusion objective and the needed optimization oracles be evaluated to sufficient accuracy in time polynomial in the input size and \(1/\epsilon\), including near critical rates?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, under the Independent Cascade model, given a directed graph \(G\), a disjoint community structure, a seed budget \(k\), and \(0<\alpha<1\), FIMM returns a seed set \(S\) with
\[ F_\alpha(S)\ge (1-1/e-\epsilon)F_\alpha(S^\star) \]
with probability at least \(1-1/n_G^\ell\). Here \(F_\alpha(S)=\sum_c n_c u_c(S)^\alpha\), and \(S^\star\) is optimal. The guarantee uses a sufficiently large number of RR sets: the paper’s bound is \(\theta\ge C\max(\theta_1,\theta_2)\), with \(\theta_1,\theta_2\) depending on the accuracy and confidence parameters, the Taylor truncation parameter \(Q\), and how close the relevant community utilities are to \(1\). The theorem’s hypotheses include \(Q\ge2\), and the paper gives an explicit parameter setting for \(\theta\). The cited NP-hardness of ordinary influence maximization is not a numbered result of this paper, so I would not use it as an anchor.

The lead mirror is Block-IC Welfare Seed-Mass Maximization. An instance has a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a community label \(g(t)\) for each type, nonnegative rational contact rates \(\beta_{tu}\), a per-capita seed budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), and rational \(0<\alpha<1\). Let \(\mu_c=\sum_{t:g(t)=c}\mu_t\). A decision is a seed-mass vector \(x\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\).

The rates define a population limit of an IC block network: with \(N\) agents, each directed edge from type \(t\) to type \(u\) is independently live with probability \(\beta_{tu}/N\), for sufficiently large \(N\). If \(a_t^{(r)}\) is the active mass of type \(t\) after round \(r\), start with \(a_t^{(0)}=x_t\) and iterate
\[ a_u^{(r+1)} = a_u^{(r)}+ \bigl(\mu_u-a_u^{(r)}\bigr) \left(1-\exp\left(-\sum_t\beta_{tu}a_t^{(r)}\right)\right). \]
Write \(a_t^\infty=\lim_{r\to\infty}a_t^{(r)}\), \(a_c^\infty=\sum_{t:g(t)=c}a_t^\infty\), and \(u_c(x)=a_c^\infty/\mu_c\). The objective is the same normalized welfare as in the paper:
\[ W_\alpha(x)=\sum_c\mu_c\,u_c(x)^\alpha. \]
The computational question is: given this finite rational instance and \(\epsilon>0\), can we return a rational feasible \(x\) with \(W_\alpha(x)\ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\sup_{x'}W_\alpha(x')\), in time polynomial in the type-description size, input bit length, and \(1/\epsilon\), independent of \(N\)? The solution is the seed mass assigned to each type.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for public-health outreach: millions of residents belong to a modest number of community-and-role groups, such as neighborhoods crossed with trusted-messenger roles. People of one type share the same community and the same type-to-type contact rates; the intervention budget is naturally expressed as a fraction of the population. Thus \(\tau=|T|\) can be small compared with \(N\), and the decision is about mass rather than selecting residents at one-person granularity. The IC edge probabilities scale with population size so that the continuum retains nontrivial propagation. That scaling is a co-modification needed to make the population limit meaningful. The mirror keeps the paper’s central question—how to choose peer-leaders to maximize community-weighted welfare under diffusion—and its authors should recognize it as a mean-field version of their problem.

I would expect this mirror’s approximation question to be Class A, especially for fixed or modest \(\tau\): the paper’s welfare objective and diminishing-returns rationale suggest that continuous optimization could support a comparable approximation. That is a research expectation, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The further questions are whether \(W_\alpha\) retains the required continuous diminishing-returns property, whether a type-level algorithm can match the finite RR-sampling guarantee, and how its running time depends on rates and community utilities.

The weakest point is the change from an arbitrary finite graph to an exchangeable block network. Theorem 1’s guarantee is proved for finite graphs using RR sets; it does not establish a guarantee for this mean-field objective, and its sampling bound can deteriorate when community utilities approach \(1\). Also, many real networks have distinctive local structures that a modest type set cannot capture. That limits the mirror to a particular, credible instance regime. It does not erase the match between the paper’s seed-mass decision, community welfare objective, and large-population diffusion question. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s welfare fair influence maximization result; it does not claim that the paper’s experiments or its estimator analyses are themselves continuous-population results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it gives an approximation guarantee for welfare-fair seed selection under IC diffusion. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes the network and the decision regime along with the population. In the paper, seeds are chosen from a known graph, and two people in the same community can have very different influence because their positions in that graph differ. Replacing the graph with type-to-type contact rates removes those differences. If types are refined to preserve them, their number may grow with the population, defeating the proposed compression. A fixed seed budget also gives zero seed mass in the limit; scaling the budget with population avoids that, but asks about per-capita intervention instead.

There is a concrete issue with the proposed dynamics, too. Its recurrence lets all previously activated people try to influence others again, whereas IC lets each person make one attempt. A block-model limit of IC would track newly activated mass \(y_t^r\) separately from cumulative active mass \(a_t^r\), for example:
\[ y_u^{r+1}=(\mu_u-a_u^r)\left(1-\exp\left(-\sum_t\beta_{tu}y_t^r\right)\right), \qquad a_u^{r+1}=a_u^r+y_u^{r+1}. \]
That correction is feasible, but it makes clear that the continuous problem is an optimization problem for a particular exchangeable network model, not a direct limit of the paper’s explicit-graph instances.

These objections narrow the mirror, but they do not defeat its best version. Large outreach campaigns with a per-capita budget and approximately homogeneous mixing are plausible, and maximizing the same community welfare by allocating seed mass across types remains recognizably related to the paper’s problem. Under the programme’s standard, a simplified but sensible instance regime can be enough. So the negative case is weak: I can challenge the proposed formulation and its reach, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 1.
