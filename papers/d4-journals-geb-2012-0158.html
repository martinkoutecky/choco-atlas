The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper offers a natural *starting point* for a population-continuum project. It does not, however, contain a named computational result that can anchor a mirror under the programme’s criteria.

The closest result is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that when there are finitely many agents and alternatives, each agent’s valuation vector ranges over an \(m\)-dimensional open interval, and the social choice function is neutral, the function is dominant-strategy implementable if and only if it always selects an alternative maximizing \(\sum_i \lambda_i t_i^a\) for some fixed nonnegative, nonzero weights \(\lambda\). That is a characterization of implementable rules, not a result about running time, complexity, or algorithms. Theorem 4, also proved here, says that adding anonymity on the stated \([0,H)^{m\times n}\) domain forces an implementable neutral rule to be efficient. It is likewise not a computational result. The other Roberts-theorem statements are cited results or structural characterizations, not complexity classifications.

A plausible adjacent setting would be a city choosing among public projects for a large population with recurring valuation types. Let \(t\) be a complete valuation type and \(\mu_t\) its population share. One could ask whether neutral implementable choices must maximize an aggregate such as \(\sum_t \mu_t w_t v_t(a)\), with any relevant per-capita welfare weight included in the type. That has a recognizable connection to Theorem 3, but it does not yet give a faithful continuous problem: an individual in an atomless population cannot change \(\mu\) by misreporting, so the paper’s unilateral incentive constraints no longer do the same work. A coalition or positive-mass deviation model would need to be specified, and that would change the underlying question.

The paper’s “continuous domain” is continuity in the values reported by a *finite* set of named agents; the population itself remains finite. So I would not count Theorem 3 or Theorem 4 as anchors, or invent a computational question to attach to them. The positive case stops at a promising neighboring question; there are no qualifying named computational anchors in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 3 characterizes which neutral social choice functions are implementable on an open valuation domain; Theorem 4 characterizes the anonymous case; Theorem 5 gives Roberts’ affine-maximizer characterization. The other propositions support those structural results. None states a complexity bound, gives an algorithm, or formulates a computational problem. Theorem 3 is the closest anchor, but its “continuous” domain is a set of valuation vectors for a fixed set of \(n\) named agents. The society itself remains finite.

The strongest proposed mirror would put residents into valuation types with masses \(\mu_t\), then ask whether neutral implementable choices maximize an aggregate such as \(\sum_t \mu_t w_t v_t(a)\). That is a coherent neighboring question, but it does not preserve the theorem’s incentive constraints. In an atomless society, one person’s report changes the distribution by zero, so a rule based only on \(\mu\) cannot respond to an individual deviation. Strategyproofness then loses the force it has in the paper. Letting a positive-mass group change its report restores influence only by replacing individual dominant-strategy incentives with a coalition incentive model; for a group whose members have different valuations, even its shared objective needs new specification.

One could instead keep finite electorates with many repeated valuation types. That preserves individual deviations, but it is a sequence of finite-agent models, not an atomless population. And it still does not mirror a computational result from this paper: the characterization says what the implementable rules look like, not how hard they are to compute. For the proposed city-planning instance with finitely many alternatives and an explicitly listed support, aggregating welfare and selecting a maximizer is direct. A harder algorithmic task would need additional structure absent from the paper.

Theorem 4 does not supply a stronger anchor. Its efficient rule would translate naturally into maximizing aggregate welfare, but that is again a structural statement, and its finite-alternative calculation is straightforward from a finite support. Theorem 5’s alternative-specific constants can likewise be carried into an aggregate objective; that changes the formula, not the paper’s contribution into a complexity result.

I cannot establish that every future computational question inspired by mechanism design is unworthy. One could study the complexity of recognizing implementability from a chosen representation of a rule, or design a mass-market incentive model. But the paper specifies no representation or computational task for those questions, and either would be new research attached to the paper rather than a continuous-population mirror of one of its results. The negative case is therefore strong under ChoCo’s scope, though it rests on that scope boundary rather than proving that no worthwhile continuous mechanism-design project could be built from these ideas.
