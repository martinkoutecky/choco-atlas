The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), group labels, rational contact rates \(\lambda_{tu}\), a seed share \(\kappa\), and accuracy \(\delta>0\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\) to return a \(\delta\)-additive approximation to \(\max_x \min_g \sum_{t\in g} z_t(x)/\mu_g\), where \(\mu_g=\sum_{t\in g}\mu_t\) and \(z(x)\) is the least fixed point reached from \(x\) under \(z_u=x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)(1-\exp(-\sum_t\lambda_{tu}z_t/\mu_u))\). What is the computational complexity in the number of groups \(m\), the number of supported types \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\)?

The model it lives in

A typed mean-field influence model with a finite support of agent profiles, rational population masses \(\mu_t\), group membership, and contact rates \(\lambda_{tu}\). The decision variable is seed mass \(x_t\); the objective is to maximize the minimum group influenced fraction at the model’s final fixed point.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the maximin seeding objective and the arbitrary-connectivity and disconnected-group regimes represented by Theorems 1 and 2. It leaves the paper’s graph-specific AGM procedures, fixed-network experiments, and empirical comparisons without a continuous analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Typed-IC Maximin Mass Seeding admit a polynomial-time \(\delta\)-additive approximation in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\)?
  • Which restrictions on the contact-rate matrix, especially zero cross-group rates, yield concavity or another tractable structure for the group response curves?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness or other hardness result. Its strongest computational anchors are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. Theorem 1 is the broader anchor: for any group structure, if \(S^\star\) is an optimal Maximin Fairness seed set and AGM-US returns \(S\) with \(|S|=k\), then
\[ \Phi(S)\ge \left(\frac{1}{m}-\xi\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{e}-\varepsilon\right)\Phi(S^\star), \qquad \xi=\frac{k\bmod m}{km}, \]
where \(m\) is the number of groups and \(\varepsilon\) is the IMM approximation parameter. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that if the groups are completely disconnected, \(\rho(G,C)=0\), then AGM-GS returns a seed set satisfying
\[ \Phi(S)\ge \left(1-\frac{1}{e}-\varepsilon\right)\Phi(S^\star). \]
The first is my lead: it covers interacting groups, the setting in which the maximin objective is hardest to optimize. The second gives a sharper, focused question for a naturally decoupled regime.

A credible continuous setting is a large public-health or information campaign in which the budget is a share of the population. Let \(T\) be a finite set of agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type specifies an agent’s protected group and complete influence profile: agents of type \(t\) have the same successful-contact rates to every type \(u\), and contacts to \(u\) are sampled uniformly from that type. Let \(\lambda_{tu}\) be the rational expected number of successful contacts an activated type-\(t\) agent makes to type \(u\). There may be millions of agents but only a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of recurring demographic and network-role profiles. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: agents within a type are interchangeable for the model’s purposes.

The action is a seed mass allocation \(x_t\), with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\), where \(\kappa\) is the fraction of the population the campaign can seed. Write \(z_t(x)\) for the final influenced mass of type \(t\). In the typed mean-field IC model, it is the least fixed point reachable by iteration from \(x\) of
\[ z_u=x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_{tu}z_t}{\mu_u}\right)\right). \]
For group \(g\), let \(\mu_g=\sum_{t\in g}\mu_t\) and \(u_g(x)=\sum_{t\in g}z_t(x)/\mu_g\). The objective is \(\Phi(x)=\min_g u_g(x)\).

This defines Typed-IC Maximin Mass Seeding: given the type masses, group map, contact-rate matrix, seed share \(\kappa\), and accuracy \(\delta>0\), return a feasible allocation \(x\) with \(\Phi(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\delta\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_x\Phi(x)\). Theorem 1’s mirror is this problem with arbitrary cross-group rates. Its computational question is whether one can optimize the worst-off group efficiently when society is represented by a small number of types, and whether allowing fractional seed mass removes the discrete rounding loss represented by \(\xi\). The paper’s \(1/m\)-scale AGM-US guarantee motivates that question; I would not claim that its guarantee carries over, since the mean-field influence function and the algorithm’s discrete candidate lists are different.

For Theorem 2, take the same instance with \(\lambda_{tu}=0\) whenever \(t\) and \(u\) belong to different groups. The resulting Disconnected Typed-IC Maximin Mass Seeding problem asks for the same optimal mass allocation under that restriction. The paper’s \((1-1/e-\varepsilon)\) guarantee is the anchor, not a promised continuous guarantee. My working prediction is that this restriction is Class A: without cross-group effects, one can seek groupwise cost-to-reach curves and allocate mass to balance the groups. The paper’s Lemma 1, which proves that each group’s influence spread is submodular under IC, gives a reason to investigate that route. Establishing the needed concavity or another usable structure is itself part of the question.

For arbitrary cross-group rates, I would expect a harder landscape, possibly Class C. The maximin objective couples the groups, and the typed final-size equations are nonlinear. There is no hardness result in this paper from which to claim that discrete hardness transfers. The central follow-up is whether the arbitrary-interaction problem has a useful approximation or parameterized algorithm in \(m,\tau\), and input bit length, and where its boundary lies as the rate matrix becomes more structured.

This mirror covers the maximin influence objective and the two approximation regimes in Theorems 1 and 2. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s particular AGM procedures or its empirical results on fixed graphs. The continuous object is the population and its seed allocation; the typed contact process is the co-modified influence model needed to make that population limit meaningful. Mean-field dynamics provide modeling machinery here. The computational question remains the choice of intervention that maximizes the least group’s influenced share.

The weak point is the network abstraction. In the paper’s graphs, overlaps among particular nodes’ reachable sets matter; a type-level contact matrix can erase that topology. If the only types are demographic labels, agents may not be interchangeable in the sense required by high multiplicity. Types therefore need to include network role as well as group, and that can make \(\tau\) grow until the model loses its advantage. The case survives in regimes where large populations really do have a bounded menu of recurring group-and-contact profiles and campaigns choose a population share. That is a plausible regime for scaled outreach, though it is not a faithful mirror of every finite network instance the paper studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does have computational results to mirror: Theorems 1 and 2 give approximation guarantees for algorithms. So the negative case cannot rest on the absence of named algorithmic results, and existing high multiplicity work would support a mirror rather than undermine one.

The strongest objection to Theorem 1 is that the typed model preserves the population scale by changing what determines influence. In the paper, a seed’s value depends on its position in a particular graph: paths, bottlenecks, and overlap among reachable sets all matter. A matrix of expected contact rates between types does not preserve that information. If types are refined to capture each node’s full network role, their number can grow with the population, removing the proposed high multiplicity advantage. Keeping a small type set instead requires an exchangeable, random contact model and a mean field approximation. That gives a coherent maximin seeding problem, but it is a problem about choosing seed rates in a typed contagion model, not a continuous version of the paper’s arbitrary graph problem or its AGM guarantee.

That is a real limit on the mirror’s fidelity, but it does not defeat the better version of the case. A large campaign in a population with a small set of recurring contact profiles is plausible, and the resulting optimization is still computational. The programme explicitly allows co-modifications required by a population continuum. Mean field influence optimization is also an adjacent research direction, while some work called “continuous influence maximization” instead varies node-level marketing strategies on a finite graph; neither fact establishes that this maximin mass-seeding question is already answered. ([Microsoft Research’s continuous influence maximization work](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/gradient-method-for-continuous-influence-maximization-with-budget-saving-considerations/), [a mean field treatment of influence optimization in voting dynamics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8213046/))

Theorem 2 is a narrower target. Disconnected groups are a natural restriction for a typed population model, but the theorem’s guarantee concerns coordinating discrete, ordered seed lists under a shared cardinality budget. With seed mass, that coordination becomes allocation across group response curves. The proof’s particular mechanism does not carry over; a new analysis would be needed for the mass-allocation problem. Still, that does not make the new question incoherent or uninteresting: groupwise contagion responses under a shared campaign budget are a recognizable optimization problem.

So the best negative case is that neither theorem transfers its computational substance directly: preserving arbitrary graph structure undermines compression, while obtaining a compact continuum model changes the diffusion assumptions and the algorithmic problem. But the proponent’s typed mean field formulation is sufficiently plausible that this does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The negative case is strongest against calling it a mirror of these particular guarantees; it is weak against treating it as a worthwhile continuous-population problem in its own right.
