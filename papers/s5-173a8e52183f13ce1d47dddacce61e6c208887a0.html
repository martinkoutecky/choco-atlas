The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor set is empty. The paper numbers Definitions 2.1–2.4, but contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its findings—such as the counts of elections exhibiting each paradox and the two case studies—are empirical results, not named complexity anchors. I can’t honestly restate an anchor or assign one a tractability class.

The strongest positive mirror I see is a computational question about the paper’s upward monotonicity paradox: can raising a winning candidate on some ballots make that candidate lose? A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large union or municipal STV election with a fixed candidate slate and many voters sharing recurring rankings. A type is a complete ballot, including any truncation; its mass is the fraction of voters casting it. The regime would have \(n\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported ballot types. For the question below, voters of the same type are interchangeable because the model uses only their ballots and the amount of mass moved.

One precise candidate is Continuous Upward-Paradox Detection for STV. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a seat count \(S\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) ballot types, a candidate \(x\) who wins under the specified continuous STV rule, and a rational budget \(b\). Normalize total mass to \(1\); use quota \(1/(S+1)\), transfer surplus ballot mass proportionally, eliminate the lowest-count continuing candidate when nobody exceeds quota, and fix a deterministic tie-breaking order. The quota and fractional transfers are part of the continuous population model. The question is whether there is a mass transfer \(y_{t,t'}\) totaling at most \(b\), from ballots containing \(x\) only to ballots obtained by moving \(x\) higher while preserving the other candidates’ relative order, such that \(x\) is no longer a winner. A solution is the transfer itself, together with the resulting winner set. The budget gives a per-capita measure of the campaign shift; varying \(b\) asks for the least mass needed to create the paradox.

This is a close analogue of the paper’s upward-paradox question: the ballot change, the target candidate, and the elimination-and-transfer mechanism remain central. Large electorates make mass a natural action unit, and a continuum retains the possibility that a small share shift changes the order of rounds. I would tentatively expect this detection problem to be continuum-specifically hard: its outcome depends on a sequence of eliminations and elections, while fractional mass shifts can alter that sequence. That is a research guess, not a result established by this paper. Natural follow-ups include whether the problem is tractable in \(m,\tau,L\), whether it is fixed-parameter tractable in \(m\) or \(S\), and how sensitive it is to quota and tie conventions.

This mirror covers upward-monotonicity paradoxes only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s committee-size or no-show findings. Its weakest point is also decisive for the requested anchoring standard: the paper supplies finite examples and empirical counts, but no named computational result to mirror. The proposed question is a credible next computational question suggested by the article, not an anchor-compliant continuous mirror of one of its stated complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a qualifying mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its numbered statements are paradox definitions; its main findings are empirical counts and case studies of particular Scottish elections. The authors searched 1,079 profiles, but the paper does not state a complexity problem or establish an algorithmic result. So the proponent’s proposed detection problem is a new research question prompted by the paper, not a continuous version of one of its computational results.

That distinction does not make the proposed question ill-posed. Ballot rankings are sensible types, and large elections with many voters sharing ballots are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. One can define a continuous STV variant and ask for the least mass that must be shifted to make a winner lose. I also cannot rule it out by pointing to quota conventions, ties, or the finite examples: those can be handled in a model the authors would recognize.

A stronger version could ask for the minimum mass change that creates any of the paper’s paradoxes, including committee-size or no-show paradoxes. That would be a coherent computational agenda, but it would upgrade empirical observations into new optimization problems. The paper gives no complexity result whose continuous behavior those problems would explain.

So I would reject the proposal as an *anchor-compliant mirror* of this paper. I would not claim the broader statement that no continuous computational project here could be worthwhile: the upward-paradox problem is plausible, and the paper’s ballot data make its population regime credible. The negative case is strong on fidelity to the paper’s actual contributions, but weak as a universal claim about research value.
