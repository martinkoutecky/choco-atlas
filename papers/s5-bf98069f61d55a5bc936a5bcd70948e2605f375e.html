What the mirror covers

The proposed LP reflects MAUI’s total-utility objective, but it is not anchored in a qualifying numbered result and leaves out the evaluated policies’ allocation dynamics and user-state changes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is limited: the supplied paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, and no formal complexity classification. Its computational evidence is empirical: it compares policies in simulation and reports measured running times. So the set of qualifying anchors is empty; there is no named result here that I can restate as the required basis for a mirror.

A plausible mirror of one underlying problem is High-Multiplicity Maximum-Utility Radio Allocation, reflecting MAUI’s stated goal of maximizing total user utility. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete active-user types, a mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types, and a per-capita resource supply \(b\). A type records the service and current transmission state relevant to allocation, including its utility \(U_t(r)\) for receiving \(r\) timeslots, where \(r\) remains an integer from \(0\) to at most \(8\). Choose masses \(x_{t,r}\geq 0\), where \(x_{t,r}\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\) assigned \(r\) timeslots, to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r=0}^{c_t} U_t(r)x_{t,r} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{r=0}^{c_t}x_{t,r}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r=0}^{c_t}r x_{t,r}\leq b. \]
Here \(c_t\leq 8\) is the type’s maximum useful request. Each person still receives an integral number of timeslots; only the mass of people receiving each bundle is continuous. Service priority can resolve ties between utility-maximizing allocations.

The credible regime would be many simultaneous sessions sharing a bounded set of service and radio-state types, with capacity scaling to keep resources per user roughly fixed: \(n\gg |T|\). The paper’s actual simulations use 16 users per cell in the reference scenario and model changing session and channel states, so this is an extrapolated regime, not the paper’s own. Under the stated finite-type model, the mirror is a linear program with \(O(8|T|)\) allocation variables and is tractable. The authors could recognize its objective from MAUI, but this covers that objective only; it does not establish a continuous version of the DCEAM/DCELM fairness policies or their simulation findings.

The weak point is that this is a plausible model of an objective the paper studies, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result. The paper’s contribution is principally its bankruptcy-based policy designs and their simulated behavior, while the LP above abstracts a static allocation round. I would offer it as a candidate question for the programme, but not as satisfying the requested anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is the anchor. The paper gives named procedures—DCEAM, DCELM, MAUI and RATE—and reports simulation results and measured runtimes. But it states no numbered complexity, approximation, or algorithmic theorem. The proponent is therefore right that there is no qualifying named computational result to mirror.

MAUI is the best rescue attempt, but the proposed LP is a new benchmark problem, not a continuous version of a result the paper establishes. MAUI greedily assigns each slot to the user with the largest marginal utility increase; the paper explicitly says this need not maximize total utility across all possible allocations. The LP instead finds a static, globally optimal distribution. It leaves out the repeated arrivals and completions, the special treatment of active video users, waiting-time priorities, and the way a user’s transmission state affects later allocations. Those dynamics matter to the policy the paper evaluates.

A more faithful mirror would be a finite-horizon fluid RRM problem: track masses by service, remaining work, deadline, channel state and waiting time; allocate integral slot bundles to those masses; and model how allocations change the state distribution over time. That could be a worthwhile research project. But its state dynamics, arrival model, and capacity scaling would need to be specified; the paper does not define or analyze that computational problem. It is a follow-up inspired by the paper, not a mirror of a named computational result.

The resource scale sharpens the gap. Keeping the paper’s per-cell capacity at eight timeslots while the user population grows makes per-capita supply vanish. Scaling capacity with population yields a different system; replicating the original cells gives repeated small allocation problems; pooling those cells’ resources adds a new architecture.

So the case against is strong under ChoCo’s named-anchor requirement, but weaker as the literal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any scenario. The static LP is a credible research lead, and a dynamic fluid model may be too. Neither supplies the missing paper-specific computational anchor.
