The continuous mirror question

Given a resource set \(R\), a population scale \(N\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and each type’s feasible allocation family \(\mathcal S_t\subseteq 2^R\), choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\). For linear resource costs \(c_r(y)=a_ry+b_r\), where \(a_r,b_r\ge 0\), set \(y_r=N\sum_{t,S:r\in S}x_{t,S}\) and minimize \(\sum_{r\in R}y_rc_r(y_r)\). Determine the complexity of computing a feasible allocation with cost at most \((1+\epsilon)\) times optimum, as a function of the encoded instance, \(\tau\), the input bit length, \(\log N\), \(1/\epsilon\), and the complexity of optimizing over implicitly represented \(\mathcal S_t\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete commodity types, each with its own feasible integral resource bundles, assigns fractions of each type’s mass to those bundles. Resource loads are \(y_r=N\sum_{t,S:r\in S}x_{t,S}\), and the objective is the original total congestion cost \(\sum_r y_rc_r(y_r)\). In repeated source-destination network regimes, route families can be represented by flow variables and priced using shortest paths.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the resource-allocation minimization problem underlying Theorem 1.15. It does not mirror the thesis’s locality-gap analysis, local-search algorithm, or results in Chapters 2 and 3.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which implicitly represented feasible-allocation families can the continuous optimum be computed in polynomial time using a pricing or separation oracle?
  • When \(N\mu_t\) is integral for each type, what rounding guarantees relate a continuous optimum to a discrete allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is Chapter 1’s resource allocation problem. I would mirror its underlying cost minimization, rather than try to continuize the local search dynamics.

The anchor is Theorem 1.15, proved in this thesis by reduction from MAXCUT. It states that, unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time \(\alpha\)-approximation exists for unweighted resource allocation with linear resource cost functions when \(\alpha<52/51\). In the reduction, each graph vertex becomes a commodity with two feasible allocations, and each graph edge becomes two resources; a resource’s cost is its load. An approximation better than \(52/51\) would yield a MAXCUT approximation better than \(16/17\), contradicting the cited MAXCUT hardness. The resource-allocation theorem is the authors’ result; the MAXCUT hardness it invokes is prior work.

Here is a continuous counterpart I would hand to a prover. Call it Continuous Population Resource Allocation. An instance has a resource set \(R\), a population scale \(N\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A type completely specifies an unweighted commodity’s feasible allocation family \(\mathcal S_t\subseteq 2^R\): two agents share a type only if they have the same feasible choices and all other problem-relevant attributes. Each resource \(r\) has cost \(c_r(y)=a_r y+b_r\), where \(a_r,b_r\ge 0\).

For each type \(t\) and feasible allocation \(S\in\mathcal S_t\), choose a mass \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\). The load on resource \(r\) is \(y_r=N\sum_{t,S:\,r\in S}x_{t,S}\), and the objective is to minimize
\[ \sum_{r\in R} y_r c_r(y_r) =\sum_{r\in R}(a_r y_r^2+b_r y_r). \]
A solution is the mass assigned to each feasible allocation, together with its objective value. Each individual still receives one integral allocation; \(x_{t,S}\) records what fraction of a type’s population takes each choice. When \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), this is the fractional high-multiplicity relaxation of the discrete instance with \(n_t\) commodities of type \(t\).

A plausible regime is a large packet-routing or commuter population with many repeated source-destination pairs and the same route eligibility within each pair: \(N\) is large while \(\tau\), the number of distinct pairs and service classes, is comparatively small. Resources are network links, and feasible allocations are routes. This is a credible regime for the thesis’s congestion model, and the type definition preserves the complete information relevant to routing.

For networks, I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The objective is convex quadratic and the flow constraints are linear; path choices can be represented by flow variables, with shortest-path pricing when paths are implicit. With explicit feasible allocation lists, it is a convex quadratic program. The natural complexity question is whether one can compute an \(\epsilon\)-optimal mass allocation in time polynomial in the network or resource description, \(\tau\), the rational input bit length \(L\), and the requested precision. For general succinctly described families \(\mathcal S_t\), the boundary question is how the complexity of pricing—finding a feasible allocation of minimum weight under resource prices—controls the continuous problem. This mirror therefore asks whether the discrete approximation hardness survives divisibility; it does not assume that Theorem 1.15’s threshold transfers.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it retains their resources, feasible allocations, congestion costs, and total-cost objective. Their discussion of fractional resource sharing as the splittable counterpart to their model also supports the connection. The mirror covers Theorem 1.15’s underlying minimization problem, not the thesis’s locality-gap analysis or its specific local search algorithm. Natural follow-up questions are whether a continuous optimum can be rounded with useful additive guarantees for finite populations, and which compactly represented allocation families make pricing—and hence continuous optimization—hard.

My weakest point is that Theorem 1.15’s MAXCUT construction gives commodities different feasible allocation families. Since a complete type includes its feasible family, the reduction can have \(\tau\) as large as \(N\); it does not itself establish hardness in the high-multiplicity regime. The positive case rests on the same resource-allocation problem having a convincing separate regime with many repeated types. I would not extend this mirror to Chapter 2, where individual identity is central to impartiality, or to Chapter 3 by making release time continuous: that would continuize time rather than the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed optimization problem is already the standard fractional counterpart of the chapter’s model. Let \(P_t=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{1}_S:S\in\mathcal S_t\}\). The masses \(x_{t,S}\) are equivalent to choosing \(z_t\in P_t\), with resource loads \(y=N\sum_t\mu_tz_t\). So the proposed problem is convex quadratic optimization over a sum of fractional allocation sets. The thesis itself describes its discrete model as the unsplittable counterpart of fractional resource-sharing problems. Its cited literature includes work on convex resource-sharing and multicommodity flow, including algorithms for block-structured convex programs and fractional packing problems ([Grigoriadis and Khachiyan](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/0804004); [Garg and Könemann](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/abs/10.1137/S0097539704446232)).

That remains true in the proponent’s better commuter example. Repeated source–destination types give a splittable multicommodity flow problem with a convex separable objective. With explicit allocation choices, the mirror is a convex quadratic program; with path choices, it has a flow formulation. For succinct arbitrary allocation families, the extra question is which types admit efficient pricing. The continuous formulation supplies the familiar convex-optimization wrapper, while the difficulty lies in the chosen family’s pricing problem. The cited MAXCUT construction does not establish a small-support regime: its commodities have graph-specific feasible sets, so preserving those sets can require \(\tau\) to grow with the population.

This makes the proposed task—compute an \(\epsilon\)-optimal mass allocation—a poor claim for a new continuous mirror: its mathematical model and main algorithmic route are already familiar fractional resource sharing. But that objection does not rule out a worthwhile follow-up asking when finite high-multiplicity solutions approximate the fractional optimum, or what rounding guarantees hold as \(N\) grows. That bridge could be valuable, and the paper’s MAXCUT result does not settle it. So the negative case is weak as a universal claim: it challenges the novelty of this particular optimization mirror, but cannot honestly establish that no scenario would yield a useful continuous question.
