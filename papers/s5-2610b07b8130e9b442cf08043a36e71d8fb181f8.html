The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real at the model level, but there is a decisive qualification: the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor it to. Its numbered theorems concern strategyproofness and envy-freeness. Section 5’s game tree is used to calculate expected utility, but the paper states no named complexity bound for that calculation. Theorem 13, proved here, claims that Weighted Balanced LIKE is envy-free ex ante in the paper’s arbitrary-entitlement, \(0/1\)-utility setting. That is a natural point of contact, but it is an axiomatic claim, not a result that the problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. So there are no eligible anchors, and no honest anchor-to-mirror list to give.

My strongest candidate lead is a new computational question about the same mechanism: Continuous Weighted Balanced-LIKE Evaluation. An instance has a finite set \(G\) of item categories and a finite support \(T\) of agent types. Type \(t\) has entitlement \(w_t>0\), binary utility vector \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^{G}\), and mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The ordered item stream supplies \(Q\) goods per capita, described by a finite piecewise-constant sequence of categories. At each arrival, apply the paper’s rule: allocate to a willing agent with the lowest ratio of goods received to entitlement, breaking ties uniformly. The continuous state tracks mass in type-and-bundle cells; each person still receives an integral bundle, while a cell can contain fractional mass.

The task is to compute the limiting terminal masses \(x_{t,B}\) over types \(t\) and integral bundles \(B\), as population size \(N\) grows with type counts \(N\mu_t\) and supply \(QN\). Equivalently, for a threshold \(z\), decide whether the limiting expected utility per capita,
\[ \sum_{t,B} x_{t,B}\sum_{g\in B}v_t(g), \]
is at least \(z\). A natural regime is a large set of households or service sites served by a food bank, with many agents but relatively few combinations of entitlement and item preferences. The authors could plausibly recognize the question: it preserves their online rule and the expected-bundle calculation used in their analysis. I would initially expect tractability for a fixed item alphabet and polynomially bounded per-capita supply, with the number of reachable bundle states as a useful parameter.

The mirror would cover computation underlying the expected-utility analysis that supports Theorem 13; it would not establish that theorem’s fairness claim. Its weakest point is also substantial: the paper has no complexity anchor for this evaluation problem, and making a continuum limit meaningful requires supply to grow with population. The result is therefore a plausible extension of the authors’ mechanism, rather than a mirror directly backed by one of their named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–13 concern strategyproofness and envy-freeness; none classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithmic guarantee. Section 5’s game tree is a way to calculate probabilities in the proofs. Its size suggests that direct enumeration can be large, but the paper does not formulate or analyze the complexity of evaluating the mechanism. Continuizing the theorems themselves would continue axiomatic claims, which is outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The proponent’s best rescue is a coherent population model: use finitely many agent types, each defined by entitlement and a \(0/1\) utility vector, and scale both the population and a stream of repeated item categories. Track the limiting mass of agents with each integral bundle, then compute type-pair expected utilities or the resulting envy matrix. Large cohorts of similar households or service sites could make that regime sensible. There is no good objection based simply on agents having identities, goods remaining indivisible, or supplies needing to scale with population.

But this model asks a new computational question. Theorem 13 claims ex-ante envy-freeness; it does not claim that computing expected utility is hard, easy, or even a central algorithmic task. Computing aggregate welfare, as the proposed threshold version does, would not establish that envy claim. A stronger version that computes the full envy matrix would connect more closely to Theorem 13, but its target would still be the computation of an axiomatic property, not a computational result in the paper. Calling that “computation underlying” the theorem does not turn it into a result the paper made.

So the negative case is strong for the narrow verdict that this paper offers no direct, in-scope computational mirror. It is weak as a claim that *no worthwhile continuous question* could arise in any scenario. The scaled-cohort evaluation problem looks like a plausible research direction; the paper just does not provide the computational anchor that would make it a mirror of one of its results.
