The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal{A}\), rational population masses \(\mu_a\), and a coalition structure with blocks \(q^k\) summing to \(\mu\), let each block have an explicitly listed rational polynomial worth function \(F_k(r)\), where \(0\le r\le q^k\), \(F_k(0)=0\), and aggregate worth at population scale \(n\) is \(nF_k(r)\). For integer replications \(n\) making all masses integral, compute the limit, as \(n\to\infty\), of each type's per-player Shapley payoff when the remainder of its block is treated as singleton agents and the other blocks are held fixed. Is the limit \(u_a^k=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial F_k(tq^k)}{\partial r_a}\,dt\), and can these exact payoffs be computed from the explicit polynomial input?

The model it lives in

A continuum population with type set \(\mathcal{A}\) and mass vector \(\mu\) is arranged in coalition blocks \(q^k\). Each block's worth for a subcoalition of mass \(r\) is a rational polynomial \(F_k(r)\), with the outside structure fixed and the rest of that block treated as singletons. Scale aggregate worth as \(nF_k(r)\) in finite replications; the per-unit payoff for type \(a\) is the radial Shapley limit \(u_a^k=\int_0^1 \partial_a F_k(tq^k)\,dt\), and its block total is \(q_a^k u_a^k\).

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition in this paper that asserts a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. So under a strict reading of the anchor requirement, the paper has no qualifying computational anchor. The strongest foothold for a positive case is Proposition 4, proved here: for every partition-function game, there is a unique payoff configuration that is partition efficient and satisfies balanced contributions. In a coalition \(S\) of partition \(\Pi\), player \(i\)’s payoff is the Shapley value of the induced characteristic-function game
\[ \widehat v_{S,\Pi}(T)=v\bigl(T,\{T,\{j\}_{j\in S\setminus T}\}\cup\Pi_{-S}\bigr), \quad \varnothing\ne T\subseteq S. \]
In particular, for the grand coalition, \(\operatorname{pc}^*(\{N\})=\sigma^*(v)\). This is a proved payoff formula, not a complexity classification; it is the closest honest anchor for a computational mirror.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large market of small producers forming consortia, where firms of the same type share all relevant technology, costs, prices, and bargaining parameters. There could be millions of firms but only a few types. Coalition worths depend on the consortium’s composition and on how the remaining firms organize, matching the paper’s partition-function setting. A type’s mass is its fraction of the producer population.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous Balanced-Contributions Value Computation. An instance gives \(\tau\) types and a fixed coalition structure with blocks \(k=1,\ldots,K\), each described by a rational mass vector \(q^k\), with the block masses summing to the population masses. For each block, it gives a rational polynomial \(F_k(r)\), where \(F_k(r)\) is the worth of a subcoalition of composition \(r\le q^k\), holding the other blocks fixed and treating the rest of block \(k\) as singleton agents. Require \(F_k(0)=0\). The task is to compute each type’s per-unit payoff \(u_a^k\), and hence its total block payoff \(x_a^k=q_a^k u_a^k\).

The direct continuous counterpart of Proposition 4 is to require block efficiency, \(\sum_a q_a^k u_a^k=F_k(q^k)\), and infinitesimal balanced contributions: for types \(a,b\), \(\partial u_a^k/\partial q_b^k=\partial u_b^k/\partial q_a^k\). The natural candidate solution is
\[ u_a^k=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial F_k(tq^k)}{\partial r_a}\,dt. \]
It preserves the paper’s payoff question and its distinction between a coalition’s intrinsic contributions and the externalities encoded by its fixed outside structure. It is also a natural large-replication limit: a random arrival order sweeps through fractions of the type masses, and discrete marginal contributions approach this radial integral.

For the stated explicit-polynomial input, I expect tractability: integrate each monomial and compute the resulting rational payoffs in time polynomial in \(K,\tau\), the number of monomials, degree, and coefficient bit length. That is a proposed algorithmic consequence, not a result of this paper. Further questions include whether Shapley payoffs in finite replicated games converge to this allocation, and how the answer changes when worth functions or the coalition structure are given by succinct representations.

The soft spot is substantial: Proposition 4 is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result, and the paper does not study large populations. The differential balanced-contributions condition and smooth worth functions are a genuine extension. Still, the mirror keeps the same partition-function environment, payoff allocation, efficiency requirement, and contribution principle. It is therefore a defensible continuous analogue of Proposition 4, though a referee could reasonably judge that it opens a new computational question rather than continuizes one the authors posed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–6 characterize payoff rules or coalition-formation outcomes from axioms; none studies an algorithmic problem, its input representation, or its complexity. Proposition 4 is the closest anchor, but it gives the payoff directly as a Shapley value. Turning that characterization into “compute the payoff” adds a task the paper never poses.

The producer example is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and there is a clean stronger version of the proposed mirror. Make worth depend smoothly on the masses of each type in a coalition, with the other blocks fixed. For a coalition with mass vector \(q\), the large-replication limit of finite Shapley payoffs is the radial integral
\[ u_a(q)=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial F(tq)}{\partial r_a}\,dt. \]
This follows the random-order interpretation of the Shapley value: as the population grows, the predecessors of a player trace out fractions \(tq\). But it is a limit theorem for an allocation rule, not a computational problem extracted from Proposition 4. With an explicit polynomial \(F(r)=\sum_\alpha c_\alpha r^\alpha\), the integral reduces to
\[ u_a(q)=\sum_{\alpha:\alpha_a>0} c_\alpha\frac{\alpha_a}{|\alpha|}q^{\alpha-e_a}, \]
so the proposed input makes computation a direct symbolic calculation. A harder question would need a choice of succinct representation or oracle for \(F\); then the complexity would turn on that added encoding, just as it does for finite Shapley-value computation. The paper supplies no reason to privilege one such choice.

There is also a gap in the proposed axiomatic translation. Efficiency and the stated cross-derivative symmetry do not, by themselves, uniquely select the radial integral. If \(h\) is homogeneous of degree zero, then adding \(\nabla h(q)\) preserves both: \(q\cdot\nabla h(q)=0\), and its cross derivatives are symmetric. For example, on the positive two-type domain, \(h(q)=q_1/q_2\) gives a nonzero adjustment with both properties. Boundary or regularity conditions might rule such adjustments out, or the finite-replication limit could define the value directly. Either repair adds a modelling choice; the direct-limit repair returns to the analytic allocation question above.

So the negative case is strongest as a judgment about fit to ChoCo, not as a claim that no continuous population model can be made. A continuous Shapley limit may be worthwhile in cooperative-game theory. But Proposition 4 does not anchor a computational landscape, and the natural continuous version supplies a formula rather than a computational challenge. The paper’s lone proposed anchor therefore does not establish a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror.
