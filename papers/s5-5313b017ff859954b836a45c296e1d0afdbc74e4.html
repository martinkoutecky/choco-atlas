The continuous mirror question

Given tasks \(J\) with positive integer processing times \(p_i\), at most three complete preferred schedules \(V_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(B\), is there a permutation \(S\) of the tasks such that \(\sum_t \mu_t \sum_{i \in J} |C_i(S)-d_i(t)| \le B\), where \(C_i(S)\) is task \(i\)'s completion time in \(S\) and \(d_i(t)\) is its completion time in \(V_t\)?

The model it lives in

Keep tasks indivisible and schedules as permutations; represent the population by a rational distribution over at most three complete preferred-schedule types. Choose one collective schedule to minimize the mass-weighted total completion-time deviation.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2's unweighted ΣD-Dec hardness with three preference types. It does not establish the three-type weighted-objective result, which the paper leaves open, or mirror the paper's two-voter tractability result.

Open questions for a prover

  • For the weighted objective \(\sum_t \mu_t \sum_{i \in J} p_i |C_i(S)-d_i(t)|\), is the decision problem NP-hard with exactly three preference types, or is it polynomial-time solvable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous version of the paper’s three-voter \(\Sigma D\) decision problem. I would not make Theorem 1 a separate anchor: its four-voter hardness concerns the same unweighted problem, and Theorem 2 already gives the stronger support bound.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: “The \(\Sigma D\)-Dec problem is NP-hard, even when there are only 3 voters.” In full, \(\Sigma D\)-Dec takes a set of tasks with processing times, three preferred schedules of those tasks, and an integer threshold \(Z\); it asks whether there is a schedule whose sum, over voters and tasks, of the absolute difference between actual and preferred completion times is at most \(Z\). The authors prove this by reduction from 3-Partition.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: 3-Type Continuous \(\Sigma D\)-Dec. An instance consists of tasks \(J=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) with positive integer processing times \(p_i\); at most three types \(t\), each specifying a complete preferred ordering of the tasks; a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); and a rational threshold \(B\). Write \(d_i(t)\) for task \(i\)’s completion time in type \(t\)’s preferred ordering, and \(C_i(S)\) for its completion time in the proposed schedule \(S\). The question is whether there is a permutation \(S\) of the tasks such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t \sum_{i\in J} \left|C_i(S)-d_i(t)\right| \le B. \]
A solution is the witnessing schedule \(S\). Mass is the fraction of the population belonging to each complete preference type; the objective is the population-average deviation.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large infrastructure project with many affected residents in three broad constituencies, each sharing a preferred order for the project phases. The phases have common durations, and each resident’s cost is the total deviation of phase completion times from their preferred schedule. The population can be very large while \(\tau\le3\); for the hardness embedding, take \(N=3k\) residents with \(k\) in each type. This is the paper’s same scheduling question with a per-person objective. The authors should recognize it: the preferred schedules, completion-time deviations, and chosen collective schedule are unchanged.

The reduction is exact. Give the three voter schedules from Theorem 2 mass \(1/3\) each. Then for every schedule \(S\), the continuous objective is the discrete \(\Sigma D\) cost divided by \(3\), so the original threshold \(Z\) becomes \(B=Z/3\). If any voters have the same ordering, combine their masses into one type. Thus this continuous problem is NP-hard even with at most three types. I expect Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction’s combinatorics are in the task set and processing times, not in population multiplicity. The mirror keeps the schedule discrete while making the society continuous, which is within scope.

This covers Theorem 2’s unweighted \(\Sigma D\)-Dec result; it does not claim a hardness result for the weighted-candidate objective. Natural follow-up questions include whether the weighted objective is tractable with three preference types—the paper identifies the corresponding three-voter case as open—and how the problem behaves when parameterized by maximum task length or by the range of preferred completion times. With two types, the continuous unweighted problem is easy: the more massive type’s preferred schedule attains each task’s weighted median completion time (and with equal masses either preferred schedule does).

The weak point is that the high-multiplicity scenario idealizes broad constituencies as having identical complete rankings, and the hardness embedding itself only needs three equally sized types. So this is a narrow, strong Class B mirror, not evidence that continuization unlocks new tractability. It remains a sensible mirror of the authors’ underlying problem: many people may share a small set of complete preference types, and the decision is still which collective schedule to choose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong case against Theorem 2’s mirror. It is a named computational result, and the proposed population objective is a natural continuization: each type is a group with the same preferred schedule, and the objective measures average completion-time deviation across the population. A large project with recurring stakeholder groups is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. The tasks can remain indivisible; the population is what becomes continuous.

The strongest concern is that the paper’s reduction uses deliberately engineered task lengths and preferred schedules, while the infrastructure example may suggest more natural, less regular projects. But that attacks the example, not the model. The authors could instead study recurring project portfolios with many residents in a modest number of preference cohorts. That remains a mass distribution over complete preference types, and it asks the same scheduling aggregation question. There is no fundamental obstacle in the paper to recognizing it as their problem.

Equal masses in the reduction make the continuous objective a normalized copy of the discrete one. That shows the hardness transfers; it does not show the mirror is a collision or otherwise out of scope. Arbitrary rational masses also give a broader weighted-population problem. Calling this uninteresting because the hardness comes from the tasks, or because the answer may resemble the discrete one, would rely on grounds the programme explicitly rules out.

So the honest negative case is weak: the mirror may be a narrow Class B result rather than a demonstration of new tractability, but that is not a reason to reject it. This anchor survives.
