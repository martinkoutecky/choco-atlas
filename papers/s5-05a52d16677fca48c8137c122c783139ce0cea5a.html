The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational row-stochastic mixing matrix \(K\), reference opinions \(r_q\in\mathbb{Q}^D\), fixed per-capita allocations \(u_{q,t}\) for \(q\ne p\), a per-capita budget \(\beta\), and a rational threshold \(a\), decide whether there are allocations \(u_{p,t}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_{p,t}\le\beta\) and stationary opinions \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^D\) satisfying \(\left(1+\sum_q u_{q,t}\right)x_t-\sum_{s\in T}K_{ts}x_s=\sum_q u_{q,t}r_q\) for every \(t\), such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t r_p^\top x_t\ge a\). Assume \(K\) is irreducible and the fixed players exert positive influence on at least one type, ensuring a unique stationary state.

The model it lives in

A continuum of users divided among \(\tau\) audience types with masses \(\mu_t\), each type sharing a trust profile and per-capita campaign dose. The finite set of external players keeps the paper’s strategic setup; player \(p\) chooses type-level doses \(u_{p,t}\) under a mass-weighted budget to maximize the mass-weighted projection of the stationary opinion onto \(r_p\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s best-response problem for a role-mixing network. It leaves aside the paper’s empirical solver comparisons, observed network-topology effects, budget-scaling results, and any Nash-equilibrium computation.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(\tau\), what is the exact complexity of the rational threshold problem?
  • Does the problem admit an FPT algorithm in \(\tau\), or a useful approximation guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a segment-level, high-multiplicity Social Influence Game. My one anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, with the other players’ allocations fixed, player \(p\)’s problem is to choose nonnegative individual influence weights \(W_p\), with \(\sum_i W_{pi}\le\lambda\), to maximize the projection of the network’s asymptotic mean opinion onto \(r_p\). The paper shows this best response can be written as a difference-of-convex program: it introduces an auxiliary variable \(z\) for the inverse-matrix expression in the objective, and represents both the objective and its equality constraints as differences of convex functions. The authors compute with this formulation in their DCCP comparisons, so it is a computational anchor under the formulation-result allowance.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Role-Continuous SIG Best Response. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of audience roles, \(\tau=|T|\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). It also has a rational row-stochastic matrix \(K\): \(K_{ts}\) is the share of a role-\(t\) user’s baseline trust placed in role \(s\). There are \(P\) players with reference opinions \(r_q\in\mathbb Q^D\); the other players’ per-capita influence levels \(u_{q,t}\) are fixed. The instance gives player \(p\) a per-capita budget \(\beta\). Assume the role-mixing network is irreducible and the fixed players anchor at least one role, so the asymptotic opinion is unique.

Player \(p\) chooses \(u_{p,t}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_tu_{p,t}\le\beta\). The resulting asymptotic opinion \(x_t\in\mathbb R^D\) for role \(t\) satisfies

\[ \left(1+\sum_{q=1}^P u_{q,t}\right)x_t-\sum_{s\in T}K_{ts}x_s =\sum_{q=1}^P u_{q,t}r_q. \]

Given a rational threshold \(a\), ask whether there is a feasible allocation for which

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,r_p^\top x_t\ge a. \]

A solution is a feasible allocation and its induced asymptotic opinions; equivalently, the optimization version returns the global best-response value and an attaining allocation. All input data are rational. This is the same persistent external influence, DeGroot mixing, fixed opponents, budgeted action, and target-projection objective as the paper. The discrete regime behind it is a platform with millions of users in a relatively small number of audience roles, where users in a role share a trust profile and campaigns buy a common per-capita dose for that segment. As population \(n\) grows with \(\tau\) fixed or moderate, the discrete budget is scaled as \(n\beta\), so the limit has a nonvanishing per-capita supply.

For this variable-\(\tau\) problem, my provisional bet is Class C: continuum-specific hardness. The stationary-state equations couple the controls and opinions nonlinearly, and Theorem 2’s DC formulation is no guarantee of a polynomial-time global algorithm. The paper’s DCCP and Iterated Linear experiments find approximate solutions; they do not establish exact global complexity. I would test for hardness in the rational nonlinear optimization first, while also looking for tractability when \(\tau\), \(P\), and \(D\) are fixed or when \(K\) has extra structure. I am not claiming a hardness result: the mirror’s further questions are whether the threshold problem is \(\exists\mathbb R\)-hard, whether it admits an FPT algorithm in \(\tau\), and whether a useful approximation guarantee can be proved.

The scope is specifically Theorem 2’s best-response optimization in a stationary, persistent-influence contest. It does not mirror the paper’s experimental runtime claims, solve for a Nash equilibrium, or continuize outcome spaces, time, or axioms.

My weakest point is that the paper’s model allows a player to target individuals separately, whereas this mirror gives the campaign a segment-level control. That is a real restriction, and the authors may accept it only as a distinct regime of their game. I think it survives because segment-based buying is a plausible campaign mechanism, and the mirror preserves the paper’s central dynamic and strategic question. Rare hubs and highly irregular networks are poor fits: if they must each be represented as their own type, \(\tau\) may grow with the population and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it changes the network regime substantially. Theorem 2 concerns a player choosing weights on individual nodes of an arbitrary network. The role model replaces that network with a \(\tau\)-state mixing matrix \(K\), requires the campaign to use the same per-capita dose for everyone in each role, and scales the budget with population size. Its equations are an exact reduction for block-symmetric networks, but that symmetry removes the individual centrality and hub-targeting effects the paper studies. In generic networks, a complete type would have to encode enough of each node’s position to distinguish it, so the number of types could grow with the population.

That objection does not defeat the strongest version of the mirror. The programme allows instance regimes and co-modifications of the model. Large audience segments with shared trust profiles and segment-level campaign spending are plausible; finite networks with block-level mixing realize the proposed equations. With a fixed or moderate number of roles, the population is genuinely high-multiplicity. The paper’s theorem does not settle the resulting problem’s complexity: a DC formulation supplies neither an exact algorithm nor a complexity classification. So the mirror asks a real new computational question, even though it covers a restricted regime.

There is also a scope concern: the paper’s objective is to maximize an opinion projection, and its model has no voting rule or winner condition. The electoral motivation in the introduction does not appear in the optimization problem. If ChoCo requires a social-choice outcome, the proposed threshold question may sit outside that boundary; if it counts persuasion campaigns aimed at shifting public opinion, this objection weakens. Existing mean-field work already studies continuous opinion distributions and influencer strategies, but it does not by itself answer the exact computational-complexity question here. [One such model](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-46187-9) is relevant background, not a collision.

So the negative case is weak at its central burden. The role model is narrower than the paper’s arbitrary-network formulation, and its connection to social choice depends on how broadly ChoCo treats campaigning. But I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile, author-recognizable continuous mirror of the paper’s best-response problem. The sole anchor raised remains standing.
