The continuous mirror question

For a finite horizon, suppose a finite-support distribution of EV types \((a,d,v)\), with rational masses, arrives over time. Given known aggregate cost curves \(C_t(q)=\int_0^q c(t,z)\,dz\), can one compute an online feasible allocation and payment rule that assigns each type’s mass among integral per-EV charging schedules, maximizes welfare for the types observed so far, and makes truthful reporting a dominant strategy for each EV?

The model it lives in

Represent each type’s mass by its allocation across feasible integral per-EV schedules. Maximize mass-weighted value minus aggregate charging costs \(C_t(q)\), subject to online information, feasible charging windows, irrevocable past allocations, and dominant-strategy truthfulness for each EV.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s truthfulness guarantee and the welfare scheduler as an optimization target; it leaves the empirical benchmark, mechanism comparisons, and profit-efficiency tradeoff aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can welfare-maximizing DSIC rules be computed in time polynomial in the horizon, support size, and input bit length for piecewise-linear convex cost curves?
  • How does the paper’s pricing condition translate when an individual EV has zero mass and cannot change aggregate costs?

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1, proved here, says that with limited misreports and non-increasing marginal valuations, a mechanism is DSIC if its pricing function satisfies Eq. 1 and its scheduler satisfies Eq. 8. Lemma 1 proves that the scheduling constraints can be met by a feasible schedule. Neither classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard. The paper’s MIP implementation and its reported 52-second run for 300 agents are computational evidence, not a numbered complexity result. So I can’t honestly provide a one-per-anchor set of continuous complexity questions.

The strongest positive candidate is a continuous version of the paper’s online EV-charging problem. Imagine a large fleet served by an aggregator, with many owners sharing the same arrival and departure window and marginal-value vector. A type is \((a,d,v)\); its mass \(\mu_{(a,d,v)}\) is its fraction of the fleet. The aggregator makes online charging and payment decisions as types arrive. Its objective is total value from charging minus electricity cost, with aggregate cost at time \(t\) given by \(C_t(q)=\int_0^q c(t,z)\,dz\), where \(q\) is the mass charged then. Each vehicle still receives an integral charging schedule; the aggregate allocation can be fractional because only part of a type’s mass need receive a given schedule.

The resulting question would be: given the finite support of types and their masses, can one compute an online feasible schedule and payment rule that maximizes aggregate welfare while making truthful type reports a dominant strategy? This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a city-scale fleet may have many vehicles but comparatively few distinct charging windows and valuation profiles. Theorem 1 is relevant support for the mechanism-design aspect, and Section 3.3 computes a particular DSIC allocation mechanism. But this candidate is not anchored to a named complexity result in the paper, so it does not meet the requested standard. Its complexity class is undetermined by the paper.

The weakest point is that a continuum model could change the strategic problem: an individual vehicle has negligible effect on aggregate prices, so part of the incentive issue may collapse into posted pricing. The paper establishes a credible setting for a population mirror, but it does not establish a named complexity result whose discrete hardness or tractability the mirror could test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 establishes dominant-strategy incentive compatibility, and Lemma 1 establishes schedule feasibility; neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The MIP in Section 3.3 and its reported runtime are empirical evidence for one implementation, not a worst-case complexity result. So under the requested anchor standard, the proposed mirror has no eligible anchor.

The best rescue is a large fleet with repeated charging windows and valuation vectors, represented by masses over types \((a,d,v)\), with aggregate charging cost \(C_t(q)\). That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A careful model could allocate each type’s mass among individual charging schedules and preserve per-owner utilities and truthful reporting. So the objection cannot honestly be that EV owners have no meaningful types, or that fractional mass averages are inherently invalid.

The harder question is whether this mirrors the paper’s computational contribution. Its central theorem concerns incentives in an online mechanism; in an atomless population, one owner’s report has no effect on aggregate prices or costs. The exclusion-based pricing rationale therefore loses its pivotal-agent content. One could instead study the complexity of maximizing welfare subject to continuous-population incentive constraints, but that requires specifying new allocation and payment rules and a new computational problem. The paper neither states nor analyzes that problem’s complexity.

That makes this a strong reason to reject the proposed case as an *anchored* mirror, but a weak basis for claiming that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The continuous fleet model is credible, and a computational study of it could be useful. The negative case is strongest on the missing complexity anchor; it cannot establish that the broader continuous problem is not worth studying.
