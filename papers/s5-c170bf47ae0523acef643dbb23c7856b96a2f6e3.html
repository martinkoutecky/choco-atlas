What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover fractional roommate welfare optimization and stable outcomes; they leave the finite-population parity and removal results aside. The paper already treats the corresponding finite-type continuum analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is algorithmic. This paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class theorem; its usable computational anchors are optimization results that the authors actually use. I would anchor the case on Lemma 9 and Theorem 3, both proved in the paper. There is no hardness anchor here, so this is a Class A argument.

The regime is large professional partnerships: for example, a market with many doctors or consultants but a moderate number \(\tau\) of skill and specialty types. A type fixes everything relevant to partnership surplus, and two people of that type are interchangeable for the model. Let \(f_x\) be the fraction of the workforce of type \(x\), and let \(\Phi_{xy}=\Phi_{yx}\) be the transferable surplus from pairing types \(x\) and \(y\). The continuum describes a distribution of agents, not fractional partnerships: each person still gets one partner or remains single, while \(q_{xy}\) records the mass of such pairs. This is plausible in the paper’s own setting of large labor markets and close substitutes.

Lemma 9 is proved in the appendix. It states that the maximum surplus over fractional roommate matchings equals the maximum in the associated bipartite matching problem, \(\mathcal W_{\mathcal F}(n,\Phi)=\mathcal W_{\mathcal B}(n,n,\Phi/2)\); it also states that the fractional roommate problem has a half-integral optimum.

Its continuous mirror is Continuum Roommate Welfare Optimization. The input is a finite type set \(\mathcal X\), rational masses \(f_x\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric rational surplus matrix \(\Phi\). Choose symmetric nonnegative pair masses \(q_{xy}=q_{yx}\) to maximize
\[ \sum_x q_{xx}\Phi_{xx}+\sum_{x\ne y}q_{xy}\frac{\Phi_{xy}}{2}, \qquad 2q_{xx}+\sum_{y\ne x}q_{xy}\leq f_x\quad\text{for each }x. \]
The output is an optimal pair-mass matrix and its welfare. I expect this to be tractable: it is a linear program with \(O(\tau^2)\) variables, equivalently the bipartite matching LP in Lemma 9, so rational instances can be solved in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). The half-integrality claim itself is specific to the finite formulation; the mirror’s main payoff is that fractional pair masses are directly meaningful in an atomless population.

Theorem 3, also proved here, is the stronger anchor for the paper’s central stability question. Under symmetric surplus, it says: (i) a stable roommate matching exists if and only if the integer roommate optimum \(\mathcal W_{\mathcal P}(n,\Phi)\) equals the associated bipartite optimum \(\mathcal W_{\mathcal B}(n,n,\Phi/2)\); (ii) any stable roommate matching attains \(\mathcal W_{\mathcal P}(n,\Phi)\); and (iii) whenever stable matchings exist, their utilities solve the stated dual program minimizing \(\sum_x n_xu_x\), subject to \(u_x\geq0\), \(u_x+u_y\geq\Phi_{xy}+A_{xy}\), and \(A_{xy}=-A_{yx}\).

Its mirror is Continuum Stable Roommate Outcome Search. With the same input \(f,\Phi\), return pair masses \(q\) and per-type payoffs \(u\) such that the mass constraints above hold, \(u_x\geq0\), \(u_x+u_y\geq\Phi_{xy}\) for every pair of types, and
\[ \sum_x f_xu_x = \sum_x q_{xx}\Phi_{xx}+\sum_{x\ne y}q_{xy}\frac{\Phi_{xy}}{2}. \]
For every type with positive single mass, require \(u_x=0\); for every pair type used with positive mass, require \(u_x+u_y=\Phi_{xy}\). A solution is thus an actual stable matching allocation and payoff vector, not an encoding. I expect this search problem to be tractable by the primal and dual LPs. This is the natural continuous form of the stability question: with fractional mass allocations, the integer-versus-fractional obstruction in Theorem 3 disappears, and the paper itself says that its finite-type continuum case recovers continuum-agent stability results.

The scope is deliberately narrow: symmetric transferable-utility roommate matching with two-person teams and finitely many observable types. The paper’s large-population results, including Proposition 6’s convergence of average surplus to the bipartite LP value, reinforce that this regime is natural, but I would not present that convergence as a separate anchor. Follow-up computational questions include handling a very large or implicitly described type space, and optimizing subsidies or mass changes when the surplus matrix is perturbed.

The weakest point is also substantial: the paper already discusses the continuum-agent, finite-type case, and its LP connection makes the expected tractability answer apparent. So this is a credible population-continuization and a clean computational problem, but not a strong claim to a new complexity landscape. Its positive case is that the mirror preserves the paper’s actual question—stable team formation—and reveals why stability becomes computable when a large workforce can be treated through type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchors are unusually exposed because the paper already develops their proposed continuous counterparts. For Lemma 9, the proponent’s pair-mass program is exactly the paper’s fractional roommate matching program \(\mathcal W_{\mathcal F}\), after normalizing populations to fractions. The appendix proves its value equals the bipartite matching LP and gives the half-integral optimum result. Section 4 then shows that this same LP gives the limit average welfare as populations grow, and explicitly connects the finite-type continuum of agents to AWW. That is a genuine population continuum, already present in the paper—not an outcome-space or noise-model substitute.

The stronger version of the Lemma 9 mirror therefore does not escape the collision. Asking for the optimal mass allocation, rather than just its value, still asks for the optimizer of the LP the paper has formulated and linked to bipartite matching. The paper does not state a complexity-class theorem, but its LP has already been solved as a mathematical object; relabeling that result as a computational question does not create a missing result to mirror.

The proposed Theorem 3 mirror is covered even more directly. The paper’s stability proof constructs the fractional roommate allocation from a bipartite optimum and its dual payoffs. Those are the allocation and utilities the proponent asks an algorithm to return. The paper also says that, with finitely many types and a continuum of agents, stability follows from AWW. The integer-versus-fractional gap in Theorem 3 is a feature of the finite matching model; the proposed mass formulation is the fractional object already used to establish the continuum result. This objection is about duplication of the model and result, not about whether the continuous answer is easy or whether it matches the discrete answer.

A larger type space is the plausible escape hatch: one could study measure-valued matchings over a continuum of skill profiles. But that changes the computational target substantially. The paper itself presents this as a future extension requiring optimal-transport machinery; it does not supply a named computational problem whose complexity the two anchors leave open. With an uncountable type space, one must also specify how types, surpluses, and input distributions are represented before a finite-input complexity question can be posed. That may be worthwhile work, but it is a new model and computational agenda, not a stronger mirror of Lemma 9 or Theorem 3 under the paper’s finite-type setup.

So the negative case is strongest for the two anchors actually offered: both are already answered in the paper’s fractional and continuum analysis. It is weaker against a broader programme built around continuum type spaces. The paper explicitly points toward that extension, and I cannot honestly rule out a useful computational project there; I can only say it does not rescue these proposed mirrors.
