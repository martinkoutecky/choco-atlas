The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, the paper has no numbered theorem that classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, or similar. The strongest named result for a population mirror is Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper: for \(n\ge4\) agents with additive ternary valuations, the price of EF1 is \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\). Here the price is the worst-case ratio of unrestricted maximum welfare to maximum welfare among EF1 allocations. The theorem’s proof partitions the items into \(\sqrt n\) groups, gives each group a specialist who values its items at \(a\), and has the remaining agents value every item at \(b\), with normalization requiring \(a=b\sqrt n\). EF1 forces every generalist to receive an item, limiting EF1 welfare to \(b(2n-\sqrt n)\), while unrestricted welfare is \(bn\sqrt n\). This is a proved welfare bound, not a complexity classification.

My lead mirror is Mass-Ternary-EF1-Welfare. Its input is \(K\) item categories with rational supply \(q_j\) per unit of population, and a finite set \(T\) of agent types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type has a complete additive value vector \(v_t\in\{a,b,0\}^K\), for common \(a>b>0\); require equal total value for every type, \(\sum_j q_jv_t(j)=V\). An allocation is a finite rational list \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(B\in\mathbb N^K\) is an integral bundle and \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving that bundle. It must satisfy \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_j=q_j\). It is EF1 if, for every pair of bundles \(B,B'\) used by positive mass and every type \(t\) assigned \(B\), there is an item category \(j\) with \(B'_j>0\) such that \(v_t\cdot B\ge v_t\cdot(B'-e_j)\). The task is to maximize \(W(x)=\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}(v_t\cdot B)\) subject to EF1, and compare it with the unrestricted maximum \(W^*\). Equivalently, the decision version asks whether an EF1 allocation of welfare at least a given rational threshold exists.

This keeps each agent’s bundle integral and each item indivisible. Only the distribution of agents across bundles is fractional. That is the natural co-modification: item supplies scale with population, while a type’s average allocation may be fractional. Types record everything relevant to valuation; agents within a type are interchangeable.

The large-population regime is plausible for standardized consumer or housing markets. It also gives a high-multiplicity version of Theorem 2.1’s construction. Take \(N=K^2H\) agents, with \(K\) specialist types of \(H\) agents each and one generalist type of \(KH(K-1)\) agents. There are \(K\) item categories, each with \(KH\) items. A specialist values their own category at \(1\) and the others at \(0\); generalists value every item at \(1/K\). Total values are normalized: each type values the entire inventory at \(KH\). Here \(N\) can greatly exceed the \(K+1\) types, and \(H\) can be large so every type has many agents.

In unit-mass form, each category has supply \(1/K\), each specialist type has mass \(1/K^2\), and the generalist type has mass \(1-1/K\). Unrestricted welfare is \(1\), achieved by assigning each category to its specialist type. In any EF1 allocation, either every generalist receives at least one item, leaving at most \(1/K\) item mass for specialists, or some positive mass of generalists receives nothing. In the latter case, EF1 forbids any supported bundle from containing two or more items, since a generalist values every item positively. Either way, EF1 welfare is at most \((2K-1)/K^2\). Thus the price is at least \(K^2/(2K-1)=\Theta(K)=\Theta(\tau)\), where \(\tau=K+1\). This multiplicity-amplified construction follows the theorem’s counting argument; the paper does not state it as a separate theorem.

For Mass-Ternary-EF1-Welfare, my provisional prediction is hardness transfers: the individual bundle choices and EF1 comparisons remain, and rational mass allocations have finite replicated-population representations. The lower-bound phenomenon itself transfers in the construction above. The exact computational classification is a separate question, though: the paper’s Theorem 2.1 does not prove hardness. The introduction cites NP-hardness of maximizing welfare subject to EF1 as [11], but that is neither proved nor numbered here, and it does not establish hardness for this ternary mass problem.

The scope of this mirror is just Theorem 2.1’s many-agent price lower bound; I would not use the two- and three-agent constants as evidence for the population regime. Further questions include whether the worst-case price is \(O(\tau)\) for ternary mass societies, and whether the exact EF1-welfare problem has a tractable configuration or pricing formulation.

My weakest point is that the original theorem’s specialist agents each occur once. The construction above repairs that by duplicating every specialist type \(H\) times, but the paper does not prove a complexity result for that replicated regime. Also, the lower bound grows with the number of types; it does not show a growing price at fixed \(\tau\). That limits what this anchor says about algorithmic complexity, while leaving a credible population mirror of its welfare-loss result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a fit objection: Theorem 2.1 proves a welfare distortion bound, not a computational result. It shows that some EF1 allocations can lose a factor of \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) in welfare. It gives no complexity classification, runtime, or algorithmic guarantee to carry into a continuous society. The proposed Mass-Ternary-EF1-Welfare task—maximize EF1 welfare over distributions of integral bundles—is a reasonable computational problem, but it is a new task. The theorem’s lower-bound construction does not establish anything about its complexity.

The model itself is not the weak point. The proponent’s construction has \(K+1\) complete valuation types, with many agents of each type when \(H\) is large. Its per-capita item supplies and integral bundles preserve indivisibility, and the EF1 comparison remains meaningful. Taking the supremum of the welfare ratio over such mass societies would give a natural continuous price-of-EF1 question. The construction even gives that question a concrete lower bound, \(K^2/(2K-1)\). Shrinking specialist-type masses as \(K\) grows does not invalidate the high-multiplicity regime: their populations can still grow with \(H\).

The two other named algorithmic results, Theorems 3.2 and 4.2, are poor substitutes for a population anchor. Their guarantees concern exactly two or three agents. Continuizing the items would miss ChoCo’s population focus; replacing those fixed numbers of agents with a continuum would change the problem those theorems address.

So, under ChoCo’s computational scope, I would reject Theorem 2.1 as a strong anchor: its central result is a distortion bound, and turning it into an optimization or complexity question requires adding a computational target. I would not claim that every continuous mirror is worthless. The mass-society price question is coherent and its high-multiplicity interpretation is plausible. The honest negative case is therefore weak against the broader claim that some worthwhile mirror exists; it is strongest as an argument that this paper does not itself supply a computational result for that mirror.
