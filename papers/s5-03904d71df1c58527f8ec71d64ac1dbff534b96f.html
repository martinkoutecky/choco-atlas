The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational outcome set \(S=\{(\alpha_j,\beta_j)\}_j\), rival types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-team score gaps \(c_t\), and rational masses \(q_{tu}\) of fixtures with type \(t\) at home against type \(u\), does there exist an outcome allocation with masses \(x_{tu,j}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_j x_{tu,j}=q_{tu}\) for every \(t,u\), and \(\sum_{u,j}(\alpha_jx_{tu,j}+\beta_jx_{ut,j})\le \mu_t c_t\) for every \(t\)? The schedule and allocation are type-regular: every team of type \(t\) has the same per-capita fixture and outcome proportions against each type, so these inequalities ensure no rival finishes above the target’s fixed score.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of rival teams divided into types \(t\), each with mass \(\mu_t\), common per-team score gap \(c_t\), and identical exposure to each opponent type. Fixtures have masses \(q_{tu}\), and decision variables \(x_{tu,j}\) allocate fixture mass to discrete outcomes \((\alpha_j,\beta_j)\). Feasibility asks whether every type’s resulting per-team score stays at or below the target’s fixed final score; there is no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The typewise outcome-balance condition suppresses individual-rival incidence used by the 3DM reductions, and the case gives no finite-limit argument showing the original authors would accept this restricted regime; its acknowledgement of the lost combinatorics does not settle that recognition question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It mirrors Theorem 6.1’s feasibility question on dense type-regular instances, while leaving arbitrary schedule incidence, the full \mathrm{GSC}(S)\) setting, and the paper’s general complexity classification for unrestricted schedules outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can feasible continuous allocations be rounded to finite type-regular tournaments with a controlled score error?
  • Which sparse or irregular schedule restrictions restore the hardness from Theorem 6.1?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I find convincing is a dense, type-regular version of the paper’s partial sports competition problem. It keeps the central question intact: after the target has finished playing, can the remaining fixtures be assigned outcomes from \(S\) so that no rival finishes above the target?

The anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It says that, after normalization, \(\mathrm{PSC}(S)\) is polynomially solvable exactly when \(S=\{(i,n-i):0\le i\le n\}\) for some \(n\in\mathbb N\); for every other normalized outcome set, \(\mathrm{PSC}(S)\) is NP-complete. The authors prove the hard cases by reductions from 3-dimensional matching and handle the tractable case with a flow formulation. This is a good anchor because it is a hardness result, and because the paper’s proved Theorem 3.1 establishes that \(\mathrm{GSC}(S)\) and \(\mathrm{PSC}(S)\) are polynomially equivalent. The partial problem is therefore central to the paper’s elimination question, not a side issue.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Type-Regular Continuous Partial Sports Elimination. The input is a finite rational outcome set \(S=\{(\alpha_j,\beta_j)\}_j\); a finite set \(T\) of rival-team types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); each type’s current score, expressed per team on the population scale; the target’s fixed final score on that same scale; and rational match masses \(q_{tu}\) for fixtures with a type-\(t\) home team and a type-\(u\) away team. A type completely specifies the rival’s relevant information: its current score and its home and away fixture exposure to every type. The schedule is type-regular, so every team in a type has the same exposure to each other type.

Let \(c_t\) be the target’s final score minus a type-\(t\) team’s current score. A solution assigns each infinitesimal fixture exactly one allowed outcome, with the same outcome proportions across each type-pair schedule as seen by every participating team. Equivalently, it gives nonnegative masses \(x_{tu,j}\), where \(x_{tu,j}\) is the mass of type-\(t\)-home/type-\(u\)-away fixtures assigned outcome \(j\), such that
\(\sum_j x_{tu,j}=q_{tu}\) for every \(t,u\), and
\(\sum_{u,j}(\alpha_jx_{tu,j}+\beta_jx_{ut,j})\le \mu_t c_t\) for every \(t\).
The question is whether such an assignment exists. There is no optimization objective: feasibility is the paper’s question. Each individual fixture still receives a discrete outcome from \(S\); only the mass of fixtures assigned to each outcome is continuous.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large online or long-horizon tournament with \(N\) teams and \(N\gg\tau=|T|\), where many teams share the same score headroom and schedule role. For example, the schedule could give each team a dense, balanced set of opponents from each class. The team population is the continuous object; scores and fixture masses are scaled per population size, so normalized totals can be fractional even though every played match has an allowed discrete result. The question remains whether a distinguished team can win. Theorem 6.1 concerns arbitrary finite schedules, while this mirror asks the same feasibility question in an explicit, symmetric instance regime.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Its feasibility conditions are a linear program with \(O(\tau^2|S|)\) variables, so it is polynomial in the support size, outcome-set size, and input bit length. The continuous problem appears tractable for every finite \(S\), including the outcome sets for which Theorem 6.1 proves discrete NP-completeness. That would make the mirror informative about where the discrete difficulty goes: the continuous schedule records type-pair match mass and permits it to be distributed across outcomes, rather than making one indivisible choice for each named fixture.

My weakest point is the schedule regime. Ordinary leagues often have few teams and sparse schedules, whereas this mirror needs many teams per type and enough fixtures for normalized scores to be meaningful. It also replaces individual schedule structure with type-level exposure, which could erase combinatorics that matter in the paper’s arbitrary multigraphs. I still think it is a defensible mirror because it preserves the elimination decision and the discrete outcome set, while making the population large and the teams indistinguishable in the respects the model uses.

The next questions are whether a feasible continuous assignment can be rounded to finite type-regular tournaments with a controlled score error, and which sparse or irregular schedule restrictions restore the hardness seen in Theorem 6.1. Those would make the connection to the paper’s discrete results precise and identify the boundary of this tractable regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.1 is a real computational anchor: it classifies \(\mathrm{PSC}(S)\), and Theorem 3.1 connects that problem to the paper’s general elimination question. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror gets its small LP by removing the schedule structure that makes this theorem interesting.

The instance is not just a population of teams with score headrooms. It is a graph whose vertices are teams and whose edges are fixtures. The 3-dimensional matching reductions encode choices in which particular triple gadget is connected to particular copies of \(x,y,w\). Counts of fixtures between team types do not retain that incidence. Teams with the same score and the same number of fixtures against each type can still occupy different positions in the schedule, and those positions affect whether every individual rival stays below the target. Thus the proposed mass constraints describe a schedule-balanced variant; they do not follow from high multiplicity alone. The type description is incomplete for the paper’s problem unless it also records the relevant network position.

There are two ways to repair that gap, and neither makes this a strong mirror of Theorem 6.1. Keep only finitely many types and type-pair fixture masses, and the resulting LP asks about a specially balanced, densely mixed competition. Its polynomiality is immediate from the imposed averaging, while the paper’s hard instances use fixture incidence the model has discarded. Preserve incidence by giving the continuum a schedule kernel or a separate coordinate for each network role, and the input is no longer a society summarized by support size \(\tau\): its schedule can carry the full combinatorial instance, and the target’s requirement that *every* rival be kept below its score remains a pointwise constraint. That may lead to worthwhile graphon or continuous network optimization, but it is not a computational question made manageable by the population distribution.

The proposed large round-robin scenario is the best reply to this objection. It is mathematically coherent, and a large dense tournament with many interchangeable teams is plausible. I cannot honestly rule out that its balanced continuous version would be useful. But its appeal depends on a narrow symmetry assumption, and the missing bridge is substantial: the finite problem permits an individual rival to be decisive, while the continuous masses and equalized per-team outcome proportions suppress precisely those exceptional scores. Without showing that this regime is one the authors would recognize as a meaningful high-multiplicity version of their elimination problem, the LP is better viewed as a new regular-tournament model than as a mirror of their result. That is the negative case’s weak point: it challenges the value and fidelity of the proposed mirror, but it cannot establish that the balanced-round-robin question is not worth studying.
