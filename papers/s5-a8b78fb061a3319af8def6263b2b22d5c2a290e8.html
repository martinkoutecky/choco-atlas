The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor, so I can’t make an honest anchor-based case for a continuous mirror. The paper’s numbered Results 1–5 are empirical findings: equal division under certainty, heterogeneous allocations under uncertainty, lower average allocations to the risk-exposed recipient as risk rises, associations with decision-makers’ own risk preferences, and a classification of decision-makers across four justice views. None asserts a computational complexity result or gives an algorithm for an underlying computational social-choice problem. The equations in Section 2 derive allocation formulas, but they are not such results either.

The strongest foothold for a future extension is the observed heterogeneity in justice views (Result 2, with the classification in Result 5). One could imagine a large electorate whose complete types include their justice and risk preferences, and ask whether a proposed allocation policy can be made to win. But that would add an election and a population of voters to a paper about individual third-party allocation decisions; it has no qualifying anchor here. The paper’s uncertain outcomes and monetary allocation variable do not themselves make its society continuous.

So the paper supplies zero valid anchors for the requested per-anchor continuous problems. Any such problem would be a follow-on proposal, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper contains no computational result to mirror. Results 1–5 report experimental patterns in allocation choices. Equations (2)–(5) derive allocations implied by four justice principles. The paper does not define a computational problem with an input, an output, and a complexity or algorithmic question. Its decision maker is a single uninvolved allocator choosing how to split resources between two recipients; the results concern what people choose, not how to compute a collective choice. So there are no qualifying anchors.

The strongest candidate is the heterogeneity in justice views: imagine a large population of decision makers, described by their risk attitudes and justice views, choosing a public allocation policy. That could be a worthwhile new social-choice problem, but it does not follow from Result 2 or 5. The four justice categories are researchers’ best-fit classifications of observed decisions across a small set of problems. They are not elicited rankings over a policy agenda, and the paper specifies no rule for turning them into a collective decision or any computational task to perform. Adding an agenda, an aggregation rule, and a question such as manipulation or winner robustness would supply the missing problem from outside the paper.

A second route is to make the recipients a large population, perhaps people in jobs or projects with different risks, and allocate resources across recipient types. That is a plausible policy model, but the paper’s own setup has two equally deserving recipients and derives allocations for fixed lotteries. To get a computational problem, one would need to add features such as many risk types, constraints, or intervention costs. Those additions might lead to worthwhile research, but they would not continue a computational question this paper posed.

This case is strong against treating the paper as a source of a continuous mirror. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile project could ever be built from its subject matter: a new model of collective choice under uncertainty could be valuable. But that possibility does not give this paper an anchor. The absence of a computational result—not the small experiment, the two recipients, or the uncertain outcomes—is the sound basis for the negative verdict.
