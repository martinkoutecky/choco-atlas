The strongest possible salvage is a new algorithmic question: given a weighted party game and a designated coalition leader, find a winning coalition maximizing the leader’s Penrose power, perhaps subject to closure, minimum size, or minimum range. But that would be a problem proposed on top of the paper, not a mirror of one of its results. Its actors are parties: coalition membership and ideological order depend on which parties are present, while seat counts serve as weights. Replacing voters or legislators with continuous mass changes the game being analyzed.

Treating parties themselves as the population is a closer fit, but the paper offers little reason to expect a useful high-multiplicity regime of interchangeable party organizations. And the Penrose measure depends on a probability over subsets of actors; for an atomless population, the paper supplies no natural extension of that probability. Preserving a distinguished leader as an individual atom may avoid losing the leader, but it requires a new mixed finite–continuous model and new assumptions.

I cannot rule out a separately motivated study of coalition optimization in such a model. The negative case is strongest, and weakest, at the same point: this paper provides no computational theorem to mirror, while ruling out every worthwhile new model would require more than the paper establishes.
