The continuous mirror question

Given \(n,k,\epsilon,\delta\) and a society \(\mu\) over strict-ranking types whose induced pairwise probabilities satisfy SST and STI, with access only through iid pairwise polls, what is the worst-case minimum expected number of polls needed by an adaptive policy to return \(U\), \(|U|=k\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\) such that \(p_{ij}\ge \frac12-\epsilon\) for every \(i\in U\) and \(j\notin U\)? Theorem 5 gives an \(O(n\epsilon^{-2}\log(k/\delta))\) upper bound, and Theorem 2 gives a matching worst-case lower bound up to constants in its stated parameter range.

The model it lives in

Types are strict rankings \(\pi\) of the \(n\) items, with rational masses \(\mu_\pi\) and support size \(\tau\le n!\). A queried pair \(i,j\) returns the preferred item from an independent voter draw, so \(p_{ij}=\sum_{\pi:\,i\succ_\pi j}\mu_\pi\); the policy has poll access to these probabilities but not the masses themselves. Its decision is an adaptive query policy returning \(U\subseteq[n]\), \(|U|=k\), minimizing worst-case expected polls subject to \(\Pr[\forall i\in U,\ j\notin U:\ p_{ij}\ge \frac12-\epsilon]\ge 1-\delta\).

The objection that survived

The explicit-mass ChoCo input would let an algorithm calculate every \(p_{ij}\) and find the best-\(k\) set directly; the mirror needs the population masses to be hidden behind poll access.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers PAC \(k\)-selection’s upper bound in Theorem 5 and query lower bound in Theorem 2, including the MNL/Plackett–Luce lower-bound family. It makes no claim for the exact-selection results in Theorems 7–9.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do the query bounds change when the ranking distribution has small support \(\tau\)?
  • What bounds hold when polls sample a finite electorate without replacement?
  • What population-polling bounds hold for exact best-\(k\) identification?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is PAC best-\(k\) selection from a continuously sized electorate. It gives the paper’s noisy comparison probabilities a concrete population meaning: each person has a fixed ranking, and a pairwise poll samples one person and reveals which of two items they prefer.

Formally, let the \(n\) items be candidates or policies, and let each voter’s type be a strict ranking \(\pi\) of them. The electorate is a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, with \(\mu_\pi\) the fraction of voters of type \(\pi\) and support size \(\tau\). A poll of \(i\) against \(j\) independently draws a voter from this population and returns their preference, so \(p_{ij}=\sum_{\pi:\,i\succ_\pi j}\mu_\pi\). The society is large relative to its number of ranking types: for a fixed shortlist of \(n\) items, there can be many voters per type, with \(\tau\le n!\). Assume the resulting pairwise probabilities satisfy SST and STI and induce a strict majority order.

A solution is an adaptive polling policy: choose a pair after each history, stop, and return a \(k\)-item set \(U\). It succeeds if, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), every \(i\in U\) and \(j\notin U\) satisfy \(p_{ij}\ge 1/2-\epsilon\). Its cost is the expected number of polls. This asks for the audience’s approximately best \(k\) items, not for a ranking of every voter or for fractional outcomes.

The lead anchor is Theorem 5, “Theoretical Performance of TKS,” proved in this paper. Its continuous question is: for every population in that SST/STI class, can an adaptive policy find such a set using \(O(n\epsilon^{-2}\log(k/\delta))\) expected polls? The theorem says yes: TKS terminates within that expected comparison bound and, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), returns an \((\epsilon,k)\)-optimal set. This is a strong fit to the polling interpretation: each comparison is a fresh draw from the population, and the theorem’s guarantee applies to the induced probabilities. It does not require the policy to know the ranking masses.

The complementary anchor is Theorem 2, “Lower bound for PAC \(k\)-selection,” also proved in this paper, although its proof invokes lower bounds for pure-exploration bandits from earlier work. For \(\epsilon\in(0,1/128)\), \(\delta\in(0,e^{-4}/4)\), \(n\ge2\), and \(1\le k\le n/2\), it proves that there is an \(n\)-item instance satisfying SST and STI for which any algorithm that returns an \((\epsilon,k)\)-optimal subset with probability at least \(1-\delta\) needs \(\Omega(n\epsilon^{-2}\log(k/\delta))\) comparisons in expectation. The mirror question is whether some electorate forces every polling policy to spend that many consultations. The paper’s lower-bound construction uses MNL pairwise probabilities; these have a Plackett–Luce random-ranking interpretation, so the probabilities can arise from a distribution over complete voter rankings. The lower bound therefore has a population realization, though its hard examples can use all \(n!\) ranking types.

Together, these results give the clearest case: TKS is the lead positive result, and Theorem 2 says its polling cost is optimal up to constants. Under the programme’s trichotomy, the algorithm is Class A in query complexity: it provides an efficient adaptive policy with a matching information lower bound. That is not a claim of polynomial-time tractability for a known, explicitly supplied \(\mu\), nor is Theorem 2 NP-hardness. Its lower bound says that an unknown population cannot always be learned with fewer polls.

The regime is plausible when a large user base has stable preference segments over a small shortlist—for example, a platform polling its audience to choose which few recommendations to feature. The population size \(N\) can greatly exceed \(\tau\); the paper’s \(n\) counts items, not people. The MNL lower-bound examples may have \(\tau=n!\), so they support the mirror most directly for fixed, modest shortlists and very large audiences. They do not establish the same lower bound for societies with a small support relative to a growing \(n\).

The main weakness is the information interface. In ChoCo’s central model, the society’s support and rational masses are given. If the policy receives \(\mu\) explicitly, it can sum the masses to calculate the \(p_{ij}\) values and sort the induced order; the paper’s sampling problem disappears. My mirror instead hides the masses and gives the analyst only pairwise poll access. That makes the question a genuine continuous-population problem, but also makes it a statistical learning problem rather than the usual computational problem on an explicitly represented society. The authors could reasonably accept the voter interpretation while rejecting it as a new computational analogue of their work.

This case covers the paper’s PAC-\(k\) sample-complexity results, especially Theorems 2 and 5. It does not claim a mirror for the paper’s exact-selection results. Natural follow-on questions would be whether the bounds sharpen when \(\tau\) is small, how the sampling model changes for a finite electorate polled without replacement, and what matching population bounds hold for exact best-\(k\) identification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 and Theorem 2 are real algorithmic results, but their resource is comparison-oracle calls, not computation on a supplied society. Both concern the same hidden-information problem: pairwise outcomes have unknown probabilities, and an algorithm must learn enough from noisy samples to return a PAC best-\(k\) set.

That distinction breaks the proposed mirror at its strongest point. In ChoCo’s model, the support and rational masses are part of the input. Given them, one can compute each pairwise probability as
\[ p_{ij}=\sum_{\pi:\,i\succ_\pi j}\mu_\pi \]
and, under SST, determine the order from those probabilities. The population is not hidden behind polls. Theorems 5 and 2 therefore do not give a polling-cost result for a known continuous society: their sample cost arises because the probabilities are unknown.

The proponent’s random-voter interpretation is coherent, including for Plackett–Luce instances. But it changes the information interface. To preserve the theorems, each query must act like a fresh independent draw from an unknown population. Then the same query process could be generated by a finite electorate sampled with replacement, or by a synthetic Bernoulli oracle; the total population size and the masses over ranking types do not affect the guarantees. The society supplies the oracle’s probabilities, but the algorithms never use its support or masses as an input.

The lower bound does not repair this gap. Its Plackett–Luce realization shows that some hard comparison oracles can come from ranking populations. It does not show that the hardness is computationally caused by the population representation or its multiplicity: the hard instances are parameterized by item scores, and the bound is about uncertainty in those parameters. Theorem 2 and Theorem 5 thus reinforce each other as a sharp result about noisy active learning, rather than supplying separate evidence about algorithms over continuous mass vectors.

A stronger follow-on might ask how query complexity changes when the unknown ranking distribution has small support, or when polls sample without replacement. That could be worthwhile active-learning research, but it would require a new result and a different access model; it is not a continuous mirror of these theorems in ChoCo’s given-society model.

The negative case has a real limit: polling a large, segmented audience to choose a shortlist is a plausible social-choice application, and the paper’s guarantees transfer naturally to that unknown-population query problem. So I would not claim that no worthwhile population interpretation exists in any scenario. My case is narrower: the proponent’s anchors do not establish a worthwhile mirror for the programme’s explicit continuous-society input, because hiding the masses is what makes their sample-complexity question nontrivial.
