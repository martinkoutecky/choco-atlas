What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 6’s conflict-freeness preservation result only; it leaves the paper’s other preservation results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. Theorem 6 is the paper’s closest named anchor: for any argumentation framework with an attack between two distinct, non-self-attacking arguments, a quota rule \(F_q\) preserves conflict-freeness if and only if \(q>n/2\). Chen and Endriss prove this theorem in the paper, adapting a result of Grandi and Endriss on binary aggregation with integrity constraints. It is a preservation theorem, though—not a numbered claim that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another complexity classification. The paper has no named result of that latter kind, and its other impossibility theorems are also preservation results, not hardness results.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large online forum or collective-editing community considering one fixed issue. Take the argumentation framework as fixed, and let a voter type be the exact extension—the subset of arguments that voter accepts. The mass \(\mu_\Delta\) is the fraction of the community reporting extension \(\Delta\). This is a complete type for the paper’s model: its aggregation rule uses the reported extension, not the voter’s identity. A large community could have many participants but relatively few distinct report types, so \(\tau\ll n\). The output remains a set of accepted arguments; the society, rather than the outcome, is what becomes continuous.

Continuous Conflict-Freeness Preservation is a precise mirror of Theorem 6. Its instance is an argumentation framework \(AF=(Arg,\rightharpoonup)\) containing an attack \(A\rightharpoonup B\) between distinct arguments with no self-attacks, and a rational threshold \(\alpha\in(0,1]\). For every finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu\) over conflict-free extensions, form

\[ F_\alpha(\mu)=\left\{A\in Arg:\sum_{\Delta\ni A}\mu_\Delta\ge\alpha\right\}. \]

The question is whether \(F_\alpha(\mu)\) is conflict-free for every such \(\mu\). The answer is yes exactly when \(\alpha>1/2\). Above one half, the supporters of two attacking arguments cannot both reach the threshold, since no conflict-free report contains both. At or below one half, put half the mass on \(\{A\}\) and half on \(\{B\}\); the aggregate contains both and is not conflict-free.

This is a faithful population version that the authors could recognize: it asks their preservation question for the same fixed framework and the same extension reports. The finite-to-continuous dictionary is direct: equal voter masses give \(\mu_\Delta=n_\Delta/n\), and threshold \(\alpha=q/n\) reproduces \(F_q\). The mirror’s expected direction is tractability: its preservation answer follows from the threshold comparison and the framework condition. Evaluating a particular explicitly listed society also takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the encoding length \(L\). That algorithmic assessment is for the mirror; it is not a complexity result established in the paper.

The mirror covers only Theorem 6’s conflict-freeness result. It does not cover the paper’s other preservation theorems, and it produces no difficult pricing or optimization problem. That is its weakness: the continuous statement follows almost verbatim from the finite theorem, so it is a credible continuization of the population, but a weak fit for ChoCo’s complexity agenda. I would count it as a positive, narrow mirror—not as evidence that this paper supplies a named computational-complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive gap is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorems 6, 10, 14, and 15 characterize when quota rules preserve semantic properties; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper notes that quota-rule outputs are straightforward to compute, but states no complexity theorem about that task. A computational question built around preservation would be an added problem, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The forum scenario is credible: many participants can assess the same argumentation framework, with an extension as their complete type. For Theorem 6, the continuous rule \(F_\alpha(\mu)=\{A:\sum_{\Delta\ni A}\mu_\Delta\ge\alpha\}\) is also a legitimate population mirror. With rational \(\mu\) and \(\alpha\), a common denominator turns them into a finite profile and quota, so it recasts the theorem’s preservation question in mass notation. That correspondence is not itself a flaw; it shows the mirror is well-posed. But the theorem it mirrors remains a preservation characterization, not a computational result.

One could ask whether a given framework and threshold preserve conflict-freeness for every admissible mass distribution. That would put a decision-problem wrapper around Theorem 6, but it would not supply the missing computational target from the paper. Computing the aggregate for a supplied distribution is just summing support mass for each argument. To get a different algorithmic problem—say, finding a minimum-cost change to the society that restores preservation—one must add an objective the authors do not study. That may be a worthwhile follow-up, but it is not a continuous mirror of their result. So the negative case is strongest on ChoCo’s scope, not on whether the population model makes sense: this paper offers a sound axiomatic mirror, but no worthwhile mirror of a named computational result.
