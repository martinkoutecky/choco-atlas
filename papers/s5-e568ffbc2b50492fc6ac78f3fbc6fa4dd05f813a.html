What the mirror covers

The proposed extension weights the Condorcet and Closeness winner calculations inspired by Theorems 2, 10, and 11; it does not cover the paper's other centrality characterizations or establish a complexity landscape.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results establish properties of centrality measures; none classifies the complexity of computing a winner, solving an optimization problem, or deciding a computational variant. In particular, Theorem 2, proved here, says that Closeness centrality is Condorcet consistent on trees. Theorem 10, also proved here, says that for every graph and every edge \(\{u,v\}\), \(u \succeq v\) exactly when \(C_u(G)\ge C_v(G)\). Theorem 11, proved here, says that Closeness and all other distance-based centralities are not Condorcet consistent in general graphs. These are useful mathematical hooks, but they do not meet the requested complexity-anchor criterion.

The strongest positive mirror I can make from those hooks is a mass-weighted version of the paper’s node-as-voter model. Fix a connected graph \(H=(P,E)\) of candidate sites, and let \(T\subseteq P\) be the occupied voter locations. A type is a location \(x\in T\), whose ranking of candidates is determined by distance in \(H\); its mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of the population at that location, with rational masses summing to one. This fits a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many residents distributed over a fixed set of recurring locations, so the population size is much larger than the number of supported types.

A concrete follow-up problem would be: given \(H,T,\mu\), compute both the weighted Condorcet winners and the weighted Closeness winners. For candidates \(p,q\), define
\[ M_\mu(p,q)=\sum_{x:d(x,p)<d(x,q)}\mu_x-\sum_{x:d(x,q)<d(x,p)}\mu_x, \qquad D_\mu(p)=\sum_{x\in T}\mu_x d(x,p). \]
The solution is the set \(\{p:M_\mu(p,q)>0\text{ for every }q\ne p\}\), together with \(\arg\min_p D_\mu(p)\). Theorem 10’s comparison has a natural weighted counterpart for adjacent candidates: the difference in weighted distance sums is determined by the mass on each side of the edge. Theorem 2 suggests the corresponding tree question, and the paper’s general-graph counterexample remains available by assigning equal mass to its vertices.

This is a plausible population-level extension, but it is not an anchor-compliant complexity case. The paper neither studies weighted populations nor analyzes the computation of these sets; once distances are known, the proposed task is just exact weighted summation. The weakest point is therefore also the central one: this mirror yields a natural continuous model of the paper’s voting interpretation, but no meaningful complexity landscape or algorithmic obstacle grounded in a named result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its theorems characterize relationships between centrality rankings and Condorcet comparisons; they do not study the complexity of computing, optimizing, or deciding anything. That matters for ChoCo: a weighted restatement of a structural theorem can be sound and interesting, yet still leave no computational question for the programme to classify.

The best population model is plausible. Fix a graph of candidate sites and put a rational mass of residents at each site, with preferences determined by distance. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, so I would not object that residents are individuated or that the population cannot be a continuum. But for \(q\) sites and support size \(\tau\), the proposed quantities are just weighted sums: compute distances, then evaluate pairwise margins and \(\sum_x \mu_x d(x,p)\) for each candidate \(p\). The winner sets follow by checking those values.

The three results do not supply a harder anchor. Theorem 2’s tree property extends through weighted subtree masses and leads to the familiar weighted-median calculation. Theorem 10’s adjacent-node identity becomes the same mass-balance equation with weights. Theorem 11’s counterexample can be retained with rational atomic masses, but it remains an impossibility example, not a computational problem. None of these extensions creates the algorithmic question that the paper itself did not pose.

One could add a new task, such as finding the least-cost mass transfer that changes a winner. With fixed sites and transfer costs, that can be formulated using linear constraints over finitely many types. To get a different complexity landscape, one would need to add further combinatorial choices—such as which edges to change or which types can be targeted. Those could be worthwhile research questions, but their computational substance would come from the added intervention model, not from continuizing a result in this paper.

So I would argue against calling this a ChoCo complexity mirror: the natural continuous extension is coherent, but it extends axiomatic characterizations and leaves only routine computation. The universal version of the negative claim is weaker. A weighted population is entirely plausible, and someone could build a valuable follow-up around it; what this paper does not provide is a computational anchor for that work.
