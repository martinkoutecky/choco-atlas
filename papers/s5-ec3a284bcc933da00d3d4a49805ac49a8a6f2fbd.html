What the mirror covers

No result qualifies for a computational mirror; Theorem 1 and the propositions about correspondences establish axiomatic properties and implementability, not computational guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive lead is Theorem 1: for \(n\ge 3\), a social choice correspondence in the single-dipped allocation problem is Nash implementable if and only if it satisfies Maskin monotonicity. The paper derives this from Propositions 1 and 2; the sufficiency argument routes through Doghmi and Ziad’s earlier theorem, while necessity is the standard Maskin condition. This is a substantial implementation result, but it is not a computational-complexity result: it says nothing about whether deciding implementability, finding an allocation, or constructing a mechanism is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally tractable. The other numbered results likewise establish properties such as monotonicity and no-veto-power failure, not complexity bounds. So the paper has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s rule.

There is a plausible continuous-population research direction. Consider a large cooperative or service population with a small number \(\tau\) of recurring preference classes. A type must be the agent’s complete continuous single-dipped preference relation over their consumption, not merely their dip. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of the population with type \(t\), and scale the total resource with population size so that the per-capita supply is \(\omega\). An allocation is then a type-contingent quantity \(x_t\in[0,\omega]\), with feasibility condition
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=\omega. \]
This makes the society itself continuous while retaining the paper’s single-dipped allocation problem. The plausible regime is \(n\gg\tau\), not a small committee.

A corresponding problem could be called Continuous Single-Dipped Nash Implementation: given a finite support \(T\), its preference relations, and a specified correspondence \(F\) from population distributions to feasible allocations, determine whether there is a mechanism whose Nash-equilibrium outcome set at each \(\mu\) is exactly \(F(\mu)\). A solution would be the mechanism and a proof of that equality over the stated domain. That is recognizably an extension of the paper’s implementation question, and it changes the population rather than merely making the commodity divisible.

But this is a proposed extension, not a mirror established by the paper. A continuum model must specify how individual deviations work when one person’s report leaves the aggregate distribution unchanged, and how allocations to individual agents are represented alongside mass feasibility. The finite-agent theorem does not settle those choices or imply its characterization carries over. Nor does the paper provide the finite encodings needed to pose this as a complexity problem for a prover. I would not assign it to Class A, B, or C on this evidence; first one would need a precise equilibrium model and computational input format.

The weakness is decisive for the requested positive case: the paper’s main result is about implementability, not computational complexity, and a continuum version would be a new theorem-level project. Its allocation setting and repeated preference classes make a sensible high-multiplicity scenario plausible, but that plausibility cannot turn Theorem 1 into the required computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only plausible anchor: for \(n\ge 3\), Nash implementability is equivalent to Maskin monotonicity. But it is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. Propositions 1–8 establish implementation or check properties of particular correspondences; none studies the complexity of deciding implementability, finding allocations, or constructing mechanisms. Under ChoCo’s stated threshold, the paper offers no computational result to mirror.

A strong population model is easy to imagine: a large service population with \(\tau\) recurring, fully specified single-dipped preference types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and a resource supply scaled to \(\omega\) per person. A type-contingent allocation \(q_t\) would satisfy \(\sum_t \mu_t q_t=\omega\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The objection is not that the population interpretation fails.

The proposed “Continuous Single-Dipped Nash Implementation” still lacks a computational input. The paper permits arbitrary continuous preference relations and asks whether some mechanism implements a correspondence over a domain of profiles. Neither the correspondence nor the mechanism has a finite representation in the paper. Restricting preferences to, say, rational piecewise-linear functions and supplying a finite encoding of the correspondence could create a complexity problem, but it would add assumptions and an input language absent from the result being mirrored.

One could instead choose a named correspondence such as no-envy and ask for a feasible allocation at a given profile. That would be a concrete allocation algorithm problem, potentially worth studying on its own. It would turn the paper’s axiomatic result into a different computational question. The continuum version of implementation also needs its own strategic model: when outcomes depend only on aggregate type masses, one person’s report does not change those masses. Letting mechanisms assign consumption pointwise to individuals, or letting positive-mass groups deviate, may address that issue, but each requires a further implementation model and theorem.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s eligibility rule: this paper has no computational result to continuize, and its most plausible computational follow-ons would be new questions rather than mirrors. I would not claim that no related algorithmic project could ever be worthwhile; the text cannot support that universal claim. I would reject this paper as a source for a continuous computational mirror.
