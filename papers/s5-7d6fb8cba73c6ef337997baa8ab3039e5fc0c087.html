The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchored case under your rule: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result or giving a named formulation that it computes with. Section 3 does describe LPs for checking compatibility and possible or necessary preference, but that method is unnumbered. The numbered propositions establish structural properties; for example, Proposition 4.5 says that for any alternatives \(a,b\) and indicators \(i,k\), either \(a\) is necessary for all DMs and \(b\) possible for at least one, or \(b\) is possible for all and \(a\) necessary for at least one; it also gives the corresponding necessary-possible / possible-necessary alternative. That is a relation property, not a complexity result.

The most plausible population setting would be a large group of decision makers sharing finitely many types of preference information. But the paper’s group relations ask whether a comparison holds for all or at least one DM; their truth does not depend on the mass assigned to each type. Asking whether it holds for a specified fraction of the group would make mass matter, but would change the paper’s question and would have no eligible named result as an anchor. Also, the paper’s central imprecision is in alternatives’ evaluations, not in the population. So there is a conceivable follow-on problem here, but no qualifying anchor for a continuous mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied an anchor that meets the assignment. Their only named candidate, Proposition 4.5, states logical implications between group preference relations; it asserts no computational result. Section 3 gives LP formulations for checking compatibility and preference relations, but the paper makes no named complexity claim about those computations. So there is no named computational result here for a continuous mirror to test.

The strongest population model would treat decision makers as agents and group them by their complete preference information. Let \(P_h(a,b)\) mean that type \(h\) necessarily prefers \(a\) to \(b\). Proposition 4.5’s “for all” and “for at least one” conditions then become, respectively, \(\mu(\{h:P_h(a,b)\})=1\) and \(\mu(\{h:P_h(a,b)\})>0\). With the paper’s finite type space, these conditions depend only on which types have positive mass, not on their proportions. The proposition’s group quantifiers therefore do not make population mass computationally relevant. The compatible value functions are uncertainty about a decision model, not a population of agents.

One could instead ask whether at least a fraction \(q\) of decision makers necessarily prefer \(a\) to \(b\). That would make the masses matter, and it may be a sensible extension of group decision theory. But it replaces the paper’s all-or-exists group relations with a new quantitative consensus rule; it does not mirror Proposition 4.5. Likewise, putting a distribution over alternatives does not help: the paper asks for relations between particular alternatives, and an aggregate over alternatives does not answer those pairwise questions.

This makes the negative case strong under the required anchoring standard, but weaker as a universal claim. A quantitative consensus extension could be worth studying in its own right. The paper, however, supplies no named computational result for that extension to mirror.
