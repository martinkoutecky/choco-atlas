The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based case: the paper contains no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or giving an algorithm for a computational problem. Its Proposition 3 gives necessary and sufficient conditions for sort-connected networks to exist: every type must have more than \(\kappa\) agents, \(\kappa>1\), and either \(\kappa\) or each type’s population must be even. Theorem 1 gives stability conditions for those networks, and Theorem 2 compares their efficiency with bridged networks. These are substantive structural results, but they are not computational anchors.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime behind the model: a large organization or platform with many agents in a small number of skill, language, or location classes. Type \(x\) would include everything relevant to an agent’s link values and quota; mass \(\mu_x\) would be the fraction of the population of that type. That makes population continuity sensible here. The paper’s real-valued types do not already do this: the realized population \(N\) is finite.

The obstacle is turning that regime into a prover-ready computational mirror of the paper’s results. Pairwise stability is defined through deviations by particular agents, while spillovers depend on network distances. A type-level link mass alone may not preserve either atomic deviations or the topology that determines distances. A continuous model would need to specify what network object retains those features.

So the positive case is a credible *research direction*, but it has no eligible named result to anchor a continuous computational question. That is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 3 characterizes when sort-connected networks exist; Theorem 1 characterizes their stability; Theorem 2 compares their efficiency with bridged networks. Proposition 4 studies implementation, and Proposition 5 gives an asymptotic sorting result. None asks for an algorithm or complexity classification. Those are the nearest named results, but they are structural equilibrium and welfare claims, not computational anchors. A new algorithmic problem inspired by the paper might be worthwhile; it would not be a mirror of a computational result the paper establishes.

The proponent’s organization and skill-class scenario is plausible, so I would not argue that high multiplicity is inherently unreasonable here. The deeper obstacle is that type masses do not capture the object these results analyze: an individual network. Theorem 1 depends on pairwise link changes and network distances; Theorem 2 depends on how a few bridge links change distances across groups. With a fixed degree quota, networks are sparse. Aggregating edges by type loses the topology that determines those distances. A dense graphon limit would also lose bounded-degree edges; preserving them calls for a sparse network limit with local neighborhoods or an explicit sequence of finite graphs. That may be a legitimate new model, but it retains much of the agent-level graph structure instead of turning the problem into computation over type masses.

Each named result makes the limitation visible. For Proposition 3, with fixed \(\kappa\), every type with positive limiting mass eventually has more than \(\kappa\) agents; the finite-count existence condition becomes automatic, while parity is a finite-population detail. Theorem 1’s pairwise deviations are between particular agents: in an atomless model they have zero mass, so an aggregate notion of stability risks making them vacuous. Keeping those deviations meaningful requires retaining agents or local network structure. Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate for an algorithmic descendant—find a welfare-maximizing network—but its bridge and distance effects are precisely what type-level edge masses fail to encode. In the hyperbolic-decay case, the paper itself notes that connected infinite networks have unbounded payoffs; a finite, per-capita limit would need a changed normalization or a different spillover regime.

So the negative case is strongest on fit with ChoCo’s stated programme: there is no computational claim here to mirror, and the obvious routes to one require a new network optimization problem whose core input remains graph structure. I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no such reformulation could ever be worthwhile. A computational study of welfare-maximizing networks in large typed populations could be interesting. But that would be a new project inspired by this paper, not a continuous computational mirror of its results.
