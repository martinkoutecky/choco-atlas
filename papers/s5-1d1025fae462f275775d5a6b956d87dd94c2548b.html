The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finitely supported distribution \(\mu\) with positive binary-rational masses over approval types \(A_t\subseteq C\), find a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) satisfying continuous JUQ. A violation consists of \(c\in W\), \(d\notin W\), and a positive-mass subpopulation \(S\) of supporters of \(d\), such that every type supporting \(c\) has \(|A_t\cap W|>\lceil k\mu(c)\rceil\), while every type represented in \(S\) has \(|A_t\cap((W\cup\{d\})\setminus\{c\})|\le\lceil k\mu(S)\rceil\).

The model it lives in

Approval types carry population mass; candidates and size-\(k\) committees remain discrete. The decision variable is the committee \(W\), with the goal of finding any committee satisfying continuous JUQ. Run weighted UQER: repeatedly remove a JUQ-relevant upper-quota violator with minimum supporter mass, then stop at size \(k\) or when no upper-quota violation remains and choose a size-\(k\) subset.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers JUQ recognition and construction via UQER. It leaves the Thiele-rule characterizations, Adams-AV optimization hardness, JNQ and SLAV results, and compatibility results involving EJR+ untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can Lemma 9 be formally extended to binary-rational type masses when a JUQ witness may take a fractional subpopulation from a type?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the search problem behind Theorem 8, proved in this paper. The theorem says that UQER runs in polynomial time and returns an exhaustive committee satisfying justified upper quota (JUQ): it starts with all candidates, repeatedly removes a candidate whose supporters are all above that candidate’s upper quota, choosing a violating candidate with the fewest supporters. It stops when the committee has size \(k\) or no upper-quota violation remains; in the latter case, it returns any \(k\)-candidate subset. The paper proves that the result satisfies JUQ.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization electing a council from a fixed slate. Members submit approval ballots, with coalitions, endorsements, or standard ballot bundles producing many repeated ballots. A type is a complete approval set \(A_t\subseteq C\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members with that ballot. The relevant regime has many members \(n\) but comparatively few supported ballot types \(\tau\). This grouping is already familiar in the paper: its apportionment setting treats voters with identical ballots as members of the same party.

Here is the lead continuous problem. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(T\subseteq 2^C\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Assume every candidate has positive support, as in the paper. For a candidate \(c\), let
\[ \mu(c)=\sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t,\qquad q_c=k\mu(c), \]
and for a committee \(W\), let \(\operatorname{sat}_W(t)=|A_t\cap W|\).

Define continuous JUQ by translating the paper’s voter groups into measurable portions of the population. A violation consists of a selected candidate \(c\in W\) such that every positive-mass type supporting \(c\) has \(\operatorname{sat}_W(t)>\lceil q_c\rceil\), and an unselected candidate \(d\) with a positive-mass subpopulation \(S\) of its supporters for which every type represented in \(S\) has
\[ \operatorname{sat}_{(W\cup\{d\})\setminus\{c\}}(t) \le \left\lceil k\mu(S)\right\rceil. \]
The problem, Continuous JUQ Committee Construction, asks for a size-\(k\) committee with no such witness. Its solution is the committee itself.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), where \(L\) is the rational-mass bit length. Run UQER on the masses: detect current upper-quota violators, delete one with least support mass, and use the same stopping and subset rule. The key comparison in the paper’s Lemma 9 carries over: supporter counts become masses, and a group \(S\) has quota \(k\mu(S)\). A violating group still has positive mass in some type, so the proof’s witness arguments have a type-level counterpart. The remaining work is to write out that adaptation formally.

The second anchor is Proposition 4, also proved in the paper: given a finite approval profile and a committee, deciding whether the committee satisfies JUQ is polynomial-time. Its continuous mirror, Continuous JUQ Recognition, takes the distribution and a proposed size-\(k\) committee and asks for a yes-or-no answer.

This recognition problem also appears tractable in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). For each possible witness pair \(c,d\), first check whether \(c\)’s positive-mass supporters all exceed \(\lceil k\mu(c)\rceil\). Then, for each integer \(h\in\{1,\dots,k\}\), sum the mass \(M_h\) of \(d\)’s supporters whose satisfaction after the swap is at most \(h\). Such supporters can form a witness group with quota ceiling \(h\) exactly when \(M_h>(h-1)/k\): one can select a submass strictly above \((h-1)/k\) and at most \(h/k\). This finite threshold check handles fractional group masses directly.

These questions stay within the programme’s scope: the population is the continuous object, while candidates, committees, and each person’s satisfaction remain discrete. JUQ itself is the paper’s target, and its quota already scales a group’s size by \(k/n\), making \(k\mu(S)\) the natural continuous counterpart. The paper’s separate statement that computing Adams-AV is NP-hard appears in unnumbered prose and concerns optimizing that rule, rather than finding any JUQ committee. I therefore use the numbered JUQ algorithms as my anchors and make no hardness-dissolution claim.

The mirror covers JUQ verification and construction, not the paper’s Thiele-rule characterizations, JNQ results, or compatibility results involving EJR+. It generates natural follow-ups: whether the continuous UQER output corresponds to limits of discrete outputs under population replication, and whether a JUQ committee can be required to satisfy additional representation axioms.

The soft spot is that Theorem 8 is already a polynomial-time result, and its algorithm and proof seem to transfer almost directly. This is a sound high-multiplicity mirror, but it does not exhibit discrete hardness disappearing in the continuum. The paper’s apportionment model and the group-based definition of JUQ make the continuous question credible; the modesty of the complexity story is the main limitation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is about their marginal research value. Both paper results are already polynomial-time, and their continuous versions appear to need only weighted versions of the same procedures. That makes them less likely to reveal a new algorithmic phenomenon than a mirror whose discrete hardness might dissolve. It does not, under ChoCo’s criteria, defeat either question.

For Theorem 8, the proposed continuous JUQ construction survives. With finitely many approval types, UQER can compare supporter masses, delete a violating candidate of minimum mass, and use the same stopping rule. The key comparisons in Lemma 9 still apply to masses, including when a witness group takes only part of a type’s population. Exact rational comparisons give a plausible \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) algorithm. The proof looks like a weighted adaptation of the paper’s proof, so the skeptical point is that it may be incremental. But the compressed running time and the continuous witness semantics are real parts of the mirror; a near-direct proof is not a fundamental obstruction.

For Proposition 4, the recognition mirror also survives. For each candidate pair and each possible integer satisfaction threshold \(h\), sum the mass \(M_h\) of supporters of the unselected candidate whose post-swap satisfaction is at most \(h\). A qualifying subgroup exists exactly when \(M_h>(h-1)/k\): it can then have mass above that threshold and at most \(h/k\). Since satisfaction is at most \(k\), this gives a finite exact test in \(m,\tau,L\). The same skepticism applies—the discrete verification result makes the extension look routine—but this is a well-posed computational question about a society represented by type masses.

The strongest alternative mirror makes a universal negative harder to defend. The paper says computing Adams-AV is NP-hard because it refines Chamberlin–Courant. A discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of approval type \(t\) embeds as masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Thus an algorithm for the continuous Adams-AV optimization problem would also solve the discrete problem on that embedding. That is a natural complexity mirror, with a clear high-multiplicity interpretation.

So the defensible negative is limited: these particular polynomial-time anchors may offer less conceptual novelty than harder computational mirrors. I cannot make the stronger case that neither anchor is worthwhile in any scenario. Both fit the programme’s population model, and both appear to survive a careful continuous formulation.
