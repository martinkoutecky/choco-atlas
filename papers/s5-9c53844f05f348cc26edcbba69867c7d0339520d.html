The continuous mirror question

Given finite goods \(G\), a finite support \(T\) of buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and each type's utility vector \(U_{tg}\), bargaining weight \(\beta_t>0\), and disagreement utility \(c_t\ge 0\), compute an optimal allocation \(x_{tg}\) and supporting equilibrium prices, or report infeasibility. The allocation must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tg}\le q_g\), \(z_t\le\sum_g U_{tg}x_{tg}\), and \(z_t>c_t\), and maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t\log(z_t-c_t)\). The algorithmic target is a running time polynomial in \(|G|\), \(|T|\), and the binary input length.

The model it lives in

A type-level nonsymmetric ADNB market: each type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\) and per-buyer allocation \(x_{tg}\); aggregate use of good \(g\) is \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tg}\), bounded by its per-capita supply \(q_g\). Maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t\log(z_t-c_t)\) over feasible allocations, with prices supporting the market-equilibrium KKT conditions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10.2's nonsymmetric ADNB computation after grouping repeated buyers by complete type; it leaves the general flow algorithm and the paper's other market applications outside the population mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass-weighted problem admit an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|G|\), \(|T|\), and the binary input length, including equilibrium-price output or infeasibility detection?
  • Can the paper's exact-recovery argument handle rational masses and supplies without expanding the denominator into individual buyers?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass-ADNB Equilibrium, a continuous-population version of the paper’s nonsymmetric Arrow–Debreu Nash bargaining problem.

Take a finite set \(G\) of divisible goods and a finite support \(T\) of buyer types, with \(\tau=|T|\). The input gives each type’s population mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); its utility vector \(U_{tg}\); its bargaining weight \(\beta_t>0\); and its disagreement utility \(c_t\ge 0\). The supply \(q_g\) is per unit of population, so total supply scales with the population. Choose a per-buyer allocation \(x_{tg}\ge0\) and utility \(z_t\) for each type, subject to
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tg}\le q_g,\qquad z_t\le\sum_{g\in G}U_{tg}x_{tg},\qquad z_t>c_t. \]
Maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\beta_t\log(z_t-c_t). \]
A solution is an optimal allocation together with supporting prices satisfying the market-equilibrium KKT conditions, or a report that no feasible allocation exists.

The regime is a large market of many buyers drawn from a small menu of standardized profiles—for example, a resource-allocation platform with many users whose valuations, bargaining weights, and outside options come from a few fixed account templates. A type’s complete description includes every parameter the model uses. Mass is the fraction of buyers of that type, rather than a count that allows one-person targeting. When \(N_t\) discrete buyers share a type, \(\mu_t=N_t/N\); with divisible goods they can receive the same per-buyer bundle, and their aggregate allocation is \(\mu_t x_{tg}\). Thus the support size can be far smaller than the population. This is a population mirror: the goods remain divisible as in the paper, but divisibility of outcomes is not the continuization being proposed.

The anchor is Theorem 10.2, proved in this paper. It states that for nonsymmetric ADNB, with nonnegative integer utilities, bargaining weights, and disagreement values, under the paper’s stated positive-utility incidence assumptions and unique-optimum setup, let \(n=|G|+|B|\), let \(m\) count positive-utility buyer–good pairs, and set \(K=nRU_{\max}\), where \(R\) is the maximum bargaining weight and \(U_{\max}\) the largest utility. With \(T=U^*=\max\{C,nK\log K\}\) and \(\varepsilon=1/(2K^nU^*)\), the conditions of Theorem 10.1 hold and the algorithm finds an optimal solution in
\[ O\!\left(m(m+n\log n)\bigl(n\log(nRU_{\max})+\log C\bigr)\right) \]
time. Here \(C\) is the largest disagreement value. The article proves this result; it is an exact algorithmic anchor, not a cited result. The paper’s account of rational optimal solutions and exact recovery is part of why an exact mass version is plausible.

There is a direct aggregate formulation in the paper’s concave generalized-flow model. Put aggregate allocation \(y_{tg}=\mu_t x_{tg}\) on a good-to-type arc with gain \(U_{tg}\), give good \(g\) supply \(q_g\), and set the type’s demand to \(\mu_t c_t\). If \(a_t\) is aggregate surplus utility on the type-to-sink arc, give that arc gain function \(\Gamma_t(a)=\mu_t\beta_t\log a\). Since \(a_t=\mu_t(z_t-c_t)\), maximizing the resulting sink objective differs from the stated objective only by the constant \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t\log\mu_t\). The market has one node per good and one per supported type, rather than one per buyer. I expect the rational, finite-support problem to be tractable (Class A): the paper’s flow machinery directly accommodates the aggregate concave model, and rational mass data can be represented by their binary encodings. Extending the exact recovery and running-time proof to those rational weights is a proof obligation; Theorem 10.2 does not already establish it. The paper’s general approximate concave-flow algorithm is the immediate route to an approximate version.

The mirror covers Theorem 10.2’s nonsymmetric ADNB result and its market-equilibrium application. It does not recast the paper’s general flow problem as a population problem. Further questions are whether exact recovery remains polynomial in \(|G|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length of the masses; whether the algorithm can return quantitatively controlled equilibrium prices as well as an optimal allocation; and how approximation of the Nash objective translates into market-clearing and demand errors.

The weakest point is that Theorem 10.2 lists buyers individually and does not state a support-size bound for repeated profiles or rational population weights. The proposed mirror depends on proving that aggregation by complete type preserves the equilibrium and that the exact rational-recovery argument survives the mass coefficients. The concave-flow representation gives a concrete path to that proof, but the anchor alone does not finish it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 10.2 does not prove the proposed mass result. Its input has one node per named buyer, integer data, and an exact-recovery argument that relies on uniqueness of an allocation. It gives no running-time bound in the support size \(\tau\) and the bit length of rational masses. Theorem 2.1 offers an approximation route, but its value-oracle and unit-cost arithmetic model does not by itself establish exact bit complexity. So the proponent should not present the mass theorem as already following from either result.

That objection does not defeat the mirror. If \(N_t\) buyers share a complete type, averaging their bundles preserves feasibility, and concavity of the log objective means equal per-buyer bundles are optimal for that type. Conversely, a type-level allocation can be given to every copy. For rational masses, choose a common denominator \(D\) and write \(N_t=D\mu_t\). The resulting finite market has \(N_t\) copies of each type, and its objective is \(D\) times the mass objective, so the optimizers agree. A direct expansion may take time polynomial in \(D\), which need not be polynomial in the mass input’s bit length. But the type-level flow formulation has only \(|G|+\tau\) nodes; adapting the exact-recovery argument to its scaled rational coefficients is a concrete algorithmic question, not a modeling dead end.

Nor can I dismiss the regime as implausible: standardized buyer profiles with repeated valuations and outside options are a recognizable market scenario. Completeness of types may make \(\tau\) large in some markets, but it does not rule out low-support regimes. And the paper’s divisible goods do not make the population continuous; the proposed mirror changes the buyer population itself.

So the case against can establish a gap in what the paper proves and a proof obligation for the proposed exact algorithm. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 10.2 is a strong anchor, and the compressed rational-mass version appears to survive the strongest objection.
