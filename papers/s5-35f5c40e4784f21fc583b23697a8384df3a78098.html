The case FOR (proponent)

I see a plausible continuous population model for this paper. Imagine a large annotation platform with many workers drawn from a small number \(\tau\) of stable behavioral types. A type would specify everything the mechanism uses: labeling accuracy, effort costs, and how the worker’s effort and reporting respond to payments. The mass \(\mu_\theta\) of type \(\theta\) is that type’s share of the workforce. Each round, the requester chooses the paper’s shared payment scale \(a_t\), workers label a fixed task load per capita, and the mechanism infers labels from the reports. The objective is to maximize discounted requester utility per unit of population while inducing truthful, high-effort work. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for a large platform; it does not rely on making labels or payments themselves continuous.

A precise candidate is Continuous Crowdsourcing Incentive Control. Its instance consists of a finite type set \(\Theta\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), the binary-label distribution and per-capita task load, a finite horizon and action set, the paper’s scoring and utility functions, and each type’s complete response model, including its effort costs and state transitions under payments. The response model is part of the mathematical instance but is hidden from the policy, which observes only reports and inference outputs. The question is to find a policy mapping those observations to payment scales that maximizes expected discounted requester utility per capita and makes truthful reporting with high effort an equilibrium for every positive-mass type. A solution is such an observation-based policy and its expected utility. This mirrors the paper’s sequential incentive-design problem, though specifying a type-level response model is a modeling choice needed to make the optimization question well-posed.

There is a strict limitation on the anchors: the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the requested kind—no theorem that establishes a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest named results are proved here, but they are convergence and incentive-compatibility results, not complexity classifications. Theorem 2 says that when \(M\gg1\), \(\prod_{i=1}^N(2P_{i,H})^2\ge M\), and \(a_t>\max_i c_{i,H}/(P_{i,H}-0.5)\), truthful reporting and high effort are utility-maximizing for each worker at equilibrium when the others use that strategy. Theorem 3 adds the condition that the learned \(Q\)-function approaches the real one and, for each worker \(i\), \(\eta M\sum_{x\ne i}P_{x,H}G_A>[F(1)-F(1-\psi_i)]/(1-\gamma)\), where \(\psi_i=(\tau_{-1}/\tau_{+1}+\tau_{+1}/\tau_{-1})\prod_{x\ne i}\sqrt{4P_{x,H}(1-P_{x,H})}\); it concludes that truthful reporting and high effort are utility-maximizing in the long run when the other workers do so. Theorem 1 bounds estimation error for the inference algorithm under its stated large-\(M\) and worker-quality conditions. These results support the model’s plausibility, but none can serve as the required complexity anchor.

So the honest positive case is that a continuous worker-population version of the paper’s incentive problem is recognizable and worth asking. Its complexity class is not established by this paper; the natural follow-up is whether the finite-type, finite-horizon policy problem admits efficient exact or approximate algorithms, and how that changes with \(\tau\), horizon, and the hidden response models. The weakest point is also substantial: the paper’s guarantees scale with the number of tasks \(M\), while its worker population \(N\) is finite, and its payment rule scores individual workers. Extending its inference and incentive guarantees to a continuum of worker mass would require new work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on fit to ChoCo’s computational question, rather than on whether crowdsourcing can have a high-multiplicity regime. A large annotation platform with many workers per behavioral type is plausible. The problem is that the paper proves no computational-complexity result to continue: Theorem 1 is an estimation-convergence guarantee, and Theorems 2 and 3 are incentive-compatibility guarantees. None classifies a computational problem by its running time or hardness. The proposed continuous policy-optimization problem would therefore be a new result agenda, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 1 is a tempting anchor because it has an algorithm and a convergence guarantee. But its limit is in the number of tasks \(M\), for a finite panel of individually tracked workers whose accuracies are estimated from their repeated reports. Turning workers into a continuum leaves a consequential modeling choice. If infinitely many workers report on each of finitely many shared tasks, the aggregate can reveal the task truths by a law of large numbers, while individual accuracies disappear from the aggregate. If each worker instead has a fixed per-capita task load and each task receives finitely many reports, the individual sample size does not grow with population mass, so the theorem’s concentration argument does not transfer. One could impose a type-based reporting and task-assignment model and prove a new convergence result for it. That would be a statistical limit theorem with additional assumptions about task overlap and observations, rather than a computational-complexity question raised by this paper.

Theorem 2 is about a named worker’s one-step best response: with the other workers truthful and high-effort, a payment scale above the stated cost-to-quality threshold makes those actions utility-maximizing. A continuum version can preserve individual scores and ask for the same incentive guarantee by type; its threshold would be expressed over the types in the population. That is a coherent economic question, but it remains an incentive theorem, not an algorithmic complexity problem. If instead scores and inference are aggregated by type, the proof’s estimate of each worker’s own PoBC no longer directly applies, and establishing incentives requires a different mechanism and a new proof.

Theorem 3 extends that incentive claim over time, assuming the learned \(Q\)-function approaches the true one and imposing a sufficient condition involving the effects of other workers’ payments. A mass version could replace the finite sum over workers with an integral. That would again be a continuum incentive-compatibility theorem. The proponent’s stronger alternative—optimize the full observation-based payment policy—goes beyond this result: the paper presents RIL as an approximate reinforcement-learning method, not as a solution to an exact policy-optimization problem. Supplying type masses, response models, and a prior could turn the alternative into a finite-horizon partially observed control problem, but its computational challenge would come from generic planning under hidden dynamics, rather than from a computational question this paper formulated.

So the three named results do not yield three computational mirrors: one concerns statistical estimation, and two concern incentives. A useful continuous crowdsourcing project may exist, especially for large platforms, but making it fit ChoCo’s complexity programme would require adding a new algorithmic problem that the paper neither states nor analyzes. The universal claim that no scenario could ever make such work worthwhile is too strong; the case against is that the proposed mirror is not yet a continuation of this paper’s computational results.
