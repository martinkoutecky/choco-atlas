Theorem 1 represents WIC merging operators with plausibility preorders; Theorem 2 proves that a class of distance based operators satisfies the WIC postulates; Proposition 4 specializes that result to sum and \(G_{\max}\) aggregation. These are logical characterization results, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise has a computational complexity classification. So the number of anchors under your rule is zero, and I can’t honestly attach a complexity class to a continuous problem *per anchor*.

The strongest plausible mirror, if an unanchored candidate is useful, is continuous weighted distance based belief merging. Let each type \(t\) be a consistent belief formula \(\varphi_t\) and reliability \(\alpha_t>0\), with rational population mass \(\lambda_t\), where \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\). Given a consistent integrity constraint \(\chi\), a finite propositional alphabet, and Hamming distance \(d_H\), find the set of interpretations minimizing
\[ \sum_t \lambda_t\alpha_t\, d_H(\omega,\varphi_t), \qquad \omega\models\chi, \]
where \(d_H(\omega,\varphi_t)\) is the minimum Hamming distance from \(\omega\) to a model of \(\varphi_t\). A plausible high multiplicity regime is a large institutional consultation on a fixed agenda, where many participants share both a canonicalized belief base and a reliability category: the number of participants is large, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is relatively small. Normalizing the counts to masses preserves the sum operator’s minimizers, and the mirror keeps the paper’s sources, weights, constraints, and decision over interpretations.

That candidate tracks Theorem 2 and Proposition 4’s operator construction, but neither result establishes its computational complexity. The paper therefore supports a credible continuous model while supplying no named computational result to anchor the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes operators by plausibility preorders; Theorem 2 shows that distance based constructions satisfy the postulates; Propositions 4–8 classify further operator behavior. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a hardness result. The introduction’s references to complexity work do not supply a result of this paper. So the proponent’s case has no eligible anchor.

Their proposed optimization is a sensible adjacent question, and a large consultation with repeated belief bases and reliability categories is a plausible high multiplicity setting. But with their chosen sum aggregation and multiplicative weights, its objective is
\[ \sum_t \lambda_t\alpha_t\,d_H(\omega,\varphi_t). \]
Set \(\alpha'_t=\lambda_t\alpha_t\). This is exactly the finite weighted profile objective with one source per supported type and reliability \(\alpha'_t\); the paper allows arbitrary positive real reliabilities. In this version, population mass adds no independent computational input: it is absorbed into reliability. That makes the proposal a reparameterization of the paper’s operator, not a continuous computational result established by it.

Other aggregation choices could preserve a distinct role for mass, but they would require defining a new continuum operator and proving new properties. For example, the paper’s \(G_{\max}\) aggregation uses a lexicographically ordered list of per-agent distances, and the paper gives no canonical interpretation of that list for fractional mass. Developing one may be worthwhile, but it would be a new project rather than a mirror of a named computational finding here.

That is a strong case against treating this paper as an anchored continuous-complexity mirror. The broader claim—that no continuous-population optimization problem arising from belief merging could be worth studying—is weaker: the consultation scenario is credible, and its complexity could be studied as new work. The paper itself simply gives no computational result for that work to mirror.
