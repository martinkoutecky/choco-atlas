The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a positive case, but the anchor rule exposes a real gap: this paper has named theorems about optimization structure and statistical consistency, but no theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The experiments do not supply such an anchor either. So there is no fully compliant complexity-anchored case here. The closest results support a precise candidate mirror, but they do not prove its computational complexity.

The strongest adjacent anchor is Theorem 8, proved here (its “if” direction uses the earlier result of Khetan and Oh). It states that for the Plackett–Luce model, RBCML\((G,W)\) is consistent if and only if \(G\) is a weighted union of position-\(k\) breakings and \(W\) is connected and symmetric. A second, narrower result is Theorem 9, also proved here: for a RUM with a common symmetric utility distribution \(\pi\) satisfying the condition in Theorem 7—that \((\ln \pi(x))'\) is monotonically decreasing and tends to \(+\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\)—RBCML\((G,W)\) is consistent if and only if \(G\) is uniform and \(W\) is connected and symmetric. These are characterizations of when an estimator recovers the true parameter as sample size grows; neither is a runtime or hardness theorem.

A concrete lead mirror is Population Plackett–Luce Composite-Likelihood Estimation. Fix \(m\) alternatives, a breaking \(G\), and weights \(W\). The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings \(R\in L(A)\), listed by its \(\tau\) positive-mass rankings. Here a type is a ranking, and \(\mu_R\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. For each pair \(a_i,a_j\), let
\[ \kappa_{ij}(\mu)=\sum_R \mu_R\,G^{a_i\succ a_j}(R), \]
where \(G^{a_i\succ a_j}(R)\) is the breaking weight assigned to that comparison in ranking \(R\). With \(\theta_m=0\), the task is to return a global maximizer of
\[ F_{G,W}(\theta;\mu) =\sum_{i\ne j}\kappa_{ij}(\mu)w_{ij} \log \Pr_{\mathrm{PL}}(a_i\succ a_j\mid\theta). \]
Require the weighted comparison graph to be strongly connected so a finite maximizer exists. The solution is the maximizing utility vector. The corresponding second mirror replaces Plackett–Luce probabilities with pairwise probabilities from the fixed common symmetric RUM in Theorem 9, and uses uniform \(G\).

A plausible regime is a large consumer panel ranking a fixed menu: perhaps hundreds of thousands of respondents but only dozens or hundreds of distinct rankings, with respondents sharing a type receiving identical treatment in the estimator. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; it does not rely on the paper’s particular experimental setting. For Plackett–Luce, I would tentatively expect the population optimization problem to be tractable: the objective has the concave optimization structure discussed in Theorem 3, and the pairwise probabilities have closed forms. For the symmetric-RUM version, tractability depends on being able to evaluate its pairwise probabilities efficiently. Those are expectations, not complexity results established by Theorems 8 or 9; bit complexity and approximation guarantees remain questions.

The mirror covers the paper’s RBCML estimation and consistency results, especially Theorems 8 and 9. It does not mirror the paper’s separate analytic results on log-concavity preservation. The key weakness is that this continuous objective is very close to the population limit the paper already analyzes: its expected composite log-likelihood is the limit of the empirical objective. Moreover, an empirical profile can already be compressed into ranking masses. So this may be a useful high-multiplicity formulation, but the paper offers no hardness anchor whose difficulty might dissolve, and the proposed mirror may be judged a restatement of its statistical population analysis rather than a new computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither Theorem 8 nor Theorem 9 is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 8 characterizes when a Plackett–Luce estimator is statistically consistent; Theorem 9 does the same for a class of symmetric RUMs. The log-concavity results give useful structure, and the paper reports runtime experiments, but none of these states a complexity bound or hardness classification for a computational problem. Under the requested anchor rule, neither theorem supports a compliant mirror.

The strongest version of the first proposed mirror would take a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types and maximize the Plackett–Luce composite likelihood \(F_{G,W}(\theta;\mu)\). That is a coherent high-multiplicity formulation: a large panel on a fixed menu can plausibly contain many respondents with the same observed ranking. But when \(\mu_R=n_R/n\), its pairwise weights are exactly the paper’s empirical \(\kappa_{ij}\), so it uses the same objective and sufficient statistics. This overlap weakens the claim that Theorem 8 supplies a new computational question; it does not, by itself, make the population formulation invalid. If \(\mu\) is arbitrary rather than generated by the stated model, the task becomes a new quasi-likelihood problem, and Theorem 8’s consistency characterization no longer anchors it.

The same problem applies to Theorem 9’s symmetric-RUM mirror. A distribution over observed ranking types can sensibly describe a large population, but the theorem characterizes statistical recovery under a specified data-generating model. It does not classify the cost of optimizing over that population. If pairwise probabilities are expensive to evaluate, that difficulty comes from the utility-distribution integrals, not from representing the society by ranking masses. If \(\mu\) is outside the model, the proposed computation is again a new estimation problem rather than a mirror of Theorem 9.

A stronger adjacent project would use a population distribution to choose \(G\) and \(W\) for statistical efficiency—the paper itself identifies optimal breaking and weights as future work. That could be a worthwhile algorithmic question. But it is a new estimator-design problem, not a continuous version of either consistency theorem. So the best negative case is strong against the proponent’s claimed anchors, but weak as a universal claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile. The population regime is sensible; what is missing here is a named computational result for it to mirror.
