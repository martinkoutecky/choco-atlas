The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper under the stated anchor rule. It contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or parameterized complexity.

The numbered theorems are about asymptotic probabilities. For example, Theorem 1 proves that, for any positive sequence \((h_n)\), there is a continuous competence distribution with \(P_e(n)\le h_n\); Theorem 2 gives a distribution for which \(P_e(n)\to1\). Theorems 3–10 bound or construct possible asymptotic behaviours of \(P_m(n)\). These are substantive results, but they do not anchor a complexity question.

A continuum of experts with competence types \(p\) and mass \(\mu\) is imaginable, but the paper’s expert rule follows one highest-ranked individual, while its results concern rare order-statistic events in finite random committees. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, so preserving that rule and those probabilities would require changing the problem. I don’t think that would be an honest mirror of the paper’s named results.

So my case for a continuous mirror is no eligible anchor: the paper studies asymptotic probability, not computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor objection is decisive, but the stronger reason is that the paper’s probability model and a continuous society are different objects. Theorems 1 and 2 concern the expert rule under independent draws of \(n\) individually ranked experts. Theorems 3–6 do the same for the majority rule with continuous competence laws; Theorems 7–10 cover discontinuous laws. They give asymptotic probability bounds and constructions, but none states a computational problem’s complexity or gives an algorithm to mirror.

The competence law \(G\) does not make the society continuous in ChoCo’s sense. It is the sampling law for each expert’s competence. The realized committee remains a finite list, and the results depend on its order statistics. The strongest proposed mass reformulation would treat competence levels as types with fixed masses. That loses the expert rule’s individual maximum: in an atomless population, the most competent individual has zero mass, while giving that individual positive mass changes the rule. For majority, the mass version compares fixed integrals over the lower and upper halves of the competence distribution. For a nontrivial distribution, that is a deterministic comparison; it removes the rare finite-sample event whose probability the paper studies.

One could retain the sampling and ask for an algorithm to compute \(P_e(n)\) or \(P_m(n)\) from an explicit \(G\). That is a possible computational follow-up, but \(n\) still counts sampled individuals; no high-multiplicity population has replaced them. A large-deviation formulation over empirical measures is the better attempt at a population mirror, but it misses key behavior: the expert results can depend on an extreme individual invisible to the limiting empirical measure, while for continuous \(G\), Theorems 5–6 allow majority probabilities to decay faster than every fixed exponential rate yet at arbitrarily varying subleading rates. Capturing those distinctions needs the finite-sample extremes and fluctuations back in the model.

So the negative case is strong for rejecting this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror. The universal claim needs one qualification: a new computational study could be built around competence distributions or sampling probabilities. But that would be a follow-up to the paper’s probabilistic model, not a worthwhile continuous-population mirror of its named results.
