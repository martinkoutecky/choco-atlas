The anchor — algorithmic

Proposition 11

Every anchor argued

  • Proposition 11 — survived: The opponent engaged this anchor, but its concern that population mass adds no computational structure bounds the mirror to homogeneous replication rather than eliminating it.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(X_m=\{1,\ldots,m\}^2\) and a unit-mass society whose sole preference type is the coordinatewise preorder \(\succsim_m\), choose a common complete preorder \(\rho\) minimizing \(\int_T D(\succsim_t,\rho)\,d\mu(t)\); determine the minimizer.

The model it lives in

The society has one type, \(\succsim_m\), with mass \(\mu_{\succsim_m}=1\). Its common complete preorder \(\rho\) minimizes average top-difference loss, which reduces to \(D(\succsim_m,\rho)\); Proposition 11 gives the sum order \(\succsim_m^+\).

The objection that survived

The paper treats one preference relation at a time, so the mass-weighted objective is vacuous when the population has only one type; the mirror therefore does not support claims about heterogeneous aggregation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 11 for a society with one preference type; it leaves the paper’s general best-approximation problem and any heterogeneous population objective unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For \(\tau>1\), when can a common complete preorder minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t D(\succsim_t,\rho)\) be computed efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead is a homogeneous-population Pareto best-completion problem. The paper does not give a named complexity classification for computing best complete approximations in general. The anchor here is narrower: a named exact solution for a structured family.

Anchor — Proposition 11, proved here by applying Theorem 5. For every positive integer \(m\), let \(X_m=\{1,\ldots,m\}^2\), and define the coordinatewise order by \(x\succsim_m y\) exactly when \(x_1\ge y_1\) and \(x_2\ge y_2\). Among all complete preference relations, the unique one minimizing the top-difference distance from \(\succsim_m\) is the sum order \(x\succsim_m^+ y\) exactly when \(x_1+x_2\ge y_1+y_2\). The paper says the result follows because this coordinatewise order satisfies Condition \((\ast)\); it leaves that verification to the reader.

Here is the continuous mirror I would put to a prover. There is a large population of decision-makers who evaluate the same \(m\times m\) set of alternatives by the same two criteria and share the same scales. Thus there is one preference type, the incomplete coordinatewise order \(\succsim_m\), with mass \(\mu_{\succsim_m}=1\). A common complete ranking \(\rho\) will be used for downstream choices. Find a total preorder \(\rho\) minimizing the population’s average choice discrepancy,
\[ \int_T D(\succsim_t,\rho)\,d\mu(t). \]
For this promised one-type instance, the objective is simply \(D(\succsim_m,\rho)\), and the solution is \(\rho=\succsim_m^+\). The discrete high-multiplicity counterpart has \(N\) such decision-makers, all of the same type; the continuous input records their type’s mass rather than \(N\) separate copies. The number of agents can be very large while the support size is \(\tau=1\).

This is a credible mirror of the paper’s underlying problem: its decision variable is still a complete approximation, and its objective still measures choice discrepancies across menus. The authors explicitly motivate complete approximations in settings with incomplete preferences, including voting data and multi-agent decision units. In the stated family, I expect tractability: Proposition 11 gives the answer directly by ranking alternatives according to the sum of their two coordinates.

The scope is just Proposition 11’s Pareto-grid family. A natural next question is whether the same common-proxy objective is tractable when \(\mu\) has several distinct incomplete-preference types:
\[ \min_{\rho\in\mathbb P_C(X)}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t D(\succsim_t,\rho). \]
Proposition 11 does not settle that heterogeneous problem.

The weak point is substantial: in the anchored family, the continuous population is homogeneous, so its mass adds no computational structure; the result is the paper’s original single-preference problem repeated across many agents. And Proposition 11 is an exact-solution result, not a named complexity classification. I would present this as a narrow, faithful mirror, not evidence that the paper establishes a broader continuous complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 11 is the strongest anchor, but its coordinates are not people: \(X_m\) is a set of two-dimensional alternatives, ordered by Pareto dominance. The result says that for this one partial order, the closest complete preference is the sum order. In ChoCo terms, \(m\) controls the alternative space; there is no population-size or support parameter in the problem. The paper’s Section 6 also treats computational methods for finding best complete approximations as future work, rather than giving a complexity analysis.

The proposed one-type population does not change that problem. Its average loss is
\[ \int_T D(\succsim_m,\rho)\,d\mu(t)=D(\succsim_m,\rho), \]
so the mass contributes no input to the optimization. That alone would not make the question worthless, but it leaves no population-driven computational question to study: the theorem remains about completing a preorder on a grid of alternatives. Reading the two coordinates as agents would give only two criteria; extending them to a large or continuous set of agents changes the alternative space and the Pareto order, so Proposition 11 no longer supplies the anchor.

A stronger candidate would use a distribution of different incomplete preferences on the same alternatives and seek one common completion minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_tD(\succsim_t,\rho)\). That is a coherent population problem, and the paper’s discussion of multi-agent decision units gives it some motivation. But the weighted common-proxy objective is an added aggregation rule: the paper approximates one relation at a time. Its group example instead forms a single relation by intersecting members’ preferences, which depends on the types present, not their masses. So the weighted version could be worthwhile research, but it is not a continuous mirror of Proposition 11; the intersection version has no mass-sensitive continuization.

The negative case is therefore strongest as a scope judgment, not a proof that no plausible scenario exists. Large groups with incomplete preferences are credible, and the authors might recognize a population-weighted approximation problem as a useful extension. What the paper itself does not establish is a population-computational result for that problem.
