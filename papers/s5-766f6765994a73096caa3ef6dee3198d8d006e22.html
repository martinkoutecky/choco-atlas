The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of researcher types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational effort weights \(w_t>0\), with reward \(F(w)=w^2\) and equal credit \(\phi=\tfrac12\), find a rational allocation of mass to solo projects and pairs that is pairwise stable under the paper’s deviation rule. A pair of types \(t,u\) gives each participant utility \(\tfrac12(w_t+w_u)^2\); a solo participant of type \(t\) gets \(w_t^2\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of researcher types with masses \(\mu_t\) and effort weights \(w_t\). Output rational pair masses \(q_{tu}=q_{ut}\) and solo masses \(s_t\) satisfying \(\mu_t=s_t+2q_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}q_{tu}\), such that no researcher prefers going solo and no pair of researchers can both strictly benefit by pairing. This is a feasibility problem with no separate objective.

The objection that survived

With equal credit, the mirror strips away any strategic effect of author order and reduces to a simple type-matching problem; this weakens its relevance to the programme, though it does not invalidate the mirrored stability question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s existence and polynomial-time computation result for alphabetical ordering with indivisible budgets. It leaves the contribution-based results, free-riding bounds, research-quality comparisons, and multiple-project rotations aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Alphabetical Mass-Stability, a population version of the paper’s one-project-per-researcher game.

The anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: for an academic game with different agents and indivisible budgets, alphabetical ordering guarantees a pairwise-stable coalition structure, and one can be computed in polynomial time. Here, pairwise stability means that no researcher benefits by working alone and no pair can both benefit by forming a collaboration. The theorem’s algorithm sorts researchers by effort and greedily considers the two heaviest remaining researchers. The paper contains no named NP-hardness result to anchor; its relevant computational results are tractability claims.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large research programme running a cycle of interchangeable two-person projects. Each researcher has one project slot and an effort budget from a small set of standard tiers. Researchers in the same tier are indistinguishable for the model’s purposes: they have the same effort, reward function, and credit convention. The population grows while the number \(\tau\) of effort tiers stays modest. Alphabetical ordering gives equal credit, as in the theorem.

Formally, let the types be \(T\), with effort weight \(w_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) and population mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) for each \(t\in T\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Fix the paper’s reward function to \(F(w)=w^2\), a case covered by its model. A solo researcher of type \(t\) gets utility \(w_t^2\); each member of a pair of types \(t,u\) gets utility \(\tfrac12(w_t+w_u)^2\).

Let \(q_{tu}=q_{ut}\) be the mass of projects pairing types \(t\) and \(u\), and let \(s_t\) be the mass of type-\(t\) researchers working alone. Feasibility requires
\[ \mu_t=s_t+2q_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}q_{tu}. \]
The problem is: given the rational masses and weights, find feasible \(q,s\) such that no researcher prefers going solo and no pair of researchers would both strictly benefit by pairing, evaluated at their current utilities as in the paper’s pairwise-stability notion. A solution is the exact rational table of pair and singleton masses. Each researcher still receives an integral assignment to one solo project or one two-person project; continuity represents how mass is distributed across these assignments.

This mirror is Class A. Set \(q_{tt}=\mu_t/2\) and all other \(q,s\) to zero: pair every cohort internally. Each type-\(t\) researcher then gets \(2w_t^2\), which is at least the solo utility. For any proposed cross-type pair, the heavier type already gets at least as much utility from its same-type partner as from the proposed pair, so the two researchers cannot both strictly gain. This gives a stable solution in \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations, polynomial in the input bit length \(L\). The simplicity is a consequence of allowing cohort mass to split: the mass of each type can be paired internally without the parity issue a finite group may have.

The connection to the authors’ problem is direct: the mirror retains their project rewards, credit convention, one-project budgets, and stability question, while representing repeated researchers by type masses. The co-modification is the mass allocation needed to describe those repeated agents. This covers the alphabetical, indivisible-budget results of Section 3, not the paper’s multiple-project rotations, free-riding bounds, or contribution-based ordering. The latter is a natural follow-up: characterize stable type-mass allocations when first authors receive unequal credit.

The soft spot is that internal same-type pairing makes the continuous version much easier than the finite matching problem. The mirror therefore does not show that discrete hardness dissolves, and this paper supplies no hardness anchor in the first place. It is a credible, narrow population mirror of Theorem 5, though its subject—stable collaboration matching—is adjacent to ChoCo’s core of social-choice problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about whether the mirror still studies the paper’s authorship question. Theorem 5 is a named computational result, so the “no result to mirror” objection does not apply. Its continuous counterpart also has a plausible population: many interchangeable researchers with effort from a small set of tiers. In the paper’s model, those researchers really are indistinguishable for the alphabetical case, where \(\phi=\tfrac12\).

But that faithful specialization drains the authorship convention of its role. Equal credit makes alphabetical order irrelevant to utility; the proposed same-tier pairing is stable because each researcher gets \(2w_t^2\), and a heavier researcher cannot gain from pairing with a lighter one. The result follows directly from the reward function. It says little about how an ordering convention affects a research community.

A richer story—many researchers in a large programme choosing collaborators for repeated projects—could make the population credible. Yet to make author order consequential, it must reintroduce individual distinctions such as names, contribution ranks, or reputations. If those distinctions affect payoffs, they belong in the type; the number of types may then grow with the population, undermining the proposed high-multiplicity regime. If they do not affect payoffs, the model remains an aggregate matching problem rather than a study of authorship credit.

That is a real reason to question the mirror’s fit with ChoCo: the continuous version seems to be a very simple stable-matching problem, not a new computational question about collective choice. It is not, however, enough to defeat the anchor. The brief explicitly allows a mirror whose answer is simple, and the paper’s formal model omits the identity effects that would make my objection decisive. A mass version of Theorem 5 is mathematically coherent and arguably recognizable to the authors. The negative case is therefore weak here: I can question its value to the programme, but I cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
