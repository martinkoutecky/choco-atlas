The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is deliberately narrow: the paper’s two-type bipartite case, where the central object is the mass of unmatched requests by type.

The anchor is Lemma 24, proved in this paper. It considers two points \(a,b\), with same-side matching forbidden, \(d(a,a)=d(b,b)=\infty\), and cross-side matching free, \(d(a,b)=d(b,a)=0\). Requests arrive at each point with rate \(1/2\). For a random sequence of \(m\) requests, greedily matching any pending \(a\)-request with a pending \(b\)-request has expected cost \(\Omega(m\sqrt m)\). The paper says this algorithm is optimal both online and offline. Its proof writes the cost as \(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}P_iW_i\), where \(P_i\) is the outstanding type imbalance after request \(i\), and \(W_i\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)\) is the next interarrival time, independent of \(P_i\). The imbalance is a symmetric random walk, so \(\mathbb E[P_i]\sim\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt i\). This is a named algorithmic cost bound, not a complexity-class result; the paper gives no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity theorem.

A plausible regime is a large two-sided clearing market: many applicants and openings arrive over a market period, but within each side there are only a few common eligibility profiles, locations, and cost parameters. In the anchor instance there are just two complete types, with equal arrival shares, while \(m\) is large. The stochastic arrival times remain individual; what becomes continuous is the amount of unmatched population waiting on each side.

I would hand a prover this problem: Brownian-Queue Mass Matching. An instance consists of a horizon \(H>0\), a fluctuation scale \(\sigma>0\), a holding-cost rate \(h>0\), and two types \(R,B\) that may only be matched across sides at zero connection cost. The diffusion-scale imbalance is \(Z_t=\sigma W_t\), where \(W\) is standard Brownian motion. A solution is a causal fractional matching policy, represented by nonnegative unmatched masses \(Q_R(t),Q_B(t)\), with \(Q_R(t)-Q_B(t)=Z_t\); a match removes equal mass from both queues. Find a policy minimizing
\[ h\,\mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^H \bigl(Q_R(t)+Q_B(t)\bigr)\,dt\right]. \]
The optimal policy matches every available opposite-type mass immediately, leaving \(Q_R(t)=Z_t^+\) and \(Q_B(t)=(-Z_t)^+\). Its value is
\[ \frac{2h\sigma}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,H^{3/2}. \]
For the anchor’s equal-rate case, the random-walk limit suggests that the discrete expected cost divided by \(m^{3/2}\) converges to this Brownian-area value with \(H=h=\sigma=1\). That coefficient is an inference from the proof’s random-walk estimate, not a claim of Lemma 24 itself. This mirror is tractable: its optimal fractional flow and value are explicit.

The match to the paper is unusually direct for a narrow case. Its proof already identifies the random-walk queue as the source of the bound, and the mirror replaces that queue’s integer imbalance with real-valued mass while retaining the same cross-type matching and delay objective. I would expect the authors to recognize it as a diffusion-scale version of their bipartite queueing example. It covers Lemma 24 and the bipartite extension discussed in Section 10.1; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s general non-bipartite results.

The natural next question is whether this remains computationally tractable with finitely many types, a general compatibility graph, nonzero connection costs, and a covariance matrix induced by type shares. One could ask for an optimal causal fractional matching policy, or an approximation algorithm, in the number of types and input bit length.

The weak point is that the ordinary high-multiplicity limit erases the phenomenon: the normalized type shares converge to \((1/2,1/2)\), while the unmatched fraction tends to zero. To retain the queue, this mirror uses diffusion scaling—unmatched mass at the \(\sqrt m\) scale and cost at the \(m^{3/2}\) scale. It is therefore a specialized stochastic population limit, not simply a static distribution over types. And since Lemma 24 is a cost lower bound rather than a hardness result, this mirror does not answer whether discrete computational hardness dissolves. The case for it is narrower: the paper’s own random-walk imbalance has a precise continuous mass counterpart, and the authors’ two-type result survives there as an explicit optimization problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 24 is the right place to look for a mirror, but the Brownian model does not continue the paper’s population. It continues the fluctuations in the arrival sequence. In the usual high-multiplicity scaling, take \(N\) requests over a fixed period with fixed type shares and arrival rates. In the equal-rate two-type example, the mean arrivals grow like \(N\), while the unmatched imbalance grows like \(\sqrt N\). As a fraction of the population, that queue vanishes. Retaining it requires centering and rescaling by \(\sqrt N\); \(Z_t\) is then a diffusion-scale random walk, not a distribution of population mass. The \(m^{3/2}\) cost is a second-order fluctuation quantity, not a per-capita objective for a continuous society.

There is also a type problem. In MPMD, arrival time affects delay and determines what the online algorithm has observed. Requests at the same location but different arrival times are not interchangeable in all the respects the problem uses. Grouping by location discards the information needed to define the online decision; grouping by location and time gives almost every request its own type. Replacing the event stream with a diffuse mass over location and time can preserve causality, but then the continuous object is a time-indexed flow or stochastic process. That is a different continuization from making a fixed society over types continuous.

The proposed Brownian problem is especially thin as a computational anchor. With only two sides, zero cross-side cost, and no other compatible pairs, matching available opposite mass immediately minimizes holding cost. Thus the policy is forced by the model; the displayed value is an expected Brownian-area calculation. Generalizing to a compatibility graph, nonzero costs, and a richer policy class could create a substantial stochastic-control question, but that decision problem is not supplied by Lemma 24. It changes the model precisely where the lemma has no choices to analyze.

Section 10.1 does not add a separate computational anchor: it points to the bipartite stochastic problem and explains why queues grow in the example. That supports the diffusion reading, but it does not turn the imbalance into population mass. The lemma is a named algorithmic performance bound, yet it does not classify computational complexity or establish an algorithmic problem over continuous type masses.

The closest escape is a first-order fluid model for a large market with finitely many location or eligibility classes. That could be a sensible research question, but the symmetric example’s queueing cost vanishes per capita in that limit. Keeping a nonzero queueing objective takes the analysis back to diffusion-scale fluctuations; retaining first-order costs instead produces a time-indexed mass-flow problem. Either may be worthwhile on its own, but neither is the continuous-population mirror of the result the proponent anchored on.

This is a strong objection to the proposed anchor, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists anywhere. A fluid formulation for some other cost regime or market scenario remains the honest weak point in a universal negative case.
