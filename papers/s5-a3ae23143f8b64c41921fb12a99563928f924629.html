The case FOR (proponent)

The honest answer is that this paper has no admissible computational anchor. Its named results are structural and axiomatic:

  • Theorem 1 characterizes solidarity as equivalent to inducing non-circular coalition-formation problems.
  • Corollary 1 derives non-emptiness of the core.
  • Corollary 2 characterizes interval rules among order-preserving ranking rules.

None asserts NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), parameterized tractability, or an algorithmic formulation. Section 5 only says that computing stable partitions is an interesting direction, citing Ballester et al. externally, and explicitly leaves the complexity of non-circular problems for future work. Thus I cannot honestly provide the required “one continuous problem per anchor”: there is no numbered computational result in the paper to mirror.

There is nevertheless a plausible unanchored research question. Let \(T\) be finitely many complete agent types, with mass vector \(\mu\). A coalition would be represented by a composition vector \(q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{T}\), its endowment by a function \(E(q)\), and a sharing rule would assign every type in that coalition a payoff \(F_t(q,E(q))\). A solution would be a measurable partition of the population into coalitions such that no measurable coalition strictly benefits all of its members by deviating. One could ask whether such a stable partition exists or compute one.

This is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s coalition-formation model: repeated agents share preferences, claims, rankings, and rule parameters, while mass replaces headcount. Solidarity could plausibly yield a tractable continuum problem through a measurable analogue of the paper’s non-circularity argument. But that would be a new computational programme built around Theorem 1, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. I would therefore grade the paper as offering no qualifying positive case under the stated anchoring requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has identified the decisive objection: this paper contains no admissible computational anchor. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic equivalence, Corollary 1 is an existence consequence, and Corollary 2 is another axiomatic characterization. None gives a decision problem, complexity classification, algorithm, optimization formulation, or parameterized result. Section 5 explicitly leaves computation of stable partitions and non-circular problems for future work. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is fatal: there is no named computational result whose continuous-population counterpart can be assessed.

The proposed measurable-partition question does not repair this. It is a potentially interesting new model, but not a mirror of a result in the paper. More importantly, it is not yet a computational problem. The finite paper supplies an endowment \(E_C\) for every coalition \(C\subseteq N\). With finitely many types, the proposed analogue requires an endowment function \(E(q)\) over composition vectors \(q\in\mathbb{R}_+^T\), together with a sharing rule \(F_t(q,E(q))\). Unless these functions belong to a specified finitely representable class, the input is infinite-dimensional and “compute a stable partition” has no meaningful complexity measure. If they are restricted to additive, piecewise-linear, or otherwise succinct forms, that restriction creates a new computational model rather than continuizing one of the paper’s results.

The ranking application makes the same problem especially clear. The paper’s ranking \(\mathcal R\) is a bijection assigning distinct positions to named agents, and coalitional payoffs depend on the induced relative order. A mass vector of types does not determine that order. One can replace it by quantile ranks, tied rank-types, or a continuum priority measure, but each is a substantive redesign. Likewise, arbitrary coalition-specific endowments cannot simply be recovered from type masses. These are not objections to high multiplicity itself; they show that the paper does not provide the finite-dimensional computational structure needed for a high-multiplicity mirror.

A charitable reconstruction—finite types, composition-dependent endowments, a parametrically described sharing rule, and partitions represented by finitely many coalition compositions—could certainly define a worthwhile ChoCo problem. Theorem 1 might provide axiomatic inspiration for it. But its algorithmic content would have to be invented from scratch: the paper gives no separation problem, LP, hardness reduction, or computational representation to carry over. It would therefore be a new continuum coalition-formation programme, not a continuous mirror of this paper.

The honest qualification is that I would not claim no valuable high-multiplicity research could ever be built around these ideas. I would claim the stronger point relevant here: no such research is anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The proponent’s only positive case is consequently outside the programme’s qualifying notion of a continuous mirror.
