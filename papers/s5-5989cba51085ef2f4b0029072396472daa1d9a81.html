The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-backed case for a continuous computational mirror under the stated criteria: the paper has no named result classifying the complexity of a problem—no result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results concern mechanism properties and impossibility.

The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here in Appendix 8.2, which says that for a networked housing market with \(n \geq 3\), no mechanism can simultaneously satisfy individual rationality, strategy-proofness, and Pareto efficiency over unrestricted preferences; and Theorem 2, proved here in Appendix 8.3, which identifies competition between an agent and a descendant for certain houses as making a mechanism not strategy-proof. These are substantive results, but neither is a computational-complexity anchor. Lemma 5’s claim that TTCD produces more swaps than modified TTC is a mechanism comparison, not a complexity result either.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for future work: many repeated local housing-exchange markets with the same rooted referral structure, where types include an agent’s preference order, network role, and invitation rights, and mass represents the fraction of agents of each type. But the invitation links and local competition are central to the paper’s incentive arguments; replacing them with type masses would require a careful model of how referrals and deviations operate. More importantly, that model would not turn Theorem 1 or Theorem 2 into results about computational complexity.

So there is no valid anchor, and consequently no continuous problem to state *per anchor* here. The strongest honest positive observation is that repeated referral markets could motivate a continuous extension. It does not support a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to continue. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result about IR, strategy-proofness, and Pareto efficiency; Theorem 2 identifies circumstances in which competition makes a mechanism not strategy-proof. Lemma 5 compares the swaps produced by two mechanisms. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous version might be interesting mechanism design, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The best version of a Theorem 1 mirror would ask whether a mechanism over a continuous society of networked agents can satisfy mass versions of IR, strategy-proofness, and efficiency. The obstacle is that the theorem’s force comes from particular agents’ deviations and particular houses changing hands. A distribution over preference and role types does not, by itself, say which agent owns which house or who can invite whom. Retaining those details preserves the strategic question, but the model must then carry the network and individual assignments alongside the masses. If instead agents are interchangeable within types, a type-mass description may not distinguish the individual deviation the theorem is about. This is a real modeling obstacle, though not a proof that no continuous mechanism-design model could handle it.

Theorem 2 has the same problem in sharper form: its claim turns on which two agents compete for a particular house, and on the ancestry and descendants linking them. A plausible large-market story is many repeated copies of a finite referral tree, with types recording preference and position in the tree. That makes multiplicity credible, but if the copies are independent, the incentive question remains inside each finite tree; aggregating copies does not create a new computational problem over the housing market. Connecting the copies to make network-wide trades possible requires a growing network description, and type masses alone do not preserve the links and house ownership that determine the conflicts. In an atomless model, a single invitation may also leave the aggregate distribution unchanged; preserving its effect on an agent’s own allocation still requires more than aggregate mass.

Lemma 5 is the weakest computational anchor. One could ask for the limiting fraction of agents who swap, or optimize that fraction over mechanisms satisfying incentive constraints. But the first is an asymptotic performance comparison, not a complexity question; the second adds an optimization problem the paper does not study. Repeated finite motifs again leave the strategic work inside each motif, while a connected growing network brings back the topology and identity information that a simple type distribution omits.

So the repeated-market suggestion does not rescue these anchors as continuous computational mirrors. It does point toward a possible new project on large networked housing markets, but making that project meaningful would require formulating its computational problem and preserving its strategic network structure from scratch. That is where the negative case is weakest: a sufficiently rich continuous network model could be worthwhile. The paper itself, however, gives little reason to think that such a project is a continuization of its named results.
