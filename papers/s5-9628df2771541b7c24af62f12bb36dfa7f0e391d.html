The continuous mirror question

Given a finite project catalogue \(P\), lecturers \(L\), and a list of \(\tau\) student preference types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), together with rational per-capita project and lecturer capacities, compute an allocation \(q_{t,r}\geq 0\), where \(r\in A_t\cup\{\bot\}\), that maximizes \(\sum_{t\in T,p\in A_t}q_{t,p}\). Require each type’s assigned mass to sum to \(\mu_t\), all capacities to hold, and no positive mass to form a blocking pair under the paper’s lecturer conditions. Also require the envy graph on occupied categories \((t,r)\), with an edge to \((u,s)\) when type \(t\) prefers \(s\) to \(r\), to be acyclic. Equivalently, decide for a rational threshold \(z\) whether such an allocation assigning mass at least \(z\) exists.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete student preference types, with rational mass per type and rational per-capita capacities for projects and lecturers. Decision variables \(q_{t,r}\) assign each unit of mass at most one project or leave it unmatched; the objective maximizes assigned mass subject to capacity limits, the positive-mass analogue of SPA-P blocking pairs, and an acyclic envy graph.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the maximum-cardinality stable assignment problem represented by Theorem 1. It leaves the paper’s empirical comparisons of approximation algorithms and finite-instance runtime results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of computing the maximum assigned mass, or deciding whether it is at least \(z\), for rational finite-support instances?
  • Can stability and coalition-freedom be encoded by a tractable optimization formulation, or does fractional splitting yield hardness specific to the continuum?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Maximum Stable Mass SPA-P\(_\infty\). It keeps the paper’s central question—how many students can be assigned while preserving SPA-P stability—and changes the population from named students to masses of students with identical preferences.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for an SPA-P instance \(I\), a feasible solution to the paper’s integer program \(J\) is optimal if and only if its corresponding matching in \(I\) has maximum cardinality. Here \(J\) encodes capacity-feasible assignments, the absence of blocking pairs, and an acyclic envy graph to rule out coalitions; its objective maximizes the number assigned. The paper computes with this formulation, so this is an eligible formulation anchor. It is a correctness theorem, though, not a polynomial-time or hardness theorem. The paper reports that discrete MAX-SPA-P is NP-hard, citing Manlove and O’Malley [12]; that cited result motivates the mirror but is not a numbered result proved here.

For the continuous instance, let \(P\) be a finite catalogue of projects and \(L\) a finite set of lecturers, with each project offered by one lecturer. A type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking of its acceptable projects \(A_t\). The input gives a rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each of the \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), plus per-capita project capacities \(a_p\) and lecturer capacities \(b_l\), all binary rationals. Lecturers retain strict rankings over their projects. The action is an allocation \(q_{t,r}\geq0\), where \(r\in A_t\cup\{\bot\}\), and \(\bot\) means unassigned. Require \(\sum_r q_{t,r}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_tq_{t,p}\leq a_p\), and \(\sum_t\sum_{p\in P_l}q_{t,p}\leq b_l\). Each individual receives at most one project; \(q_{t,p}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(p\), not a fractional bundle for an individual.

Lift the paper’s blocking-pair test to positive mass. A mass \(q_{t,r}>0\) is willing to move to acceptable project \(p\) when \(t\) prefers \(p\) to \(r\), treating every acceptable project as preferable to \(\bot\). It blocks if \(p\) has spare capacity and the lecturer condition from the paper holds: when \(r\) is one of that lecturer’s projects, the lecturer prefers \(p\) to \(r\); when \(r\) is outside that lecturer’s projects or is \(\bot\), the lecturer is either undersubscribed or is full and prefers \(p\) to their worst-ranked project with positive assigned mass. A feasible allocation has no such positive mass. For the coalition-free condition, make a directed graph whose vertices are occupied categories \((t,r)\), \(r\neq\bot\), and whose edge \((t,r)\to(u,s)\) means type \(t\) prefers \(s\) to \(r\). Require this graph to be acyclic: a cycle permits equal positive masses from its categories to exchange projects, just as a coalition does in the finite model.

The problem is to find a feasible allocation maximizing \(\sum_{t,p}q_{t,p}\), the fraction assigned; equivalently, for a rational threshold \(z\), decide whether a stable, coalition-free allocation assigning mass at least \(z\) exists. A discrete instance with \(N\) students maps into this model by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), \(a_p=c_p/N\), and \(b_l=d_l/N\); every discrete stable matching gives a feasible allocation of the same normalized size. The reverse direction need not hold, because mass of a type can split across projects. Whether an integrality or other two-way correspondence holds is part of the research question, not an assumption.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large department-wide or consortium-wide project allocation using a stable catalogue of project tracks: many students submit identical rankings over that catalogue, while project and lecturer supplies scale with cohort size. Then \(N\) can greatly exceed \(\tau\), while the preference and stability structure remains the one in the paper. The authors should recognize this as their problem’s population analogue: it retains student rankings, lecturer rankings, capacities, blocking pairs, coalitions, and maximizing assignments. It covers Theorem 1’s MAX-SPA-P optimization problem, not the paper’s empirical comparison of approximation algorithms.

My tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness, not a claim that hardness has already transferred. Project and lecturer rankings, capacity interactions, and cycles in the envy graph still carry combinatorial structure even when many students share types. But the paper supplies no hardness theorem for this mass problem, and fractional splitting could erase the finite hardness. A prover should ask whether the rational decision version is NP-hard, whether an LP or separation formulation is possible, and whether coalition cycle elimination preserves stability in the fractional model.

The weakest point is exactly that gap: Theorem 1 validates an integer program for named, indivisible students; it does not validate its continuous counterpart. In realistic allocations, exact rankings may be nearly unique, so \(\tau\) may approach \(N\), and per-capita capacities may be a less natural description than the paper’s individual project capacities. Even so, a large repeated cohort with shared preference types makes the mass question recognizable and faithful enough to be worth asking.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 1, but it establishes that the paper’s integer program correctly represents MAX-SPA-P. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic bound. The NP-hardness cited for MAX-SPA-P comes from earlier work; this paper’s own results are an encoding and empirical comparisons on finite instances. So a continuous counterpart to Theorem 1 would chiefly establish that a mass formulation correctly represents a mass-allocation problem. That is a thin connection to the paper’s actual results, and the interesting complexity questions would have to be supplied independently.

That is the strongest objection, but it does not defeat the best mirror. A department or consortium allocating many students among a stable catalogue of project tracks could have repeated preference rankings and capacities that scale with cohort size. Maximizing assigned mass while forbidding positive-mass blocking pairs and exchange cycles preserves the paper’s central structure. It also avoids the objection that masses forget student identity: students of the same ranking are interchangeable for the preferences and constraints this model uses. Fractional splitting and the mass version of coalition-freedom are reasonable choices for that regime. I cannot honestly claim that no authors would recognize this as their problem.

The negative case is therefore limited: the continuous question is a plausible new problem, but its motivation comes mainly from the model and the cited MAX-SPA-P hardness result, not from a computational theorem established in this paper. The paper does not show that its IP correctness theorem leads to a distinctive continuous-optimization question. Still, that gap does not establish that the mirror is unworthy. Against the proposed high-multiplicity track scenario, the case against is weak.
