The continuous mirror question

Given \(N\) candidates and \(L\) equal-sized client populations, each described by a finite-support distribution \(\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\) over complete rankings with binary-rational masses summing to one, compute the Borda consensus permutation \(\pi\) that orders candidates by increasing pooled mean position \(B(a)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{\sigma}\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\sigma(a)\). Under the promise that these means have strict adjacent gaps in the order of a common consensus ranking \(\sigma_0\), does this computation recover \(\sigma_0\)?

The model it lives in

Each agent type is a client label \(\ell\) paired with a complete ranking \(\sigma\), carrying rational mass \(\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\). Compute the consensus permutation \(\pi\) minimizing \(\sum_a B(a)\pi(a)\), equivalently sorting candidates by their pooled mean rank positions. This is a deterministic type-mass problem, with \(\pi\) as the output decision.

The objection that survived

Under the paper’s common i.i.d. Mallows promise, exact client distributions coincide, so inter-client aggregation and communication gains vanish in the mass limit; heterogeneous jurisdiction distributions fall outside the stated recovery guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s Borda centroid recovery and also admits Theorem 4’s Lehmer-coordinate mode aggregation. It leaves the finite-sample confidence bounds, secure-aggregation protocol, quantization and truncation analysis, empirical comparisons, and cited general Kemeny hardness untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize when heterogeneous client ranking masses still make the pooled Borda order identify a common consensus ranking.
  • Bound the effect of finite-precision errors in the input masses on the recovered order as a function of the smallest adjacent Borda-score gap.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered theorem in this paper classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its named results are statistical recovery and communication guarantees. The strongest positive case is therefore an algorithmic mirror of those guarantees; it should not be presented as a complexity-class result. The paper’s prose cites hardness of general Kemeny aggregation, but that is prior work, not a numbered hardness result of this paper, so I would not use it as an anchor.

The most convincing regime is a large preference survey or election with a small, fixed slate—say eight candidates—run across many equal-sized jurisdictions. Each person’s type is their complete ranking. Mass is the fraction of a jurisdiction’s electorate with each ranking, so the global type can be written \((\ell,\sigma)\), where \(\ell\) is the jurisdiction and \(\sigma\in S_N\) is the ranking. With \(n\) voters and \(N=8\), there are at most \(8!\) ranking types per jurisdiction, even if \(n\) is in the millions; thus \(n\) can greatly exceed the total support size \(\tau\). The paper’s common-centroid Mallows assumption is plausible for a large, roughly single-consensus electorate, though it is a real modeling restriction. Mass here is the deterministic composition of the electorate, not a probability over outcomes.

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s Borda recovery result.

Anchor — Theorem 2, proved in this paper. The theorem considers independent Mallows rankings with known \(\phi\in(0,1)\), spread over \(L\) clients, and a common unknown centroid \(\sigma_0\). With \(C\) as explicitly defined in Equation (4), if every client has at least
\[ \max\left\{ \frac{256(1+\phi)^2}{(1-\phi)^2}, \frac{260C^2(1+\phi)^2}{L(1-\phi)^2} \ln\left(\frac{2N}{\delta}+\ln\frac{4L}{3}\right) \right\} \]
samples, Algorithm 2 recovers \(\sigma_0\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), with communication complexity \(NL\log N\). The method locally averages rank positions, quantizes the resulting estimates, and has the server sort their aggregate.

Continuous problem — Federated Continuous Borda Center. An instance gives \(N,L\), and for each client a list of supported rankings with binary-rational masses \(\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\geq 0\) summing to one. The decision variable is a consensus permutation \(\pi\). Define the mass-weighted mean position of candidate \(a\) by
\[ B(a)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{\sigma\in S_N}\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\sigma(a). \]
Return the permutation that orders candidates by increasing \(B(a)\); equivalently, minimize \(\sum_a B(a)\pi(a)\). On a population whose mean-position ordering has a positive margin in the order of a common Mallows centroid, this returns that centroid. Without that promise, it still returns the well-defined population Borda consensus.

This is a direct match to the paper’s target: recover a ranking from distributed ranking data, with the population itself represented by type masses. The authors should recognize Borda aggregation and centroid recovery as their problem. The continuous version is tractable: sum the weighted positions over the \(\tau\) supported client-ranking types and sort, in time polynomial in \(N,\tau,L\), and the mass bit length. I expect Class A. The high-multiplicity gain is concrete: computation can depend on \(\tau\), rather than processing every voter separately.

A distinct secondary mirror comes from Lehmer aggregation.

Anchor — Theorem 4, proved in this paper. For independent Mallows samples across \(L\) clients, if \(\phi+\phi^2<1+\phi^N\), define
\[ p=\frac{\sum_{u=1}^{N-1}\phi^u}{1+\sum_{u=3}^{N}\phi^u}. \]
If each client’s sample count meets the theorem’s displayed lower bound
\[ \max\left\{ \frac{4(1+p)^2}{(1-p)^2} \ln\!\left(\frac{6}{\ln(1/(1-p))\,\ln(1/p)}\right), \frac{8(1+p)^2}{(1-p)^2} \left(\ln 2+\frac{\ln(NL/\delta)}{L}\right) \right\}, \]
Algorithm 3 recovers \(\sigma_0\) with probability at least \(1-\epsilon-\delta\). It does so by taking local coordinate-wise Lehmer majorities, truncating and securely aggregating their histograms, and decoding the resulting majority code. For total sample count \(M\), the theorem gives communication
\[ L\left[ N\left( \log N-\log\left(\frac{2\log(MN^2/\epsilon)}{\log(1/p)}+1\right) +\left(\frac{2\log(MN^2/\epsilon)}{\log(1/p)}+1\right)\log L \right) \right], \]
which is \(O(LN\log L\max\{\log N,\log M\})\). Its proof uses a concentration proposition cited from [36].

Continuous problem — Federated Lehmer-Majority Consensus. Give the same client mass distributions. For each client \(\ell\) and Lehmer coordinate \(i\), form the mass histogram
\[ h_{\ell,i}(r)=\sum_{\sigma:L_\sigma(i)=r}\mu_{\ell,\sigma}, \qquad r\in\{0,\ldots,i-1\}. \]
Let \(v_\ell(i)\) be its mode, using a fixed tie rule. Return the inverse Lehmer code whose \(i\)-th coordinate is the mode of the client values \(v_1(i),\ldots,v_L(i)\). Equivalently, choose the permutation \(\pi\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \mathbf{1}\{v_\ell(i)=L_\pi(i)\}. \]
A precise recovery promise is that, for each coordinate, a strict majority of clients’ local mass modes equal \(L_{\sigma_0}(i)\); then the answer is \(\sigma_0\). This is an exact type-mass analogue of the local-majority/server-majority computation. Histograms, modes, and Lehmer decoding are computable in polynomial time in \(N,\tau,L\) and the input bit length, so I expect Class A here too.

The natural next questions are whether useful recovery guarantees can be stated using only mass gaps, how they change under unequal client weights or heterogeneous centroids, and whether approximate mass inputs preserve the answer. One could also ask whether the secure-aggregation communication bounds have continuous counterparts when clients hold exact masses rather than finite samples.

The weakest point is that the paper’s results are about sample complexity, communication, and stochastic recovery—not worst-case computational complexity. Replacing finite samples with exact type masses removes sampling uncertainty and much of the paper’s privacy-and-quantization challenge. The mirrors preserve the underlying task of recovering a consensus ranking from many distributed rankings, but they do not extend the paper’s statistical guarantees unchanged. That makes this a credible, bounded positive case for continuous-population rank aggregation, not a claim that the paper already establishes a complexity-theoretic continuization result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorems 2 and 4 measure statistical and communication complexity, not the running time of a computation on a population. Their guarantees depend on finite random samples, confidence parameters, and secure encoding. A mass input can be a sensible ChoCo object, but it changes what these theorems are about.

For Theorem 2, the best version of the proposed mirror is a rational ranking histogram for each jurisdiction, with rankings as types. That fits a large electorate with a small slate, and computing the mass-weighted Borda scores takes time polynomial in \(N\), the support size \(\tau\), and the mass bit lengths. But exact masses remove the sampling uncertainty Theorem 2 analyzes. Under its Mallows promise, the population’s expected rank positions already order candidates by the centroid, as the paper’s Lemma 2 shows; the continuous computation is just weighted sums and sorting. If the masses instead represent observed samples, the mirror is a compressed encoding of the original finite data. It may save work when ballots repeat, but it does not carry over the theorem’s sample-complexity question.

Theorem 4 has the same gap. Replacing each client’s sample histogram with exact masses makes its Lehmer-coordinate modes deterministic; the server can then take coordinate-wise majorities and decode. That is a well-defined mass-input task, but the theorem’s concentration and truncation results no longer do work. Under the common Mallows law, exact client distributions are identical, so the client-majority stage adds nothing. For arbitrary client distributions it remains computable, but the paper’s recovery guarantee no longer applies. Preserving privacy and communication as part of the continuous problem would require additional modeling of precision and secure aggregation; that would shift the main question toward protocol design.

A stronger nearby proposal is to give the population’s ranking masses and compute a Kemeny consensus by minimizing \(\sum_\sigma \mu_\sigma K_\tau(\pi,\sigma)\). The paper explicitly connects Kemeny aggregation to Mallows maximum likelihood, so this is a recognizable continuous-population problem. But it replaces the Borda and Lehmer estimators in the nominated theorems with a different objective. It could be a worthwhile adjacent ChoCo question; it does not rescue these particular anchors.

This makes the negative case limited. A high-volume election with repeated rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and both mass formulations are easy to recognize as ranking aggregation. My case is only that their mass versions look like direct weighted implementations of the estimators, while the theorems’ contributions—sample recovery and private communication—fall away. If a straightforward support-compressed Class A result is sufficient for a worthwhile mirror, these anchors may survive; I cannot honestly rule out every useful scenario.
