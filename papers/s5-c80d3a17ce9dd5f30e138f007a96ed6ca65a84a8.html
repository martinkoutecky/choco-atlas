What the mirror covers

The proposed mass evaluation covers assignment ranks under IA or DA for finite-support societies; it leaves the paper’s random-market expected-rank asymptotics and coupon-collector analysis without a computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: the paper studies mechanisms that have a natural continuous-population version, but it contains no named computational result of the kind this task requires as an anchor. Its named results concern asymptotic expected ranks, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: in a balanced market with \(n\) students and \(n\) schools, i.i.d. uniform student preferences, and any sequence of fixed school-priority profiles, IA’s expected average rank divided by \(\log n\) tends to \(1\). Corollary 1 says the ratio of IA’s and DA’s expected average ranks tends to \(1\), using Theorem 1 and earlier results on DA. Theorem 2 gives the same leading term for IA with skips; Theorem 3 gives it for IA and DA with a fixed common quota; and Proposition 1 proves the corresponding total-application result for bounded quotas. These are analytical asymptotic claims, not computational-complexity claims, so none qualifies as an anchor under your criteria.

There is nevertheless a plausible *nearby* continuous problem. Let a society have total mass \(1\), rational school capacities \(q_s\) summing to \(1\), and a finite set of complete student types \(T\). A type records a strict school ranking and its school-priority class; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\). Run IA or DA on masses: each round, unmatched mass applies to its next school, and a school accepts mass by priority up to its capacity, splitting a tied type’s mass when needed. The output is the assignment mass \(x^A_{t,s}\); the quantity to compute is
\[ R_A(\mu)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{s\in S}x^A_{t,s}\,\operatorname{rk}_t(s). \]
A plausible regime is a large school district with many students but a moderate number of repeated preference-and-priority profiles, with capacities scaling with cohort size. Computing the resulting IA or DA average rank looks tractable for finite support: the mechanism advances mass through finitely many preference positions and processes priority classes at each school. This would be a sensible high-multiplicity version of the mechanisms in the paper, though it is not a result the paper proves.

The gap is substantial. In the paper’s balanced uniform model, the number of possible preference types grows as \(n!\), while only \(n\) students are sampled. In a continuum with exactly uniform mass over rankings, each school receives precisely \(1/n\) first-choice mass; with capacity \(1/n\), everyone gets their first choice. The continuum answer is then rank \(1\), not the paper’s \(\log n\). The coupon-collector effect driving the theorem comes from finite-market sampling fluctuations, which the deterministic mass model removes.

So the continuous mechanism-evaluation problem is a credible adjacent question, and I would expect its finite-type version to be tractable. But it is not an anchored mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The weakest point is also the central one: a natural continuous version of IA/DA does not preserve the stochastic effect that the paper’s main theorem measures.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper states no computational problem. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3, and Proposition 1 are asymptotic claims about expected ranks and application counts. They specify no computational input-output task or complexity question. The sequential implementation and coupon-collector argument are tools for proving those claims; they do not turn them into algorithmic results.

The proposed mass version of IA or DA is a valid nearby model. Give the population mass \(1\), rational school capacities summing to \(1\), and finitely many types recording rankings and priority classes. To compute IA’s average rank, advance each type’s remaining mass through its preference list, fill each school by priority, and split mass at a capacity boundary when needed. This is direct finite-support simulation over at most \(m\) rounds. It supplies no evident computational bottleneck for a complexity programme. Theorem 1’s uniform random-market law illustrates the gap: in the deterministic continuum, each school receives exactly \(1/m\) first-choice mass, so capacities of \(1/m\) fill in the first round. That outcome is not itself an objection; the relevant point is that the continuum has removed the random occupancy fluctuations whose accumulated effect the theorem measures.

A stronger attempt would preserve the theorem’s expected rank by sampling \(n\) students from a type distribution and running the finite mechanism. But then the quantity again depends on finite-sample fluctuations and \(n\); the continuous population is only the sampling distribution. Theorems 2 and 3 and Proposition 1 have the same coupon-collection structure, so they do not supply a separate computational anchor that escapes this problem. Corollary 1 compares two expected ranks and adds no computational task.

One could formulate a new problem about optimizing or stress-testing mass populations, capacities, or priority classes. That might become computationally interesting with additional constraints. The paper provides no such choice problem, however; adding one would create a new project rather than mirror one of its results. I cannot rule out every worthwhile project inspired by school-choice mass flows. The stronger, defensible negative claim is that this paper offers no worthwhile continuous *computational mirror*: its stated contribution is stochastic asymptotic analysis, while deterministic mass evaluation is a different and apparently routine task.
