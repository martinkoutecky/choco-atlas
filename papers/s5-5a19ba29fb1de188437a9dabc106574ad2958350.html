The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a conforming anchor-backed case from this paper: it has no numbered result classifying MAX-SMTI or MAX-HRT as hard, tractable, or otherwise. Proposition 1 says that if a stable matching under tie-breaking \(S_1\) has no blocking pair involving a type whose preference list changed, it remains stable under \(S_2\). Proposition 2 says that when removing a blocking pair creates new blocking pairs, they must involve an agent displaced in that removal. Both are local correctness facts for the search procedure, not complexity results about the underlying matching problem. The NP-hardness claims appear in prose and cite earlier work; the paper’s own algorithmic comparisons are experimental.

The strongest plausible population problem suggested by the paper is Mass-MAX-SMTI. Its instance has finite sets of men’s and women’s preference types, rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and for each type a weak order over acceptable types on the opposite side. A type must give a complete description of preferences: everyone in it has the same list and ties. Choose nonnegative matching masses \(x_{ij}\), with unmatched masses \(u_i=\mu_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=\nu_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\), to maximize \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). Require weak stability: for each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), it cannot be that both
\[ u_i+\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}>0 \quad\text{and}\quad v_j+\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}>0. \]
That is, there must not be positive mass on both sides who would strictly prefer each other to their current assignment. The flows are aggregate assignments; each individual still receives at most one partner.

A plausible regime is a large recurring placement market where many applicants and positions share a relatively small number \(\tau\) of full preference templates over role or program types. Then the population can be large while \(\tau\) stays modest. This mirrors the paper’s maximum-cardinality weak-stability objective closely, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in a repeated-type market. It covers that objective only, not TBLS’s tie-breaking search or TBLS-E’s equity objective.

I would leave the continuous problem’s complexity open from the evidence here. The continuous stability constraints have disjunctive interactions across type pairs, so tractability does not follow merely from replacing counts with masses; hardness in \(\tau\) is plausible, while fixed-\(\tau\) cases may be easier. Those are research questions, not conclusions supported by a numbered anchor. A useful next question is whether rational mass solutions correspond to finite high-multiplicity instances with weakly stable matchings, and with what approximation guarantees.

The soft spot is decisive for the requested case: this is a credible mirror of the paper’s *problem*, but the paper supplies no qualifying numbered result to anchor it. The cited discrete hardness makes the question worth asking; it cannot substitute for the required anchor from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch between the proposed mirror and what this paper actually establishes. The paper defines MAX-SMTI and MAX-HRT and reports that TBLS performs well on its test instances. But it gives no complexity classification or approximation guarantee for either problem. Its numbered Propositions 1 and 2 are local correctness facts for the search procedure: one limits which agents need checking after a tie adjustment; the other identifies who can be involved in newly created blocking pairs after a blocking-pair removal. The NP-hardness and approximation results in the introduction are prior work, not results of this paper.

The proposed Mass-MAX-SMTI is coherent. Repeated admissions or hiring markets with many applicants and positions sharing a small number of preference templates are a plausible high-multiplicity setting. The flow \(x_{ij}\) can represent the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\), while the stability condition rules out a positive mass on both sides that would strictly prefer one another. I would not object that agents are individuated in every matching market, or that fractional assignments cannot represent individual matches.

But that model does not mirror a result of this paper. It formulates a new aggregate optimization problem; the paper neither studies preference types nor proves anything about matching masses. Extending it to HRT with scaled capacities, or defining an aggregate rank-sum objective corresponding to sex equality, makes the model broader but does not supply the missing paper result.

The algorithmic alternatives have a similar gap. A literal TBLS adjustment targets an individual agent. In a continuum, that agent has zero mass, so the adjustment cannot change matching size or aggregate fairness. One could instead adjust a positive-mass cohort or fraction of a type, but that changes the algorithm’s neighborhood and requires a new design. Likewise, the mass versions of Propositions 1 and 2 look like local bookkeeping statements about affected types; they do not by themselves pose the kind of complexity question the programme is charting. A study comparing a new mass-based heuristic with other methods could be useful, but it would be a fresh empirical project rather than a continuization of a result this paper proves.

So the against case is strongest when it is narrow: this paper offers no solid, paper-specific complexity result to carry into the programme, and its algorithm’s key individual-level moves do not transfer directly. It is weak as a universal claim. The mass maximum-stability problem remains a natural question in its own right, and nothing in this paper shows that it is unworthy of the programme’s time.
