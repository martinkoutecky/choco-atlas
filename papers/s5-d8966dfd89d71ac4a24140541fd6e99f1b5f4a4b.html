The continuous mirror question

Given an issue set ℐ, a DNNF integrity constraint Γ, a finite support Τ of complete ballots satisfying Γ with positive binary-rational masses μ_t summing to \(1\), and a partial ballot \(s\), is there a ballot \(y \models Γ\) agreeing with \(s\) that minimizes \(\max_{t \in Τ} d_{\mathrm H}(y,t)\) over all ballots satisfying Γ?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rational ballots \(t \in Τ\); the society assigns each type positive mass μ_t. A feasible collective ballot \(y\) has loss \(\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{t \sim μ} d_{\mathrm H}(y,t)=\max_{t \in Τ} d_{\mathrm H}(y,t)\); ask whether a loss-minimizing \(y \models Γ\) agrees with the given partial ballot \(s\).

The objection that survived

The essential-supremum objective can change discontinuously when a type’s mass tends to zero; the proponent acknowledges that every positive-mass type counts, but this limits how the mirror behaves under population limits that lose rare types.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Max-Hamming outcome determination from Corollary 9, whose hardness already holds for Γ = ⊤; it leaves the paper’s results for other aggregation rules and constraint languages aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the continuous problem’s complexity change when support size \(\tau\) is bounded or used as a parameter?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is outcome determination for Max-Hamming aggregation. Corollary 9, as printed, says that \(\Outcome{\MaxHamming}\) is \(\Theta_2^{\mathrm p}\)-complete when the integrity constraint \(\Gamma\) is a DNNF circuit. The paper proves the hardness through its reduction for the starred Max-Hamming proposition, already with \(\Gamma=\top\); the membership bound comes from the general result it cites. In full, the underlying question is: given an issue set \(\mathcal I\), a satisfiable DNNF constraint \(\Gamma\), a profile of \(\Gamma\)-consistent ballots, and a partial ballot \(s\), is there a \(\Gamma\)-consistent ballot minimizing the maximum Hamming distance to the profile that agrees with \(s\)?

Here is the continuous version. An instance gives \(\mathcal I\), the DNNF circuit \(\Gamma\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\mathcal R(\mathcal I,\Gamma)\) of complete rational ballots, and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), plus a partial ballot \(s\). The type is a complete ballot: agents sharing one are indistinguishable for this problem, since Max-Hamming uses their judgments and the common constraint. The society is the distribution \(\mu\). For a feasible collective ballot \(y\), define its loss as
\[ R_\mu(y)=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{t\sim\mu}d_{\mathrm H}(y,t) =\max_{t\in T}d_{\mathrm H}(y,t). \]
The decision question is whether some \(y\models\Gamma\) agreeing with \(s\) attains \(\min_{z\models\Gamma}R_\mu(z)\). Such a \(y\) is the solution. This preserves the paper’s minimax objective; replacing it with expected Hamming distance would ask a different question.

A plausible regime is a large civic electorate considering a shared, short agenda of policy choices or funding bundles under common feasibility rules. There may be hundreds of thousands of residents but far fewer distinct complete judgment patterns: \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|T|\). A repeated ballot is a complete type for this rule; shared agenda and constraint are part of the common instance. The paper’s participatory-budgeting setting gives a natural application context, and DNNF constraints can represent the feasible bundles.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. Given any discrete profile, assign each distinct ballot its fraction of the electorate. The maximum distance over voters is exactly the maximum over positive-mass types, so the continuous instance preserves the original optimal ballots and the answer to the partial-ballot query. Conversely, duplicating every voter any number of times leaves Max-Hamming outcomes unchanged. Thus one can have an arbitrarily large electorate relative to its support without changing the question. The paper’s hardness reduction already uses \(\Gamma=\top\), a DNNF constraint, so it embeds directly. With support listed explicitly, the continuous problem should retain the \(\Theta_2^{\mathrm p}\)-complete classification; its size is measured in the agenda size \(m\), support size \(\tau\), circuit size, and mass bit length \(L\).

The authors should recognize this as their problem: the only population-level change is to represent repeated or heterogeneous ballots by a distribution over complete ballot types. The collective decision, feasibility condition, distance, and worst-case objective remain intact. This case also stays within scope: the population is continuous; neither ballots nor outcomes are made fractional.

The soft spot is that the mass values do not affect this particular rule once they are positive. A tiny positive-mass type can constrain the minimax outcome just as much as a large one. That is faithful to Max-Hamming’s maximum-over-individuals definition, and the continuous limit is naturally the essential supremum, but it means this mirror uses the distribution mainly through its support. Further questions are whether the complexity changes when \(\tau\) is restricted, and whether a quantile-based rule that discounts very small groups has a useful continuous analogue. The latter would be a new rule, not this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that Max-Hamming aggregation has no real mass variable. Corollary 9 is a named computational result, and a society with many residents sharing ballots is plausible. But for any \(y\), its loss is just the maximum distance over the support. The masses do no work: a discrete profile can be compressed to one copy of each distinct ballot and the Max-Hamming outcomes remain exactly the same. This mirror therefore supplies no new population-scale optimization problem; it recasts the rule as a finite support problem.

There is also a sharp limit discontinuity. A ballot type with mass \(\varepsilon>0\) can constrain the optimum as much as a type with much larger mass; at mass \(0\), it vanishes. Thus one outlier among \(N\) voters can constrain every finite electorate while disappearing from the limiting distribution as \(N\) grows. Preserving that outlier in the limit would require carrying information beyond the population measure. A quantile or tail objective could make small groups count less, but that would define a different rule.

These are real limits on what this mirror adds, though they do not defeat it. The authors could reasonably want to study essential-supremum robustness over positive-mass ballot types, and a result showing that the continuous version collapses to support alone could itself be informative. The paper does not already give that continuous treatment. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case stick here: this anchor is strong, and the case against it is weak.
