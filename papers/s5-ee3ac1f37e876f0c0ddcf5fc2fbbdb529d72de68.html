What the mirror covers

The proposed computation covers population quantile evaluation related to Propositions 3 and 4; it leaves the axiomatic characterizations and the empirical demand model results outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive candidate is Distributional-CV Quantile Evaluation. An instance has baseline and alternative price vectors \(p_0,p'\), a quantile \(\tau\), and a finite set of complete consumer types \(T\). Each type \(t\) has income \(y_t\), utility \(v_t\), and rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For each type, calculate the payment \(c_t\) that makes the consumer indifferent between the two price regimes. The question is to return
\[ Q_\tau=\inf\left\{a:\sum_{t:c_t\le a}\mu_t\ge\tau\right\}, \]
the \(\tau\)-quantile of compensating variation. The mass represents population share; the type-level payment is the common payment being evaluated.

A plausible regime is a large consumer market in which millions of households are represented by a much smaller set of repeated preference and income profiles. The authors would recognize this welfare object: Proposition 3, proved here, says that the population median compensating variation equals the stochastic compensating variation of a planner whose weights match population shares. Proposition 4, also proved here, extends the identity to an \(\alpha\)-weighted \(\tau\)-quantile. The proposed computation covers those quantile-welfare results, not the paper’s axiomatic characterizations or empirical model estimation. With finite support and efficiently computable type-level compensating variations, it is tractable by weighted selection; further questions include evaluating the CDF from a parametric continuous type distribution or computing several weighted quantiles.

There is a decisive limitation under the requested anchor rule: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 2, proved here, characterizes weighted utilitarian social choice through behavioral and Pareto conditions; it does not classify an algorithmic problem. Theorem 1 is a cited welfare representation result, and Propositions 3–7 give identities and analytical welfare comparisons. None asserts membership in P, hardness, or another computational class. So the quantile computation is a plausible new computational question around the paper’s subject, but it is not an eligible mirror anchored in a named computational result.

The weakest point is also the main scope issue: the paper already models the population through distributions over preferences and explicitly interprets stochastic choice as population shares. A finite-support quantile computation would therefore computationalize an object the paper has already made continuous, rather than reveal a new continuous-population model. And with finite support it may be little more than sorting once the per-type values are available.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor fails first on the programme’s eligibility test: the paper states no computational problem whose complexity could be mirrored. Theorem 2 characterizes a social choice rule axiomatically; Propositions 3 and 4 identify quantile compensating variation with stochastic compensating variation. Neither gives an algorithmic result to continue. That is not a complaint that the continuous answer might be easy or unchanged. It means there is no named computational result here to mirror.

More fundamentally, this paper has already made the population continuous. Section 4.1 explicitly studies a unit-mass, non-atomic population with a distribution \(\mu\) over preferences. Section 2 works with population distributions over consumer preferences, and Section 5 studies quantiles of the resulting compensating-variation distribution. Propositions 3–5 connect those quantiles to population-weighted stochastic choice and to demand. So the candidate \(Q_\tau\) restates an object the paper already defines over a population distribution. The type masses in the proposed finite-support formulation encode that distribution; they do not introduce a continuous society.

The strongest version of the proposed computation would take a compactly represented preference distribution and ask for an exact or approximate quantile, perhaps while optimizing over policy prices. That could be useful algorithmic research. But it would be computation over a population model the paper already treats continuously, not a continuous mirror of a discrete computational result. Conversely, if the input is an explicit list of type-level compensating variations and rational masses, the task is weighted quantile selection; the proposal leaves the substantive cost of deriving those values outside the problem.

I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile algorithmic question could be built from this paper. I can argue that the proponent’s quantile question—and stronger variants—do not satisfy the stated aim of continuizing the population: the population is already continuous, and the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a mirror.
