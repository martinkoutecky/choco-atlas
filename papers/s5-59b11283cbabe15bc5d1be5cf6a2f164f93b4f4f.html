The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper’s named results. Its only numbered theorem is Theorem 10, attributed to Alon and Edelman (2010), not proved here. It says that if a simple game on \(n>k\) voters assigns total normalized Banzhaf power at most \(\varepsilon<1/(k+1)\) to voters \(k+1,\ldots,n\), then there is a simple game on the same \(n\) voters, with \(n-k\) dummies, whose Banzhaf vector is within
\[ \frac{(2k+1)\varepsilon}{1-(k+1)\varepsilon}+\varepsilon \]
in \(\ell_1\) distance. That is a structural approximation bound, not a complexity result or an algorithmic formulation computed with in the paper.

The paper’s algorithmic statements are unnumbered summaries of cited work; its other numbered items are conjectures and research questions. The continuous-population material—non-atomic chains and limits of power indices—is relevant background, but it is analytic rather than a named computational result. A possible scenario is a very large committee with a few recurring voter classes, where mass is each class’s population share and the design goal is to choose voting weights to approximate a target distribution of aggregate power. But making that into a continuous inverse-power problem would not give it a qualifying anchor here, and in the non-atomic limit the Penrose results discussed in the paper can make the weighted version nearly direct. I would not present it as an established positive mirror of this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Its sole numbered theorem, Theorem 10, is a structural approximation bound on Banzhaf vectors, attributed to Alon and Edelman. The algorithmic material appears as summaries of other work and open research questions, not as the paper’s own complexity or algorithmic results. So there is no qualifying computational result here for ChoCo to chart.

The best continuous lift of Theorem 10 would replace the low-power tail of voters with diffuse mass and ask whether a finite “core” approximates the resulting power distribution. But that remains a structural limit question, not a computational one. In a nonatomic population, power assigned to an individual voter no longer has its usual pivotal meaning; a class-level power density could be defined, but then the result concerns a newly specified power index. The paper’s discussion of nonatomic limits is relevant background, not an existing computational treatment.

The proposed scenario—many members in recurring voter classes, with weights chosen to fit a target power distribution—is the strongest possible mirror. It has a plausible high-multiplicity setting. A class-level inverse-design problem could be worthwhile, provided the authors specify how power is measured for a continuum of members. But it would be a new computational problem motivated by the paper’s open research questions, not a mirror of a result the paper establishes.

That is enough to reject a claim that this paper already contains a strong continuous-computation anchor. It is not enough to support the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror. The inverse-design extension is plausible, and the honest negative case is weak on that broader claim.
