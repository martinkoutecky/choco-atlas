The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is for SGASP, anchored on Theorem 12, proved in this paper. The theorem says that deciding whether a simplified group activity selection instance has a stable assignment is W[1]-hard when parameterized by the number \(t\) of agent types alone; the number of activities is unrestricted. Here an agent type is its approval set over activity–size pairs. The paper’s reduction proceeds through its strongly W[1]-hard Simple Multidimensional Partitioned Subset Sum problem.

A plausible regime is a very large online event or community programme: many participants choose among the same set of groups, their preferences are collected through a questionnaire with relatively few response patterns, and the relevant group size is the group’s share of the cohort. The population might be in the millions while the number \(\tau\) of distinct preference types remains modest. That makes the paper’s type parameter a natural description of a high-multiplicity instance, rather than a property claimed of every group-selection setting.

I would call the mirror Mass-SGASP. An instance has activities \(A\), a finite support \(T\) of types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has, for every activity \(a\), an explicit finite set \(Q_t(a)\subseteq[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\) of approved population shares. A solution is a matrix \(x_{t,a}\geq 0\), where \(x_{t,a}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(a\), with
\[ \sum_{a\in A}x_{t,a}\leq \mu_t,\qquad y_a=\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,a}. \]
The question is whether there is such a matrix giving a stable assignment. Individual rationality requires \(y_a\in Q_t(a)\) whenever \(x_{t,a}>0\). Write \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_a x_{t,a}\) for type \(t\)’s unassigned mass. Using the atomless-population convention that one person has zero mass, stability requires \(y_a\notin Q_t(a)\) for every activity \(a\) whenever \(r_t>0\). With approval preferences, assigned agents are indifferent among approved outcomes, so these conditions capture individual rationality and the possibility that an unassigned agent joins an activity. The output is an assignment, not an optimization objective.

This is a recognizable continuous version of the paper’s underlying problem: agents still choose activities, and their approval still depends on the resulting group size. The co-modification is to measure group size as a population share and allow a type’s mass to split across activities. Instance size can be stated in \(|A|\), \(\tau\), and the binary encoding length \(L\) of the masses and approval menus.

My tentative expectation is hardness transfers (Class B), though Theorem 12 alone does not establish it. Its reduction encodes a multidimensional partition through choices of activity sizes and totals for each type. Those choices can be represented as rational masses, so the underlying selection structure appears capable of surviving divisibility. A proof would need to rebuild or verify the reduction’s stability gadgets under the continuous deviation convention.

The paper’s treatment of agent types is evidence for the regime, not a novelty collision: its introduction explicitly motivates many agents sharing preferences, but its assignments and activity sizes remain discrete. The mirror covers Theorem 12 only; I would not extend it to the graph variant, where individual links add structure that the type count does not capture.

The weakest point is the change from a discrete deviation to an atomless one. In the paper, an unassigned agent considers joining an activity of size \(s\), producing size \(s+1\). In Mass-SGASP, joining does not change \(y_a\). That changes the stability condition, and the paper’s hardness theorem cannot simply be carried over by replacing counts with fractions. This leaves the complexity prediction open, but it does not make the population-share question artificial: the model retains the paper’s agents, activities, type-level preferences, and stability question while making the population the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 12 is a real computational anchor, and this is a hard paper to argue against: the authors explicitly motivate agent types with large populations sharing preferences. So the best case against is not that high multiplicity is implausible here. It is that the proposed Mass-SGASP stability condition is not clearly the high-multiplicity limit of the stability condition in the theorem.

In a finite population of \(n\) agents, an unassigned agent considering activity \(a\) changes its size from \(s\) to \(s+1\), or its share from \(y_a\) to \(y_a+1/n\). In the proposed atomless model, the entrant has zero mass, so the size remains \(y_a\). Those tests can disagree even when approvals are scale invariant: if a type approves exactly share \(q\), a finite entrant at a group of share \(q\) faces \(q+1/n\) and is not approved, while the proposed continuum rule treats \(q\) itself as a blocking option. For interval approvals, the discrepancy remains at endpoints. The difference comes from the meaning of a unilateral deviation, not merely from replacing counts with shares.

A stronger mirror could give types continuous utility over shares and use a limiting or robust notion of stability. But preserving finite-agent deviation semantics then requires tracking how preferences behave under small positive increments; allowing a positive mass to move instead makes the deviation coalition-sized. Either choice can be studied, but neither is specified by Theorem 12, whose approval lists allow arbitrary exact group sizes. The paper’s type parameter supports a plausible large-population regime; it does not by itself settle which of these continuum stability questions its authors would regard as the same problem.

That is a serious qualification to this particular mirror, not a decisive case that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large online community with genuinely share-based preferences remains plausible, and the resulting atomless stability problem is computationally well-posed. On the material here, I cannot honestly defend the universal negative claim: the strongest objection is that the proponent has not yet connected their stability semantics to the theorem’s finite-population semantics, and that gap may be repairable.
