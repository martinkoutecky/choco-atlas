The continuous mirror question

Given two populations with binary-rational type masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\), acceptable pairs, and rankings over counterpart types with ties of size at most two, does there exist an algorithm polynomial in the number of types, acceptable pairs, and mass bit length that returns a weakly stable mass matching \(x\) with \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\ge 3\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}/4\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\) is the maximum matched mass among weakly stable assignments? Is the \(4/3\) approximation factor tight for this model?

The model it lives in

A two-sided high-multiplicity market whose types carry mass and rank counterpart types. The decision variables \(x_{ij}\) are matched masses subject to type-mass capacity constraints; stability forbids any acceptable pair for which both types have positive mass strictly preferring that pair to their current assignment.

The objection that survived

The paper’s local proposal analysis and tightness example rely on ties among individuals of size at most two; the type-level tie bound does not preserve those mechanics, so the \(4/3\) guarantee has no established transfer.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s \(4/3\) approximation guarantee for maximum-cardinality stable matching; it leaves the paper’s specific proposal dynamics and charging proof untransferred.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time algorithm achieve a \(4/3\) approximation for maximum stable mass matching with ties of size at most two among counterpart types?
  • Is the \(4/3\) factor tight in the continuous model?
  • How does the optimum compare with finite markets formed by cloning each type in proportion to its rational mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a maximum stable matching problem for a high-multiplicity, two-sided market. The paper’s anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: for an instance with ties of size two, if \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is a maximum-cardinality stable matching and \(M\) is the matching output by Huang and Kavitha’s algorithm, then \(|\mathrm{OPT}|/|M|\le 4/3\). Example 1 shows that this bound is tight for an execution of the algorithm. The NP-hardness and UGC statements in the introduction are cited claims, not numbered results of this paper, so I do not use them as anchors.

A plausible regime is a large, repeated hiring or placement market: many applicants and positions, but relatively few complete preference types on each side. Let \(I\) and \(J\) be the applicant and position types, with binary-rational masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\), each side’s masses summing to \(1\). A type specifies its side, acceptable counterpart types, and ranking of them; ties have size at most two among counterpart types. The intended regime has millions of agents but a much smaller support \(\tau=|I|+|J|\).

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: \(4/3\)-Approximate Stable Mass Matching with Two-Type Ties. Given those masses, acceptable pairs, and rankings, find a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\). Require \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le p_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le q_j\). Each infinitesimal agent still gets either one partner or none; \(x\) records how the population of a type is divided among those assignments.

Use the paper’s weak stability convention: a blocking pair requires both sides to strictly prefer each other to their current assignment, or to being unmatched. For an acceptable pair \((i,j)\), define the mass of type \(i\) willing to match with \(j\) as
\[ W_i(j;x)=p_i-\sum_{j'}x_{ij'}+\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}. \]
Define \(W_j(i;x)\) symmetrically. The matrix is stable exactly when, for every acceptable pair, \(W_i(j;x)=0\) or \(W_j(i;x)=0\): if both are positive, equal positive amounts can be paired and would strictly prefer the new match. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\) be the largest \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\) among stable matrices. The computational question is whether an algorithm running in time polynomial in the number of types, acceptable pairs, and mass bit length can always return a stable \(x\) with \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\ge 3\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}/4\), and whether that factor is tight.

I expect the approximation question to be Class A, though this is a conjecture, not a claimed extension of the theorem. The paper’s local charging analysis looks like a plausible starting point for a mass-weighted argument. But its stability constraints are disjunctive: for each type pair, at least one side must have no willing mass. Fractional assignments alone do not turn that into an LP. The exact maximum-mass problem could therefore remain hard even if the \(4/3\)-approximation extends. Natural follow-up questions are whether the factor is tight in the continuous model, whether exact optimization is tractable, and how the mass optimum relates to large finite markets formed from clones of each type.

This is a credible mirror because its central object is the population distribution over preference types, and its objective, stability notion, and approximation question are those of the paper’s underlying matching problem. Fractional entries in \(x\) are the aggregate assignment induced by matching an atomless population; they do not give individual agents fractional partners. The mirror covers Theorem 2’s approximation result, not the paper’s entire proposal algorithm or proof.

My weakest point is the tie-size restriction. In a clone population, agents of one counterpart type are indistinguishable, so their individual-level tie can contain many agents. The proposed model preserves the bound of two at the type level, rather than literally preserving the paper’s bound of two named people. This is a real change, and the authors might reject it as a direct mirror. I think it remains defensible because it keeps the same matching, blocking, and cardinality questions while expressing preferences at the level where the continuous population is specified.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 2 has no nondegenerate high-multiplicity limit that preserves its defining tie restriction. In the paper, a tie is between at most two named people. In a market with repeated types, agents of one type must be indistinguishable in every way the matching problem uses—including how the other side ranks them. If a cohort is treated symmetrically, a preference for that cohort ties together all its members. A tie between two types therefore covers macroscopic masses, not two people.

There is a sharp choice. Preserve ties between named individuals, and in a fixed-support continuum those individuals have zero mass; keeping their identities distinct instead makes the number of types grow with the population. Or let agents rank counterpart types, as the proposed mirror does. That is a coherent model, but it replaces ties of size two with ties between whole cohorts. This is a central change to the paper’s regime: its proposal rules and tight analysis use local interactions among individual proposals, including a special case that relies on a tie containing at most two people.

The proposed mass-stability condition itself is sensible. If both sides have positive mass willing to match, an equal positive amount can be paired, so the \(W_i(j;x)\) test captures blocking in an atomless market. But the resulting question—whether a stable mass matching admits a \(4/3\)-approximation—is not yet a continuous version of Theorem 2’s analysis of Huang and Kavitha’s particular algorithm. An aggregate process would need its own proposal dynamics and proof; an abstract approximation question would concern the broader class-level-ties problem.

That leaves a real weakness in the negative case: class-level preferences and ties can describe plausible large markets, and the mass-matching problem is worth studying on its own. I can’t rule out that the authors would recognize it as their problem. My case is that the paper’s stated \(4/3\) result does not, by itself, anchor that mirror: faithfully keeping the two-person tie condition makes the continuum degenerate, while the viable type-level model changes that defining condition.
