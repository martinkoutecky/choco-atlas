The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity result such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. Its closest results are empirical: Table 1 reports that MOTTTS-interleaved identifies the best arm more often than the start and end query schedules, and Figure 3 reports an improvement for MCBULR2 on generated instances. Those findings support the value of optimizing query timing, but they are not the named results your anchor requirement calls for. So there are no anchors here, and no per-anchor mirror questions.

The strongest plausible *prospective* mirror is a group version of the paper’s best-arm problem. Imagine a platform choosing among policies for a large customer cohort. A type is a user’s complete preference profile \(w_t\in\Delta^{d-1}\), together with their response-noise parameter; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the cohort. There are many users but relatively few types. Arm pulls sample each policy’s \(d\)-objective outcomes. A query compares two outcome vectors by asking a randomly sampled cohort member which they prefer. Given a prior over possible rational population distributions \(\mu\), the task is to choose adaptively between arm pulls and preference queries, within separate budgets, to maximize the probability of recommending an arm that maximizes
\[ U_\mu(a)=\sum_t \mu_t\, w_t^\top \mathbb{E}[R_a]. \]
A solution is an adaptive policy and its terminal arm recommendation; the decision version asks whether some policy reaches a specified success probability. This keeps the paper’s central interaction—timing preference queries against experimental pulls—while making the population distribution the object being learned.

I think authors could recognize this as a group-decision-maker extension: the paper explicitly allows that the decision maker may be a group, and the query, pull, and best-arm components remain. For this proposed problem I would expect hardness to transfer from a finite-cohort version: a cohort with \(n_t\) members of each type corresponds to rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and sampling respondents with replacement preserves the query process. Establishing hardness for that finite-cohort problem would be the next step; this paper does not do so.

The soft spot is substantial: heterogeneous cohort utility replaces the paper’s single unknown utility function, and the authors’ experiments do not establish anything about the complexity of optimizing a population-level query policy. Nor do the continuous reward distributions or utility-weight simplex in the paper count as population continuization; they make rewards and preferences continuous while leaving the decision maker singular. This is therefore a plausible extension, not an anchored positive case for the paper as written.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result in the paper to anchor a mirror to. Its headline results are empirical comparisons of query schedules and MCBUL variants; the paper states no complexity bound or hardness result for the adaptive policy problem. That defeats a paper-anchored case, though it does not by itself show that a related question would be uninteresting.

The proposed cohort version has a real fork. If the group supplies one coherent utilitarian judgment, with type \(t\) having weight vector \(w_t\), then
\[ U_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t\,w_t^\top\mathbb{E}[R_a] =\left(\sum_t\mu_t w_t\right)^\top\mathbb{E}[R_a]. \]
For choosing the best arm, the population reduces to its average weight vector. A collective oracle that answers according to this utility therefore gives the paper the same single unknown utility it already models; the type masses are not the object driving the decision.

If queries instead go to randomly sampled individuals, the mass distribution matters, but there is no longer one preference operator \(\succ\) governing all answers. For noiseless linear preferences, for example, a query’s answer probability is \(\sum_{t:\,w_t^\top r_0>w_t^\top r_1}\mu_t\), which need not agree with the comparison under the average weight vector. The task now needs a choice of group objective and a model of heterogeneous responses. Majority preference, average utility, and committee consensus define different problems. That is a substantial preference-aggregation extension, rather than a continuous version of the problem the paper studies.

This is the strongest case against: the paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its most plausible population extension either collapses to a single utility or changes the feedback and group-choice model. But I cannot honestly make the universal negative airtight. The paper explicitly mentions a group decision maker, and a sampled-cohort version could be a worthwhile best-arm problem if its aggregation rule and response model are made explicit. The case against is strong for a mirror of this paper’s results, weaker for a group-preference-learning extension its authors might recognize.
