The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited. The paper has named analytical results—Proposition 1 gives a unique within-team symmetric equilibrium in the effort subgame, Proposition 3 characterizes equilibrium sharing rules under unbounded meritocracy, and Proposition 4 gives the threshold for the group-size paradox. But none is a computational-complexity result of the kind this task requires: none establishes that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. I therefore have no qualifying anchor, and can’t responsibly state a continuous problem “per anchor.”

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the model: large, stable departments competing for funds, whose members share a valuation and effort cost and face a common, public sharing rule. One could represent membership in the two departments by masses and ask for equilibrium effort and sharing rules. That would continuize the population, rather than the already divisible prize.

But that candidate does not supply the requested ChoCo case. The paper studies equilibrium analytically, and its named results are equilibrium characterizations and comparative statics, not computational questions. Its model already assumes homogeneity within each group; group sizes are parameters, not a large profile of distinct voter-like types. Also, an atomless population can change members’ incentives because an individual’s effect on aggregate contest success vanishes, so a continuous version would need careful scaling to preserve the paper’s game. The potential mirror is therefore an analytic continuum contest, not an anchored computational-complexity mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is the strongest point against a mirror: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1, 3, and 4 characterize an effort equilibrium, equilibrium sharing rules, and a threshold for the group-size paradox. None asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity.

The best computational recasting would take rational \(n_A,n_B,p\) as input and ask for the equilibrium or whether monopolization or the paradox occurs. But the model always has two groups and two sharing-rule choices. Replacing group sizes with masses leaves the same fixed-dimensional calculation; it does not create a growing type support or a pricing problem. The paper’s explicit formulas already expose the structure a complexity result would need to study. Adding many groups or heterogeneous types could create a richer computational problem, but that would be a new model rather than a continuous mirror of one of these results.

The proposed department scenario is sensible as a high-multiplicity regime. Group members are identical by assumption, so multiplicity itself is not an objection. The difficulty is that the paper has already compressed each department to its size and a representative member. More literally, an atomless population changes the effort game: one member’s action has zero effect on aggregate effort and contest success, while the member still pays the effort cost. That removes the individual incentive that drives Proposition 1; if everyone exerts zero effort, the contest-success ratio also needs a new definition. Treating each whole department as a strategic player avoids that problem, but returns to a two-player model where “continuization” only reparameterizes group size.

A distribution over heterogeneous valuations or costs is the strongest escape route. The paper itself mentions such heterogeneity as an omitted case, so it could motivate a genuine follow-on project. But a computational question about equilibrium in that richer model would not be anchored in a computational result of this paper, and it would need its own case that the authors would recognize the new problem as central.

So the negative case is strong for this paper’s anchors: the natural high-multiplicity setting exists, but the paper offers no computational problem whose continuous-population version would advance ChoCo’s complexity programme. I would not claim the universal negative is proved. Heterogeneous extensions may be worthwhile; they are simply not mirrors of the results this paper establishes.
