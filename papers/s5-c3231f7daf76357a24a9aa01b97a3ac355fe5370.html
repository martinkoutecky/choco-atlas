The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, gives the exact critical relative Hamming distance \(r(\alpha)\) for which a large finite electorate can exhibit the \(\alpha\)-paradox: \(r(\alpha)=1/[4(1-\alpha)]\) for \(\alpha\le 1/4\), and \(r(\alpha)=(\sqrt{\alpha}-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)\) for \(\alpha\ge 1/4\). Theorem 2 bounds the share of controversial issues. These are substantive mathematical results, but neither asserts a computational complexity classification. The other named propositions are also structural results; Propositions 2 and 3 cite results proved elsewhere. I would not present any of them as an anchor under your stated rule.

The strongest positive direction I see is a new computational question inspired by Theorem 1. Consider a large referendum electorate in which many residents share the same complete ballot, a vector in \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^C\). Let \(\mu_x\) be the fraction of the population with ballot \(x\). In a plausible regime, the number \(N\) of residents is much larger than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct ballots: for example, a jurisdiction voting on a fixed set of issues, with recurring political blocs and \(N\gg\tau\).

One precise problem is Continuous-Domain Anscombe Witness. An instance is an issue count \(C\), an explicitly listed set of admissible ballots \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^C\), and a rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Ask whether there is a population distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\) for which issue-wise majority produces an outcome \(y\) and more than an \(\alpha\) fraction of the population has Hamming distance greater than \(C/2\) from \(y\). Define ties by choosing \(0\): \(y_c=1\) exactly when \(\sum_{x:x_c=1}\mu_x>1/2\). A solution is \(\mu\) and its majority outcome \(y\), with
\[ \sum_{x:d_H(x,y)>C/2}\mu_x>\alpha. \]

This preserves the paper’s distinction between a vote domain and a distribution of voters over that domain; the population itself is the continuous object. It asks which admissible opinion cultures can support the paradox under some distribution, rather than merely replacing discrete counts in a fixed profile. For fixed \(C\), one can enumerate the \(2^C\) possible majority outcomes and solve a linear feasibility problem for each, so the question is tractable in that parameter. I would expect any broader hardness to come from the agenda size \(C\), but that is a hypothesis, not a result of this paper.

The scope would be the domain-dependent \(\alpha\)-paradox behind Theorem 1, not the paper’s result on controversial issues or its discussion of alternative dissatisfaction measures. The weak point is central: this computational problem is a plausible continuation of the paper, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named complexity result. Moreover, Theorem 1 already studies large electorates and may capture much of the limiting mathematics a continuous formulation would recover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 gives an exact threshold for a large-electorate extremal question; Theorem 2 bounds the number of controversial issues. The propositions are structural. None classifies the complexity of a problem or presents an algorithmic task. The paper’s “large electorates” do not by themselves create a missing computational landscape: the authors use them to derive mathematical bounds.

A direct continuous model is easy to write down and natural. Treat each ballot \(x\in T\) as a type with mass \(\mu_x\). Given \(\mu\), computing the issue-wise majority and the fraction with \(d_H(x,y)>C/2\) takes a pass over the \(C\) issues and \(\tau\) types. The same is true for counting controversial issues. That is a sensible high-multiplicity society, but the direct mirror offers little computational question beyond evaluating the paper’s quantities.

The proponent’s Continuous-Domain Anscombe Witness is the strongest escape from that objection: it asks whether *some* distribution over an admissible domain \(T\) can produce the paradox. For each proposed majority outcome \(y\), the mass constraints are linear, so one can test that outcome with an LP and enumerate the \(2^C\) possibilities. But this changes the input from a society to a ballot domain and searches over possible societies. It is a domain-design or extremal question, not a computation about a supplied continuous society. Theorem 1 studies a broad version of the same “can some profile exhibit the paradox?” question through a sharp universal distance threshold; the domain-specific search may be richer, but it is a new computational problem attached to the paper’s subject, not a mirror of one of its computational results.

A still stronger variant could ask for the least redistribution of mass needed to create the paradox. That would be a recognizable population-level robustness problem, but it needs a baseline society and a cost for changing its composition—structure the paper does not supply. Adding those would make a plausible ChoCo problem, not show that this paper contains a computational problem waiting to be continuized.

So I would argue against treating this paper as an anchored continuous mirror. The case does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Ballots are complete types, large electorates over a fixed agenda are plausible, and the population continuum does not degenerate. The domain-witness question could be worth pursuing on its own; its weakness as a mirror is that it changes the quantifier and computational task, not that the continuous population model is implausible.
