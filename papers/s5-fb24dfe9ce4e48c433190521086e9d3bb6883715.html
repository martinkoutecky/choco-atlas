The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(M\), a finite set of buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), explicit OR bid lists \(B_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), decide whether a feasible mass allocation reaches revenue \(K\). For each type, let \(\mathcal C_t\) be its collections of pairwise-disjoint atomic bids, including the empty collection; choose masses \(y_{t,Q}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=\mu_t\), total use of each good \(j\) at most \(q_j\), and revenue at least \(K\). In the anchor restriction, \(T=\{t\}\), \(\mu_t=1\), \(q_j=1\), and all bids have value \(1\) and size at most \(3\).

The model it lives in

A configuration LP over types and integral OR bid collections: variables \(y_{t,Q}\) are masses of agents receiving collection \(Q\); maximize \(\sum_{t,Q} y_{t,Q}\sum_{b\in Q}p_b\), subject to type-mass equalities and aggregate per-capita good-supply constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.2 and the WDPOR part of Corollary 4.5; it leaves the XOR, budget-constraint, multi-unit approximation, and small-item dynamic-programming results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the configuration problem's complexity depend on the number of types, supply scarcity, and bid-incidence structure?
  • For which structured bid families can the pricing problem over pairwise-disjoint bid collections be solved efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a repeated-buyer version of WDPOR: many buyers share the same complete OR-bid schedule, while the supply of each good scales with population mass. Its complexity remains in the choice among bundles, which is exactly where the chapter’s set-packing hardness lives.

Take a finite set of goods \(M\), buyer types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and for each type an explicit list \(B_t\) of atomic bids \(b=(S_b,p_b)\). A type is the full bid schedule, including its prices. Let \(q_j\) be the rational supply of good \(j\) per unit of population. An individual still receives an integral collection of pairwise-disjoint bids; mass records how much of a type receives each collection.

Write \(\mathcal C_t\) for the pairwise-disjoint collections of bids in \(B_t\), including the empty collection. A continuous allocation is a rational mass \(y_{t,Q}\ge 0\) for each \(Q\in\mathcal C_t\), with \(\sum_Q y_{t,Q}=\mu_t\). It must satisfy \(\sum_{t,Q}y_{t,Q}\sum_{b\in Q}\mathbf 1[j\in S_b]\le q_j\) for each good \(j\). Its revenue is \(\sum_{t,Q}y_{t,Q}\sum_{b\in Q}p_b\). Call the problem Mass-WDP\(_{\mathrm{OR}}\): given this instance and a threshold \(K\), decide whether a feasible mass allocation has revenue at least \(K\); the optimization version returns a maximum-revenue allocation \(y\). The bid collections remain indivisible for each agent: \(y_{t,Q}\) means that mass \(y_{t,Q}\) receives collection \(Q\).

The regime is a large cohort of, say, branches or facilities that use the same procurement template and compete for repeated resource categories. With \(n\) identical buyers and \(nq_j\) copies of each good \(j\), dividing the finite allocation and revenue by \(n\) gives the per-capita quantities above. Thus \(n\) can grow while the number of types stays at \(\tau=1\) (or a small fixed \(\tau\)). Replenishable goods, licenses, or capacity slots make supply proportional to the cohort plausible. The chapter itself discusses multi-unit WDP and symmetric item supplies, so this remains recognizable as its allocation problem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.2. It states that the decision version of WDPOR is NP-complete even when every bid has value \(1\) and every bidder bids only on sets of at most \(3\) items. The chapter attributes this result to Rothkopf et al. (1998), describing the reduction as coming from 3-set packing; it cites rather than reproves the theorem.

The corresponding continuous question is the exact threshold problem for Mass-WDP\(_{\mathrm{OR}}\), already in the restriction \(T=\{t\}\), \(\mu_t=1\), and \(q_j=1\) for every good. Put each set of a 3-set-packing instance on the single type’s OR bid list at price \(1\). Each \(Q\in\mathcal C_t\) is then a packing. Because \(Q\) uses each good at most once and the total mass is \(1\), the supply constraints hold for every such \(Q\). Any feasible \(y\) is a distribution over packings, so its revenue is an average of their sizes; a best pure packing attains the maximum. Consequently the continuous optimum equals the discrete 3-set-packing optimum, and the threshold question is NP-complete on this one-type subfamily. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). The chapter’s bid lists can be exponential in \(m\), so the natural follow-up is whether separation over these collections is tractable for structured bid families.

A second useful anchor is Corollary 4.5. It states that, unless \(P=NP\), there is no PTAS for WDPOR or WDPXOR even when bids have size at most \(3\), every item appears in at most two bids, and all bid prices are \(1\). The chapter derives the corollary by the same transformation from the cited bounded-degree stable-set result of Berman and Fujito (1999). For the WDPOR side, the continuous question is whether Mass-WDP\(_{\mathrm{OR}}\) admits a PTAS under those restrictions, even at \(\tau=1\) and \(q_j=1\). Given a graph of maximum degree \(3\), make one unit bid per vertex on the set of its incident edges. A pair of bids is disjoint exactly when the vertices are nonadjacent; each bid uses at most three goods, and each edge-good occurs in two bids. The continuous optimum is the graph’s maximum stable-set size, by the same averaging argument. So this restricted continuous problem has no PTAS unless \(P=NP\), again Class B. This anchor adds an approximation boundary under sparse incidence, rather than merely restating the exact hardness result.

The mirror covers Theorem 3.2 and the WDPOR side of Corollary 4.5. It does not claim to cover the chapter’s XOR, budget-constraint, or small-item dynamic-programming results. Further questions include whether pricing becomes tractable under incidence restrictions, how the landscape changes when \(q_j\) is scarce and types compete for goods, and which parameters—such as \(\tau\), maximum bid size, or incidence structure—yield useful algorithms.

The weakest point is that the clean hardness embedding uses one type and one per-capita unit of each good. Interbuyer scarcity disappears in that subcase, leaving the within-type OR package choice as the hard part. This is a narrow mirror of WDPOR, and the chapter’s most familiar auction instances may have fixed inventory and many distinct buyers. The case survives because it keeps the chapter’s OR semantics, integral packages, revenue objective, and goods constraints, while scaling a multi-unit supply with a genuinely high-multiplicity buyer population. It is also a precise boundary result: the bundle combinatorics remain hard even when population multiplicity itself is no longer the source of hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against both anchors is that, in the proposed subfamily, the population does no computational work. With one type and per-capita supply \(q_j=1\), every allowed package \(Q\) uses each good at most once, so supply never binds. A mass allocation \(y\) is just a distribution over packages, and its revenue is an average; an optimal pure package is at least as good. The continuous problem therefore collapses to choosing a maximum-value packing of one OR bidder’s bids. The hard combinatorics sit entirely inside that bidder’s bid schedule, not in competition among masses of buyers.

Corollary 4.5 does not add a separate population phenomenon: its stable-set construction gives the same collapse, now with an approximation lower bound. These results show that the package-choice problem remains hard after wrapping it in a repeated-buyer story. They do not, on their own, show that continuizing the population reveals a new computational landscape. The particularly strong “one type” framing hides an arbitrarily complex set-packing instance inside the type itself.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror. If supplies are scarce per capita, or several types compete, the mass model becomes a configuration LP: choose masses \(y_{t,Q}\) of buyers receiving integral packages \(Q\), subject to \(\sum_Q y_{t,Q}=\mu_t\) and aggregate good-supply constraints. Its pricing problem optimizes over disjoint bid collections. This is a coherent high-multiplicity version of WDP, and the chapter’s discussion of multi-unit WDP makes the setting recognizable. Whether it is easy or hard is a Class A/B question; hardness transferring from set packing is not a reason to reject it.

So the anchors support a narrower criticism: their cleanest reductions establish hardness in a regime where population aggregation is inert. They do not establish that every credible scarce-supply, multi-type mirror is inert or uninteresting. I cannot honestly make the universal case against this paper; the negative case is weak precisely at that stronger model.
