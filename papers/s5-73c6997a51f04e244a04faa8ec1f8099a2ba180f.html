What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Theorem 1, Corollary 2, Theorems 2 and 3, and the Extreme Liberalism impossibility result are axiomatic claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a plausible population extension, but this paper provides no named computational result to anchor it. Theorem 1 says that, for finite \(N\) with \(|N|>2\), Symmetry and Decisiveness uniquely characterize the Strong Liberal CIF. Corollary 2 gives the analogous result with Minimal Decisiveness. Theorems 2 and 3 uniquely characterize the Inclusive and Unanimity CIFs under their stated axioms. These are axiomatic characterizations, not complexity or algorithmic results. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no continuous problem per anchor to state or classify.

A plausible adjacent model would have a large panel judging a fixed finite roster \(M\): a type \(S\subseteq M\) records one complete opinion, and \(\mu_S\) is the fraction of the panel holding it. The decision is a subset \(J\subseteq M\). This could fit a setting where many respondents share a small number of views. But separating respondents from the people being classified changes the paper’s setup: its agents are both opinion-holders and possible members of \(J\), and axioms such as Decisiveness depend on that identity. Keeping the original setup as the population grows also makes the opinion-type space \(2^N\) grow with \(N\).

That is the weak point: there is a credible route to a related continuous population model, but the paper’s results do not establish a computational question for that model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 characterize the Strong Liberal CIF; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize the Inclusive and Unanimity CIFs. The impossibility result is axiomatic too. None defines an algorithmic problem, an input encoding, an objective, or a complexity question. So the proponent’s named results are not computational anchors. A continuous characterization of CIFs would still be an axiomatic project, outside the programme’s scope.

The fixed-roster panel is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and I would not reject it just because the voters and the people being classified are separate. But that separation removes the paper’s defining link between an agent’s opinions and their own possible membership. One could adapt the axioms to the panel model; even accepting those changes, the paper supplies no computational question to answer. Evaluating its named rules from a distribution over opinion types would amount to checking support or unanimity. A more substantial task—such as minimizing the mass of opinion changes needed to alter a classification—would require adding intervention costs and semantics the paper never studies. That could be worthwhile work, but it would be a new computational project, not a mirror of these results.

The case is weaker if taken as a claim that no continuous group-identification problem could ever be worthwhile: this paper cannot establish that. Its stronger, supportable conclusion is that no proposed scenario turns this paper’s contributions into a ChoCo computational mirror.
