What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror uses Theorem 6's maximum-inconsistency graph and value \(Y(n)\); it does not provide a continuous version of the paper's index computation or set-cover discussion.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a population version of the paper’s maximum inconsistency with ties problem. The panel scenario is a large public consultation on a shortlist of \(n\) alternatives: many respondents share the same complete ranking, so the number \(N\) of respondents can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings represented. A type is one complete strict ranking \(\sigma\); its mass \(\mu_\sigma\) is the fraction of respondents with that ranking. For each pair \(c_i,c_j\), compare the mass ranking \(c_i\) above \(c_j\) with \(1/2\): a greater mass gives \(c_i\to c_j\), a smaller mass gives \(c_j\to c_i\), and exactly \(1/2\) gives a tie. Thus individual types are transitive, while the population’s pairwise-majority relation may contain the inconsistent triads studied in the paper. Aggregate ties need not mean that individual respondents were indifferent.

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n>3\), a generalized tournament graph formed from two maximal tournament graphs on disjoint sets of sizes \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) and \(\lceil n/2\rceil\), with every cross-set pair tied, attains the paper’s upper bound on the number of inconsistent triads. The number of directed edges at this maximum is
\[ X(n)=\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{2}+\binom{\lceil n/2\rceil}{2}, \]
and the maximum number of inconsistent triads is
\[ Y(n)=\binom n3- \left[ \binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{3}-I(\lfloor n/2\rfloor) +\binom{\lceil n/2\rceil}{3}-I(\lceil n/2\rceil) \right], \]
where \(I(r)=(r^3-r)/24\) for odd \(r\), and \(I(r)=(r^3-4r)/24\) for even \(r\). The result identifies both the maximizing edge count and a graph structure attaining the maximum. The paper does not state a formal complexity-class theorem; this is its strongest named computational anchor because it gives an exact solution to the underlying extremal optimization problem.

The continuous problem is Population Majority Inconsistency Maximization. Its instance is a set \(C\) of \(n\ge4\) alternatives and a threshold \(K\), with all strict rankings over \(C\) available as types. Ask whether there is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types whose pairwise-majority graph has at least \(K\) inconsistent triads. A solution is such a distribution, together with its induced graph. Equivalently, maximize the inconsistent-triad count over \(\mu_\sigma\ge0\) with \(\sum_\sigma\mu_\sigma=1\). The types are represented implicitly; a solution can be given with finite rational support.

The paper’s bound applies because every resulting majority graph is a generalized tournament graph. It is also attainable by such a population: take the two maximal tournament graphs from Theorem 6 as the within-group majority relations, and choose the order between the groups in opposite directions for equal mass. Then cross-group pairs tie, while the within-group majority relations remain intact. Maximal tournaments can be realized by distributions over strict rankings. So the continuous optimum is exactly \(Y(n)\), and the threshold problem is tractable. The graph extremum is the paper’s result; its realization as a ranking population is the additional argument for this mirror.

This covers Theorem 6’s extremal result, not the paper’s index-computation discussion or its connection to set cover. The set-cover passage does not establish that the paper’s pairwise-comparison problem is NP-hard: it describes a related cover formulation and gives a special construction.

The weakest point is the change from assessing one given pairwise-comparison matrix to choosing a population whose majority relation produces such a matrix. A paper author might see this as a group-aggregation problem adjacent to their own, rather than as their original problem. The panel scenario makes that shift meaningful—the matrix is the group’s collective comparison record—but the mirror is strongest as a population-source model for the paper’s extremal result, not as a claim that the paper already studies high-multiplicity societies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The weak point in this anchor is that Theorem 6 is an extremal theorem about a comparison graph, not a computational result on a society. Its vertices are alternatives; its edges encode one set of judgments. The theorem gives no complexity classification or algorithmic question whose input is a population. That makes the proposed mirror do extra work: it must supply a population model and an aggregation rule before there is a continuous object to study.

The proposed panel of respondents supplies both. But it changes what inconsistency means. In the paper, the judgments themselves may be nontransitive, and triad inconsistency diagnoses that expert’s comparisons. In the panel model, each person has a transitive ranking, and inconsistency belongs to the majority relation. That is a sensible social-choice question, but it is a question about Condorcet cycles in collective rankings, not the paper’s diagnosis of inconsistent pairwise assessments. Aggregate ties also mean a \(50\)-\(50\) split, whereas the paper’s ties are judgments of equality. The graph notation matches; the object being assessed does not.

The better attempt is to make each type a complete pairwise-comparison matrix, including ties and possibly nontransitive judgments, then aggregate a population of such experts. That preserves the paper’s kind of data, but now the result concerns a collective aggregation rule and a distribution over experts—neither appears in the paper. If instead types are restricted to transitive rankings so that population mass has the proposed interpretation, the mirror returns to the change in meaning above. A further question about reweighting a fixed panel to increase its inconsistency could be computationally interesting, but it needs an intervention model, costs, or constraints that the paper does not motivate. It is a nearby new problem, not a continuous version of Theorem 6.

There is also a basic input mismatch. If the society is supplied, its pairwise-majority graph and triad count can be computed from the support and masses. If the society is freely chosen, the population is only a way to represent a graph being chosen; it is not a given high-multiplicity instance. Adding support restrictions could make that choice substantive, but then those restrictions determine a new problem. So the negative case is that the strongest anchor offers no natural population-input computation to continuize. I cannot make the universal claim airtight: a large panel whose members rank alternatives is plausible, and an author may regard its majority inconsistency as close enough to their subject. That is the point where this case is weakest.
