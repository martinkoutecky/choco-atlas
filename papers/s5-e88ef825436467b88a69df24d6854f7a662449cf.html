  • Lemma 2: Possible Winner is NP-hard for \((m-2)\)-approval. — survived: The opponent engaged the mass-splitting difference and accepts it as the intended atomless relaxation, so the continuous question survives.

The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(c^\star\), and a finite support of partial orders \(p\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_p\) summing to one, determine whether each type’s mass can be distributed among its valid linear extensions so that \(c^\star\) is a co-winner under \((m-2)\)-approval. Equivalently, for each feasible unordered bottom pair \(\{a,b\}\) of each \(p\), choose \(x_{p,\{a,b\}}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\{a,b\}}x_{p,\{a,b\}}=\mu_p\), so that the total mass placing \(c^\star\) in the bottom pair is no greater than the corresponding total for any other candidate.

The model it lives in

Use partial orders as voter types and their rational population masses as input. Let \(x_{p,\{a,b\}}\) be the mass of type \(p\) assigned to valid completions with bottom pair \(\{a,b\}\); impose mass-conservation constraints and require each candidate’s bottom-pair mass to be at least that of \(c^\star\). Feasible bottom pairs can be checked in polynomial time, yielding a rational LP with at most \(\tau\binom{m}{2}\) variables.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 2 for \((m-2)\)-approval and leaves the paper’s other scoring-rule results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What rounding or additive-approximation guarantees relate the mass LP to finite profiles with \(n\) voters and moderate support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the continuous mirror of Lemma 2, proved in this paper by a reduction from Multicolored Clique. The lemma states that Possible Winner is NP-hard for \((m-2)\)-approval: with an unbounded number \(m\) of candidates and unweighted voters, the input is a profile of partial orders and a distinguished candidate, and the question is whether some completion of every partial order makes that candidate a winner. The paper also says its results extend to the unique-winner version; my mirror below addresses the co-winner question. This is my only anchor: it gives a concrete hardness result to test, rather than stretching the case to the paper’s full scoring-rule classification.

Consider a large consultation or ranking platform with millions of participants and a fixed slate of \(m\) options. Participants may leave some comparisons unresolved, perhaps because a guided form asks only for selected comparisons. Suppose the platform uses \((m-2)\)-approval: each completed ranking gives a point to every option except the two ranked last. Reusable form patterns or recurring partial rankings could give a regime with millions of people but only hundreds or thousands of distinct partial orders. That is the high-multiplicity setting: mass is the fraction of the population with each partial-order pattern.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Fractional Possible Winner for \((m-2)\)-Approval. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a distinguished candidate \(c^\star\), and a support list of partial orders \(p\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_p\), where \(\sum_p\mu_p=1\). Let \(\tau\) be the number of supported partial orders. For each \(p\), let \(F(p)\) be the unordered pairs \(\{a,b\}\subseteq C\) that can occupy the last two positions in some linear extension of \(p\). Choose nonnegative real masses \(x_{p,\{a,b\}}\), requiring
\[ \sum_{\{a,b\}\in F(p)}x_{p,\{a,b\}}=\mu_p \quad\text{for every }p. \]
For each candidate \(c\), define its mass in the last two positions as
\[ B(c)=\sum_p\ \sum_{\substack{\{a,b\}\in F(p)\\c\in\{a,b\}}}x_{p,\{a,b\}}. \]
The question is whether there is such an allocation with \(B(c^\star)\le B(c)\) for every \(c\in C\). That is exactly the winner condition: \(c\)’s score is \(1-B(c)\). A solution is the allocation \(x\); each portion of mass can be assigned any valid full ranking witnessing its chosen bottom pair. Thus every individual in the continuum still receives an ordinary linear ranking. It is the population mass of a shared partial-order type that can be divided among valid completions.

I think the authors should recognize this as their Possible Winner question at population scale. Partial preferences and completion are central to the paper, not an add-on, and a partial order fully specifies which completions are available to that type. The mirror changes the population representation and permits mass-level assignment of completions; it keeps the candidates, scoring rule, target-winner condition, and uncertainty about how partial votes are completed.

This mirror is tractable. For each type and each candidate pair, whether that pair can be last in an extension is testable in polynomial time: add the constraints putting all other candidates above the pair and check whether the resulting order constraints are consistent. The resulting rational LP has at most \(\tau\binom{m}{2}\) variables and polynomially many constraints, so it is solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The expected outcome is therefore that the NP-hardness in Lemma 2 does not survive this continuization.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this is a mirror of Lemma 2’s \((m-2)\)-approval possible-winner problem, not of the paper’s entire dichotomy. It also raises useful follow-ups: when does a fractional winning allocation round to an integral completion of a finite profile, and how much score error can rounding cause? For other scoring rules, which aspects of a completion must be tracked before a similarly small LP is possible?

My weakest point is also the reason the algorithm may work: the reduction behind Lemma 2 relies on indivisible choices of zero-score positions, while this mirror lets a type’s mass split across bottom pairs. So its NP-hardness does not transfer directly. That means the mirror does not preserve every finite instance’s yes-or-no answer. But fractional mass assignment is the population relaxation being tested here, and the underlying question—whether the target can win under valid completions of the reported partial preferences—remains intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against Lemma 2 is weak. I can identify a real difference between the finite problem and the proposed mirror, but under ChoCo’s stated standard it does not defeat the mirror.

In the paper’s Possible Winner problem, each named voter gets one completion of their partial order. In the proposed continuum, mass of one partial-order type can be split among several completions. So an LP-feasible mirror need not preserve the finite instance’s yes-or-no answer. But that is exactly the intended population relaxation: every individual still receives an ordinary ranking, while an atomless group can divide across rankings. A fixed consultation form or recurring partial-ranking pattern gives a plausible shared type. Treating prior high-multiplicity work as a collision would be wrong; it supports the regime instead. [The paper’s Lemma 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.3492) supplies the computational anchor, and [work on high-multiplicity elections](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2018/pdfs/p1558.pdf) supports the population representation.

The mirror also survives a stronger formulation. Let each type’s mass be distributed over its full linear extensions. For \((m-2)\)-approval, only the bottom pair affects scores, so those distributions can be projected exactly onto variables \(x_{p,\{a,b\}}\). A pair is feasible when the partial order can be extended with both candidates last; this can be checked by testing order consistency. The constraints that each type’s mass is fully assigned and that \(c^\star\)’s bottom-pair mass is no greater than any rival’s are linear. Thus this is not merely a convenient approximation: it decides whether the target can win in the continuous society.

The best adverse point is about research depth. This particular mirror appears to yield directly to a polynomial-size LP, with no hard pricing problem or deep structural theorem. That may make it a modest result, and the gap between fractional feasibility and finite completions may deserve much of the follow-up work. But “straightforward” and “narrow” can argue for lower priority; they do not show that the question is outside the programme or not worth asking.

So I cannot honestly defeat the sole anchor, even after considering a better version than the one initially stated. Lemma 2 has a coherent, tractable continuous-population mirror, and the negative case should concede that it survives.
