The continuous mirror question

For \(n\) types of mass \(\mu_t=1/n\), availability probabilities \(p_t\), budget \(b=k/n\), and a normalized monotone submodular set utility \(f\), let \(G(z)=F_f(nz)\), where \(F_f\) is the multilinear extension of \(f\). Choose \(x_t\in[0,1]\); independently, type \(t\) contributes its full mass with probability \(p_tx_t\), giving realized mass vector \(Z\). Maximize \(\mathbb{E}[G(Z)]\) subject to \(\sum_t\mu_tp_tx_t\le b\): does any constant-factor approximation better than \(\rho(k)=\frac{1-e^{-k/2}}{1-(k/2+1)e^{-k}}\) yield a polynomial-time algorithm for Planted Clique?

The model it lives in

A cohort-level stochastic seeding problem over a population distribution: each type has mass \(\mu_t\), control \(x_t\) sets its activation probability \(p_tx_t\), the whole cohort’s mass is realized together, and the objective is expected type-mass utility under an expected-mass budget.

The objection that survived

The SOSP reduction depends on whole cohorts sharing Bernoulli availability; independent member-level availability concentrates, and the edge-sensitive utility need not retain its value in that fluid limit.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.10’s SOSP hardness result; it does not cover Theorem 3.1’s adaptive-seeding approximation or the other adaptivity-gap and algorithmic results.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s SOSP hardness result. In a large online campaign, let each type be a community role: its members share a response profile and the same coverage relationships. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types. There may be \(N\gg\tau\) users, with many members in each cohort.

Call the mirror Cohort-SOSP\(_\infty\). Its input is rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), small availability parameters \(p_t\), an expected recruited-mass budget \(b\), and a normalized monotone DR-submodular utility \(G\) over type-mass vectors. Choose exposure intensities \(x_t\in[0,1]\). A cohort becomes available with probability \(p_tx_t\), independently across types, so the realized mass is \(Z_t=\mu_t\) if it becomes available and \(0\) otherwise. The objective is to maximize \(\mathbb{E}[G(Z)]\), subject to
\[ \sum_t \mu_t p_t x_t\le b. \]
For example, \(G\) can be the multilinear extension of a monotone submodular utility on active cohorts. The control \(x_t\) determines expected recruited mass; the objective measures the expected benefit of the realized population. A plausible regime is outreach through large communities where availability is a shared channel or administrator decision, rather than an independent event for every member.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2.10, proved in this paper. It states that if SOSP with constant budget \(k\) can be approximated by any constant factor better than
\[ \rho(k)=\frac{1-e^{-k/2}}{1-(k/2+1)e^{-k}}, \]
then there is a polynomial-time algorithm for Planted Clique that succeeds with high probability. At \(k=1.7\), the threshold is approximately \(0.865\). The paper proves this by reducing from a Planted-Clique-hard Densest-\(l\)-subgraph distinction, using a monotone submodular objective that gives value \(1\) when the realized set contains an edge and otherwise increases with its size. The underlying Planted-Clique hardness is cited; Theorem 2.10’s reduction and implication are proved here.

The type-cohort model preserves that question. Given an SOSP instance on \(n\) elements, use \(n\) types of mass \(1/n\), set the mirror budget to \(b=k/n\), and let the utility at full-cohort mass vectors agree with the paper’s set function \(f\). Its multilinear extension supplies \(G\) between those vectors. Then the mirror’s expected utility is the SOSP expected utility, and its expected-mass constraint is the SOSP expected-size constraint scaled by \(1/n\). The reduction’s graph structure lives in the \(\tau=n\) types and their interactions. Adding many indistinguishable users to each type does not remove that combinatorics. I therefore expect the result to remain Class B hardness for this mirror, under the paper’s Planted-Clique implication.

This is a credible analogue for authors working on stochastic submodular seeding: the expected-value objective and resource constraint remain central, while the decision and outcome are expressed in population mass. Existing work on continuous outcome spaces would not itself answer this question; the object being varied here is the population distribution over network roles. The mirror covers Theorem 2.10’s SOSP hardness, not the paper’s full adaptive-seeding result or Theorem 3.1.

The soft spot is the cohort-wide availability shock. The paper models independent neighbor appearances, whereas members of a continuous type cohort would often respond independently. With many such independent members, the realized mass may concentrate around its expectation, potentially erasing the stochastic effect that makes SOSP hard. So this case stands or falls on whether shared availability—such as a community channel opening or a group administrator responding—is a convincing instance regime. A useful next question is whether the hardness survives when availability is independent at the member level, and what continuous limit results when it does not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2.10 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but its mass rescaling does not yet give a high-multiplicity mirror. With \(n\) graph vertices treated as types of mass \(1/n\), an SOSP choice with expected size at most \(k\) uses expected population mass at most \(k/n\). In the theorem’s hard instances, \(k\) is constant while \(n\) grows. The reduction therefore gets a constant expected value from a vanishing fraction of the society: its set function rewards the presence of an edge, however little mass is assigned to either endpoint. That is a singular sparse-intervention regime, not a stable aggregate-population objective.

The shared-availability assumption preserves the theorem’s random set, but it does so by making every member of a type cohort share one Bernoulli event. The paper instead assumes independent appearances of individual neighbors. If cohort members appear independently, their realized mass concentrates; moreover, an objective that rewards any positive mass at both ends of an edge no longer behaves like the paper’s edge-presence function. Defining a smooth mass utility or fixing a positive per-capita budget could produce a sensible fluid seeding problem, but the planted-clique reduction in Theorem 2.10 does not establish a result for that model. The fixed-budget construction cannot simply be carried over to a fixed positive mass budget.

There is a more exact formal rescue: set \(y_i=q_i/n\) and define \(H(y)=F_f(ny)\), where \(F_f\) is the multilinear extension of the paper’s set function. Then maximizing \(H\) under \(\sum_i y_i\leq k/n\) reproduces SOSP exactly. But the randomness still belongs to the original \(n\) graph labels; the masses merely rescale their activation probabilities. In the planted-clique instances, arbitrary adjacency patterns make those labels distinct types. This is a continuous reparameterization of the SOSP input, not evidence that aggregating many indistinguishable agents preserves its computational question.

The main theorem offers no better anchor for ChoCo. Its locally adaptive policies rely on choosing different individual neighbors after observing their realizations; their blocks have bounded cardinality, so their population mass vanishes as the society grows. A fluid version could be worth studying, but it would ask about mass allocation under concentrated, effectively deterministic availability rather than the paper’s realization-contingent seeding problem.

There is also a scope issue: this paper studies stochastic network optimization, not a social-choice problem involving collective decisions. Its continuous seeding variants may interest a separate continuous-optimization programme, but that alone does not make them ChoCo mirrors. I would reject the proposed SOSP mirror as evidence for this programme. I would not claim to have proved that no worthwhile continuous version exists in any scenario: a mean-field network campaign could be a sensible problem in its own right. The case against is strongest on programme fit and on the failure of the planted-clique anchor to survive genuine population aggregation.
