The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational result: it numbers no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification. Its claim that minimum feedback arc set is NP-hard is background about the method from Procaccia et al., not a numbered result proved here. The finding that Kemeny aggregation and simple voting give the same top three emotions is an empirical result, not a complexity result. So I can’t honestly supply a paper-anchored result under the requested rule.

The strongest candidate mirror is the paper’s Kemeny aggregation problem. Imagine a large crowdsourcing platform where many evaluators infer emotions from text and return rankings. A type is an evaluator’s response: for the paper’s task, an ordered top three among the six emotions, with the unranked emotions tied below them. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these response types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of responses of type \(t\). The decision is a full ranking \(\pi\) of the emotions. Choose \(\pi\) to minimize expected Kendall-style pairwise disagreement:
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,d(\pi,t), \]
where \(d\) charges one for each pair ordered contrary to a type’s strict preference and a fixed \(1/2\) for each pair that type ties. Return the minimizing ranking and, as in the paper, its top three.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario: an established platform may collect far more annotations than there are distinct response patterns, and the aggregation method uses each evaluator’s reported ranking, not their identity. The paper itself discusses rankings, weighted tournaments, Kemeny aggregation, and its NP-hardness, so its authors would likely recognize the optimization question. For rational \(\mu\), multiplying by a common denominator gives a finite weighted electorate with the same optima. Thus, when the number of emotions is part of the input, the natural expectation is hardness transfers; with the paper’s fixed six emotions, the output space is only \(6!\), so the case study itself offers no asymptotic hardness question.

The weak point is that this is a mirror of the computation the paper uses, not an anchor the paper establishes, and with six emotions it is a small fixed problem. It makes a credible case that the underlying aggregation problem has a continuous-population form, but it does not turn the paper’s empirical comparison into a result about complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continue. Its findings are empirical comparisons on twelve transcripts. The NP-hardness of minimum feedback arc set is background for an aggregation method the authors use; the paper proves no complexity result about it.

The best version of the proposed Kemeny mirror is coherent: a large annotation platform, ranking responses as types, and rational masses as their population shares. That makes a sensible high-multiplicity problem. But it asks for a complexity classification of a method’s optimization problem, not a continuous version of a result this paper establishes. The paper fixes six emotions and reports whether Kemeny and simple voting agree on its data; it does not study how that computation scales. Making the number or kind of emotions variable broadens the question further. A population-level version of the agreement finding would also need assumptions about how annotators produce responses, which the paper does not supply.

RQ2 does not provide a stronger anchor: it reports machine–human match rates for predictions based on individual transcripts, rather than a computational social-choice result about an aggregate population.
