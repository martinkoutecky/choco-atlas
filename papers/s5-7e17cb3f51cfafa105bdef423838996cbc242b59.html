The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor check gives no qualifying result: the paper has no numbered theorem, proposition, or corollary asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. Its numbered results characterize Bayesian Nash equilibria. So I can offer a plausible computational sequel, but I can’t honestly call it an anchor-compliant mirror of a complexity result.

The strongest bridge is Proposition 2, proved in the paper. It says that for \(r\in(1/m,(m-1)/m]\), full turnout, full abstention, and either one-sided-turnout profile are equilibria for every parameter setting. Certain profiles with mixed turnout are equilibria for some parameter settings; profiles where one group abstains for sure while the other mixes are never equilibria. This is a useful anchor for an equilibrium-computation question, though not a computational-complexity result.

My lead candidate is Asymptotic Referendum Equilibrium Set. The society is \(\mu\delta_A+(1-\mu)\delta_B\): type A prefers yes, type B prefers no, and mass is each type’s share. A plausible regime is a large referendum population with many voters of each type, sharing the same preferences and payoffs. Given rational \(\mu,r,v\in(0,1)\) and precision \(\epsilon>0\), consider the paper’s game for each odd population size \(N\), with types drawn independently using probability \(\mu\), quorum the odd integer nearest to \(rN\), and the same voting and payoff rules. Let \(E_N\subseteq[0,1]^2\) be its symmetric Bayesian Nash equilibria, where each coordinate is a type’s turnout probability. The task is to return a finite union of rational boxes within Hausdorff distance \(\epsilon\) of the accumulation set of \(E_N\) as odd \(N\to\infty\). The answer is an approximation to the equilibrium set, not a welfare optimum. For \(r\in(0,1)\), Proposition 2’s four pure profiles persist in the finite approximants; the computational question is what happens to the mixed-equilibrium branches in the limit.

I would expect this two-type version to be Class A: its strategy space is two-dimensional, and the finite games’ pivotal probabilities are binomial expressions. The main work is to control their limits uniformly, especially near quorum and payoff boundaries. Further questions include the convergence rate of \(E_N\) to its limit set and how the problem changes as the number of voter types grows.

The soft spot is substantial: in a literal nonatomic game, an individual’s action does not change aggregate turnout, so the paper’s pivotal-voting incentives disappear. The proposed question preserves them by defining the continuous object through limits of finite games. The authors would likely recognize that as a large-electorate extension of their model, but a referee could reasonably say it is an asymptotic-equilibrium problem rather than a direct continuum game. That is why this is a credible research direction, not a strong anchored positive case under the requested complexity-result standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strict anchor check is decisive here: Proposition 2 classifies symmetric Bayesian Nash equilibria; it does not give the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 1 and 3 and the corollaries are equilibrium characterizations too. The experiment reports turnout and outcome patterns. None supplies a computational result whose continuous mirror this paper could anchor.

That is not because the population lacks a plausible high multiplicity regime. A national referendum can have millions of voters grouped into two complete types, yes-preferrers and no-preferrers. That is a particularly clean type model, and the voters’ identities do not matter. I would not reject the mirror on realism grounds.

The more fundamental difficulty is what individual pivotality does in the continuum. If the society has masses \(\mu\) and \(1-\mu\), one voter has measure zero. Changing that voter’s action cannot change turnout mass, satisfy or violate the quorum, or change which side wins. Since the paper assumes no voting cost, voting and abstaining then give the same payoff to each individual. Every action is a best response, so the continuum game loses the strategic incentive behind Proposition 2. That is a degeneration of the model’s central mechanism, not a problem with carrying over its literal notation.

The proposed accumulation set \(E_N\) is the strongest repair. It retains each voter’s \(1/N\) influence in finite games and asks how their equilibria behave as \(N\) grows. That is a coherent asymptotic equilibrium problem, and it could be worthwhile on its own. But its strategic content comes from the finite games’ pivotal events; it is not equilibrium computation in the atomless society. It therefore does not turn Proposition 2 into a computational result about a continuous population. Treating blocs as players could restore pivotality, but would replace individual voters with strategic coalitions and change the object of the paper.

So I would not endorse this as an anchored ChoCo mirror. My case is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous computational project can be built from the paper: the \(E_N\) problem may be a good project if the programme accepts asymptotic game computation. What the paper itself supports is a continuous limit of an equilibrium model, not a computational-complexity result for the programme to continuize.
