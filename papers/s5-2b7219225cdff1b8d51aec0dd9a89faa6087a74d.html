The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) good categories, \(\tau\) buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational per-unit values, and rational category supplies \(q_s\) summing to two, each buyer wants two distinct units and every welfare-maximizing allocation gives almost every buyer two units. The welfare benchmark is the maximum of the bundle-allocation LP over type masses and category capacities. Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the binary input length that outputs a compact state-feedback pricing rule, evaluable in polynomial time, which posts category prices using only the remaining type masses and supplies and achieves the benchmark for every measurable arrival path with type distribution \(\mu\) and every measurable choice among utility-maximizing bundles?

The model it lives in

A fluid market with \(m\) categories of individually indivisible but same-valued units, category supplies \(q_s\), and \(\tau\) complete buyer types of masses \(\mu_t\). Each buyer wants two units; type-level bundle masses are decision variables, category use is capacity-constrained, and welfare is the sum of assigned bundle values. The seller uses a compact rule mapping remaining type masses and supplies to category prices, without observing the next type.

The objection that survived

The finite theorem does not establish that a compact state-feedback policy remains well-defined and welfare-optimal along every measurable atomless arrival path.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 14 for bi-demand markets satisfying the fluid version of (OPT). It leaves Theorems 10 and 11, general multi-demand markets, and instances that do not satisfy (OPT) outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a compact pricing rule guarantee optimal welfare for every measurable type-arrival path and every measurable utility-maximizing choice?
  • Can the finite adequate-ordering argument be replaced by a fractional condition that can be characterized and computed efficiently?
  • Does the guarantee extend to demand at most three or to markets where some welfare-maximizing allocations leave buyers short of demand?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass Bi-Demand Dynamic Pricing, a fluid version of the problem in Theorem 14. Proved in this paper, Theorem 14 says that every bi-demand market satisfying (OPT) admits an optimal dynamic pricing scheme, and that the prices can be computed in polynomial time. Here (OPT) means every buyer receives exactly two items in every optimal allocation. “Optimal” preserves the paper’s demanding guarantee: buyers may arrive in any order, each may choose any utility-maximizing bundle, and the seller updates prices using the remaining market without knowing who arrives next; every resulting final allocation must maximize social welfare.

For the mirror, let \(S\) be \(m\) good categories and \(T\) the \(\tau\) complete buyer types, where a type specifies the buyer’s value for every category and demand is two. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Category \(s\) has rational supply \(q_s\), with \(\sum_s q_s=2\). Each category represents a continuum of distinct, individually indivisible units with the same values for every type. Thus each individual still chooses at most two distinct units, while a mass of identical buyers can split across different bundles. Assume the fluid version of (OPT): every welfare-maximizing allocation gives almost every buyer two units.

The welfare benchmark is explicit. Let \(y_{tB}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\), where \(B\) contains at most two units and may contain two distinct units from the same category. Require \(\sum_B y_{tB}=\mu_t\) and that total use of category \(s\) is at most \(q_s\). Maximize \(\sum_{t,B} y_{tB}v_t(B)\), where bundle value is the sum of its unit values.

The question is whether, for every such instance, one can compute in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\) a compact state-feedback rule \(\Phi(\nu,r)\). At each state, \(\nu\) is the remaining mass of each type and \(r\) the remaining supply of each category; \(\Phi\) posts a price per category without knowing the next type. For every measurable arrival order and every choice among utility-maximizing bundles, the resulting fluid allocation must attain the welfare benchmark. The rule should be evaluable in polynomial time on rational states. This is a question about dynamic pricing for the underlying market, not about an encoding of it.

A plausible regime is a large procurement or allocation platform with many repeat buyers, where each buyer wants two units from a standard catalogue. Buyers who share their complete value vector are grouped into types, so the number of buyers can be very large while \(\tau\) remains modest. Supply scales with the population, perhaps through planned quotas that make full demand optimal. This retains the paper’s unknown arrival order, changing inventory, dynamic prices, arbitrary best responses, and welfare guarantee. The fluid stock is the natural co-modification needed to scale supply; each individual still receives an integral bundle.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable, through capacitated matching or configuration optimization, dual prices, and a fractional counterpart to the paper’s adequate ordering. Theorem 14’s algorithm works on a finite market; simply expanding rational masses into copies does not settle the compressed problem, since their common denominator may be exponential in \(L\). A genuine result would need a compact policy with complexity tied to support and bit length.

The soft spot is that Theorem 14’s proof depends on discrete \(2\)-factors and an ordering of individual items. Its dangerous-set argument may not yield a valid state-feedback rule for fractional supplies and continuously varying arrivals. That makes this more than a direct corollary, but it is also where the mirror could fail or reveal continuum-specific difficulty.

This case mirrors Theorem 14 only; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s other results or for general multi-demand markets without (OPT). Further questions include whether a fractional adequate-ordering condition can be characterized and computed efficiently, and whether the same policy guarantee extends to demand at most three or survives when (OPT) is removed. The paper offers no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question, so the positive case rests on its main algorithmic theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 14 is the paper’s strongest anchor for a mirror: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for optimal dynamic prices under every buyer order and every utility-maximizing tie choice. The buyers can be repeated customers whose complete value vectors fall into a small set of types, and the items can be units of standardized categories. The welfare objective does not depend on buyer identities, and per-capita supply can make the fluid version of (OPT) sensible. I can’t make a credible case that no high-multiplicity regime exists.

The best objection is about what happens to the theorem’s sequential guarantee in the continuum. In the finite algorithm, each arrival removes one buyer and two indivisible items; prices are recomputed after that event. In a fluid model, each buyer has zero mass, so an individual arrival does not change the state. To keep prices responsive, the mirror needs a precise continuous-time process: an arbitrary arrival flow, state evolution for remaining type masses and supplies, and a guarantee covering almost every buyer and every measurable choice among tied bundles. The theorem’s ordering of individual items and its finite update steps do not supply that process automatically.

That gap remains under a stronger formulation. A price profile over ranked units within each category could preserve the finite scheme’s tie-breaking role, and a fluid state-feedback policy could be required to work against every admissible arrival path. Those choices make the mirror more faithful; they also define a robust dynamic-control problem whose computational representation needs to be specified. This is a real modeling cost, but it does not show that the question is unworthy. Keeping buyers as atoms instead yields a high-multiplicity problem whose size may depend on a large denominator, also a legitimate question.

Continuum-buyer dynamic-arrival models exist, but the example I found concerns seller profit with unit demand and no capacity constraint; it does not settle this theorem’s exact welfare guarantee under adversarial arrivals and ties. [Dynamic Theory of Random Price Discounts](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article/93/3/1671/8243883)

So my case against this anchor is weak. The continuum formulation needs care, but a natural fluid version is available and preserves the central guarantee. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
