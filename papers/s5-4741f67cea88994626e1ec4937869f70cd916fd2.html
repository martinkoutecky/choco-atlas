The continuous mirror question

Given two types with rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2>0\), \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), task-demand vectors \(d_1,d_2\), and per-tenant capacity \(c\), let \(\mathcal A\) be the saturated vectors \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2\) satisfying \(\sum_{t=1}^2\mu_t a_t d_{tr}\le c_r\) for every resource \(r\), where saturation means no coordinate can be increased by one while remaining feasible. Set \(s_t=\max_r d_{tr}/c_r\), and consider the Case II regime where \(\mathcal A\) contains vectors with \(s_1a_1>s_2a_2\) and vectors with \(s_2a_2>s_1a_1\), but none with equality. A schedule chooses finitely many \(a^j\in\mathcal A\) and durations \(\lambda_j\ge0\) summing to one; does maximizing the common average share subject to \(s_1\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_1=s_2\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_2\) always admit an optimum using at most two distinct allocation vectors?

The model it lives in

A two-type society with masses \(\mu_t\), where each type receives a common integral task bundle per slot. The masses weight resource feasibility against per-tenant capacity \(c\); slot durations average the dominant shares, and the objective maximizes the lower of the two cohorts’ average shares.

The objection that survived

Because every member of a type receives the same integral task count in each slot, \(\mu_t\) only changes feasibility coefficients and cannot split that type’s mass among bundles; the support result may therefore be a simple two-coordinate fact rather than a consequence of population divisibility.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3.5’s two-slot conclusion for the mass-weighted Case II problem. It leaves the thesis’s other allocation cases, Chapter 4 performance bounds, simulations, and more-than-two-type systems aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an optimal schedule be computed in time polynomial in the number of resources and the binary input length without enumerating all saturated allocations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I would defend is a high-multiplicity version of the thesis’s two-user time-division fairness problem. Consider a cloud with many tenants, each using one of two standardized workload profiles—for example, CPU-heavy and memory-heavy jobs. A type is the full per-task resource-demand vector \(d_t\), along with the same scheduling parameters for every tenant of that type. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the tenant population, with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\). As the population grows, total capacity grows with it; write \(c\) for capacity per tenant. This is a plausible regime in the paper’s setting: it discusses millions of tasks drawn from a small number of job types.

Call the resulting problem Two-Cohort TDA\(_\infty\). An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2>0\), positive task-demand vectors \(d_1,d_2\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), and per-tenant capacity \(c\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\), all encoded in binary. A schedule chooses finitely many slots with durations \(\lambda_j\ge0\), \(\sum_j\lambda_j=1\). In slot \(j\), every tenant of type \(t\) receives \(a_{jt}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) tasks, so the per-tenant aggregate resource use must satisfy \(\sum_{t=1}^2\mu_t a_{jt}d_t\le c\), componentwise. Thus each tenant gets an integral bundle in each slot, while population mass and long-run averages may be fractional. Define \(s_t=\max_r d_{tr}/c_r\). The objective is to maximize \(\min_{t\in\{1,2\}} s_t\sum_j\lambda_j a_{jt}\), the minimum average dominant-resource share across the two cohorts. A solution is an optimal finite schedule, including its slot durations and task counts.

My anchor is Proposition 3.5, proved in this thesis. In the paper’s Case II problem (3.5), the schedule chooses allocations from \(G_1\cup G_2\), where \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) are the allocations in which one or the other user has the higher dominant-resource share. It maximizes their common average share, subject to the two users’ average shares being equal and the slot durations summing to one. Proposition 3.5 says that for every fixed \(K=v\ge4\), the optimum of that problem is the same as the optimum with \(K=2\). The preceding analysis in §3.4.2 reduces \(K=3\) to \(K=2\), completing the thesis’s at-most-two-slot conclusion. This is a structural optimization result, not an NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification. Section 3.6’s algorithm enumerates saturated allocations and searches pairs in \(O(N^2)\), where \(N\) is the number of such allocations; that bound is not a polynomial-time guarantee in the binary input length.

The mirror question is whether every Two-Cohort TDA\(_\infty\) instance has an optimal schedule using at most two distinct slot allocation vectors, and, if so, whether one can compute an optimal schedule from the compact rational input. I expect the structural result to survive: the cohort masses change which integer allocations are feasible, but the fairness objective still has two coordinates, and the paper’s argument is built around the geometry of those two users’ shares. That makes this a Class A candidate in the thesis’s output-sensitive sense. A polynomial-time result in the compact binary encoding would require more: the paper’s \(O(N^2)\) enumeration alone does not establish it. The immediate further questions are whether the two-slot theorem holds under mass-weighted feasibility and whether its allocation search admits a compact pricing or optimization method.

This is a mirror of the population, not a claim that the thesis’s time slots or resource outcomes are themselves the continuization. The time division already appears in the paper; the mirror replaces two named tenants with two cohorts whose masses scale aggregate demand and capacity. The authors would have a clear reason to recognize it: the thesis is about heterogeneous user demands in shared cloud clusters, and repeated workload profiles are already part of its motivation.

My weakest point is that Proposition 3.5 concerns exactly two users, while Two-Cohort TDA\(_\infty\) is a new mass-weighted feasible-allocation problem. The thesis does not prove that its two-slot argument transfers, and its enumeration may be costly for binary-encoded capacities. The cohort scenario is also clearest when a scheduler treats each workload class uniformly within a slot. Those are real limits, but they leave a specific, testable continuous-population analogue of the thesis’s central optimization question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3.5 is the proponent’s only anchor, and its two-cohort version faces a choice that weakens the case either way.

If every member of a cohort must receive the same integral task count in each slot, the proposed model is still the thesis’s two-user problem: there are two allocation coordinates, and the masses \(\mu_t\) only change the resource-feasibility coefficients. The population size itself has disappeared. This is a coherent weighted variant, but its at-most-two-slot claim is not a high-multiplicity consequence; the model preserves the thesis’s indivisible, two-user allocation frontier.

If the continuum is allowed to do the work of a continuum, each type’s mass can be split among different integral task bundles. Then its average task rate \(x_t\) can be fractional, while each unit of mass still receives an integral bundle. For common per-type dominant share \(z\), set \(x_t=z/s_t\), where \(s_t=\max_r d_{tr}/c_r\). Feasibility reduces to
\[ z\sum_t\mu_t\frac{d_{tr}}{s_t c_r}\le 1 \quad\text{for every resource }r. \]
That is a small linear optimization problem. More importantly for this anchor, the finite integer allocation frontier that Proposition 3.5 analyzes has been convexified by splitting mass; the two-slot question becomes a support question in two share coordinates. Chapter 4 already studies the closely related continuous-task relaxation and derives its optimal average-share bound. A weighted population formulation would need a careful dictionary, but it appears to inherit that relaxation’s simple optimization structure rather than open a new complexity landscape.

The proponent’s uniform-per-cohort schedule avoids this collapse, but it also prevents the population from being meaningfully divisible. The alternative that uses divisibility removes the thesis’s central indivisibility phenomenon and is closely covered by the thesis’s own continuous relaxation. That is the strongest case against this mirror.

It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large population of tenants sharing a few workload profiles is plausible, and the weighted integral-allocation variant is a well-posed problem the authors could recognize. The negative case is therefore strongest as an argument that this particular anchor offers little new computational terrain, not as a convincing universal rejection of every continuous mirror.
