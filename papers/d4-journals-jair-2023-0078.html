The continuous mirror question

Given \(N\), \(V\), \(D\), rational masses \(\mu_{q,c}\) over provider service classes \(q\) and costs \(c\), known cost priors, and rationally represented duration CDFs \(G_q\), compute an \(\epsilon\)-optimal direct procurement auction. The auction chooses finite-stage invocation schedules of real-valued provider mass, conditional on prior noncompletion, and payments to maximize expected consumer utility subject to BIC and interim IR; ask whether the stronger DSIC and ex-post IR guarantees also hold.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity market with \(N\mu_{q,c}\) providers of each service and cost type, individual report-dependent allocation and payment rules, and real-valued mass tranches recruited over time. If \(x_{q,b}\) denotes recruited mass, task failure follows \(R(u)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{q,b}\int_{[0,u]}\log(1-G_q(u-s))\,dx_{q,b}(s)\right)\); expected utility balances \(V(1-R(D))\) against payments weighted by the probability each tranche is reached.

The objection that survived

With fixed \(V\) and per-provider success probabilities and costs, the optimal recruited count can stay \(O(1)\) as \(N\) grows, so its population fraction vanishes; a nondegenerate asymptotic limit may require an additional scaling.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It states that OCPA gives the consumer the maximum expected utility achievable by any auction satisfying BIC and interim IR. The paper obtains this by optimizing an allocation rule against providers’ virtual costs and then applying its weighted-threshold payment rule; it also proves separately that OCPA is DSIC and ex-post IR. The allocation is computed in Section 6 by searching provider sequences and optimizing their invocation times.

There is an important limit to that anchor: the paper gives no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise. Proposition 3 is an optimality result, not a complexity classification. The paper does compute the allocation, so it can support a computational question about the underlying optimization, but it cannot support a claim that the authors established a complexity bound.

A plausible regime is a large cloud procurement market for one standardized task. Think of thousands of independently schedulable worker instances drawn from a few service classes: within a class, instances have the same completion-time distribution and execution cost. A complete type is the pair \((q,c)\), where \(q\) is the service class and \(c\) is the instance’s true execution cost. The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu_{q,c}\) over these types, with \(N\mu_{q,c}\) available instances of each type. Here \(\tau\) counts the supported class-and-cost pairs, and can be much smaller than \(N\). Shared service classes and contract cost bands make that a credible instance regime. The providers still have private costs and must report them to the procurement mechanism.

My lead question is Continuous OCPA. An instance gives \(N\), \(V\), \(D\), the rational masses \(\mu_{q,c}\), and for each service class \(q\) a rationally represented duration CDF \(G_q\). The mechanism receives providers’ reported costs. For each class \(q\) and reported cost \(b\), it chooses a nonnegative mass schedule \(x_{q,b}\) over invocation times in \([0,D]\): each scheduled tranche is recruited only if the task remains unfinished. The schedule cannot use more than the reported mass available in that class.

To extend the paper’s “the task succeeds if any provider finishes” semantics to fractional mass, define the probability that it is still unfinished at time \(u\) along the failure path as

\[ R(u)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{q,b}\int_{[0,u]} \log\!\bigl(1-G_q(u-s)\bigr)\,dx_{q,b}(s)\right). \]

This product-integral agrees with the paper’s independent-provider failure probability when the masses are integer counts of identical providers. A provider recruited at time \(s\) is paid \(\pi_{q,b}\), so expected total payments are the sum, over tranches, of \(\pi_{q,b}R(s^-)\,dx_{q,b}(s)\). On truthful reports, the consumer’s expected utility is task value \(V(1-R(D))\) minus those payments.

The problem is to compute an \(\epsilon\)-optimal direct mechanism: one that chooses these schedules and per-provider payments to maximize the consumer’s expected utility under the stated type distribution, while satisfying BIC and interim IR. Its output must also satisfy the stronger conditions the paper proves for OCPA: truthful reporting is a dominant strategy and recruited providers have nonnegative utility ex post. These constraints must remain meaningful at the individual level: allocation and payment may depend on a provider’s own report, even though that provider has zero mass. A mechanism based only on aggregate masses would make individual truthfulness vacuous and would not be the intended mirror. A finite-stage schedule gives a concrete output format; its invocation times and recruited masses are part of the solution.

I would expect the unrestricted version to be a Class C candidate, rather than claim it is tractable. Aggregating identical suppliers removes much of the paper’s search over provider orderings, but the mass schedule still interacts with a nonlinear, failure-contingent survival process. The paper itself supplies no convexity result or complexity analysis for that problem. Exponential duration laws and a fixed number of service classes look like promising Class A subcases, but that is a research direction, not a result to credit to the paper. Natural follow-up questions are whether the general problem admits an \(\epsilon\)-approximation with exact incentive constraints, whether the fixed-\(\tau\) exponential case has a convex formulation, and whether a finite-population auction converges to this mass formulation with its incentive guarantees intact.

This mirror covers OCPA’s utility-maximizing result, especially Proposition 3 and the allocation optimization used to obtain it. It does not claim to mirror the social-welfare auctions, the heuristic auction’s empirical performance, or the paper as a whole.

The weak point is substantial: the paper has finitely many named providers, whereas this mirror replaces their sequence of invocations with a continuous mass schedule and extends the failure probability by a product-integral. The authors should recognize the procurement objective, private execution costs, deadline, redundancy, and contingent recruitment as their problem; they may still regard the mass-level reliability model as an extension rather than a direct high-multiplicity restatement. A formal finite-clone convergence result would strengthen the case. The absence of a named complexity-class result also limits what this paper can anchor, even if the optimization question itself is worth asking.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is a named result, but it proves mechanism-design optimality, not a complexity classification. The paper shows that OCPA maximizes the consumer’s expected utility among BIC and interim-IR auctions, then proves stronger incentive and participation properties. Its allocation routine searches provider orderings and optimizes invocation times; the paper gives no complexity bound for that computation. A complexity study could still be useful, but it would classify a new optimization problem rather than continue a complexity result established here.

The proposed continuum also has a scaling problem at the heart of the paper’s objective. Take \(N\) identical providers, each with fixed probability \(p>0\) of completing the task by the deadline. If a fraction \(x\) of them is recruited at time zero, the probability the task remains unfinished is
\[ (1-p)^{Nx}=\exp\!\bigl(Nx\log(1-p)\bigr). \]
For every fixed \(x>0\), this tends to zero as \(N\) grows. Yet recruiting that fraction incurs payments proportional to \(N x\). With fixed positive per-provider costs and a fixed task value \(V\), the useful number of providers is generally finite: success gains saturate while costs continue to accrue. In population-fraction units, the selected mass then tends to zero. If costs are rescaled to keep spending finite, any positive mass makes success essentially certain, leaving a degenerate choice at zero mass. Preserving a nontrivial interior tradeoff requires another scaling, such as making each provider’s success probability shrink with \(N\); that changes the task or duration model.

The proposed product integral exposes the same problem at the level of meaning. For fractional count \(x\), it assigns failure probability \((1-p)^x\). For \(0<x<1\), that is not the reliability of a fractional number of providers, nor the reliability of a lottery over whole providers. It is an analytic extension of the finite-provider formula. A continuum of infinitesimal contributors could instead generate a cumulative hazard, but then no individual provider completes the paper’s indivisible, all-or-nothing task. That is a different reliability model.

There is a plausible repair for truthfulness: specify a pointwise allocation and payment rule for each provider’s report, then aggregate its outcomes over the population. That could make individual IC meaningful even when each provider has zero mass. It does not fix the reliability scaling. Another repair is to make the task divisible or to model many tasks per market so aggregate demand grows with supply. Those changes may be worthwhile research problems, but they add task allocation or capacity constraints absent from OCPA’s single-task result.

Large pools of identical cloud providers are conceivable, so I would not base the negative case on a claim that repeated provider types never occur. The difficulty is that the paper’s central tradeoff concerns whether a whole provider completes one fixed task, while a normalized continuum makes either the selected mass disappear or the task succeed trivially. A fractional-count relaxation could still be studied, but it is a relaxation of provider counts, not a continuous-population mirror in ChoCo’s sense. That leaves the proposed Continuous OCPA without a convincing, nondegenerate population interpretation that preserves the authors’ problem.
