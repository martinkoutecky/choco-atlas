The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case anchored to this paper’s named results: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing the complexity of coalition formation or equilibrium computation.

The closest result is the unnumbered complexity summary in Section 2.2, attributed to Brewka and Eiter: under the stated kernel assumptions, deciding whether a literal occurs in some equilibrium is in \(\Sigma_{k+1}^{p}\), and deciding whether it occurs in every equilibrium is in \(\Pi_{k+1}^{p}\). It is cited background, not a numbered result of this paper. The cited Proposition 5 of Yin et al. says that the classical projection of a possibilistic MCS’s possibilistic grounded equilibrium is the grounded equilibrium of its classical projection; that is a semantic correspondence, not a complexity result. The paper’s coalition computations are examples, and its algorithm discussion reports results from other work.

A repeated fleet of standardized service robots could motivate a population mirror: types would capture each robot’s local logic, capabilities, dependencies, and action reliabilities; mass would represent the share of each type; decisions would allocate that mass among task plans, with certainty or cost as an objective. But this would be a proposed new problem, not a continuous version anchored to a qualifying result here. In particular, the paper’s dependencies refer to specific agents, so preserving its equilibrium question under aggregation would need careful justification.

There are therefore no qualifying anchors, no per-anchor prover-ready questions, and no responsible A/B/C classification to give.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no qualifying anchor to defend. This paper states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity of a computational problem. Its main contribution is a modeling method and examples of MCS equilibria, followed by suggestions for choosing among coalitions. The complexity bound in Section 2.2 is attributed to prior work on MCS, and Proposition 5 is a semantic correspondence from prior work on possibilistic MCS. Neither is a result established by this paper. There is no paper result here for ChoCo to continuize.

The strongest candidate to rescue is equilibrium computation: given a system and a goal, decide whether that goal occurs in some or every equilibrium, perhaps when many agents share a finite set of types. That is a coherent question, but it is a proposed extension, not an anchor. The MCS model connects named contexts with bridge rules, and equilibrium depends on which rules apply across those particular contexts. If a type includes its dependency neighborhood and local knowledge, realistic differences can make the number of types grow with the number of agents. If types omit those details, the mass model loses information that affects equilibria. A highly symmetric fleet might avoid this problem, but the paper neither studies that regime nor supplies a result about it.

The proposed standardized fleet and recurring task setting is plausible, but its cleanest mass formulation would likely aggregate robot capabilities, task demand, costs, and reliability into a flow over robot and task types. That may be a useful model in its own right. It does not follow from the paper’s equilibrium examples, though: its MCS semantics concerns Boolean beliefs and mutually dependent actions, while a fractional assignment records shares of work. Preserving those dependencies in a type-level model would require additional structure and a separate computational question. The possibilistic extension does not fill the gap: its degrees represent uncertainty about actions, not a continuous population distribution.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a result-mirroring target: there is no computational result of the paper to anchor the work to. It is weaker as a universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A carefully designed high-multiplicity coalition model for repeated service systems could be sensible. But that possibility points to a new problem, not a continuous mirror established by this paper. I would not prioritize this paper as a ChoCo mirror target.
