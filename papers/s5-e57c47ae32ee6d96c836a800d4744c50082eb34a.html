What the mirror covers

The proposed question draws on Theorems 10 and 11, but neither yields a population-dependent computational result in this framing. It leaves the partial envy-free allocation results and other existence claims aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a two-constituency version of the paper’s two-person division problem. Picture two equally sized municipalities dividing a fixed set of public assets. Residents within each municipality share the same strict ranking of the assets, and each municipality receives a portfolio that its residents share. With \(N\) residents, the society has \(N/2\) people of each type, so \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), while the support size is only \(\tau=2\). The assets remain indivisible; mass describes the population behind each type.

My lead anchors are Theorems 10 and 11, both proved in this paper. The paper gives no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result, so these are constructive and structural algorithmic results, not a classification of a problem as being in P.

Theorem 10 says that when \(|S|=2n\) and Condition \(D(S)\) fails, every complete balanced Pareto-optimal allocation is EF for some player up to an item in the first odd-length block, and possibly other items. In the constituency version, its question is whether any Pareto-optimal partition can make the sole exceptional asset less preferred than every asset in that first block. The theorem’s obstruction rules that out.

Theorem 11 says that, under the same assumptions, if \(k_1\) is the length of the first odd-length block, there is a complete balanced Pareto-optimal allocation that is EF for A and EF for B up to the item in B’s \(k_1\)-th position. Its construction starts from a maximal balanced partial EF allocation and assigns the missing items across the blocks. The theorem’s existence claim is proved here; the related condition characterizing when a complete balanced EF allocation exists is attributed in the paper to earlier work.

Here is the continuous optimization problem these results support. Let \(S\) be a catalogue of \(m=2n\) assets, and let the two role-labelled types have rankings \(\succ_A,\succ_B\). Require that \(D(S)\) fails, so a complete balanced EF allocation does not exist. Choose a partition \(S=L_A\mathbin{\dot\cup}L_B\) with \(|L_A|=|L_B|=n\), and require it to be Pareto-optimal and EF for one constituency, while the other is EF after deleting a single asset from the first constituency’s bundle.

For a type \(t\) that is EF after such a deletion, let \(q_t\) be the largest rank position of a deletable asset in \(\succ_t\). A larger position means a less-preferred exception and therefore an allocation closer to EF. Set \(\rho_t=m+1\) when \(t\) is fully EF. The Cohort Almost-EF Completion problem is to find a feasible partition maximizing \(\Phi=(\rho_A+\rho_B)/2\). Its output is an integral asset partition for the two types; the population is represented by their masses.

For this instance, Theorem 10 supplies the upper bound and Theorem 11 attains it: the best value is \(\Phi=(m+1+k_1)/2\). The two orientations put the exception at position \(k_1\) for one constituency and leave the other EF. The expected direction is Class A, tractable: the construction works on the ranking profile, and a bulk version over \(\tau\) supported profiles could apply it type by type. The paper does not give a formal running-time bound, so a precise bound in \(m,\tau,L\) would still be a follow-up question.

This mirror covers the paper’s result about the closest complete balanced PO allocation when complete balanced EF is impossible. It does not cover the paper’s partial EF results or its broader existence claims. A natural next question is whether the same guarantee survives when constituencies contain several preference types or have unequal masses, which would require a meaningful choice of per-capita balance.

The fragile step is interpreting the paper’s two players as large homogeneous constituencies. The mirror is most plausible where assets are shared at the constituency level and members genuinely share a ranking. It preserves the paper’s two bundles and fairness comparison, but assumes type-uniform portfolios; a referee could reasonably ask whether that describes the authors’ two-person problem or an institutional extension of it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two-constituency story is plausible: two large municipalities could share rankings and receive communal asset portfolios. Its weakness is that population mass does no work in either theorem. In the proposed instance, \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\) can be deleted from the input: \(D(S)\), the first odd block \(k_1\), Pareto optimality, and the EF conditions depend only on the two rankings and the finite item set. The output remains a partition of those items between two recipients. That is a demographic interpretation of the original problem, not a high-multiplicity computational question.

Theorem 10’s restriction on where an exception must occur is likewise entirely about the two rankings. Also, its statement allows “other items” to be exceptions, so it does not alone establish the proponent’s claimed upper bound for an objective requiring that deleting just one item restore EF. Theorem 11 does construct a complete balanced PO allocation with a single exceptional item at position \(k_1\). A corrected optimization question could ask for the best such partition. Its population masses would still have no effect, so the corrected mirror remains the finite two-player problem.

The suggested type-by-type lift does not supply the missing population dimension. Types in a common society compete over the same assets; solving a separate two-player division for each type would duplicate the asset set and would not produce one complete, Pareto-optimal allocation. A stronger version could give individual residents integral bundles and let type averages be fractional. Then fairness and Pareto comparisons must be defined across many recipients, and the bundle supply or per-capita balance must be specified. That may be a worthwhile fair-division problem, but it changes the core object behind Theorems 10 and 11: their results concern two bundles, pairwise item covers, and a parity obstruction across two recipients. It is an extension, not a continuous mirror of those results.

So the negative case is strongest for the named anchors: keeping the paper’s two bundles leaves mass inert; making mass affect allocation changes the problem the theorems answer. I would not claim that no population-based fair-division research could be worthwhile. Two large homogeneous constituencies are a sensible setting, and the paper’s results may be useful there. The narrower point is that the constituency framing adds no continuous computational landscape to these results.
