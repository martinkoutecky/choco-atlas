The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one focused case, anchored on Theorem 7 in Appendix A.6, proved in this paper. The paper has no numbered theorem claiming that a central problem is in P or NP-hard. It does mention an NP-completeness result for the exact least-core value, but attributes it to [11] in an unnumbered remark; I would not use that as a required anchor.

Theorem 7 concerns the paper’s *probable least-core value*. For a fixed allocation \(x\), \(e\) is a \(\delta\)-probable least-core value when a uniformly random coalition \(C\subseteq A\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in C}x_i+e\ge v(C)\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The theorem says: draw \(m=O(\log(1/\Delta)/\delta^2)\) coalitions uniformly from \(2^A\), and let \(\hat e\) be the smallest value satisfying \(\sum_{i\in C}\phi_i(\hat v)+\hat e\ge v(C)\) for every sampled coalition. Then, with probability at least \(1-\Delta\) over the samples, \(\hat e\) is a \(\delta\)-probable least-core value for the allocation given by the Shapley values of the estimated game \(\hat v\).

A continuous mirror would ask whether a per-capita Shapley allocation is probably stable against coalitions of types. Let \(T\) be a set of complete agent types, with population masses \(\mu_t\), and let \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) denote the mass of type \(t\) in a coalition. Its value is \(V(q)\), with \(V(0)=0\). Assume the learned game \(\hat V\) is a succinct, rational CGA polynomial. Assign each unit of type \(t\) the rate

\[ \hat a_t=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial \hat V(s\mu)}{\partial q_t}\,ds . \]

This Aumann–Shapley line integral is a natural continuous version of the Shapley allocation: for a finite game’s multilinear extension it recovers Shapley marginal shares, and here it gives one reward rate to each exchangeable type. It is budget balanced for \(\hat V\), since \(\sum_t \mu_t\hat a_t=\hat V(\mu)\).

The named problem would be Continuous Probable Least-Core Auditing. Its instance consists of \((T,\mu,\hat V,\delta,\Delta)\) and sample access to true coalition values \(V(q)\). Draw coalition masses from \(D\), where each fraction \(q_t/\mu_t\) is independently uniform on \([0,1]\). Given \(M\) samples \((q^j,V(q^j))\), output

\[ \hat e=\max_{1\le j\le M}\left(V(q^j)-\sum_t\hat a_tq_t^j\right). \]

A solution counts if, with confidence at least \(1-\Delta\) over the sampled coalitions,

\[ \Pr_{q\sim D}\!\left[\sum_t\hat a_tq_t+\hat e\ge V(q)\right]\ge 1-\delta . \]

The same threshold-class argument as in Theorem 7 gives \(M=O(\log(1/\Delta)/\delta^2)\). With explicit sparse CGA descriptions, evaluating the samples and computing the line integral take time polynomial in the number of types, model size, bit length, and the requested accuracy parameters. I expect this *sampling-based audit* to be Class A, tractable. An exact worst-case audit over every mass vector is a separate question: it asks to optimize the deficit over a continuous box, and may need additional restrictions on the CGA coefficients.

The regime I find convincing is a large collaborative workforce, such as a project or response team with many participants but relatively few repeated role-and-skill profiles. Members of one type have the same preferences, costs, and contribution characteristics for the game. There may be millions of participants and tens or hundreds of types. A discrete coalition maps to its type counts, normalized as mass; rewards become per-type rates. That preserves the paper’s question about team value and fair division while making the society itself continuous.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuation of their least-core analysis: the object being checked is still the stability of a Shapley-family allocation against coalitions, and the per-type rates follow from exchangeability rather than from assigning named individuals different treatment. This case covers Theorem 7’s sampled, probabilistic least-core guarantee; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s whole learning pipeline or prove that exact least-core hardness disappears.

The weakest point is the coalition sampling law. Uniformly sampling finite coalitions does not converge to the product-uniform mass distribution above: with many members per type, the sampled fraction of each type concentrates near one half. The continuous question therefore adopts a different, explicit interpretation of a random coalition—random participation fractions by type. That is a plausible audit target for a large workforce, but it makes the mirror an extension of Theorem 7 rather than a direct limit of its sampling scheme. The paper’s usual sports-team examples also have small teams, so the case depends on the project-workforce regime rather than those examples.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is the strongest anchor, so I would not dismiss it on the ground that the paper lacks a named result. It has one: a sample-complexity guarantee for estimating a probable least-core value. The problem is that its probability is over *uniformly random finite coalitions*, and that sampling rule has a poor continuous-population limit.

If type \(t\) has \(N_t\) members, a uniformly random coalition includes each member independently with probability \(1/2\). Its included fraction of type \(t\) therefore concentrates at \(1/2\) as \(N_t\) grows. For a continuous coalition-value function \(V(q)\), the theorem’s audit then concentrates on the single composition \(q_t=\mu_t/2\). That is a pointwise deficit check, not an audit of stability against the range of coalitions that makes the least core meaningful. The theorem’s sample bound may still hold, but the object it estimates has become much narrower.

The proposed product-uniform distribution over participation fractions avoids that collapse, but it changes what “a random coalition” means. It is a possible new audit model, yet it is not selected by the finite theorem: different distributions can make different coalition compositions decisive. And its sample guarantee comes from a generic one-dimensional threshold argument. It needs neither a CGA nor a high-multiplicity structure, so it contributes little to the programme’s computational question of what continuous populations do to the algorithmic landscape.

A worst-case continuous least-core problem could avoid the sampling issue: require stability for every mass vector and optimize over the whole type-mass box. That may be a worthwhile research question, but it is a stronger, separate mirror; Theorem 7 does not answer it. The type-level model also needs substantial anonymity: coalitions with the same type masses must have the same value. That is plausible for a homogeneous workforce, but the paper’s player-specific interactions and individual Shapley assessments do not supply that property. Making types fine enough to preserve those distinctions can erase the multiplicity gain.

So I would reject Theorem 7’s proposed continuous audit as a compelling ChoCo anchor. The negative case is limited, though: a type-anonymous continuous least-core study remains plausible, and I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror.
