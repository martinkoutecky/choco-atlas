The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), integral per-agent capacities, acceptability lists, and weak rankings over opposite-side types, compute a Pareto stable mass matching. Represent each type’s mass by distributions over integral partner-count vectors, require those distributions to sum to the input masses and their type-pair link masses to balance, and require that no positive mass of mutually improving pairs can match or that any feasible reassignment can make almost every agent weakly better off and positive mass strictly better off. Is there an exact algorithm polynomial in support size \(\tau\) and binary input length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-support population model with two sides of interchangeable types, rational masses, integral per-agent capacities and bundles, and type-level link masses as decision variables; the task is to compute a matching satisfying pairwise stability and Pareto efficiency.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.4.11’s existence and computation of Pareto stable matchings in two-sided many-to-many markets with ties and integral capacities. It leaves the Chapter 3 mechanisms and strategic results, Chapter 4 simulations, and Chapter 5 roommates results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a Pareto stable mass matching be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), without expanding rational masses into a potentially exponential number of individual copies?
  • Can such a solution be represented with polynomially many bundle patterns and polynomial bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the thesis’s two-sided many-to-many matching problem. The anchor is Theorem 2.4.11, proved in this thesis: for every finite many-to-many market with ties, integral capacities, and the chapter’s responsive preferences over bundles, a Pareto stable matching exists and the named Pareto-Stable algorithm computes one in polynomial time. Pareto stable means both pairwise stable and Pareto efficient. The result covers ties on both sides and capacities greater than one; it is not just the easier case where one side has homogeneous preferences.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a regional part-time work exchange over an annual cohort: many job seekers matched to many employer teams or recurring position holders. A seeker’s type records all relevant features, such as qualifications, location, availability, capacity, and ranking of job categories. An employer-side type records its role, capacity, and ranking of worker categories. Within a type, agents are interchangeable for the problem. A market could have tens or hundreds of thousands of agents but only dozens or hundreds of supported types. The thesis explicitly names part-time job-worker matching as an application, and observes that weak preferences are realistic when ranking a large opposite side is costly.

Here is the prover-ready question I would call Pareto-Stable Mass Matching. An instance has two finite sets of types, \(A\) and \(B\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(t\in A\cup B\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each type \(t\) has an integral capacity \(q_t\), an acceptability list, and a weak ranking of opposite-side types. Bundle preferences follow the chapter’s responsive rule. The input gives the type support and masses in binary; let \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) and \(L\) be the input bit length.

A solution assigns each individual an integral bundle of partners, while allowing the mass of a type to be divided among different bundles. One exact representation uses \(\lambda_{a,\mathbf u}\), the mass of type-\(a\) agents receiving partner-count vector \(\mathbf u\), and \(\rho_{b,\mathbf v}\) for type-\(b\) agents, with \(\sum_b u_b\le q_a\) and \(\sum_a v_a\le q_b\). The distributions must sum to their input type masses, and links must balance:
\[ \sum_{\mathbf u}\lambda_{a,\mathbf u}u_b = \sum_{\mathbf v}\rho_{b,\mathbf v}v_a \quad\text{for every }a\in A,\ b\in B. \]
The question is to compute rational bundle masses satisfying these constraints such that there is no positive-mass pair of agents who can each strictly improve by matching to one another, dropping a less-preferred partner if their capacity is full; and there is no feasible reassignment that makes almost every agent weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off. Thus the fractional quantities describe shares of agents receiving whole bundles. They give no individual a fractional job or a lottery over bundles.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps both sides’ preferences, multi-unit capacities, ties, pairwise blocking, and two-sided Pareto efficiency. The change is that repeated agents are represented by type masses. This is a population continuization, with per-capita supplies allowed to scale with the cohort. It does not continue the outcome space. The thesis also gives a direct existence bridge: clear the rational masses’ denominators, make that many copies of each type, apply Theorem 2.4.11, and aggregate the matching back into bundle masses.

I expect Class A for this restricted, finite-support mirror, using mass-based augmentations or configuration optimization. That expectation is not a consequence of the theorem’s polynomial bound: clearing denominators gives a runtime polynomial in their common denominator, which can be exponential in \(L\). The actual prize is an exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and the capacity encoding, rather than a polynomial-time algorithm in the expanded population. This also generates useful boundary questions: can a solution be returned with polynomially many bundle patterns and polynomial bit length; and at what point does identity-specific variation make \(\tau\) too large or create hardness?

The weakest point is exactly that denominator gap. The finite algorithm works through individual agents and capacity increments, so aggregating its steps into mass operations needs a proof. The scenario also depends on a genuinely moderate type support: if workers rank particular people or employers individually, the faithful types may proliferate. The case survives as a claim about standardized, repeated-role markets, rather than every matching market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that a type must preserve the whole preference structure the matching problem uses. In the thesis’s course market, for example, students submit individualized rankings of courses. In hiring, candidates may rank particular employers differently, and employers may rank candidates differently. If those differences matter, they belong in the types and the support \(\tau\) can grow with the population. Grouping people by broad attributes would then change the problem.

That objection narrows the useful setting, but it does not defeat the best version. The thesis itself names part-time job-worker matching, and a recurring market with many applicants and standardized roles could plausibly have a small set of qualification, availability, and preference types. The proposed model also keeps each person’s bundle integral while representing the population by masses. That is a coherent population continuization, and Theorem 2.4.11 gives it a clear computational question: can the matching be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the binary input length \(L\)? Clearing rational denominators proves existence, but may expand the market exponentially in \(L\); it does not settle that algorithmic question.

Existing continuum matching work makes aggregate populations familiar, but the examples I found address stable outcomes and convergence in other models. They do not appear to settle exact computation of Pareto stable outcomes for finite-support, many-to-many markets with ties. [Leshno’s framework](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/) studies supply-and-demand characterizations of stable outcomes, while [Grigoryan’s recent work](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.3982/TE5478) studies convergence from sampled finite economies.

So the negative case rests on relevance: a low-support population with shared preferences may be a specialized regime, and the thesis’s broad finite-market theorem does not itself establish that this regime is common. I cannot honestly turn that into a case against the mirror in every scenario. Theorem 2.4.11 survives as a worthwhile, narrower mirror; whether its exact compressed-input algorithm is tractable remains open.
