The continuous mirror question

Given a prior \(\mu \in (0,1)\) and response level \(\lambda > 0\), let \(\nu\) be a mass distribution over posterior types \(p \in [0,1]\) with \(\int p\,d\nu(p)=\mu\); by the paper’s reduction, restrict to support in \(\{0,p,1\}\) for some \(p\). A type \(p\) reports \(1\) with probability \(\psi_\lambda(p)\), so the exact conditional report shares are \(r_1(\nu)=\frac{1}{\mu}\int p\psi_\lambda(p)\,d\nu(p)\) and \(r_0(\nu)=\frac{1}{1-\mu}\int(1-p)\psi_\lambda(p)\,d\nu(p)\). The decision maker observes the share \(r\) and chooses a policy \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), where \(f(r)\) is the probability of choosing state \(1\). Characterize or compute a policy minimizing worst-case regret \(\rho^*=\min_f\sup_\nu[V_\nu-U_\nu(f)]\), where \(U_\nu(f)=\mu(2f(r_1(\nu))-1)+(1-\mu)(1-2f(r_0(\nu)))\) and \(V_\nu=\max_g U_\nu(g)\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of experts is described by posterior-type mass \(\nu\), with Bayesian plausibility \(\int p\,d\nu(p)=\mu\) and shared response rule \(\psi_\lambda\). The decision variable is a policy \(f\) on the exact report share \(r\); the objective is worst-case regret against an aggregator that knows \(\nu\). The opponent’s example suggests that, without a separation restriction, this objective may collapse to value \(1\).

The objection that survived

With exact report shares, the omniscient benchmark can achieve perfect accuracy whenever the two state-conditioned shares differ; the opponent’s chain argument then forces minimax regret to \(1\). The proponent notes that finite-sample variation disappears but does not address this collapse in the regret objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1(1), which characterizes majority voting as minimax-optimal for finite groups in the stated rationality regime. It does not cover Theorem 3.1(2)'s bounded-rationality advantage or the empirical LLM results.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named complexity classification in this paper—no result says the aggregation problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its running time. The strongest anchor I can defend is the exact optimizer result in Theorem 3.1(1), proved in this paper. It is an algorithmic characterization, not a runtime theorem.

Theorem 3.1(1) says that, for \(n\) conditionally independent, identically distributed experts using the response rule \(\psi_\lambda(p)=1/(1+e^{2\lambda(1-2p)})\), majority voting minimizes worst-case regret over all admissible signal structures whenever \(\lambda\le g(n)\). The rule outputs \(0\) below \(n/2\) reports of \(1\), \(1\) above \(n/2\), and randomizes evenly at a tie. For \(n\le2\), the theorem sets \(g(n)=\infty\); for larger \(n\), \(g(n)\) is the supremum of rationality levels for which, for every \(\psi_\lambda(0)\le q_0\le q_1\le 1/2\),

\( \displaystyle \frac{[q_1(1-q_1)]^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}(1-2q_1)} {\psi_\lambda(1)-q_1} \le \frac{[q_0(1-q_0)]^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}(1-2q_0)} {\psi_\lambda(1)-q_0} \cdot \frac{2\lambda+\ln(1-q_0)-\ln q_0} {2\lambda-\ln(1-q_0)+\ln q_0}. \)

The theorem’s other claim is that a finite rationality level can outperform perfect rationality: with one expert, perfect rationality maximizes utility; for every \(n\ge2\), some signal structure and finite \(\lambda\) give higher optimal utility than perfect rationality; and for \(n>2\), majority voting achieves that advantage. I would not treat this as a second anchor for the mirror: the continuum may remove the finite-sample randomness that helps produce this effect.

My lead is Continuum Robust Aggregation. The instance gives a prior \(\mu\in(0,1)\) and a response level \(\lambda>0\). Nature chooses an unknown signal structure represented by a probability distribution \(\nu\) over posterior types \(p\in[0,1]\), with \(\int p\,d\nu(p)=\mu\). For this mirror, restrict \(\nu\) to support contained in \(\{0,p,1\}\) for some \(p\), allowing zero mass at any of these points. This is an exact restriction for the report structures at issue: the paper’s proved Lemma A.2 shows that, for \(\lambda>0\), any report structure from a general signal space has an equivalent representation of this form.

A type \(p\) is an expert with posterior belief \(p\) and the shared response rule \(\psi_\lambda\). In a continuum of experts, each type’s mass produces a fraction \(\psi_\lambda(p)\) of reports equal to \(1\). Conditional on the true state, the aggregate report masses are

\( \displaystyle r_1(\nu)=\frac{1}{\mu}\int p\,\psi_\lambda(p)\,d\nu(p), \qquad r_0(\nu)=\frac{1}{1-\mu}\int (1-p)\,\psi_\lambda(p)\,d\nu(p). \)

The decision maker observes \(r\in[0,1]\) and uses an aggregator \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), where \(f(r)\) is the probability of choosing state \(1\). With the paper’s utility of \(+1\) for a correct decision and \(-1\) for an incorrect one, utility under \(\nu\) is

\( \displaystyle U_\nu(f)=\mu\bigl(2f(r_1(\nu))-1\bigr) +(1-\mu)\bigl(1-2f(r_0(\nu))\bigr). \)

Let \(V_\nu=\max_g U_\nu(g)\) be the utility of an aggregator that knows \(\nu\). The problem is to return an aggregator \(f^*\) and value \(\rho^*\) attaining

\( \displaystyle \rho^*=\min_f\ \sup_{\nu:\,\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\subseteq\{0,p,1\}\ \text{for some }p,\ \int p\,d\nu=\mu} \bigl(V_\nu-U_\nu(f)\bigr). \)

A solution is a finite characterization of the optimal policy and its worst-case regret, ideally with an algorithm for computing an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal policy. The population remains the continuous object: agents still report \(0\) or \(1\), and the decision remains binary. The continuum is the mass of experts and the observed report share.

The regime is a large panel answering the same question under the same evidence-generating process and response behavior: for example, many independent crowd judgments with only a few posterior evidence categories, or repeated model responses where each instance has the same task and temperature. The number of experts \(N\) is large compared with the number of distinct posterior types, which the paper’s reduction bounds by three for these report structures. The type captures everything the model uses: posterior and shared rationality level. This is a direct large-population extension of the paper’s c.i.i.d. expert model, not a fractional-outcome reformulation. Its finite-\(n\) analysis is not itself a collision with the continuous question, since it studies counts and finite-sample regret.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tentatively: the three-posterior reduction leaves only a small set of signal parameters, and the observation is one-dimensional, suggesting a finite-dimensional robust optimization or separation formulation. The continuous analogue of Theorem 3.1(1) asks whether a majority threshold survives in that limit and, if not, what policy replaces majority. I would not claim the theorem’s finite-\(n\) threshold applies unchanged. Further questions include whether majority is minimax-optimal at any fixed \(\lambda>0\) in the continuum, how \(g(n)\) behaves as \(n\) grows, and whether the bounded-rationality advantage in Theorem 3.1(2) survives under exact aggregate masses.

The weakest point is also the main modeling change: as \(N\) grows, the realized mass of stochastic reports becomes deterministic conditional on the state and signal structure. That may erase the finite-sample variation behind the paper’s bounded-rationality advantage. It weakens any claim to mirror that part of the paper, but the robust-aggregator question remains recognizable as theirs: it keeps their unknown signal structures, common objective, response rule, and regret benchmark, while asking what happens when the society itself becomes a continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational target. Theorem 3.1(1) proves that a particular rule is minimax optimal; it gives no complexity classification for a computational problem. The paper specifies no encoded instance whose size grows with \(n\), and its finite-\(n\) policy is simply majority voting. Computing that policy is immediate. A task to compute a minimax policy for a continuous population could be interesting, but it would introduce a new computational problem rather than continuize a complexity result from this paper.

The large-panel and repeated-LLM scenarios are plausible high-multiplicity regimes. The proponent’s use of Lemma A.2 is also legitimate: the reduction preserves the report statistics relevant to aggregation. The deeper difficulty is what happens when the population limit makes the observed report share exact. For example, take prior \(\mu=\tfrac12\), \(\lambda=10\), and a posterior distribution \(\nu^*\) placing mass \(50/51\) at \(p=0.49\) and \(1/51\) at \(p=1\). Its conditional report shares are
\[ q_0=\psi_{10}(0.49)\approx 0.4013,\qquad q_1=\frac{49}{51}\psi_{10}(0.49)+\frac{2}{51}\psi_{10}(1)\approx 0.4248. \]
Both lie below \(1/2\), with \(q_0<q_1\). Mix this signal structure with the uninformative structure \(\delta_{1/2}\), in proportion \(t\). The resulting shares are \(q_0(t)=\tfrac12-bt\) and \(q_1(t)=\tfrac12-at\), for constants \(0<a<b\). Each informative structure lets an omniscient aggregator distinguish the state perfectly from the exact share.

This creates a singular robust-regret problem. For any policy \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), regret on this structure is \(1-[f(q_1(t))-f(q_0(t))]\). Since \(q_0(t)=q_1((b/a)t)\), requiring regret below \(1\) by any fixed margin would force \(f\) to increase by that margin along arbitrarily long chains of shares. That is impossible for a function bounded between \(0\) and \(1\). The minimax regret is therefore \(1\) in this instance. The obstruction is the exact-share observation combined with unrestricted, arbitrarily weak signal structures and the paper’s omniscient benchmark: the benchmark’s value jumps when the two state-conditioned shares separate by any amount.

An information-separation assumption or a finite-precision observation model could remove this singularity and yield a robust optimization problem. Each adds structure absent from the paper; finite-sample noise, in particular, brings back the count variation the continuum limit removes. Such an extension may interest the authors, and the collapse itself could help mark where continuization stops helping. Still, it would be a decision-theoretic extension rather than a computational mirror of a named complexity result. That is the strongest negative case I can support; the paper’s large-population scenario is credible, so a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists would go beyond the evidence.
