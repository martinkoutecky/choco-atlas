What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-up draws on serial-dictatorship allocations related to Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, but it does not mirror their axiomatic claims; it leaves the thesis's consistency characterizations and Theorem 4's lottery impossibility without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the setting, rather than a result in the thesis. Large university housing allocation can have many students but relatively few complete preference types over a fixed set of room categories. A type would include the student’s ranking and priority cohort; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students in that cohort. If room supplies \(q_h\) scale with the population, an aggregate assignment is a flow \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=q_h\). Each student still receives one room; \(x_{t,h}\) records the fraction of a cohort receiving category \(h\).

A plausible follow-up problem is to compute the serial-dictatorship assignment for an ordered list of such cohorts: process each cohort in priority order, giving its mass to its most-preferred category while supply remains, then to its next choice, and so on. The solution is the resulting rational matrix \(x\). This is a natural high-multiplicity object, and the authors would likely recognize its connection to their serial-dictatorship rules. It is also a new computational task, rather than a computational result established by this thesis.

That distinction leaves the requested anchor set empty. Theorem 1, proved in the thesis, says every pairwise-consistent and pairwise-neutral rule is a simple sequential solution. Corollary 1, also proved here, says that adding weak Pareto optimality makes the rule a simple serial dictatorship. Theorem 4, proved here, says no ex-post Pareto-optimal, consistent, ex-ante-anonymous lottery-valued rule exists. These are characterizations and an impossibility result about allocation rules; none asserts a complexity classification such as polynomial-time solvability, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. The thesis contains no named computational result of the required kind, so there is no compliant per-anchor continuous problem to hand a prover.

The weak point is therefore decisive for the ChoCo case: the housing scenario supports a plausible continuous-population model, but the thesis’s results are axiomatic, not computational. The serial-allocation computation above is a modest possible extension, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed graduate-housing model is plausible, but it does not mirror a computational result in this thesis. Theorem 1 characterizes rules on finite problems with named agents and a strict priority order; Corollary 1 adds an efficiency condition; Theorem 4 is an impossibility result about lottery rules and axioms. None asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity. The other analyst’s proposed task—compute a cohort assignment from rankings and scaled room supplies—is a new problem that borrows the serial-dictatorship idea. It is not a continuous version of any of those results.

The stronger version of that proposal still has this gap. Grouping students into preference and priority cohorts can make an aggregate assignment sensible, but it changes the object being studied. The thesis’s consistency condition asks whether each subgroup’s restriction of a particular assignment is selected in the reduced problem. A cohort flow records how much mass receives each room category; it does not by itself say which students receive which rooms, or preserve the thesis’s identity-sensitive restrictions. One could define a mass version of consistency, or retain individual identities and track priority positions, but the former becomes a new axiomatic model and the latter removes the hoped-for compression. Neither supplies a computational mirror of the thesis.

The same issue defeats the other named anchors. A continuum version of Theorem 1 or Corollary 1 would ask whether an axiomatic characterization survives a change of population model. A version of Theorem 4 would ask whether an impossibility among lottery rules persists. Those may be coherent research questions, but they are not the computational questions ChoCo is targeting. The thesis offers no named computational result for a continuous society to mirror.

That is a strong objection to treating this thesis as an anchored ChoCo case, but it does not prove that every continuous housing-allocation question is unworthwhile. The large-cohort scenario is credible, and a computational study built around it could merit attention on its own. The negative case is strongest at the paper-to-programme boundary: this paper provides axiomatic inspiration, not a computational result whose population axis can be continuized.
