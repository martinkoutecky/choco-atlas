Its numbered propositions are equilibrium characterizations. That is a substantive analytic result, but it does not establish the computational complexity of a problem. Propositions 2 and 3 likewise characterize equilibria and comparative statics.

The paper studies equilibrium existence, uniqueness, and polarization—not computational questions about algorithms for a population distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–3 characterize equilibrium platforms and comparative statics; they do not classify the complexity of computing an equilibrium, or of any intervention problem. Proposition 1 is the clearest test: its voters already form a unit mass with uniformly distributed ideal policies, and the equilibrium is given explicitly as \(p_L^{**}=\frac12-\frac{1}{2n}\) and \(p_R^{**}=\frac12+\frac{1}{2n}\). Propositions 2 and 3 likewise study equilibrium in a continuous electorate. None supplies a discrete computational result for a continuous mirror to preserve.

For the uniform case, the paper already gives the answer in closed form. For the general case, it does not specify an input representation for \(F\), so a complexity question would first need additional modeling choices. Such a project might be worthwhile on its own, but it is not an anchor supplied by this paper.

The negative case is strongest for the paper as a ChoCo mirror, not as a claim that no related computational project could ever be worthwhile.
