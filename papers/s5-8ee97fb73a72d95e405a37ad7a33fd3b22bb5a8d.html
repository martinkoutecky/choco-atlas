The continuous mirror question

Given two insolvent creditor populations \(j\in\{1,2\}\), each with finite claim types \(t\in T_j\), rational masses \(\mu^j_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), rational claims \(q^j_t\ge 0\), and rational asset per creditor \(a_j>0\), where \(\Lambda_j=\sum_{t\in T_j}\mu^j_tq^j_t\ge a_j\), define \(\Phi_j=\int_0^1[\min\{a_j,u\Lambda_j\}-\max\{0,a_j-(1-u)\Lambda_j\}]\,du=a_j-a_j^2/\Lambda_j\). Decide whether \(\Phi_1=\Phi_2\), returning the exact values.

The model it lives in

Each society has total creditor mass \(1\), with each type \(t\) carrying claim \(q_t\) and mass \(\mu_t\); the firm remains a distinguished player and its assets scale as \(a\) per creditor. Define its per-creditor Shapley payoff by the limit of its finite random-order payoff divided by population size, then compare the exact payoffs for two environments.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 19’s comparison of the firm’s Shapley payments through a high-multiplicity limit; leaves the constant-sum game basis, liability bounds, and monotonicity results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is a sharp finite-population error bound, in terms of \(n\) and the claim-type data, between the firm’s Shapley payoff per creditor and \(\Phi_0\) under compatible replications?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s firm-payout comparison problem, in a regime with many creditors and relatively few claim types. A plausible instance is a large restructuring with many retail creditors whose claims fall into a modest number of standard balance classes. A type is a claim amount \(q_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of creditors in that class. Creditors of the same type are indistinguishable in the liability game the paper studies. As the creditor population grows, let the firm’s assets be \(n a\), with per-creditor asset scale \(a\), and let the count of type \(t\) be \(n\mu_t\). Thus the number of creditors grows while the number \(\tau\) of claim types stays moderate.

My lead question is Continuous Firm-Shapley Equality. An instance consists of two insolvent liability environments \(j\in\{1,2\}\), each specified by a positive rational asset scale \(a_j\) and a finite list of rational type masses and claim amounts \((\mu^j_t,q^j_t)\), with \(\sum_t\mu^j_t=1\) and \(\Lambda_j=\sum_t\mu^j_tq^j_t\ge a_j\). Ask whether the firm receives the same continuum Shapley payment per creditor-population unit in both environments; a solution is the correct yes/no answer, with the exact values as a check.

Define that payment as the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s Shapley payoff. In a uniformly random ordering, the firm’s position is uniform across the creditor population. Conditional on its rank being \(u\), asymptotically a fraction \(u\) of every creditor type precedes it. The predecessor coalition therefore has liability \(u\Lambda\), and the remaining creditors have liability \((1-u)\Lambda\). The firm’s marginal contribution at that rank is \(\min\{a,u\Lambda\}-\max\{0,a-(1-u)\Lambda\}\). Averaging over its uniform rank gives \(\Phi_0(a,\mu,q)=\int_0^1[\min\{a,u\Lambda\}-\max\{0,a-(1-u)\Lambda\}]\,du=a-a^2/\Lambda\). Equivalently, for compatible replications of the rational type masses, this is the limit of the finite firm Shapley payoff divided by \(n\).

Anchor: Theorem 19, proved in this paper. It states that, given two liability problems and their induced liability games, it is NP-hard to verify whether the firm has the same Shapley value in both games. The theorem is directly about computing or comparing the firm’s payoff in the underlying liability problems, so the mirror keeps that question; it does not replace it with a question about an encoding. In the continuous problem, compute \(a_j-a_j^2/\Lambda_j\) for each environment and compare the rationals. This takes polynomial time in the input bit length and support sizes. I expect Class A: tractable. The proof’s subset-sum structure concerns exact coalitions of individual creditors; at the population limit, a random rank exposes a deterministic share of each type, and the firm’s value depends only on aggregate claims. This is a natural place for population multiplicity to dissolve hardness.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 19’s firm-payout comparison, not the paper’s monotonicity and axiomatic results. It generates useful follow-up questions about convergence rates for finite creditor populations, how many types are needed for a good approximation, and which richer creditor attributes preserve a tractable limit.

My weakest point is the bridge’s granularity. The continuum model erases precisely the exact subset-sum events that drive the paper’s finite-population reduction, so a referee could ask whether that makes the limit too coarse to count as the authors’ problem. I think the answer is that it remains their problem: the liability game, the distinguished firm, the random-order Shapley payoff, and the comparison question are all preserved, while creditor mass and per-capita assets make the high-multiplicity regime explicit. There is also a proof-direction caveat: as printed, Theorem 19’s reduction shows that a HALFSUM yes-instance yields different Shapley payoffs, and hence directly supports hardness for inequality and coNP-hardness for equality. The stated NP-hardness of equality needs a reversed reduction or another argument. That caveat does not change the continuous question, but it weakens the paper’s stated hardness anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s mirror is that it changes the regime and normalization of Theorem 19. The theorem compares exact Shapley payments in finite problems with arbitrary claim lists; its reduction turns on whether a particular subset sums exactly to \(A\). The proposed mirror instead compares per-capita limits for replicated claim types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity question, but the fact that it is a limit of the finite problem does not make it the same decision problem.

A stronger version can preserve the comparison of total payments by including each population’s total mass \(M_j\) and comparing \(M_j\Phi_0(a_j,\mu^j,q^j)\). With total claims per capita \(\Lambda\ge a\), the firm’s marginal contribution at predecessor rank \(u\), divided by population size, converges to
\[ \min\{a,u\Lambda\}-\max\{0,a-(1-u)\Lambda\}. \]
Averaging over \(u\in[0,1]\) gives \(\Phi_0=a-a^2/\Lambda\). The formula depends only on aggregate claims, so it loses the exact subset events behind the finite reduction. But that is what a high-multiplicity relaxation can do; the paper’s question remains recognizable, and mass-scaled assets make the regime plausible.

There is also a flaw in the stated hardness anchor: the proof maps a HALFSUM yes-instance to *different* firm payments. That supports NP-hardness of deciding inequality and coNP-hardness of deciding equality; it does not establish the theorem’s stated NP-hardness for equality. This weakens the paper’s claimed result, but it does not make the continuous comparison ill-posed.

So the negative case is weak. The mirror is an asymptotic, normalized version of the finite comparison, and the paper’s hardness proof appears misstated. Neither point shows that no worthwhile mirror exists. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor: it survives as a computational question the authors could recognize.
