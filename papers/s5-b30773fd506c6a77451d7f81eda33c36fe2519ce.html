The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of claim types \(Q\), rational masses \(\mu_q>0\) with \(\sum_{q\in Q}\mu_q=1\), rational claims \(q\), a per-capita estate \(e\in[0,\bar c]\), and \(\theta\in[0,1]\), where \(\bar c=\sum_{q\in Q}\mu_q q\), compute the TAL award \(a_q\) for each type such that \(\sum_{q\in Q}\mu_q a_q=e\). Use \(a_q=\min\{\theta q,\lambda\}\) when \(e\le\theta\bar c\), and \(a_q=\max\{\theta q,q-\ell\}\) when \(e\ge\theta\bar c\), choosing the threshold to satisfy balance.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over creditor types \(q\), each with claim \(q\); the estate is specified per capita as \(e\). The output variables are type awards \(a_q\), constrained by weighted balance and the TAL threshold formula. There is no optimization objective: the task is to compute the rule's allocation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's allocation procedure and TAL outcome for a society represented by claim-type masses. It leaves Proposition 1's merging and splitting properties and the paper's other rule properties aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an exact bit-complexity algorithm for computing all \(a_q\) from the rational input, and establish whether the mass version of the coalitional procedure yields those same awards.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a paper-backed computational case: the paper contains no named result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise computationally difficult. Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that for every \(\theta\in[0,1]\) and every finite bankruptcy problem, the \(\theta\)-coalitional procedure yields the same allocation as the TAL rule \(R_\theta\). Proposition 1 says that on problems with estate-to-claims ratio \(\delta\), the procedure is non-manipulable by splitting when \(\theta<\delta\), by merging when \(\theta>\delta\), and by either when \(\theta=\delta\). Both are mathematical characterization or mechanism-property results, not computational results. Calling either a complexity anchor would overstate what the paper establishes.

The most plausible *unanchored* mirror is to compute TAL allocations for a large creditor population with a small number of claim classes. Let \(Q\) be a finite set of rational per-creditor claims, and let \(\mu_q\) be the rational fraction of creditors with claim \(q\), where \(\sum_{q\in Q}\mu_q=1\). Given a rational estate per creditor \(e\), with \(0\le e\le\bar c=\sum_q\mu_q q\), and a fixed rational \(\theta\), ask for the award \(a_q\) to each creditor of type \(q\). A solution is the TAL allocation: if \(e\le\theta\bar c\), then \(a_q=\min\{\theta q,\lambda\}\); if \(e\ge\theta\bar c\), then \(a_q=\max\{\theta q,q-\ell\}\), with the threshold chosen so that \(\sum_q\mu_q a_q=e\). This asks for the paper’s bankruptcy allocation with population represented by mass, not for fractional outcomes to a finite population.

A credible regime is a large pool of creditors grouped into a few contractual claim classes: the population is large while \(|Q|\) is small, and claim size is a complete type for the rule considered. The likely answer is tractability: sorting the support and solving the resulting one-dimensional piecewise-linear equation should take time polynomial in \(|Q|\) and the input bit length. Theorem 1 supports the connection to the paper’s rule, but it does not establish that complexity claim. The weak point is substantial: the allocation formula is already explicit, so this extension looks straightforward, and the paper’s main contribution is not computation. I would offer it only as a plausible continuous-population formulation, not as an anchored case that the paper contains a computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with the paper’s actual results. Theorem 1 identifies the allocation produced by a finite coalitional procedure with \(R_\theta\); Proposition 1 characterizes when that procedure resists merging or splitting at a fixed estate-to-claims ratio \(\delta\). Neither states the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous restatement of either result therefore cannot supply the computational anchor the programme seeks.

The best population mirror of Theorem 1 is still coherent: represent creditors by a finite distribution over claim amounts and calculate the award for each claim type. But in that model, the theorem becomes an identity between the mass version of the procedure and the TAL formula. The formula already reduces allocation to finding a scalar threshold from weighted sums over the claim support. There is no search over allocations or pricing problem hidden in the finite recursion. An algorithmic analysis of this calculation would be a new task, but a narrow one with no evident computational structure beyond evaluating the rule.

Proposition 1 offers a stronger possible route: ask whether a creditor can profit by splitting or merging claims against a continuous background. A single creditor has zero mass in an atomless society, so cannot change its aggregate distribution. One can preserve the strategic question by treating that creditor as a negligible test agent and comparing its awards under alternative claims. That is a sensible continuous extension, but the paper’s proposition is already a general incentive characterization; computing the comparison would again use the explicit award formula. A more ambitious question about a positive-mass coalition needs extra choices about how its members’ claims, population mass, and awards change when it merges or splits. That may be a worthwhile new model, but it is not a direct continuous version of Proposition 1.

The proponent’s large-creditor-class scenario is plausible: many creditors can share claim amounts, so lack of multiplicity is not a convincing objection. Its weakness is computational. On the paper’s own finite-support input, sorting claim types and evaluating the threshold gives the allocation directly. That makes the proposed mirror a legitimate high-multiplicity allocation calculation, but a thin case for a programme about computational complexity. This is the strongest negative I can make; I cannot honestly claim that no sensible continuous-population question exists here. The case against rests on the gap between a valid mass model and a worthwhile computational problem, not on the model being incoherent or already continuously studied.
