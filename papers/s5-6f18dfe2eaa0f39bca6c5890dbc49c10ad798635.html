What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors are Theorems 1 and 2, which identify the minimal and maximal transfer rules with Shapley-value utilities. The paper's axiomatic characterizations, game-theoretic observations, and conjecture for more servers are not computational results anchored here.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is weak because this paper contains no named computational result of the kind this brief asks for. The closest anchors are Theorems 1 and 2, both proved here. Theorem 1 says that, for a finite queueing instance and its optimistic coalition game, any allocation giving each agent the Shapley-value utility has an efficient service grouping and the transfers specified by the minimal transfer rule. Theorem 2 says the corresponding thing for the pessimistic coalition game and the maximal transfer rule. These are results about equivalence between solution concepts; neither establishes an algorithmic complexity bound or hardness result.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large service facility processing many similar jobs, with jobs grouped into a small number of waiting-cost types. Mass could represent each type’s share of the workload. But turning that into a continuous service-and-transfer problem would require specifying how the two servers’ capacity and the finite queue’s transfer rules scale with population. The paper does not formulate or compute such a model, so I cannot give a result-backed continuous problem per anchor without inventing one. The soft spot is decisive: the paper’s finite-agent Shapley-value identities do not themselves establish a computational question whose hardness might dissolve, or persist, under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that neither Theorem 1 nor Theorem 2 states a computational result. Theorem 1 identifies the minimal-transfer allocation with Shapley-value utilities in the optimistic game; Theorem 2 does the same for the maximal-transfer rule in the pessimistic game. They prove identities between solution concepts. The paper gives no complexity bound or hardness result for either one. That is a serious mismatch with ChoCo’s aim of charting the computational landscape.

There is a plausible continuous version of both results. A large facility could process many jobs drawn from a small set of waiting-cost types. After normalizing the population and scaling service time to keep total waiting time finite, one could ask whether the optimistic or pessimistic Shapley allocations have a per-type limit, and how to compute it from the type masses. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: jobs with the same waiting cost are interchangeable for the model’s purposes. So I would not claim that queueing has no meaningful continuous population model, or that individual identity blocks one.

But even these stronger mirrors do not give the paper a computational question to continue. They turn its finite Shapley identities into questions about limiting allocation formulas. The model has one scalar cost per job, an efficient order obtained by sorting those costs, and explicit transfer expressions. A complexity question about evaluating the resulting fluid allocation would be newly imposed on the paper, rather than a continuous counterpart of a computational result it establishes. Section 5.2’s “computational difficulties” concern proving a conjectured formula for more servers; the paper does not formulate that as a complexity problem either.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit, but weak as the universal claim the brief demands. A continuous optimistic or pessimistic queueing rule is quite plausible, and the two theorems could motivate worthwhile analysis of its limiting structure. What fails is the claim that this paper offers a named computational result whose continuous mirror would chart the complexity landscape.
