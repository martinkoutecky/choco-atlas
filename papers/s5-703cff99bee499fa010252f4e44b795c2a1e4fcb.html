The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a named computational result in this paper that meets your anchor requirement. Theorem 3.2 characterizes \(C\)-robustness; Theorem 5.4 bounds robustness for polarized markets; and Theorem 6.4 gives a probabilistic robustness bound. These are mathematical bounds about preference perturbations and communication requirements, not results classifying the complexity of solving a computational problem. Table 1 summarizes earlier communication-complexity results, but they are cited from other work, not numbered results proved here.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large matching market with repeated cohorts of agents sharing complete preference and utility types. But making that a continuous population would require defining a mass-matching problem and its stability notion. The paper’s theorems do not establish results about that problem: its market still has \(n\) agents on each side, bijective assignments, and finite preference profiles. Its continuity is in utility perturbations and communication over time, rather than in the population.

So I can’t give an honest per-anchor continuous computational question for this paper. The most relevant theorems may motivate a new mass-matching research question, but they are not anchors for one under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names Theorems 3.2, 5.4 and 6.4, but gives no continuous computational question for any of them. Taking them as the strongest possible anchors, I don’t see a worthwhile mirror of this paper’s results.

Theorem 3.2 characterizes robustness by utility ratios across *all* preference profiles. In a finite type-support model, one could instead ask for the robustness of an aggregate matching outcome over the listed types. But that changes the quantifier and the object being preserved: the theorem concerns exact individual assignments under every profile, while the mirror would concern mass flows over a chosen support. For finite support, the candidate threshold may reduce to scanning utility ratios among the listed types and alternatives. For a diffuse type space, defining the corresponding essential infimum requires additional assumptions. Either way, the theorem does not itself supply a new computational problem of the kind ChoCo targets.

The geometric bounds are even less tied to a population distribution. Theorem 5.4’s generating metric space represents utilities across every possible preference profile, including profiles absent from any given society. Theorem 6.4 then uses a probabilistic embedding of that space; its randomness is a perturbation device, not a distribution of agents. One could study geometric properties of a support-restricted type space, or the robustness of mass matchings under random perturbations. But those would be newly formulated problems. The paper’s bounds do not become complexity results just by assigning masses to types.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: repeated cohorts of applicants and employers, grouped by complete preference and utility types. A sensible extension could match mass between type classes and ask how much utility distortion can occur before the extremal stable mass flows change. That would address the population axis, and I am not ruling it out as a research project. But it requires replacing identity-level bijections and male- and female-optimal assignments with a mass-matching model, then defining stability, communication, and outcome equality for flows. Those changes are permitted in principle; the difficulty is that the paper’s named results do not anchor the resulting computational questions.

So the best negative case is narrower than “no scenario could work”: this paper offers no numbered computational result whose continuous mirror is already a clear ChoCo target. Its central results are robustness and geometric bounds for finite markets, and turning them into computational questions about a continuum society requires substantial new modeling. That makes the paper a weak mirror candidate, though the universal claim that *no* worthwhile scenario exists remains hard to defend—the cohort mass-matching extension is the main reason.
