The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered results are equations, tables, and a figure. For example, the paper reports that the human player joined a core coalition in 7 of 31 single-core games and 19 of 31 multiple-core games; it also reports a McNemar test in the Results section. Those are experimental findings, not eligible anchors under your criteria. Strictly speaking, then, the paper supplies no anchor-based continuous mirror.

The strongest positive case I can make is an unanchored mirror of the paper’s underlying core-partition problem for the glove game. Let a type \(t\) specify integer endowments \((L_t,R_t)\), with rational population masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A coalition composition is a type-share vector \(p\in\Delta(T)\), with per-member payoff
\[ u(p)=\min\!\left(\sum_t p_tL_t,\sum_t p_tR_t\right). \]
Represent a continuous partition by a finite list \((p^j,\lambda_j)\), where \(\lambda_j\) is the mass assigned to coalition composition \(p^j\), and
\[ \sum_j \lambda_jp_t^j=\mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t. \]
Ask whether there is such a partition with no blocking coalition, and return one if it exists. Precisely, composition \(p\) blocks when every type with \(p_t>0\) has positive mass currently receiving less than \(u(p)\):
\[ \sum_{j:\,u(p^j)<u(p)}\lambda_jp_t^j>0 \qquad\text{for every }t\text{ with }p_t>0. \]
Those members can then form a positive-mass coalition in proportions \(p\), all strictly improving. This preserves the paper’s payoff rule and its core blocking idea; the continuous change is that coalition composition is described by type shares and mass.

A plausible regime is a large barter or pooled-production market with many participants but relatively few endowment types: \(N\gg\tau\), where people with the same glove endowments are indistinguishable for the game. The authors could recognize this as a continuous version of the cooperative-game object they study. It covers the core-partition substrate, though—not the paper’s principal experimental finding about human choices, the ABMSCORE dynamics, or its reported frequencies. If forced to forecast the proposed problem’s direction, I would weakly guess Class A, because payoffs are piecewise linear over a finite type simplex and blocking has a natural optimization form. That is a research hypothesis, not a result of this paper.

The soft spot is decisive: the article studies seven-player experiments and contains no computational anchor to continuize. A referee might accept the proposed question as a sensible follow-on to its core-partition analysis, but the paper itself does not establish that the authors would recognize it as the continuous form of one of their named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the missing anchor. This article proves no theorem about the complexity of finding a core partition, and gives no algorithmic guarantee to extend. Its results are experimental: for example, it reports how often a human player ended in a core coalition and compares the two games with a McNemar test. The core partitions themselves are hand-calculated reference points for two chosen games; ABMSCORE is used as a simulator, with its prior validation cited from other work. So the paper’s result is about human play relative to the core, not computation of the core.

The proposed mass version is coherent, and a large glove market with repeated endowment types is a plausible setting. That makes it the strongest candidate, but it also shows the gap: deciding whether an atomless hedonic game has a core partition is a new computational project built around the paper’s background solution concept. It does not continuize a computational result in the article. Nor does it preserve the experiment’s main outcome: whether a particular human player ends in a core coalition. A single focal player has zero mass in the limit; giving that player positive mass would change the model, while keeping them as an atom would make it a hybrid population.

There is also a stability mismatch to resolve. In this glove game, a coalition’s per-member payoff depends on its composition \(p\), not its total mass \(\lambda\). A mass model can therefore allow a blocking composition at arbitrarily small positive scale. But a finite core rules out deviations by any subset, including a fixed-size coalition whose normalized mass tends to zero as the population grows. Positive-mass blocking can miss such deviations; representing zero-mass blockers requires retaining individuals the continuum has erased. A positive-mass stability notion may still be sensible, but it is a substantive new modeling choice, not an automatic limit of the paper’s core criterion.

I would not call the universal negative airtight: an atomless core-computation project might be worthwhile on its own, and the endowment types are not an artificial grouping. But the best honest case against a ChoCo mirror is strong: the paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its most plausible replacement changes the research question from explaining experimental behavior to computing a new continuum game’s core.
