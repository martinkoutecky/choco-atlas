The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of agent types on the two sides, binary-rational type masses, strict rankings over partner types with being unmatched last, compatible type pairs, and nonnegative type-pair weights, find a maximum-weight popular allocation. An allocation matches individuals and may leave them unmatched; it is popular when, against every feasible challenger on the same population, the mass of agents who strictly prefer it is at least the mass who strictly prefer the challenger.

The model it lives in

Use atomless cohorts with rational masses and shared preferences and weights within each type. Represent an allocation by its type-pair masses \(x_{ij}\), subject to each type's mass capacity; every individual still receives one whole partner or remains unmatched. Define popularity by comparing the resulting individual assignments against every feasible allocation of the same population.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's weighted popular matching optimization, including its hardness and maximum-weight approximation results; it leaves Theorem 1's forced and forbidden element classification and the further corollaries aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational type masses and type-pair weights, determine the complexity of maximizing total weight over allocations that no feasible challenger defeats under mass voting.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of maximum-weight popular matching. Take atomless cohorts \(A_1,\ldots,A_p\) and \(B_1,\ldots,B_q\), with binary-rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) summing to \(1\). A type specifies a strict ranking of compatible partner types, with being unmatched last. Each compatible type pair \((i,j)\) has a nonnegative weight \(w_{ij}\), shared by every match of that kind. The regime is a large market—say, many applicants and positions in repeated cohorts with standardized preferences—where the number of agents is far larger than the number \(\tau=p+q\) of distinct types.

An allocation is a measurable matching of individuals: each unit of mass receives one partner or remains unmatched. Its type-level description is a matrix \(x\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass matched between \(A_i\) and \(B_j\), subject to
\[ x_{ij}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_j x_{ij}\le \alpha_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}\le \beta_j. \]
Its weight is \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}x_{ij}\). Compare two allocations on the same population, as in the paper: \(\Phi(M,N)\) is the mass of agents who strictly prefer their assignment in \(M\) to their assignment in \(N\), counting both sides. An allocation \(M\) is popular if \(\Phi(M,N)\ge\Phi(N,M)\) for every feasible challenger \(N\).

This gives two precise questions over the same set \(\mathcal P_\infty\) of popular mass matchings:

  • Max-PopMass\(_\infty\): given the masses, rankings and weights, return a popular matching maximizing \(\sum w_{ij}x_{ij}\).
  • Min-PopMass\(_\infty\): with the same input, return a popular matching minimizing that sum.

The output can be the type-pair mass matrix: because members of a type are interchangeable and the cohorts are atomless, different realizations of the same matrix are equivalent under relabeling within types. The mirror keeps the paper’s central question—whether any feasible alternative can defeat the chosen matching—and its weight objective. It changes the population granularity: mass can split across assignments, while every individual still receives one whole match or is unmatched. That is population continuity, not a divisible-outcome model.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. In the finite strict-preference model with nonnegative edge weights, both maximum-weight and minimum-weight popular matching are NP-hard. Unless \(P=NP\), minimum-weight popular matching has no polynomial-time approximation within any factor; maximum-weight popular matching has no polynomial-time approximation better than \(1/2\), and a polynomial-time \(1/2\)-approximation exists. The paper proves the hardness and inapproximability using its reduction; the \(1/2\) guarantee uses the popular-matching decomposition from Cseh and Kavitha [4] and polynomial algorithms for maximum-weight stable and dominant matchings.

I would put both population versions in Class A as a research hypothesis, with Max-PopMass\(_\infty\) the lead. The paper’s reduction encodes Boolean choices through particular edges of many distinct gadget vertices. In the mass model, those choices can split, so the reduction’s discrete hardness does not directly carry over. There is also a concrete route toward continuous optimization: for a fixed incumbent mass matching, divide each cohort by its incumbent partner type, including the unmatched category. A challenger’s best vote margin can then be expressed as a linear program over how much mass from each incumbent category it rematches to each compatible type pair. The key unresolved step is to turn that challenger LP into an efficient way to optimize over the set of popular mass matrices. If that set admits a polynomial-time separation procedure, both linear objectives could be optimized with continuous-optimization machinery. That is a plausible tractability target, not a result established by this paper.

The authors should recognize the mirror: it studies two-sided popularity under the same “no majority-defeating matching” rule and optimizes the same kind of edge weight. The market scenario supplies many interchangeable agents with shared preferences and weights, rather than merely retelling the paper’s gadget instance. The mirror covers Theorem 2’s weighted optimization results; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s full forced/forbidden-elements classification.

The weakest point is that the paper gives each vertex a strict ranking over named neighbours. This mirror ranks partner *types* strictly and treats members within a partner type as interchangeable. That is a meaningful high-multiplicity co-modification, but authors may see the identity-level indifference as a tied-clone extension rather than their exact original model. The other major risk is computational: popularity of a fixed mass allocation has a challenger LP, but that alone does not show that the set of popular allocations is easy to optimize over. Whether that outer problem remains tractable, or becomes continuum-specifically hard, is the central question the mirror generates.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real anchor: it gives named complexity and approximation results for maximum- and minimum-weight popular matching. I can’t make a sound case that it has no continuous mirror. A strong version is a two-sided market with finitely many standardized applicant and employer types, rational mass for each type, strict rankings over partner types, and a shared weight for each compatible type pair. Each person still receives one whole match or remains unmatched; the mass matrix records how those individual assignments are distributed. That is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem, and matching cohorts to cohorts is a natural setting for it.

The best objection is a modeling cost. In the paper, each vertex ranks named neighbours strictly. Cohorting people forces preferences to be expressed over partner types, so members of a type become indifferent among individuals of the same partner type. This changes the preference model as well as the population scale. But that change is a natural co-modification for a standardized large market, and the paper’s two-sided popularity question and edge-weight objective remain recognizable. It does not defeat the mirror.

The proponent’s algorithmic route is less secure than stated. An LP that finds the strongest challenger for a *fixed* mass matching gives a way to test popularity; it does not show that maximum- or minimum-weight optimization over popular mass matchings is an LP. The challenger constraints depend on the incumbent mass matrix, and the set where the best challenger’s margin is zero need not be convex. So this is not yet a credible Class A result. But if the continuous version proves tractable, hard, or continuum-specifically hard, each outcome would answer the programme’s question. The gap weakens the promised algorithmic leverage, not the case for asking it.

There is nearby work on popular fractional and mixed matchings, but it compares fractional assignments or lotteries over a finite set of agents. That does not answer the deterministic population-mass question here; treating it as a collision would confuse outcome-space continuity with population continuity. [Popular mixed matchings](https://www.di.ens.fr/~cchuang/work/mixed_popular.pdf), [Quasi-popular Matchings, Optimality, and Extended Formulations](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.05974).

So the honest negative case is weak. The mirror needs a careful definition of how mass-level allocations are compared, and its outer optimization may be difficult. Neither point rules out a worthwhile mirror of Theorem 2.
