The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings of \(\mathcal C\) and a maximin-losing candidate \(l\), define \(p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t\). Choose rational evidence masses \(x_{ab}\) with \(0\le x_{ab}\le p_\mu(a,b)\) and \(x_{ab}+x_{ba}\le 1\). Call \(x\) sound if every ranking distribution \(\nu\) satisfying \(p_\nu(a,b)\ge x_{ab}\) has \(l\notin\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal C}\min_{d\ne c}p_\nu(c,d)\). Compute the infimum of \(\sum_{a\ne b}x_{ab}\) and, for each rational \(\epsilon>0\), return sound evidence within \(\epsilon\) of that infimum.

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(t\in\mathcal T\), with rational population masses \(\mu_t\) and induced pairwise shares \(p_\mu(a,b)\). Evidence is a fractional partial tournament \(x\); its cost is \(\sum_{a\ne b}x_{ab}\), and its completions are distributions over rankings consistent with the revealed comparison masses.

The objection that survived

The theorem already operates on an aggregate pairwise-count matrix, so the mirror relaxes integer counts to masses without gaining compression over voter identities; ranking-realizability may also add separate consistency complexity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.3’s maximin smallest-support size and construction; it leaves the other tournament rules’ characterizations and support-size results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the matching structure behind Theorem 5.3 extend when completions must come from ranking distributions?
  • Can the least-mass value and an \(\epsilon\)-optimal sound explanation be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper. It is an algorithmic anchor; the paper gives no hardness result to mirror. The theorem says: for a complete \(n\)-weighted tournament \(G=(\mathcal C,\mu)\) with \(m\) candidates, if \(l\) loses under maximin, then every smallest destructive minimal support \(\mathcal X\) for \(l\) has size \(\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil\) when \(m=2\). For \(m>2\), if some candidate \(c\) has \(\sigma_{\mm}(c)>\sigma_{\mm}(l)\) and \(\mu(c,l)\ge n-\sigma_{\mm}(l)\), then \(|\mathcal X|=(\sigma_{\mm}(l)+1)(m-3)+n+1\); otherwise \(|\mathcal X|=(\sigma_{\mm}(l)+1)(m-2)+n+1\). The theorem also says such a support can be computed in polynomial time and has size at most \(\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil(m-2)+n+1\). ([Theorem 5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.23446))

A natural regime is a large public election over a modest candidate set: millions of voters, but far fewer distinct rankings than voters because many people share ballot types. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking, with rational population share \(\mu_t\). The pairwise share is \(p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t\), and candidate \(c\)’s maximin score is \(\sigma_\mu(c)=\min_{d\ne c}p_\mu(c,d)\). This keeps the paper’s object—the pairwise tournament induced by voters—while expressing its weights as fractions of the population.

I would call the mirror Maximin Mass-Support Explanations. Given that ranked-type distribution and a candidate \(l\) who loses maximin, choose rational evidence masses \(x_{ab}\), with \(0\le x_{ab}\le p_\mu(a,b)\) and \(x_{ab}+x_{ba}\le1\). A completion is any distribution \(\nu\) over complete rankings on the same candidates whose pairwise shares satisfy \(p_\nu(a,b)\ge x_{ab}\) for every ordered pair. The evidence is sound if \(l\) is not a maximin winner under *every* such completion. Its cost is \(\sum_{a\ne b}x_{ab}\): the total revealed voter-comparison mass, matching the paper’s measure of support size after dividing counts by \(n\).

The problem is to compute the infimum sound cost and, for every rational \(\epsilon>0\), produce sound evidence costing at most \(\epsilon\) above that infimum—or an exact optimizer when one exists. This is precise about the instance, the decision variables, the objective and what a solution certifies. The mass scale also has a meaningful limit: Theorem 5.3’s upper bound, divided by \(n\), stays bounded as the electorate grows.

I would provisionally expect Class A, since the paper’s maximin argument has a threshold and matching structure that may survive fractional weights, with optimization over types adding a column-generation or separation question. The key follow-ups are whether that structure extends to completions realizable by ranking distributions, whether the pricing problem over rankings is tractable, and whether the optimum is attained or only approached.

The weakest point is a real change forced by divisibility. The paper requires inclusion-minimal supports; with continuous masses and strict loser conditions, an evidence vector may remain sound after an arbitrarily small reduction, so an inclusion-minimal support may not exist. This mirror preserves the necessary-loser condition and the paper’s total-size objective, but asks for a least-mass explanation via an infimum instead. Also, the paper allows abstract weighted-tournament completions, whereas this mirror restricts completions to populations of rankings. That makes the question more faithful to a voter population, but means Theorem 5.3 does not itself settle its complexity. The scope here is only the paper’s maximin smallest-support result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.3 is a real computational anchor: it gives an exact smallest-support size and a polynomial-time construction for maximin. I can’t honestly dismiss it under the stated rules.

The strongest case against its *value as a continuization* is that the paper has already compressed voters into an \(m\times m\) matrix of pairwise counts. Its maximin result runs on that aggregate, with no ballot list or voter identities whose size grows with \(n\). A large election with repeated rankings is a sensible population regime, but the theorem’s computational input has already discarded the distinctions between voters that a continuous population would discard.

For the closest mirror, write \(p(a,b)=\mu(a,b)/n\) and normalize the support cost by \(n\). The theorem’s two size formulas then become, respectively,
\[ \left(q+\frac1n\right)(m-3)+1+\frac1n \quad\text{and}\quad \left(q+\frac1n\right)(m-2)+1+\frac1n, \]
where \(q=\sigma_{\mm}(l)/n\). In a divisible-mass version, the one-unit strictness margin can be replaced by an arbitrarily small \(\delta>0\); the corresponding infimum follows by letting \(\delta\) approach zero. This is a well-posed extension, yet it appears to carry over the paper’s structural proof directly. It offers little of the new algorithmic work ChoCo seeks: no population-scale bottleneck disappears, and no new optimization machinery seems necessary.

The proponent’s ranking-distribution version adds a more substantial question by requiring completions to come from distributions over rankings. That requirement brings in global consistency constraints among pairwise shares. It may be worth studying, but it changes the completion model that Theorem 5.3 analyzes: the paper allows arbitrary weighted-tournament completions. The resulting complexity could come from ranking realizability rather than from making the population continuous.

This is the strongest negative case I can defend, and it falls short of the universal claim. Maximin mass-support explanations remain a natural, recognizable mirror; the case against is about their likely incremental computational value.
