The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of voter types \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\), weights \(w_t\), and corruption budget \(\rho<1/3\), decide whether there exist corrupted masses \(b_t\) and guaranteed included correct masses \(x_t\) such that \(0\le b_t,\ 0\le x_t,\ b_t+x_t\le\mu_t,\ \sum_t b_t\le\rho,\ \sum_t x_t\ge1-2\rho\), and \(\sum_t b_tw_t\ge\sum_t x_tw_t\). Equivalently, maximize \(\sum_t b_tw_t-\sum_t x_tw_t\) subject to those constraints and determine whether the maximum reaches zero.

The model it lives in

Model the organization as a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types sharing a public weight and relevant protocol role. The decision variables \(b_t\) and \(x_t\) represent corrupted mass and guaranteed included correct mass; the objective maximizes the difference between their weighted tallies. The resulting support-sized LP tests the weighted-quorum robustness condition abstracted by Corollary 5.2.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a named computational-complexity result in this paper that meets your anchor rule. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The numbered Corollaries 5.1–5.3 concern guarantees about the voting protocol’s behavior; they are not complexity results. The message bound \(O(n^3)\) and latency bound \(O(\log n)\) appear in the unnumbered complexity analysis in §5.1. So there is no qualifying anchor on which to build the requested case.

The nearest positive candidate would be a continuous version of the robustness condition in Corollary 5.2. That corollary says that if the adversary’s total vote weight is less than the weight of every subset of \(n-2f\) correct voters, then—when all correct voters oppose the action—the adversary cannot obtain a majority.

For a large organization whose approvers fall into a small number of configured weight classes, define a type by its vote weight and relevant protocol role. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of approvers of type \(t\), and \(w_t\) their weight. Given corruption budget \(\rho<1/3\), let \(b_t\) be corrupted mass and \(x_t\) be correct mass included in the tally. Ask whether an adversary can make the weighted approval tally reach a majority, subject to
\[ 0\le b_t,\quad 0\le x_t,\quad b_t+x_t\le\mu_t,\quad \sum_t b_t\le\rho,\quad \sum_t x_t\ge 1-2\rho. \]
A witness is a feasible pair \((b,x)\) with
\[ \sum_t b_t w_t\ge\sum_t x_t w_t; \]
if none exists, the modeled guarantee holds. This is a small linear feasibility problem over the support types, so I would expect this abstraction to be tractable.

The regime is conceivable for organization-wide authorization, where many approvers share weights by role and there are far fewer roles than people. But the paper studies small voting groups—the experiments reach 61 voters—and its protocol depends on individually identified processes, fault counts, and cryptographic sharing. Aggregating those processes into fractional mass models only the tally robustness condition; it does not continuize the protocol the authors built. That is the candidate’s weakest point and why I would not present it as a valid anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that Privocracy has no named computational result to continuize. Corollaries 5.1–5.3 are guarantees about the protocol’s behavior under Byzantine faults; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The \(O(n^3)\) message bound and \(O(\log n)\) latency bound in §5.1 describe the cost of this protocol, but the paper does not formulate an algorithmic problem whose complexity it analyzes. Its contribution is a system construction and evaluation.

The proposed mirror of Corollary 5.2 is the closest candidate, and a large organization with repeated voter-weight classes is a plausible setting for a continuous weighted-quorum question. But the suggested feasibility LP turns a sufficient security condition into a new static configuration problem. The corollary itself does not pose or solve that optimization problem. A continuous quorum-robustness study could be worthwhile on its own; it would not mirror a computational result established by this paper.

Trying to continuize Privocracy itself makes the mismatch clearer. Its voting units are individually identified processes with separate shares and agreement instances; audits and delegation also refer to particular voters. A type description that preserves those features may require a separate type for each process, leaving no useful reduction to a small support. Aggregating voters by role or weight can model the threshold condition, but it cannot model the protocol’s privacy, fault tolerance, or execution.

So the negative case is strong for the paper’s main contribution, but not airtight against the adjacent quorum question: Corollary 5.2 does make that question natural. I would reject it as a qualifying mirror here because it is a new computational formulation of one protocol guarantee, rather than a continuation of a computational result the paper reports.
