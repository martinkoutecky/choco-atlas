The continuous mirror question

Let \(T_s\) and \(T_c\) be finite supports of single and couple types, with masses \(\alpha_t\) and \(\beta_u\), where couple types specify both partners' complete descriptions. Given target shares \(p_{t,h}\) and \(r_{u,h}\) over hospitals \(h\in H\), with each share vector summing to \(1\), and per-capita hospital capacities \(q_h\), where \(\sum_{t\in T_s}\alpha_t+2\sum_{u\in T_c}\beta_u=1\), does there exist a measurable assignment of the society realizing those shares and capacities? Each point of single-type mass must be assigned to one hospital; each couple household must be assigned wholly to one hospital, with both partners together. Hospital \(h\) must receive intern mass \(\sum_{t\in T_s}\alpha_t p_{t,h}+2\sum_{u\in T_c}\beta_u r_{u,h}=q_h\).

The model it lives in

An atomless society with single and couple types, each carrying mass. A measurable partition assigns each unit of mass to a hospital; couple households remain together and contribute two units of intern mass. The target is to realize the given type-level hospital shares while meeting per-capita capacities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's exact decomposability question. It does not preserve the finite discrepancy bounds and approximation analysis of Theorems 2 and 3, or the thesis's welfare optimization and empirical results.

Open questions for a prover

  • How tightly can finite lotteries over named interns approximate the type-level shares as a function of type counts and hospital capacities?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 1, the thesis’s exact decomposability result. It is proved in the thesis by reduction from 3-edge coloring of cubic graphs, whose NP-completeness is cited from Holyer (1981).

Theorem 1 says that, on domain \(P\), it is NP-complete to decide whether a target stochastic assignment matrix can be written as a convex combination of deterministic assignments. In the underlying problem, each intern has a row of assignment probabilities summing to one; the two members of every couple have identical rows; and hospital capacities are fixed by the column sums. A valid decomposition is a lottery over \(0\)-\(1\) assignments that fills those capacities and always places each couple together. The reduction uses three capacity-two hospitals for each graph edge and three capacity-one hospitals for each vertex. It builds one couple and six singles per edge, with a valid decomposition existing exactly when the graph has a proper 3-edge coloring.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a much larger centralized internship or clinical-placement match: many thousands of interns assigned to a hospital network, with capacities scaling with cohort size and a modest repertoire of complete preference profiles. Type means identical rankings and all other attributes used by the assignment problem. Couple types record both partners’ rankings and their common assignment constraint. The thesis describes a roughly 500-intern, 23-hospital market and geographically patterned preferences, but it does not establish that its full preference support is small; the larger regime is a plausible scenario, not a claim about those data.

My lead question is Coupled Type-Mass Exact Implementation. Its instance consists of hospitals \(H\); single types \(t\) with individual mass \(\alpha_t\); couple types \(u\) with household mass \(\beta_u\); a target hospital-share vector \(p_{t,h}\) for each single type and \(p_{u,h}\) for each couple type; and per-capita hospital capacities \(q_h\). A couple type contains the complete rankings of both partners, and both partners have the same target share vector. Normalize total intern mass by \(\sum_t\alpha_t+2\sum_u\beta_u=1\). The question is whether the target shares can be realized by a valid assignment of the society, with every intern assigned to one hospital, each couple assigned together, and each hospital receiving exactly its capacity. A solution partitions each single-type population into hospital-assigned pieces of mass \(\alpha_t p_{t,h}\), and each couple-type population into household pieces of mass \(\beta_u p_{u,h}\). Each couple piece sends both partners to the same hospital. Thus hospital \(h\) receives mass \(\sum_t\alpha_t p_{t,h}+2\sum_u\beta_u p_{u,h}\).

This is a real continuous-population version of the theorem’s question: the target row for a named finite intern becomes the share of that type’s mass sent to each hospital. The mass may be divided across hospitals, while every point in the population still receives an integral assignment and the couple constraint remains exact. In this atomless setting, feasibility reduces to checking the row sums and capacity equations; rational interval cuts construct the assignment in time polynomial in \(m\), the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length. I expect Class A for this mirror. It identifies a reason the finite problem is hard—indivisible named participants—not a new hardness claim about the continuum.

The authors could recognize this as their exact ex-post implementation problem with the interim marginals read as population shares. It preserves the target marginals, hospital capacities, and couple complementarity. The scope is only Theorem 1’s feasibility problem; it does not claim to mirror the thesis’s welfare optimization or its approximation analysis.

The weakest point is that a type-mass share is not the same object as a named individual’s lottery probability. The mirror asks for an allocation of a nonatomic population whose type-level proportions match the target; the finite theorem asks for a lottery over whole-population assignments with each named intern’s marginal preserved. A referee who regards that finite lottery as essential may reject the mass allocation as too different. The case survives if the authors accept the programme’s high-multiplicity interpretation: the aggregate share is the relevant marginal, and the members of each couple are still assigned together.

The mirror raises useful follow-up questions: how large can the finite-to-continuum discrepancy be as a function of type counts and hospital capacities? Can mass allocations be rounded into finite lotteries with a useful discrepancy bound? And do additional constraints—such as couples sharing a city rather than a hospital—retain tractability?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

For the one anchor raised, Theorem 1, the strongest objection is that its continuous version loses the question’s central constraint: assigning whole couples to hospitals. That constraint is what makes the finite decomposition problem hard.

Grant a credible high-multiplicity setting: many interns, a modest set of complete preference types, and repeated couple types. Represent couples as households of mass \(\beta_u\); their members share a hospital. If type \(t\) has mass \(\alpha_t\) and target shares \(p_{t,h}\), split its mass into pieces of size \(\alpha_t p_{t,h}\). Split each couple type’s household mass into pieces of size \(\beta_u p_{u,h}\), sending both partners in each piece to the same hospital. Hospital \(h\) then receives mass
\[ \sum_t \alpha_t p_{t,h}+2\sum_u \beta_u p_{u,h}. \]
When the target shares sum to one and these totals equal the capacities, this construction realizes the target exactly. It works even if every graph-gadget participant in the reduction is given a distinct type: each type’s mass can still be divided.

The proponent is right that type-level shares are a plausible high-multiplicity interpretation, and that the authors could recognize the question. But the faithful continuous version makes every admissible target directly implementable. The NP-hardness reduction relies on indivisible pairs of seats being occupied in each whole assignment; splitting a couple *population* among hospitals removes that obstruction. The result is not a new pricing problem or a nontrivial feasibility test. It is a construction from the target table itself.

Even insisting on lottery marginals does not restore the obstacle. Randomly shifting the mass partitions can give each point of each type its stated hospital probabilities while preserving capacities in every realization; for couple types, the household is assigned as a unit. The finite lottery over assignments has become unnecessary because each realization can already contain the target proportions.

The same collapse applies to Theorems 2 and 3’s approximation question: in the atomless model, the target can be matched exactly, so the finite discrepancy bound and its lower bound have no continuous counterpart with substance. A potentially interesting follow-up would study how well such mass allocations round back to finite assignments. That is a high-multiplicity rounding question, rather than a computational problem over a continuous society.

This is a strong degeneracy objection, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. The vulnerable point is that the programme may count a simple exact limit result as worthwhile evidence that population indivisibility caused the hardness. I would still judge this particular anchor thin: its continuous answer follows from dividing the input masses, and the nontrivial computational content returns only when finite granularity is restored.
