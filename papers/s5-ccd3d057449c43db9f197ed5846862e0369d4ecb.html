The continuous mirror question

Given \(N\) people represented by \(\tau\) complete baseline-access types with rational masses \(n_t/N\), agency supplies \(q_j\), and a fixed ladder \(0<\mu_1<\cdots<\mu_r\le1\), compute the maximum certified service welfare over profile-only stochastic admissions. For each positive-mass bucket profile \(a\), choose admitted masses \(z_{a\ell}\ge0\) maximizing \(\sum_{a,\ell}\mu_\ell z_{a\ell}\), subject to \(\sum_\ell z_{a\ell}\le p_a\) and \(\sum_{a,\ell:j\in S_\ell(a)}\mu_\ell z_{a\ell}\le q_j\) for every agency \(j\); output the maximizing masses or equivalent conditional admission probabilities.

The model it lives in

A finite high-multiplicity population with \(\tau\) complete types, represented by rational masses, fixed bucket reports, and per-capita supplies. The decision variables are admitted masses by bucket profile and service level; the objective is the paper’s conservative certificate, with ex-ante capacity constraints. The LP has at most \(r\tau\) variables and is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,r\), and the input bit length.

What the mirror covers

Covers the fixed-threshold and fixed-ladder certificate LPs and their finite empirical-population implementations. It leaves actual-welfare optimization, choosing ladder levels, and estimating the population distribution outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s fixed-message clearing problem. Its most suitable anchor is Proposition 6.1, “Finite LP and stochastic implementation,” proved here. The paper does not give a named NP-hardness or complexity-class theorem, so this is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result. Proposition 6.1 says that, for a fixed threshold, the clearing LP has one variable per positive-mass referral profile and \(m\) capacity constraints; for a fixed \(r\)-level ladder, it has at most \(r\) variables per positive-mass joint bucket profile and \(m\) capacity constraints. A feasible LP solution implements a stochastic clearing rule whose expected resource use respects the supplies. For an empirical distribution of \(n\) observed profiles, there are at most \(n\) positive-mass profiles, so the LP can be built from profile counts; random admission implements its fractional decisions ex ante. If capacities must hold in every realization, rounding each admitted profile-level count down and assigning distinct people preserves capacity. The certified-welfare loss is less than \(\mu_\ell/n\) per rounded level-\(\ell\) decision, hence at most \(D/n\) over \(D\) positive profile-level decisions. The proposition cautions that this is not a general bound on full actual welfare under arbitrary assignments; uniform random assignment within each profile preserves the information restriction and gives the stated bound in expectation relative to the original kernel.

Here is the mirror question I would hand to a prover. There are \(N\) people, \(m\) agencies, and a fixed ordered ladder \(0<\mu_1<\cdots<\mu_r\le1\). Each person has a complete type \(w=(w_1,\ldots,w_m)\), their baseline access to the \(m\) resources; people of one type are identical for the model’s purposes. The empirical distribution assigns mass \(n_t/N\) to type \(t\). Agency \(j\) observes only \(w_j\), sends its prescribed bucket, and has per-capita supply \(q_j\). Given the resulting bucket-profile masses \(p_a\), choose admitted masses \(z_{a\ell}\) to maximize certified service welfare
\[ \sum_{a,\ell}\mu_\ell z_{a\ell} \]
subject to \(\sum_\ell z_{a\ell}\le p_a\) for each profile and
\[ \sum_{a,\ell:\,j\in S_\ell(a)}\mu_\ell z_{a\ell}\le q_j \]
for each agency. Here \(S_\ell(a)\) is the set of agencies whose reports indicate a deficit at level \(\mu_\ell\). A solution is the admitted mass at each profile and level, equivalently the corresponding conditional admission probabilities. The objective is the paper’s conservative welfare certificate, not the maximum of full actual welfare.

I expect this problem to be tractable: if the input gives \(\tau\) positive-mass types and rational masses, capacities, and levels, then there are at most \(\tau\) positive-mass bucket profiles and at most \(r\tau\) decision variables. Standard linear programming gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,r\), and the input bit length \(L\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large public-benefits population with standardized baseline-access vectors across complementary programs might have millions of people but relatively few distinct complete profiles. The type must include every feature the allocation rule uses; named individuals with different relevant costs or needs cannot be silently grouped.

The bridge is especially plausible because the paper itself supplies much of it: for an empirical population, the masses are exactly the profile counts divided by \(N\), and the stochastic implementation has the same ex-ante constraints. That is a useful “turns out” identity between a finite high-multiplicity population and the mass formulation. The mirror covers only the fixed-message clearing optimization of Proposition 6.1. It does not settle the welfare-optimal kernel under actual top-up costs, the choice of ladder levels, or robustness to estimating the population distribution.

The weak point is also the case’s limit: the paper already models a unit-mass population, so this is a finite-population/high-multiplicity bridge to its existing continuous model, not a newly discovered continuous-population problem. And the anchor is an LP implementation result, not a discrete hardness result whose complexity might dissolve under continuization. That leaves a credible, narrow tractability mirror, but no broad complexity-landscape claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is already a continuous-population problem. The paper starts with a unit-mass society distributed according to \(F\) over baseline-access types \(w\in[0,1]^m\), and measures resource supplies per capita. Its clearing LP is built from the masses of reported profiles. For a finite high-multiplicity population, those masses are just counts divided by \(N\). So the proposed “mirror” runs backward: it takes a finite empirical population and recovers the mass formulation the paper already uses.

Proposition 6.1 makes the overlap especially direct. It gives the fixed-threshold and fixed-ladder LPs, describes their stochastic implementation, and explicitly treats empirical distributions. With \(\tau\) distinct complete types, there are at most \(\tau\) positive-mass bucket profiles and \(r\tau\) ladder variables. The resulting rational LP is polynomial in \(m,r,\tau\), and the input bit length. A high-multiplicity analysis could spell out that bound, but it would not supply a missing continuous computational question or result.

A stronger candidate would optimize actual welfare using actual top-up costs, or choose the ladder levels rather than take them as fixed. Those may be worthwhile extensions. But the population in either formulation is still the paper’s distribution \(F\); changing the objective or adding a level-selection problem does not make a previously discrete society continuous. The paper also explicitly distinguishes its conservative certificate from actual welfare and notes that it does not give an end-to-end algorithm for optimizing over a continuum of thresholds. Those are open computational details within an already continuous model, not evidence of a missing continuization.

This is a strong objection to the one anchor raised: it is a restatement of the paper’s own continuous model and fixed-message optimization, not a fresh mirror. It does not establish that no other discrete problem in this area could have a valuable continuous mirror; the paper alone cannot support that universal claim.
