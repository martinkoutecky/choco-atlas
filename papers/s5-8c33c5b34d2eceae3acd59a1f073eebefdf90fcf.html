What the mirror covers

Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.2 characterize stable sets and their union for finitely many named buyers; no computational result is covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 3.1, proved here, characterizes the stable sets as graphs of continuous, componentwise nonincreasing functions within \(X\). Theorem 4.2, also proved here, describes the union \(U\) of all stable sets as
\[ U=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{conv}(M_i\cup M_{i+1}), \]
where Proposition 4.1 identifies the vertices as marginal contribution vectors. These are substantial structural results, but none establishes a problem’s complexity or algorithmic status. Under your anchor rule, I therefore have no result to mirror as a computational result.

The strongest plausible population scenario is a single seller offering a scarce item to a very large buyer population with only \(\tau\) valuation types. A precise follow-on question would give type \(i\) mass \(\mu_i\), valuation \(a_i\), and per-mass compensation \(p_i\); the seller receives \(u\), with \(u+\sum_i\mu_i p_i=a_1\). A coalition can include the seller and any measurable submasses \(q_i\) of the buyer types. Its worth is the highest valuation among the types it includes, and it dominates an allocation if the seller and every included buyer type can be strictly better off within that worth. The question is to characterize the union of stable sets in this game; a natural conjectured description would test whether the discrete paper’s tail constraints become \(u+\sum_{j\ge i}\mu_jp_j\le a_i\).

That is a concrete, plausible high multiplicity extension of the paper’s auction and bidder ring setting. Its weakest point is also substantial: allowing arbitrarily small submasses to form coalitions changes the dominance relation that Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.2 analyze. The proposed tail inequalities are a research conjecture, not a consequence of either theorem. I would present this as a new continuum stable set problem, not as a mirror of a named computational result in the paper, and I can’t responsibly assign it a tractability or hardness class from the paper’s evidence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that neither named result is a computational result. Theorem 3.1 characterizes stable sets as continuous monotone curves in the imputation space; Theorem 4.2 describes their union as a union of convex polytopes. Neither poses a decision, optimization, or complexity question. The word “continuous” in Theorem 3.1 refers to how payoffs vary with the seller’s payoff \(u\), not to a continuous population. Theorem 4.2’s polytopes likewise describe outcomes for a finite set of named buyers. These are substantial structural results, but under ChoCo’s scope they provide no computational anchor to continuize.

The strongest population scenario is still plausible: many bidders competing for one scarce item, perhaps with only a few valuation types. But it does not supply the missing computational target. One could invent a task such as testing membership in the union of stable sets or optimizing an objective over it. That would be a new computational problem built on the paper’s characterization, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper establishes.

The proposed atomless-buyer version also needs more than replacing counts with masses. If \(p_i\) is a per-buyer payoff for type \(i\), an arbitrarily small submass of that type can join the seller’s coalition, whose worth remains \(a_i\) regardless of the submass’s size. The resulting blocking relation is not the finite game’s pairwise relation, and the suggested tail inequalities alone do not characterize stability. Alternatively, in a limit of replicated finite auctions, total buyer compensation is bounded by the item’s value, so average per-buyer compensation vanishes as the number of bidders grows. One can define a richer continuum game with distributions of payoffs or altered coalition values, but that is a new cooperative-game model, not an algorithmic formulation supplied by either theorem.

I would not claim that high multiplicity is implausible here: repeated valuation types among many bidders make sense. Nor is the paper already “continuous” in ChoCo’s population sense. The negative case is narrower and stronger: a continuous stable-set characterization could be worthwhile cooperative-game theory, but this paper offers no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror.
