The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings and a seat count \(S\), compute the size-\(S\) committees selected by sequential RCV and STV, using a fixed tie-breaking rule and STV quota \(1/(S+1)\), and determine whether the committees are disjoint.

The model it lives in

Each complete ranking is a voter type carrying mass \(\mu_t\). Apply sequential RCV by repeatedly running IRV on the remaining candidates; apply STV with quota \(1/(S+1)\) and proportional fractional surplus transfers. The outputs are two committees, whose agreement or disjointness is the comparison of interest.

The objection that survived

The paper uses computation to compare outcomes but does not analyze the complexity of computing both outputs for an arbitrary input distribution, so that broader algorithmic framing is new.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2.4’s disjoint-winner construction and the comparison of the two rules on rational profiles; it leaves the simulation estimates and the Scottish and Utah empirical comparisons unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a rational mass version of Proposition 2.4’s construction retain disjoint winners under the continuous STV quota for every \(S\)?
  • What bit-complexity bounds govern computing both committees from a support of \(\tau\) rankings with rational masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The population mirror is plausible. Take a type to be a complete ranking \(t\) of the candidates, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of voters with that ranking. For a large municipal or ward election with many voters but a much smaller support of recurring rankings, run sequential RCV and STV directly on \(\mu\). Their outputs are two \(S\)-candidate committees; the comparison can ask whether they agree or are disjoint. STV’s fractional surplus transfers already fit this model, and its normalized quota tends to \(1/(S+1)\). The authors would likely recognize this as their election problem at population scale: they already represent ballots as a profile of types and counts, and their theoretical section studies large-electorate vote distributions.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Its sole numbered proposition, Proposition 2.4, proved here, says that for every \(S\ge 2\) with \(n=2S\), there exists an election where the STV and sequential RCV winner sets are disjoint. Normalizing that election’s ballot counts gives a rational continuous society with the same property. That is a useful structural fact, but it does not classify a problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized; the simulation and empirical findings are not numbered complexity results either.

So the honest positive case is that a continuous population model fits the paper unusually naturally. A corresponding question—given a rational society \(\mu\), compute both committees and determine whether they are disjoint—would be a sensible mirror, probably tractable by directly simulating the two rules with fixed tie-breaking conventions. But it is not anchored by a qualifying result in this paper, and Proposition 2.4’s existence claim alone does not establish a complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper contains no computational result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. Proposition 2.4 is a constructive existence claim: certain elections can give STV and sequential RCV disjoint winner sets. The other main results are simulation estimates and empirical comparisons. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

That matters for the proponent’s anchor. Normalizing the ballot counts in Proposition 2.4 gives masses \(\mu_t\) over rankings and preserves the constructed disagreement between the methods. But the proposition remains an existence theorem; it does not pose a search or optimization problem over continuous societies. Asking whether such a society exists is already answered by the construction. Asking for a minimum-support witness or a robustness measure would introduce a new objective, rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

The best broader proposal is to take rational \(\mu\), compute both committees, and test whether they disagree. That is a recognizable election question, and the population model fits it naturally. But in this paper, running the two procedures is a means of comparing their outcomes, not a studied computational problem. Making its algorithmic complexity the subject would turn the paper’s comparative, empirical analysis into a new algorithmic study. Likewise, an exact continuous calculation of disagreement rates under IAC would extend its simulations, but would not mirror a named computational result.

So the negative case is about the programme’s anchor requirement, not a bad fit between the paper’s profiles and population mass. That fit is strong: the authors already describe elections as ballot-type counts and analyze large electorates. If a newly formulated outcome-computation or probability-integration task counts as a worthwhile mirror, I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no such mirror exists. The case against is strongest only if ChoCo requires a computational result in the paper to anchor the mirror.
