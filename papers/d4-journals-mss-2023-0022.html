What the mirror covers

The proposed bankruptcy mirror does not cover a computational result; the paper's axiomatic characterizations and bargaining formula also provide no qualifying computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a possible new model: a large bankruptcy or compensation settlement could have many claimants but few complete claimant types, with \(\mu_t\) the share of each type. That makes a continuous-population NTU game plausible.

But this paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror to. Theorems 1–3 give axiomatic characterizations of the equal split-off set; Theorems 4–5 give formulas for bargaining and bankruptcy games. In particular, Theorem 5, proved here, says bankruptcy games are exact partition games and that their equal split-off allocation is \((\min\{\lambda_{E,c},c_i\})_{i\in N}\). It does not classify the complexity of computing that allocation. The paper also gives no encoding assumptions for \(E\) from which a complexity claim could follow.

The paper’s players remain finite; its real-valued payoffs and divisible resources are outcome-space features, not a continuous population. A mass-claimant version would need its own model of coalitions and split-off, and would be a research direction rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no anchor-specific continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This is a poor candidate for a ChoCo mirror because it has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–3 characterize the equal split-off set axiomatically; Theorems 4–5 give formulas for bargaining and bankruptcy games. None states a complexity result, gives an algorithm with a running-time claim, or specifies an input encoding for computation. The paper’s reference to a “computational algorithm” explains the solution’s inspiration; it does not make the paper’s results computational.

The strongest proposed mirror is Theorem 5 in a large compensation or bankruptcy setting with repeated claimant types. That is a plausible population regime. For types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\) and claim caps \(c_t\), a natural analogue would assign each type per-capita awards \(\min\{\lambda,c_t\}\), with \(\lambda\) chosen so the resulting allocation belongs to an aggregate feasible set \(E_\mu\). This is not defeated by the fact that claims become part of a type, or by allowing type-level averages.

But Theorem 5 permits an arbitrary compact, comprehensive, nonleveled feasible set \(E\); it says nothing about how that set is represented or how hard it is to find \(\lambda\). A computational question needs those choices. Taking \(E_\mu\) to be a polytope, an oracle, or a particular aggregate resource model would define a new problem whose complexity comes from that representation. Extending the theorem’s formula to masses could be worthwhile in its own right, but it would be new computational work inspired by the result, not a computational result of this paper carried over to a continuum.

The other possible anchors fare no better for this programme: Theorem 4 is another solution formula, while Theorems 1–3 concern axiomatic characterizations. One could continuize those axioms, or study the computation of the resulting solution, but the former is outside the programme’s computational scope and the latter again requires a newly specified input model.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: it supplies no computational anchor, and the best claimant-type scenario does not create one retroactively. I cannot honestly defend the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population project could grow from it. The bankruptcy setting is plausible, and an explicit aggregate model could support interesting complexity questions. The narrower conclusion is that those questions would be a new research direction, not a mirror of this paper’s results.
