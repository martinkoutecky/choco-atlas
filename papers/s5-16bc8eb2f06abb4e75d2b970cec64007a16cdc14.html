The continuous mirror question

Given an online stream of rational-mass trader cohorts with types \(t=(\sigma,v,a,d)\), where each cohort is revealed only upon arrival, sellers remain active throughout the buyer horizon, and total buyer mass is at most total seller mass, does there exist a deterministic direct mechanism running in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length that is feasible, individually rational, truthful for each representative trader, and guarantees welfare at least \(\operatorname{OPT}/2\) for every admissible stream? The mechanism may split a cohort by assigning a measurable subset of its members to trade; each individual receives either a whole unit or none, and receives a specified transfer.

The model it lives in

An online double auction over a distribution of trader types and mass, revealed in batches over time. The mechanism chooses measurable subsets of arriving buyer and patient-seller cohorts to match, with individual transfers; the objective is social welfare against the offline optimum over the full stream.

The objection that survived

The theorem proves truthfulness for individual traders, while a split cohort needs a pointwise allocation rule and transfers that preserve individual incentives; the proponent flags this gap but supplies no construction.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's \(2\)-competitive welfare guarantee and requires truthfulness as established by Theorem 2. It leaves the random-arrival reduction, Corollary 1, and Theorem 6's market decomposition aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the greedy allocation and critical-payment logic be defined over mass batches so that each representative trader has truthful incentives, including at ties and when a cohort is split?
  • Can that mechanism preserve the \(2\)-competitive welfare guarantee and run in time polynomial in the support size and rational input length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that when sellers are patient and total buyer demand is no greater than seller supply, the authors’ deterministic mechanism \(M_{\mathrm{greedy}}\) achieves at least half the offline optimum’s social welfare on every input. Theorem 2, also proved here, establishes that \(M_{\mathrm{greedy}}\) is truthful. The paper contains no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or similar; its relevant computational result is this online mechanism guarantee.

My lead mirror is Continuous Patient-Seller Mass Auction (CPMA-2). An instance has a finite catalogue of complete trader types \(t=(\sigma,v,a,d)\): side \(\sigma\in\{B,S\}\), valuation \(v\), and active interval \([a,d]\). Types with the same side, valuation, and interval are indistinguishable for the market’s purposes. Their populations are masses \(\mu_t\), given as binary rationals with total mass \(1\); \(\tau\) is the number of types with positive mass. Sellers are active throughout the buyer-arrival horizon, and total buyer mass is at most total seller mass. The full population is used to evaluate the result, but the online mechanism sees traders’ reports only as they arrive, so it cannot use unrevealed buyers to make earlier decisions.

The question for a prover is: given this type catalogue and its rational mass encoding, construct a deterministic direct online auction policy, with running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the encoding length \(L\), that is feasible, individually rational and truthful, and whose allocation has welfare at least \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)/2\) for every admissible arrival stream. At each arrival, the policy chooses how much buyer mass to match to eligible unmatched seller mass and sets transfers. A solution must specify the online allocation and payment rules and prove the guarantees. For matched mass \(x_{s,b}\), welfare is

\[ W(x)=\sum_{s,b}v_bx_{s,b} +\sum_s v_s\left(\mu_s-\sum_bx_{s,b}\right), \]

where the second term counts the value of sellers’ retained units. \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\) is the maximum feasible welfare when the full market is known in advance. Each individual still receives either a whole unit or none; fractional allocation means that only a fraction of a large, indistinguishable cohort trades. As in the paper, the question does not require budget balance.

A plausible regime is a high-volume exchange for standardized energy blocks: many households or generators submit buy or sell orders, seller offers stay live through a clearing window, and buyers arrive over time. With a price and settlement-time grid, \(n\) traders may fall into a much smaller number \(\tau\) of valuation-and-availability types. In the normalized limit, each trader contributes mass \(1/n\), while total supply and demand remain macroscopic. That scaling of per-capita supply is part of the mirror. It continuizes the population while retaining the paper’s strategic traders, online information constraint, matching decision, and welfare objective. I expect the authors would recognize this as their problem in a large-market regime.

I expect CPMA-2 to be tractable. The allocation rule has an evident aggregate version: process arriving buyer mass against the cheapest eligible seller mass, splitting a type’s mass at the matching boundary. With finite support, that part need not enumerate individuals. The paper’s truthfulness proof uses critical payments, so a support-polynomial aggregate implementation of those thresholds is a natural next step. The paper itself does not give that runtime bound for the compressed representation; establishing it is part of the continuous question, not a result to attribute to the authors.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s \(2\)-competitive guarantee, with Theorem 2’s truthfulness as a required companion property. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s separate random-arrival reduction or its results for other market assumptions.

The weakest point is the passage from finite-agent truthfulness to the mass model. In a limit, an individual has negligible effect on aggregate mass, while the finite mechanism’s prices depend on critical thresholds and boundary cases. A prover must show that a representative trader still faces meaningful incentive constraints and that the mass-level payment rule preserves them, including at ties and when a cohort is split. The welfare part looks like a direct aggregate extension; truthfulness is the step that could defeat the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 3: a truthful, \(2\)-competitive online auction under patient sellers and demand no greater than supply. Its guarantee is a named algorithmic result, so the objection cannot be that the paper has nothing computational to mirror. But CPMA-2 has not yet specified a consistent online problem: if its input \(\mu\) includes future buyer masses and arrival times, the mechanism has information the paper’s online mechanism lacks; if those data are hidden, \(\mu\) alone does not describe the stream whose reports the mechanism processes. The theorem’s competitive guarantee is specifically about that adversarial sequence. ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.0198))

There is a deeper gap in the proposed mass model. The theorem’s truthfulness concerns individual traders whose reports can change their place in the matching and their critical payment. A mass-transfer rule specifies only how much of each type trades; it does not say which members of a partially matched cohort trade or what each pays. Keeping whole-unit allocations requires a selection convention, such as identities or a lottery. Alternatively, an atomless model can give each representative trader an allocation probability and payment, but then it must define individual incentives in that model; the aggregate welfare formula does not supply them. This makes the proponent’s claim that the welfare proof “evidently” aggregates too quick: the unresolved step is part of the result, not just implementation detail.

That is a real objection to CPMA-2 as stated, but it does not defeat the best mirror. A large exchange with many interchangeable orders arriving in batches on a finite time-and-price grid is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A mirror could model each batch as mass, preserve the online information boundary, and define individual allocation and payments explicitly. The paper gives no basis for ruling out such a model, and a clean proof of its truthful competitive guarantee could be worthwhile even if the aggregate greedy rule is simple.

So the honest negative case is weak. It shows that the proposed mirror needs a precise online input and an incentive model that connects mass allocations to individual traders. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
