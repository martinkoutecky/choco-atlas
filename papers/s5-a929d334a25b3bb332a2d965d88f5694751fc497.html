The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror on. Its numbered results are Propositions 1–3, proved here: for equilibria with one, two, or three candidates, respectively, every weakly undominated political equilibrium outcome can also be supported by an iteratively weakly undominated political equilibrium with the same candidate set and winset. These are results about equilibrium refinement, not about a problem being in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

The four-candidate example mentioned in the paper is attributed to De Sinopoli and Turrini (1999); it is also an equilibrium-refinement result, not a computational anchor. So I can’t honestly provide the requested anchor-based continuous problem for this paper. A voter-mass version of its model could be imagined, but the paper’s results do not establish a computational question for it, and an atomless population may change the pivotal-voter reasoning the paper relies on.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this is not a computational result waiting for a continuous version. Propositions 1–3 establish a game-theoretic equivalence between weakly undominated and iteratively weakly undominated political equilibrium outcomes for one, two, and three candidates. They give no computational problem, complexity bound, or algorithmic bottleneck to mirror. The four-candidate refinement result cited from De Sinopoli and Turrini is also a game-theoretic result, not a computational one. One could invent a problem about deciding or computing these equilibria, but that would be a new computational project, not the continuous mirror of a result in this note.

The more fundamental obstacle is that the propositions depend on individual voters being able to affect a plurality outcome. In an atomless electorate, a voter changing their ballot changes the aggregate vote distribution by zero. Their payoff is therefore unchanged by any unilateral ballot choice, even at a mass tie. No ballot strictly dominates another, and iterated deletion of weakly dominated ballots has nothing to delete. That removes the very distinction the paper studies: the refinement is defined on individual actions, and those actions have no effect in the proposed limit.

This reaches each proposition directly. Proposition 1’s sole voter has no meaningful choice once there is one candidate. Proposition 2 relies on finite voters sometimes being pivotal, which is why undominated voting is sincere in a two-candidate race. Proposition 3’s argument turns on integer vote counts, pivotality, and the threshold \(q_n\); none is an individual-level feature of an atomless electorate. The four-candidate boundary has the same dependence on finite vote arithmetic. The issue is not that the continuous version might give a different answer. It is that the individual refinement the paper analyzes loses its operative meaning.

A stronger proposed mirror might let a positive mass of one preference type switch ballots together, preserving consequential changes in vote totals. But that makes each type a coordinated bloc with a mass ballot. It changes the strategic unit from an individual citizen to a collective actor and replaces individual iterated dominance with a coalition or bloc refinement. That could define a different game, but it does not rescue these results as continuous mirrors of the paper.

The negative case is strongest against an exact atomless mirror. It cannot rule out a separate computational study of finite high-multiplicity elections, or of coordinated voting blocs. Those could be worthwhile questions, but this paper supplies neither a computational anchor nor a reason to treat bloc behavior as its problem.
