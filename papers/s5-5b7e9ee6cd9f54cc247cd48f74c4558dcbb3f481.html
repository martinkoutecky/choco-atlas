The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result that can serve as an anchor. Its “Results 1–3” are empirical findings: skipping-down by firms is associated with more unstable matchings; students tell the truth less often when firms skip down; and compromise strategies are more common than skipping-down strategies. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another complexity classification.

The paper uses deferred acceptance to calculate matchings, and cites theoretical properties of that mechanism, but it gives no numbered result about the complexity of computing those matchings or analyzing the observed strategies. So I can’t honestly state a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor* without inventing a computational result the paper does not contain. The experiment’s six-student, six-firm groups also do not themselves establish a high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case names no computational anchor, and the paper supplies none. Results 1–3 are experimental findings about reported preferences and blocking pairs in small, one-shot markets. The paper does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Deferred acceptance is used to calculate the matchings; its stability and strategy-proofness are established results cited from earlier work. There is therefore no paper result whose continuous version can be assessed as a complexity question.

The strongest candidate mirror would take distributions over student and firm preference types, scale capacities with population mass, and ask for a stable mass matching or the mass of agents involved in blocking pairs under different reporting policies. That is a coherent matching problem, but it does not mirror what this paper finds: its central findings concern how people choose reports and how those choices affect stability. A distribution over types does not explain those choices. Supplying observed response rates makes the task aggregation followed by deferred acceptance; deriving responses requires a new behavioral model. Optimizing or equilibrating reports instead would pose a new strategic problem, and would need assumptions that the paper neither states nor tests.

Cloning the six-by-six experiment into many identical markets preserves the experimental setup, but leaves a collection of independent finite experiments. Pooling agents into one mass market requires specifying how capacities, priorities, and strategic responses work among types; that can be worthwhile matching research, but it is an extension rather than a continuization of a computational result in this paper.

This is a strong case against a *mirror of this paper* under ChoCo’s computational scope, not proof that no continuous matching problem inspired by it could be worthwhile. The universal claim is weaker: a new computational model could be built, but the paper itself provides no named computational result for it to mirror.
