The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bi-valued symmetric multigraph template \(H=(V,E)\) of multiplicity \(q\), with edge weights \(\alpha>\beta\ge0\), consider a continuum of cells indexed by \(z\in[0,1]\). Each cell has one agent per vertex and one indivisible good per edge, valued by its endpoint agents as in \(H\). Does there exist a measurable rule orienting every cell’s goods so that, for almost every \(z\), the resulting allocation in that cell is EFX?

The model it lives in

Use the finite interaction template \(H\) as the input, with agents and indivisible goods repeated across a continuum of cells. Each role has mass \(1/|V|\); local incidence and edge weights are specified by \(H\), and the decision is existence of a measurable EFX orientation rule. The cell structure is part of the model because a vector of role masses alone does not encode which agents share each good.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 1 and 3 for repeated templates. It leaves Observation 2, the discussion of EFX allocations, and the open case \(\alpha\le q\beta\) outside its anchored results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can heterogeneous local interaction structures be given a finite succinct representation that preserves indivisible goods and supports a meaningful complexity analysis?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is a large population made from repeated local communities. Think of identical apartment blocks: each has the same roles for residents, the same pattern of shared goods, and the same utility for each good. The graph within a block records which residents can value which goods. The paper’s graph model already fits that story.

Formally, take a finite template multigraph \(H=(V,E)\), with symmetric edge weights \(\alpha>\beta\ge0\), and let \(\tau=|V|\). The continuous population is \(V\times[0,1]\): each role \(v\in V\) has mass \(1/\tau\), and \(z\in[0,1]\) indexes interchangeable copies of the community. Each edge \(e=vw\) gives an indivisible good \(e_z\) to the corresponding pair of residents \((v,z),(w,z)\), with weight \(w_e\) to both; a self-loop is valued only by its own role. Each resident values only goods incident to them, just as in the paper.

Replica-Cell EFX Orientation is the lead question. Its input is the template \(H\), its weights, and its copy structure. Is there a measurable rule assigning every good \(e_z\) wholly to one of its endpoints such that, for almost every \(z\), the residents in that community satisfy EFX? In other words, for each pair of distinct roles \(v,w\), and every good \(g\) assigned to \(w\) in that cell,
\[ u_{(v,z)}(B_{(v,z)}(z))\ \ge\ u_{(v,z)}(B_{(w,z)}(z)\setminus\{g\}). \]
A solution is such a rule: each good remains indivisible, and every individual receives an integral bundle.

The cell index preserves the paper’s incidence structure. The role members in different cells are interchangeable, and their goods are local, so cross-cell comparisons add no envy. A rule that satisfies EFX almost everywhere exists exactly when \(H\) has an EFX orientation: a finite orientation can be repeated in every cell, while any successful continuum rule supplies a successful finite orientation in some cell. For \(K\) copies this gives the exact clone identity
\[ H\text{ has an EFX orientation} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H^{\sqcup K}\text{ has an EFX orientation}. \]
Thus the population size can be \(K\tau\) with \(K\) arbitrarily large, while the number of types is \(\tau\).

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, for every fixed \(q\ge2\), deciding whether a bi-valued symmetric multigraph of multiplicity \(q\) has an EFX orientation is NP-complete, even when the graph is bipartite, \(\alpha>q\beta\), and the heavy edges of every heavy component induce a non-trivial odd multitree (NTOM). The paper proves this by reduction from CIRCUITSAT. The replica-cell version inherits the same NP-completeness under those restrictions: a finite graph produced by the reduction is a valid template, and its hard orientation choices remain inside each cell. I expect Class B, hardness transfers. The combinatorics live in the template’s graph structure and gadgets, not in the number of population copies; the continuous population does not make this particular hardness disappear.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 3, also proved in this paper. It states that if a bi-valued symmetric multigraph of any multiplicity \(q\ge1\) has no heavy component whose heavy edges induce an NTOM, then it has an EFX orientation that can be found in polynomial time. This gives the NTOM-Free Replica-Cell EFX Construction problem: take the same continuous template model, promise that its template has no such heavy component, and output a measurable EFX orientation rule. Run the paper’s polynomial-time construction on \(H\) and repeat that orientation in every cell. This is Class A on the promised family. It complements the lead hardness mirror with the structural boundary the paper identifies.

This is an extension with an exact rational-clone correspondence, rather than a new continuous optimization method. In particular, the type masses alone would not encode which agents share each good; the repeated interaction template is necessary to preserve the authors’ graph problem. The authors should recognize both questions as their EFX orientation problem on a high-multiplicity population: the agents, goods, values, and pointwise fairness test are all preserved. Per-capita goods scale with the copies, while the goods each person receives remain indivisible.

The mirror covers Theorems 1 and 3, not the paper’s discussion of EFX allocations or other fairness notions. A natural next question is whether the paper’s open case, \(\alpha\le q\beta\), becomes tractable in the replica-cell model; the exact correspondence means it is the same unresolved decision boundary here. A broader question is whether type-pair mass models can represent non-identical interaction networks while still enforcing individual, integral EFX.

The weakest point is that mass is passive: the continuum repeats a finite graph rather than producing a genuinely mass-sensitive LP or fluid algorithm. Also, the number of role types grows with the template, so hardness is not claimed with fixed \(\tau\). That makes this a modest mirror, but a faithful one: the program allows high-multiplicity extensions with per-capita resource scaling, and this construction preserves the paper’s problem exactly while exposing which part of its complexity survives replication.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper clears the easy objection: Theorem 1 is a named NP-completeness result, and Theorem 3 is a named polynomial-time result. The strongest objection is that the proposed continuum preserves each result by keeping the entire finite instance intact inside every cell.

For Theorem 1, the apartment-block construction is the best version of the mirror. But because goods stay within a cell and agents value only incident goods, cells do not interact. The measurable EFX rule exists exactly when the finite template \(H\) has an EFX orientation. That equivalence follows fiber by fiber: repeat a successful orientation everywhere, or take a successful cell from an almost-everywhere rule. The continuum adds no mass-level choice or optimization; the copy coordinate preserves the specific incidence relations that make the finite problem hard. The hardness lives in \(H\), while the continuum is a passive index over copies.

Theorem 3 does not provide a second, distinct mirror. It lifts the same finite construction in the same way: run the polynomial-time algorithm on \(H\), then repeat its output in each cell. The running time depends on the template, not on the population mass. These are valid continuous formulations, but their results are direct consequences of disconnected replication. That is a thin contribution for ChoCo’s computational programme: neither anchor produces a new type-mass problem or brings continuous optimization into play.

A more aggregated model runs into the feature that the cell construction preserves. Type masses alone do not specify which agents share each indivisible good; EFX depends on those incidence relations and on the bundles of particular neighbors. Retaining the relations requires extra graph structure, such as a pairing between copies. If goods instead become atomless, removing any one good has vanishing effect, so EFX tends toward envy-freeness. The two straightforward choices are therefore to retain atomic local goods and recover independent finite problems, or to smooth the goods and change the fairness condition.

There is a stronger escape route: formulate a continuum graphing with finite-degree interactions, atomic goods, and a measurable orientation rule. That could pose a nontrivial problem. But it requires an input model for the graphing and its incidence relations; with a finite graph template it returns to the paper’s discrete problem, while an infinite measurable structure calls for a different complexity framework. It is conceivable research, but this paper does not supply that framework or make it an evident continuization of its results.

So the negative case is strongest against these two anchors: their continuum is a repeated finite instance, not a new aggregate computational question. It is not airtight as a universal claim. A well-specified, finitely presented graphing could yield a worthwhile mirror, and I cannot rule that out from the paper.
