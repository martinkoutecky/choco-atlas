The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Its numbered results do not classify a problem as tractable or hard.

The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which says the ascending and descending crawlers choose the same allocation; Theorem 2, proved here, which says the crawler’s allocation is invariant across reference orders that make the profile single-peaked; and Theorem 3, proved here, which says that, for a finite society, the lottery from uniformly random endowments followed by the crawler equals the lottery from uniformly random priority orders followed by serial dictatorship. These are rule-equivalence results, not complexity results. The paper’s statement that the crawler is efficient, individually rational, and strategy-proof is unnumbered and attributed to Bade (2019); it does not assert a complexity bound either.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting nearby: a large housing exchange in which many households share the same single-peaked preferences and initial housing category. A mass-based assignment model for those households could be worth studying. But the paper’s theorems concern finite agents and individually distinct objects; extending them would require defining what the crawler and random priority mean for a continuum. That would be a new result, and these equivalence theorems cannot serve as its required computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 1 says the ascending and descending crawlers produce the same allocation; Theorem 2 says the crawler is invariant across reference orders; Theorem 3 equates two finite lotteries. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Asking for a mass version of any of them would pose a new question, not continuize a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 3 is the closest candidate, but its finite setup is doing essential work: it pairs the \(n!\) endowment profiles with the \(n!\) priority orders. A continuum has no uniform random permutation, and an i.i.d. priority order over an atomless population has no first agent from whom serial dictatorship can proceed. One could define a different random assignment mechanism or an aggregate transport problem, but that would not be this theorem’s computational mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 offer even less computational foothold: the crawler’s unit-by-unit removal process becomes a new mass-allocation procedure whose properties and complexity would need to be established from scratch.

I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous model exists in any scenario. A large housing exchange with repeated preference and housing categories is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; a mass assignment problem there could be useful. But it would be an independent computational project, not a mirror of the paper’s results. So the absolute negative claim is weak; the narrower case against treating this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor is strong.
