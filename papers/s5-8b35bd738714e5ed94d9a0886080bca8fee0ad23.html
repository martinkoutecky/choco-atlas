The case FOR (proponent)

I think there is a credible mirror for the paper’s main campaign problem in large, block-structured social networks. The paper already lets opinions vary continuously, but its society is still a finite list of named people. The mirror would make the population itself continuous and let a campaign target a share of an audience type.

A plausible setting is a platform with millions of users grouped into dozens or hundreds of audience cells. A type must describe everything relevant to the model: its members’ shared internal opinion \(s_t\), and their shared pattern of influence across the cells. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population in type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(q_{tu}\geq 0\) be the total influence a person of type \(t\) assigns to the expressed opinions of type \(u\). This is an exchangeable, block-network version of the paper’s Friedkin–Johnsen model: people within a type have the same internal opinion and the same aggregate exposure to every other type.

Call the resulting problem FJ Block-Campaign\(_\infty\). Given rational \(\mu_t,s_t,q_{tu}\) and a campaign budget \(\alpha\), choose targeted masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t=\alpha\). Targeted mass is fixed at expressed opinion \(1\). If \(z_t\) is the equilibrium opinion of untargeted members of type \(t\), the mean expressed opinion in type \(u\) is \(\bar z_u=(x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)z_u)/\mu_u\), and the equilibrium condition is \(z_t=(s_t+\sum_u q_{tu}\bar z_u)/(1+\sum_u q_{tu})\). The solution is an allocation \(x\) maximizing the population’s average equilibrium opinion, \(\sum_t[x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t]\). For each feasible \(x\), the equilibrium is uniquely determined. The question is exactly the campaign question—whom to persuade to express a positive opinion so as to improve the society’s equilibrium opinion—with the intervention and objective stated in population shares.

My anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that Campaign is NP-hard. In its decision form, the input is a social graph, internal opinions, a target budget \(k\), and a threshold \(\theta\); the question is whether a target set \(T\), with \(|T|\leq k\), achieves \(g(z\mid T)\geq\theta\). The proof reduces from Vertex Cover on Regular Graphs, a hardness result cited from elsewhere. In the constructed instance, every internal opinion is zero and the graph is regular; the threshold is chosen so that reaching it is equivalent to the target set being a vertex cover. The Campaign hardness itself is established here.

I expect FJ Block-Campaign\(_\infty\) to be tractable for fixed or moderate \(\tau\), at least in a parameterized or approximation sense. The decision variables are the \(\tau\) target masses, and for each allocation the opinion equilibrium is a linear system. That gives a plausible route through finite-dimensional rational optimization. It is not a proof: optimizing the resulting objective may be non-convex, and the running time’s dependence on \(\tau\) matters. The main follow-up is whether one can get an FPT or useful polynomial-time approximation algorithm in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and the desired accuracy. A second is whether discrete typed campaigns converge to this mass problem with a controlled additive error as cohort sizes grow. For unrestricted \(\tau\), hardness could persist.

The connection to the anchor is direct but not exact. The discrete theorem chooses individual vertices; this mirror chooses mass across exchangeable network types. It therefore asks whether the hardness survives after the target decision becomes fractional and within-type topology is summarized by aggregate exposure rates. That makes it a genuine test of whether the difficulty lies in the population’s integrality or in the network’s interaction structure.

The paper’s discussion of opinion dynamics and equilibrium gives this mirror useful footing: continuous-agent opinion models are familiar, while computational campaign design over such a society remains a distinct question. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in a block-network regime, since it preserves the Friedkin–Johnsen dynamics, the expressed-opinion intervention, and the aggregate campaign objective.

My weakest point is the block assumption. In the paper, an individual’s exact neighbors can make that person uniquely valuable; the type model erases those individual differences. If the authors regard those fine-grained positions as essential to Campaign, they may reject this as a changed problem. The case survives only for the substantial regime where large cohorts really do share the same internal opinion and aggregate exposure profile. That is a scenario-specific case, not a claim that every social network is high-multiplicity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.1’s hardness is about choosing positions in a particular network. A node’s value depends on how random walks through the whole graph reach it; sharing an internal opinion, degree, or demographic label does not make two nodes interchangeable. For an exact high multiplicity model, a type must preserve the relevant network role. In a generic sparse social graph, that can leave nearly one type per person, so the mass description brings little reduction.

The proposed block model is the strongest repair, but its exposure matrix \(q_{tu}\) replaces individual edges with type-level mixing. If users in a segment have different local connections, equal aggregate exposure does not preserve the paper’s campaign dynamics or which targets are valuable. That makes the model a meaningful campaign question in its own right, but a narrower one whose authors’ acceptance as a mirror is uncertain. This objection is strongest when the intended application is individual targeting in an observed, heterogeneous network; it is weaker when the network really does have exchangeable, heavily repeated blocks.

The limitation is decisive for the universal negative case: block networks can be a plausible regime, and their mass-targeting problem retains the Friedkin–Johnsen dynamics, the intervention, and the aggregate objective. So the paper has an explicit computational anchor, and I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror. The case against is that such a mirror may apply only to a narrow network regime—not that no scenario supports one.
