The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant computational-complexity anchor for this paper: it contains no named result classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 1 characterizes when a scoring method satisfies IIA; Proposition 1 characterizes equal-rank-difference domains; Theorem 2 identifies the rich Arrovian domains for Borda; and Theorem 3 characterizes rich domains where Borda is non-manipulable under every tie-breaking rule. These are domain and incentive characterizations, not complexity results.

There is a clean continuous-population *axiomatic* recasting: let types be complete rankings and \(\mu\) their population shares, and define each alternative’s Borda score by its expected score under \(\mu\). One could ask whether IIA holds across distributions, with Theorem 1’s equal-score-difference condition as the natural characterization to investigate. But that would continuize the population while asking an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. The manipulation results are an even weaker computational bridge: a single agent has zero mass in an atomless society, so preserving individual manipulation would make the direct continuum version vacuous; allowing positive-mass deviations changes the question to coalition manipulation.

So the paper may inspire a continuous-population extension, but it offers no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 characterize preference domains; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize when Borda has IIA or is strategy-proof under specified conditions. None classifies the complexity of a computational task.

A population distribution over ranking types is entirely sensible here: many voters can share a ballot type. But for the IIA results, the relevant object is the set of admissible rankings, not how much of the population holds each one. Replacing voter counts with shares would let us restate an axiomatic question over distributions, but the answer would depend only on the support of the distribution. Asking an algorithm to recognize the paper’s domain conditions would make a computational task, but the population masses would play no role; it would be domain recognition, not a computational question created by continuizing the population.

The manipulation results have a more basic mismatch. In an atomless society, one voter has zero mass and cannot change Borda scores, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Allowing a positive-mass group to deviate avoids that, but changes the question to coalition manipulation or bribery. One could study that as a new continuous computational problem, perhaps with costs for changing rankings. The paper’s results do not anchor it: they concern unilateral deviations, not coordinated mass transfers.

So the case against is strong on fit with ChoCo’s stated scope, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile adjacent project inspired by this paper. The high-multiplicity election setting is plausible; the obstacle is that the paper studies domain and incentive properties rather than computational problems on a population.
