The continuous mirror question

Given four atomless cohorts with rational masses μ_i>0 summing to 1, each with a distinct collective entitlement role and all sharing the same non-monotone continuous valuation v:[0,1]^2→[0,1], represented in the paper’s explicit computational model, find a contiguous partition of the cake and assign one interval A_i to each cohort so that v(A_i)≥v(A_j)-ε for every pair i,j. Determine the computational complexity as a function of the valuation representation and ε.

The model it lives in

A society supported on four cohort types with rational masses μ_i; all types share the same valuation v, while each cohort holds one collective entitlement to a connected interval. The decision variables are the three cuts and the assignment of the resulting intervals to the four cohorts; the objective is to find an ε-envy-free allocation.

The objection that survived

An atomless population of individual recipients cannot each receive a nontrivial connected piece, so this mirror applies only when cohorts hold shared collective entitlements.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8.3’s identical-valuation, non-monotone hardness result. It does not claim to cover the heterogeneous-agent communication lower bound, the identical-agent query lower bound, or the monotone algorithms.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a cohort version of the paper’s identical-agent hardness result. The anchor is Theorem 8.3, proved in this paper: *for four agents with identical non-monotone valuations, finding an \(\varepsilon\)-envy-free connected allocation is PPAD-complete.* Section 8.4 proves the hardness by reducing from End-of-Line; the paper attributes PPAD membership to prior work by Deng, Qi, and Saberi.

Call the mirror \(\textsc{Cohort-Connected-EF}_{\infty}^{(4,\mathrm{ident})}\). An instance has four standing communities, each an atomless cohort of mass \(1/4\). All residents share one complete type: the same continuous, non-monotone valuation \(v:[0,1]^2\to[0,1]\), represented in the same explicit computational model as in the paper. The cake is a contiguous public-use resource, such as a land or service region. The four communities receive exclusive collective entitlements to the four intervals of a partition; each resident values a community’s interval according to \(v\). A solution consists of cuts \(0=x_0\le x_1\le x_2\le x_3\le x_4=1\) and an assignment of those intervals to the four communities, such that for every pair \(i,j\),
\[ v(A_i)\ge v(A_j)-\varepsilon. \]
Here \(A_i\) is the interval assigned to community \(i\). The mass specifies the size of each constituency, while the common valuation and collective entitlement specify what its members receive. This is a population model with four complete claimant types and many indistinguishable residents per type; the four types have identical valuation components.

The authors could recognize this as their problem at the level of four claimants: the same four-way connected partition, the same valuation comparisons, and the same non-monotone preference structure remain. The change is to represent each claimant as a large, homogeneous constituency with a shared public entitlement. For equal cohort masses, the envy inequalities are unchanged, so an \(\varepsilon\)-solution here is exactly an \(\varepsilon\)-solution to the paper’s four-agent problem. The expected answer is therefore hardness transfers (Class B): the paper’s PPAD-hard instances carry over directly. The hardness comes from the valuation geometry, not from population multiplicity; this mirror does not claim that making the population continuous makes the problem easier.

The regime would be four large communities allocating a contiguous shared resource, with residents within each community having the same valuation type. It is more convincing when the entitlement is genuinely communal—access to a public-use region, for instance—than when it is meant to represent four ordinary individuals. This mirrors Theorem 8.3 and its identical-valuation hardness only. It does not claim to cover the communication lower bound for heterogeneous agents or the monotone algorithm.

The weakness is that the population lift is modest: equal masses do not change the inequalities, and collective public access is a co-modification of ordinary individual cake cutting. A referee might accept it as a sensible high-multiplicity claimant model, but might instead say the paper’s four-agent problem has only been relabeled. The next substantive questions would be whether the mirror remains hard with unequal cohort masses or differing valuation types, and whether an individual-level model—where residents receive private connected pieces rather than shared community entitlements—admits a useful continuous formulation at all.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 8.3 is a genuine computational result, so the “no result to mirror” objection does not apply. The problem with the proposed mirror is that its population does no work. For four community claimants with masses \(p_i>0\), the feasibility conditions remain \(v(A_i)\ge v(A_j)-\varepsilon\); they do not depend on the \(p_i\). Changing the cohort sizes leaves exactly the same four-recipient problem and the same input. The hardness carrying over is not itself an objection. The point is that the mass distribution has not become part of the computational model.

There is a stronger alternative: treat many residents as individual recipients, each entitled to a connected interval of the shared cake. But that does not yield an atomless population version of Theorem 8.3. Disjoint positive-length intervals are at most countable, so an atomless population cannot give almost every resident a nontrivial connected piece. Giving residents shared communal intervals restores the four-claimant construction; using lotteries or disconnected bundles changes the problem’s allocation model. A population-scaled supply would also require a new per-capita utility and feasibility model.

The paper’s appendix does give a real foothold for large finite populations: it reduces the problem from \(n\) agents to \(n+1\), including for identical valuations in the computational setting. That supports studying homogeneous, high-multiplicity *finite* cake cutting. It does not resolve how to preserve the theorem’s individual, connected-piece allocation when the population becomes a continuum.

So the strongest negative case is that the proposed cohort version is a relabeling, while the more substantive individual-level version loses the paper’s allocation structure at the atomless limit. This case is not airtight: public-resource claimants may be a convincing application, and the finite-\(n\) reduction is meaningful high-multiplicity evidence. It is strongest against calling that application a continuous-population mirror of Theorem 8.3, rather than against every useful extension of cake cutting.
