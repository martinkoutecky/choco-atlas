The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow. The paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or parameterized-hard. Its unnumbered remark about exhaustive partition search does not meet your anchor rule. The closest usable anchor is Theorem 3, proved by the authors: for additively separable, symmetric utility games, relaxing efficiency to \(\sum_{i\in S}\phi_i^S\le u(S)\) makes the Nash-stable core nonempty. Here \(\phi_i^S=u(i)+\sum_{j\in S}v(i,j)\), with symmetric pair values \(v(i,j)\), and \(\Delta(S)=u(S)-\sum_{i\in S}u(i)\). The proof formulates the search for those pair values as the LP maximizing \(\sum_{i<j}v(i,j)\), subject to \(\sum_{i<j,\ i,j\in S}v(i,j)\le\Delta(S)/2\) for every \(S\subseteq N\), and claims this LP is always feasible. Theorem 3 is an existence result with an LP construction, not a complexity classification; that makes it a borderline anchor under the strict rule.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation of project organizations drawing from a population with a modest number of skill and preference profiles. A type records everything relevant to utility and coalition choice. Millions of people might share a few dozen such types. Mass then measures each type’s fraction of the population, and coalition membership can split a type’s mass. This keeps the paper’s central ingredients—coalitions, transferable utility, individual deviations, and relaxed efficiency—while making the population continuous.

My lead mirror is Relaxed-Efficiency Nash Partition with a Continuum Population. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, solo utilities \(a_t\), a bound \(K\) on active coalitions, and a rational piecewise-affine coalition-value function \(U\) over mass vectors \(x\) with \(0\le x\le\mu\). The solution is a symmetric matrix \(V=(v_{ts})\) and at most \(K\) coalition profiles \(x^1,\ldots,x^q\) whose masses sum to \(\mu\). Type \(t\)’s payoff in a coalition with profile \(x\) is \(\phi_t(x)=a_t+\sum_{s\in T}v_{ts}x_s\). Require relaxed efficiency for every possible coalition profile, \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\phi_t(x)\le U(x)\), and Nash stability: every type present in a coalition \(x^j\) weakly prefers it to every other listed coalition and to being alone, so \(\phi_t(x^j)\ge\phi_t(x^\ell)\) for all \(\ell\) and \(\phi_t(x^j)\ge a_t\). The question is to find such a witness, or report that none exists.

For this specified model, I would expect tractability when the number of types and coalition slots are fixed: the constraints can be written as a finite-dimensional real-algebraic feasibility problem, including the universal efficiency condition over \(x\). Exact real-algebraic methods give a route in that bounded regime. The paper does not establish this result, and I would leave the dependence on growing \(|T|\) and \(K\) open. The next questions are whether the always-nonempty conclusion of Theorem 3 survives the continuum constraints, and whether a more efficient separation method works when the support grows.

The mirror covers only Theorem 3’s symmetric, additively separable case with relaxed efficiency. Its soft spot is substantial: the theorem works with finitely many agents and coalition values on subsets, while the mirror adds a mass-value function and permits fractional coalition profiles. Atomless deviations also leave coalition composition unchanged, which changes the stability calculation. Still, the result’s chosen subcase is already explicitly pairwise and symmetric, so the type-level interaction matrix is a direct high-multiplicity formulation of that structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is Theorem 3, but it does not assert the complexity of a computational problem. It proves nonemptiness for a restricted class of games by giving an LP that is always feasible. The other numbered results are also existence, characterization, or equivalence claims. The paper’s remark about exhaustive search over partitions is unnumbered and does not supply a named complexity result. Under the stated anchor rule, there is no qualifying result to mirror.

The LP in Theorem 3 does not reveal a hidden pricing problem either. In the paper’s finite characteristic-function model, the values \(u(S)\) are specified for coalitions \(S\subseteq N\). The LP has exponentially many constraints in \(n\), but those coalition values are already an exponentially large part of an explicit input. Its feasibility is also the theorem’s conclusion. To get a new complexity question, one must give the coalition values a compact representation, such as the type-level piecewise-affine \(U\) in the proposed mirror.

That model is coherent: a large population with a modest number of complete agent types is plausible, and type-level symmetric interactions give a sensible mass version of the theorem’s additive case. Atomless deviations do not by themselves defeat it; the stability condition can compare a type’s payoff across coalition profiles. But the proposed task adds a cap \(K\) on coalition profiles, a new representation of coalition values, and a computational search over both interactions and partitions. Fixed \(|T|\) and \(K\) may make the resulting real-algebraic feasibility problem tractable, but that would establish a result about this newly specified model, not mirror a computational result in the paper.

A better version could remove the coalition cap and use a succinct, type-level representation of utilities. That would still be a plausible research problem, and its complexity might be interesting. I cannot honestly rule it out as worthwhile: there are realistic high-multiplicity settings, and the paper’s LP offers a recognizable starting point. The strongest case against is therefore narrower than the requested universal claim: this paper does not provide a named computational result for the programme to continuize. If the programme accepts computational problems newly derived from existence proofs, the proposed mirror may survive that objection.
