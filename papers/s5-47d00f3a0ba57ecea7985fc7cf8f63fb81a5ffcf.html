The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) object categories with rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\) summing to \(1\), known rational masses of ordinal ranking types, and an explicit finite menu of consistent rational unit-sum utility vectors, the actual distribution \(\nu\) over complete ranking–utility types is hidden. A deterministic protocol precommits to one threshold \(q_{rj}\) for each acceptable ranking–object pair, observes the mass \(y_{r,z}\) of each ranking and threshold-response class, then returns mass flows \(x_{r,z,j}\) assigning each unit to at most one acceptable category within its supply. Require the allocation to be Pareto efficient under the ordinal rankings, and measure loss against the full-information welfare optimum over Pareto-efficient allocations for the true \(\nu\), in the worst case over distributions consistent with the known ranking masses and observed responses. What is the best achievable worst-case ratio as a function of \(m\), the finite type-menu size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\); in particular, is a polynomial-time \(O(m^{2/3})\) protocol possible, and does an \(\Omega(\sqrt{m})\) lower bound hold?

The model it lives in

A continuum of students represented by mass over ordinal rankings and hidden utility vectors from a known finite menu. Threshold answers reveal response-class masses; the decision variables are mass flows from observed ranking–response classes to object categories, with per-capita supplies and ordinal Pareto efficiency.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5 grows the object and ranking spaces with \(n\), so its \(\Omega(\sqrt{n})\) bound gives no evidence for an \(\Omega(\sqrt{m})\) mass lower bound; that needs a new construction that survives fractional allocation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s unit-sum, non-adaptive Pareto-optimal guarantee and Theorem 5’s one-query lower bound for the four matching notions; it leaves the adaptive and unit-range results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the best deterministic approximation guarantee as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?
  • Can a fractional mass-allocation construction establish an \(\Omega(\sqrt{m})\) lower bound, or can fractional allocation beat it?
  • Can the protocol and allocation be computed in polynomial time while guaranteeing ordinal Pareto efficiency?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Pareto-optimal welfare problem with one non-adaptive threshold query per agent–object pair. Theorem 5 is my lead anchor: the paper proves that for unit-sum valuations, every deterministic non-adaptive algorithm that asks at most one threshold query per pair and always returns a matching with any one of the four properties—Pareto optimal, rank-maximal, max-cardinality rank-maximal, or fair—has worst-case welfare loss \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\). Theorem 3 supplies the constructive counterpart: the paper proves that, for unit-sum valuations, its non-adaptive one-query-per-pair algorithm returns a Pareto-optimal matching with welfare loss \(O(n^{2/3})\). Both results are proved in this paper. Neither is an NP-hardness result; Theorem 5 is an approximation lower bound.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is annual school placement: many students choose among a fixed set of school–program categories, each with capacity proportional to the cohort. A type is a student’s complete ordinal ranking and unit-sum utility vector; mass is the fraction of the cohort with that complete type. The number of students can be far larger than the number of object categories or distinct types. The cardinal part is hidden from the planner, as in the paper, and threshold answers reveal information about it. This is a natural regime for asking whether cardinal information improves welfare while retaining Pareto optimality.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Continuous One-Query PO Welfare Approximation. An instance has \(m\) object categories with rational supply masses \(b_j\), \(\sum_j b_j=1\); a finite set of ordinal ranking types \(r\), with known rational masses \(a_r\), \(\sum_r a_r=1\); and a finite menu of possible rational unit-sum utility vectors consistent with each ranking. The actual mass distribution over complete types \((r,v)\) is hidden, though its ordinal marginal is known. Before seeing answers, a deterministic protocol sets one threshold \(q_{rj}\in[0,1]\) for each ordinal type–object pair. It observes the mass of each response class \(z\in\{0,1\}^m\), where \(z_j=1\) exactly when the person’s value for object \(j\) meets \(q_{rj}\). From those masses it must return a feasible mass allocation: each unit of mass receives at most one acceptable object, and no object category receives more than its supply. The allocation must be Pareto optimal, and the protocol’s objective is to minimize the worst-case ratio between the welfare of a full-information, Pareto-optimal allocation and its own welfare, over all hidden type distributions consistent with the observations. The allocation variables are masses assigned to objects; each unit still receives an integral object. Unmatched mass can be allowed, as in the paper’s partial matchings.

The continuous question corresponding to Theorem 3 is: can this problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m\), the number \(\tau\) of supported complete types, and the input bit length \(L\), with worst-case loss \(O(m^{2/3})\) or better? A solution is a concrete threshold rule and response-to-allocation algorithm, plus a proof of Pareto optimality, the approximation guarantee, and the running time. The \(m^{2/3}\) target is a proposed translation of the paper’s \(n^{2/3}\) bound, since its \(n\) counts both agents and objects; it is not a consequence of the theorem. I expect the allocation step to admit a flow or LP formulation, making a tractable answer plausible, though the welfare guarantee needs a new proof.

The corresponding question from Theorem 5 is whether an \(\Omega(\sqrt m)\) lower bound on the best approximation can be proved for this mass-allocation problem, or whether fractional mass allocation beats it. A proof of the lower bound must give a family of finite-support continuous instances and establish that every deterministic one-query protocol suffers the claimed loss; a counteranswer must provide a protocol with a better guarantee. I would not predict that the theorem’s \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) bound survives simply as population size grows. Its construction uses indivisible matchings and a growing collection of rankings and objects. In a continuum, cohorts can be split across objects, and replication itself is not a parameter of the mass allocation. The lower bound may still have a counterpart in \(m\) or \(\tau\), but it needs a construction that survives fractional allocation.

The authors should recognize these questions as mirrors of their problem: they retain threshold elicitation, cardinal welfare, and the requirement to output a Pareto-optimal assignment. Replacing unit-capacity objects by per-capita supplies and matchings by mass flows is the co-modification needed to make a large population meaningful; the objects and each individual’s assignment remain discrete. This covers the paper’s Pareto-optimal, unit-sum, non-adaptive results, not its other efficiency notions or unit-range case.

The weak point is the gap between the paper’s \(n\)-agent, \(n\)-object lower-bound construction and a regime with many agents but fixed \(m\) and \(\tau\). The theorem establishes a strong barrier for its discrete instances, but does not establish that the barrier survives high multiplicity. That limitation does not erase the mirror: the authors’ central question—how much welfare threshold information can recover while preserving Pareto optimality—remains intact, and the continuous version turns it into an explicit approximation and flow-optimization problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the weaker anchor for a continuous-population claim. Its \(n\) counts both agents and objects, and the lower-bound construction uses a growing supply of distinct objects \(h_{i+1}\), one for each block. Those blocks have different rankings; when complete types include utilities, the construction also has support growing with \(n\), with only a few agents per type. It is a carefully designed finite-instance lower bound, not a lower bound driven by many repeated agents.

In a high-multiplicity regime with a fixed set of object categories and per-capita supplies, the cohort size \(N\) disappears from the mass-allocation instance. Theorem 5’s \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) therefore has no direct population-size counterpart. A more defensible mirror would ask for a lower bound in the number of categories \(m\) or supported types \(\tau\), using fractional allocations. That is a legitimate new question, but the paper’s proof supplies no reason to expect its bound to survive: its block construction and individual matching choices would need a new fractional analogue. This defeats Theorem 5 as evidence for a worthwhile high-multiplicity mirror; it does not show that such a lower bound is impossible.

Theorem 3 has a more serious modelling mismatch. In this paper, cardinal utilities are hidden because eliciting them is the point. In ChoCo’s central model, a society is supplied as masses on *complete* types. Here a complete type must include the utility vector: utilities determine both threshold answers and welfare. Supplying those types and masses would reveal the cardinal information the queries are meant to obtain. Hiding them preserves the paper’s information problem, but changes the input to a known ordinal marginal plus an unknown distribution over utilities and observations of response-class masses. That is a coherent model, as the other analyst proposes, but it is a distributional elicitation problem rather than the usual continuous-society problem with known support and masses.

The scale also changes. With a fixed number \(m\) of object categories, growing the population to \(N\) does not reproduce Theorem 3’s \(O(n^{2/3})\) guarantee: its \(n\) is also the number of objects. The literal threshold scale \(n^{-1/3}\) then tends to zero as \(N\) grows, while the mass problem’s natural structural parameter is \(m\). Replacing \(n\) by \(m\) may yield an interesting bound, but it is a proposed new theorem, not a consequence of the paper’s result. The paper’s computation is standard maximum-weight matching; its principal resource is the number and adaptivity of private queries. A continuous version would chiefly study query protocols over hidden utility distributions, with a flow or LP allocation step. That is adjacent to the programme’s computational agenda, but it does not directly chart the complexity of a problem over a known continuous society.

The same scope concern applies to the stronger adaptive result, Theorem 2: its guarantee is about how many questions must be asked to recover cardinal information. The population limit does not by itself simplify that information constraint, and aggregating answers by type requires the hidden utility types to be known or inferred.

So the strongest negative case is that neither anchor currently establishes the programme’s intended kind of mirror: Theorem 5’s hard instances grow the object and type spaces rather than the multiplicity of a fixed society, while Theorem 3’s central resource is elicitation from hidden cardinal data. The case has a clear limit. The proposed hidden-distribution, mass-response formulation is coherent and preserves the paper’s welfare, threshold, and Pareto-optimality goals. A broader programme on continuous query complexity could reasonably pursue it. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; I can argue that these results are weak anchors for ChoCo’s continuous-complexity programme.
