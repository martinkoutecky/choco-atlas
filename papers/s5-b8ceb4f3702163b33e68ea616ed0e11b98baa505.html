The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed solved exchange network and a finite set of complete contract types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), scale each type’s capacities by its mass and define coalition value \(v(z)\) from the resulting network-flow problem for \(0\le z\le\mu\). Can one compute the continuous egalitarian allocation of per-person payments \(p_t\), subject to \(\sum_t z_t p_t\ge v(z)\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\) equal to available profit, by a maximum-flow characterization minimizing deviation in per-person payments, in time polynomial in network size, \(|T|\), and input bit length?

The model it lives in

A fixed profit-sharing network with complete contract types \(t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and capacities scaled with mass. Flow variables determine each type’s total payment \(q_t\); the per-person payment is \(p_t=q_t/\mu_t\), and the objective is the mass-weighted leximin distribution of those payments subject to the coalition constraints.

The objection that survived

After aggregation, the main input remains the exchange network, so this may be a weighted fairness extension without a distinct pricing problem or a new population-driven complexity phenomenon.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers computing the egalitarian allocation after an exchange solution is fixed, through the almost-equal-flow characterization and iterative algorithm; leaves winner selection and the whole-instance empty-core issue aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass-scaled network preserve the flow-to-core correspondence for coalitions containing arbitrary fractions of each type?
  • Does minimizing the deviation in per-person payments characterize the continuous egalitarian allocation and admit a cohort-compressed algorithm with a polynomial bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an egalitarian profit-sharing problem for a large, homogeneous population of contracts in a combinatorial exchange. A type specifies everything the paper’s game uses about a contract: its position in the exchange network, time profile, bid or ask price, capacities, and any other relevant terms. In a plausible regime, many households or small energy providers submit one of a relatively small number of standardized contract profiles. The type masses \(\mu_t\) sum to \(1\); the population may be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles and the exchange network remain modest. Each participant receives a per-unit payment \(p_t\), while the total payment to type \(t\) is \(\mu_t p_t\). Exchange capacities and available profit scale with the cohort mass.

This changes the population into a continuum. The paper already uses divisible energy flows, but that is outcome-space continuity; it is not the continuization at issue here. The mirror keeps the paper’s fixed exchange solution and its profit-sharing construction, extending participation in a coalition to any mass vector \(z\in[0,\mu]\). Given that network and its induced coalition value \(v(z)\), a continuous core allocation is a payment vector \(p\) with \(p_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\) equal to the available profit, and \(\sum_t z_t p_t\ge v(z)\) for every coalition mass vector \(z\). The continuous EA is the lexicographically maximal sorted distribution of per-person payments over the unit mass of society. A solution is that type-level payment vector, together with a flow realizing it.

Theorem 3.3 (Characterization as AEMF) is the lead anchor, proved in this paper. Its full assertion is: “The almost equal flow with the smallest deviation value corresponds to the EA.” The continuous question is whether, in the mass-scaled profit-sharing network, the continuous EA is likewise the maximum-flow payment allocation with the smallest deviation in per-person payments. The relevant comparison is between \(p_t\), not aggregate payments \(\mu_t p_t\): a large cohort’s larger total payment does not mean its members are less equal. I expect this question to be tractable, Class A, if the type-level construction preserves the paper’s flow/core correspondence and its almost-equal characterization.

Lemma 3.2, also proved here, supplies the running-time piece: for a profit-sharing graph with \(n\) nodes and \(m\) edges, the described algorithm runs in \(O(m\cdot T_{EMF}(n,m,1))\), where \(T_{EMF}(n,m,1)\) is the time for an equal maximum-flow computation with one homologous edge set. Its continuous mirror asks for the same iterative computation on the cohort-compressed graph, fixing cohorts as their per-person payment rates become fixed. The paper cites, rather than proves, that constant AEMFP has a strongly polynomial algorithm (Haese et al., 2020); combined with this lemma and Theorem 3.3, that is the basis for the paper’s polynomial-time EA claim. I expect the mass version to be in P in the network size, \(\tau\), and input bit length, though establishing that the normalization by cohort mass preserves the cited algorithm’s guarantees is part of the question.

The anchors support one focused scope: computing the EA for a given exchange solution. They do not cover selecting the winning bids or resolving the paper’s observation that the whole-instance flow game can have an empty core. The authors should recognize this mirror: it retains their player roles, fixed exchange solution, coalition rationality, and egalitarian objective. Aggregating indistinguishable contract holders by mass is exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation, not a change in who matters to the game.

The weakest point is that the paper proves these claims for a finite set of players, not for an atomless population with partial-mass coalitions. Its use of continuous flows makes the extension look natural, but does not establish it. A prover would need to show that the fractional coalition-value extension, type aggregation, and weighted lexicographic EA still agree with the cohort-level almost-equal maximum flow. That is a real theorem to prove. If it holds, the result is a convincing continuous mirror; if it fails, the failure would locate precisely where this paper’s finite-player egalitarian characterization stops extending.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a convincing universal case against a continuous mirror here. The paper does have computational results: Lemma 3.2 gives a running time for an algorithm, and Theorem 3.3 connects its output to the egalitarian allocation. That rules out the cleanest objection, that there is no computational result to mirror.

There is a real gap in the proponent’s version of Theorem 3.3. The paper compares flows on player-indexed edges; with type mass \(\mu_t\), fairness should compare per-person payments, such as \(q_t/\mu_t\), where \(q_t\) is the type’s total payment. The cited equal-flow algorithm does not establish a weighted, per-capita version. Nor does the finite-player proof establish that minimizing a payment range still gives the lexicographically maximal allocation when types have unequal masses and coalitions can take any fraction of each type. Lemma 3.2 has the same limitation: its running time is in the graph’s edges, and it does not prove that cohort compression preserves the algorithm’s guarantees. Those are genuine missing results.

But they do not defeat the best mirror. A plausible version has many households or providers holding identical contracts, with each type specifying its full network role and terms. Scale the relevant capacities with type mass, define coalition value by the resulting network-flow problem, and seek a leximin allocation of per-person payments. Equal type masses already give a particularly clean case; unequal masses motivate the weighted variant. This is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s flow construction gives a natural starting point. The absence of a ready-made theorem is the research question, not a reason to dismiss it.

The strongest negative judgment is about fit and likely scope: after identical contracts are aggregated, the main input is still the exchange network and its capacities. The mirror may amount to a weighted fairness extension of flow algorithms, with no separate complexity dimension for population size or a pricing problem over a huge type space. That could make it a modest ChoCo contribution, focused on the boundary between finite-player and mass-weighted fairness, rather than a strong example of the programme’s broader complexity landscape.

That is not enough to support “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario.” The anchors are not defeated: the proposed per-capita theorem and cohort-compressed algorithm remain open, plausible questions. The honest negative case is weak, chiefly because a natural, complete-type scenario survives it.
