The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a mirror of the paper’s mechanism-design lower bound, Theorem 3. Proved in this paper, it says that no strategyproof and anonymous mechanism can achieve a bounded approximation ratio for social cost, even when there are only two agents and two facilities. The bound concerns the ratio over facility environments and reported positions; it is an approximation impossibility, not an NP-hardness result.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large population in two residential corridors served by two fixed facilities. Each agent’s type is their location; agents of the same type have identical connection costs and face the same facility charges. Let the society be \(\mu=p\delta_{x_1}+(1-p)\delta_{x_2}\), with distinct locations and rational \(p\in(0,1)\). The population can grow while the support stays at two types. To preserve the paper’s per-agent cost-sharing economics as the population grows, interpret \(b_j\) as per-capita building supply: at population size \(N\), facility \(j\)’s total building cost is \(N b_j\). If fraction \(q_j\) uses it, each user pays \(b_j/q_j\), and its contribution to average social cost is \(b_j\).

Call the question Two-Atom Continuous FAG-FCS Mechanism Approximation. An instance is \(\mu\) and a two-facility environment \(e=((b_1,b_2),(\ell_1,\ell_2))\), with positive rational costs and rational facility positions. A deterministic anonymous direct mechanism is a family of rules \(g_{\mu,e}:\mathbb R\to\{1,2\}\): an agent reporting \(r\) is assigned to \(g_{\mu,e}(r)\), and the same report receives the same assignment regardless of identity. The induced load is
\[ q_j=\mu(\{x:g_{\mu,e}(x)=j\}). \]
Because one agent has zero mass in the continuum, a unilateral misreport leaves these loads unchanged. Strategyproofness therefore requires, for every supported true type \(x\) and every report \(r\),
\[ |x-\ell_{g_{\mu,e}(x)}|+\frac{b_{g_{\mu,e}(x)}}{q_{g_{\mu,e}(x)}} \le |x-\ell_{g_{\mu,e}(r)}|+\frac{b_{g_{\mu,e}(r)}}{q_{g_{\mu,e}(r)}}. \]
Use infinite cost when \(q_j=0\), the limit of a sole user bearing the facility’s scaled cost.

The mechanism’s average social cost is
\[ \mathrm{SC}(\mu,g)= \sum_{x\in\{x_1,x_2\}}\mu_x|x-\ell_{g_{\mu,e}(x)}| +\sum_{j:q_j>0}b_j. \]
The benchmark is the minimum of this objective over assignments of the two types to facilities. The question is whether there is a mechanism family and a finite constant \(C\), independent of \(p\), locations, and facility parameters, whose ratio to this benchmark is always at most \(C\). A solution would be either such a mechanism with a proof, or a proof that every strategyproof anonymous family has unbounded ratio.

I expect the lower bound to survive as a continuum-specific impossibility: truthful assignment must respect each type’s connection cost plus its endogenous share of fixed costs, while the social optimum trades those costs against opening another facility. The paper’s theorem does not establish that prediction. In particular, its finite two-agent proof cannot simply be carried over: a finite agent changes the facility loads by moving, while an infinitesimal one does not. In ChoCo’s trichotomy, this is closest to Class C, but technically the predicted result is an approximation impossibility, not a complexity-hardness classification.

This mirror covers only the mechanism-design result and Theorem 3’s social-cost guarantee; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s PNE algorithm or its \(\ln n\) bound. Its weakest point is the changed incentive constraint, especially the infinite cost of deviating to an empty facility. That change is exactly what makes the population atomless, but it gives a referee a real reason to question whether the resulting mechanism problem remains close enough to the authors’ question. The natural follow-ups are whether a bounded ratio becomes possible when each type has mass bounded away from zero, and how the answer changes when agents have positive atomic mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is the paper’s weakest route into ChoCo. Theorem 3 is a named, quantitative result, but it bounds the social-cost ratio of strategyproof mechanisms; it does not give a complexity result or an algorithmic guarantee. More fundamentally, its two-agent incentives do not pass to the proposed nonatomic model.

With two finite agents, a deviation changes facility loads, and the mechanism can respond to the full reported profile. In the proposed continuum, one person’s report leaves \(q_j\) unchanged. Truthfulness therefore becomes a price-taking condition: each supported type must prefer its assigned facility at the shares induced by the population as a whole. That is an equilibrium-selection or mechanism-quality question, not the finite theorem’s load-responsive incentive problem. Treating a whole type’s mass as the deviator would restore load changes, but would replace unilateral strategyproofness with a coalition-level condition.

Scaling building costs with population size is a sensible way to keep per-capita costs nonzero, but it does not restore the missing individual effect. The resulting mirror may still be an interesting question. Its strategyproof outcomes must respect types’ preferences given fixed facility shares, and one could ask whether a mechanism selecting among those outcomes has bounded ratio. But Theorem 3 supplies no answer: its lower bound cannot simply be carried over. Moreover, because an unused facility has infinite deviation cost in this formulation, assigning everyone to one facility is always strategyproof. That makes the continuous mechanism question qualitatively different, even if its approximation ratio remains worth investigating.

The paper’s exact PNE algorithm is a better potential anchor, but its direct continuum version also degenerates. With costs \(b_j/q_j\), any all-to-one assignment is a Wardrop equilibrium: deviating to an empty facility costs infinity. So computing *some* equilibrium is immediate. The finite potential does not transfer cleanly either: its building-cost term becomes \(\int_0^{q_j} b_j/u\,du\), which diverges for every used facility. The paper’s dynamic program and its \(\ln n\) quality guarantee therefore do not provide a ready-made continuous computation problem.

A more ambitious repair would ask for the minimum-social-cost Wardrop equilibrium, or for an efficient algorithm that finds an equilibrium with a good social-cost guarantee on rational finite-support societies. That would connect to the paper’s equilibrium-quality result, but it changes “compute a PNE” into selecting a good one from the continuum’s equilibrium set. It may be worthwhile; I cannot honestly rule it out.

So the negative case is strongest against the proponent’s Theorem 3 mirror: its finite-player incentive effect disappears in the proposed model, and its surviving question is not a complexity result. The universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror is much weaker. The equilibrium-quality variant is a plausible counterexample, and dismissing it would require more than the degeneracy of finding an arbitrary continuum equilibrium.
