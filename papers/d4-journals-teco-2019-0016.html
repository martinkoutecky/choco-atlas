The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor, so a fully compliant positive case cannot be made.

Its numbered results are structural or axiomatic: Theorem 4.1 characterizes weakly separable rules via non-crossing monotonicity; Theorem 4.3 relates prefix monotonicity to decomposability; Theorem 4.5 characterizes representation-focused rules; Theorem 4.7 characterizes separable rules via committee-enlargement monotonicity; and Propositions 3.1–3.6 and Corollary 3.8 establish class containments and separations. None asserts that a computational problem is in \( \mathrm{P} \), NP-hard, FPT, W[1]-hard, or has an approximation guarantee.

The prose does mention that weakly separable rules are polynomial-time computable and that representation-focused, OWA-based, and many top-\(k\)-counting rules are NP-hard, but these are literature summaries, not named computational results proved by this paper. The apparent exception, Proposition 5.1, is enclosed in \iffalse...\fi; it is not part of the printed paper and should not be used as an anchor.

There is nevertheless a plausible continuous question associated with the paper’s subject matter. Let \(C\) be \(m\) candidates, let \(k\) be the committee size, let \(T\) be a finite set of complete rankings, and let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over \(T\). For a committee scoring family \(f\), define

\[ F_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_t(W)). \]

The problem is: given \((C,k,T,\mu,f)\), output a size-\(k\) committee maximizing \(F_\mu(W)\), or an approximation meeting a specified ratio. This is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s committee-selection problem. A natural regime is a large customer population for the paper’s T-shirt-store example: many customers share a relatively small number of preference types, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of customers of type \(t\).

For weakly separable rules, this continuous problem is tractable by aggregating each candidate’s expected single-winner score and selecting the \(k\) largest values. For representation-focused or OWA-based rules such as Chamberlin–Courant, one would expect hardness to survive, because the discrete election with counts \(n_t\) embeds as \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). That would give a sensible Class A/B research programme, and it is not a novelty collision: the paper studies finite voter collections, not societies given as distributions.

The weakness is decisive for the requested evaluation: this question is not anchored to a named computational result of the paper. The paper is therefore a plausible source of a continuous mirror in substance, but not a compliant positive case under the programme’s anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong, and it rests on a decisive scope point: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor.

Its numbered contributions are axiomatic or structural. Theorem 4.1 characterizes weak separability by non-crossing monotonicity; Theorem 4.3 connects prefix monotonicity with decomposability; Theorem 4.5 characterizes representation-focused rules; Theorem 4.7 characterizes separable rules by committee-enlargement monotonicity. Propositions 3.1–3.6 and Corollary 3.8 establish containments and separations between classes. None gives a complexity classification, an algorithm, a parameterized result, or an approximation guarantee.

The computational claims in the introduction and related-work section are literature summaries. They cannot serve as results to mirror. Proposition 5.1 is even weaker as an anchor: it appears only inside \iffalse...\fi, so it is not part of the paper. Thus the proposed question
\[ F_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_t(W)) \]
is a legitimate high-multiplicity election problem, but it is not a continuous reformulation of a computational theorem proved here. It is a new computational study of committee scoring rules, largely importing questions from the cited literature.

The best possible rescue would be to mirror the paper’s main axiomatic theorems rather than its absent algorithms. That also fails to produce a worthwhile population continuization. Every committee-scoring rule already aggregates voters additively. Replacing a finite profile by rational masses merely replaces
\[ \sum_{v\in V} f(\operatorname{pos}_v(W)) \]
with a weighted average over ranking types. All the axioms in the paper concern comparisons between these aggregate scores. Their proofs therefore survive by linearity or by approximation with rational profiles; the population simplex adds no new object that the theorems need. The substantive classification remains a statement about the committee-scoring function \(f_{m,k}\), not about population multiplicity.

The proposed computational variants do not repair this mismatch. For weakly separable rules, the distributional problem collapses to computing each candidate’s expected single-winner score and selecting the \(k\) largest. That is a sensible high-multiplicity calculation, but it contributes nothing to the paper’s axiomatic hierarchy. For Chamberlin–Courant, OWA-based rules, or general decomposable rules, the problem becomes an external winner-determination problem over weighted ranking types. It may be interesting, and the paper’s T-shirt example supplies a plausible story, but it is a new study of the computational literature the paper cites—not a mirror of any named result in this paper.

The usual population objections do not apply here: voters are anonymous, preferences are precisely the kind of repeated type that admits high multiplicity, and no individual identity is needed. Nor is the continuous question invalid merely because it is a normalized finite election. The issue is narrower and more damaging: after the population is continuized, the paper’s actual contribution has not been continuized into a computational question. One would have to add a new optimization problem, choose which rule family to study, and supply the missing complexity result oneself.

So the honest negative verdict is not that committee scoring elections lack sensible high-multiplicity variants. They plainly have them. It is that this paper offers no computational theorem for such a mirror to preserve, and its actual axiomatic content is already invariant under replacing voter counts by masses. Under the programme’s anchor requirement, there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
