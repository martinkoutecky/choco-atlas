The continuous mirror question

Given a GAI attribute structure, monotonicity conditions, fixed background constraints, a focal report \(P^\star\), and a society with rational type masses \(\mu_t\) over optional reports \(P_t\), find a subset \(Q\) of report types minimizing \(\sum_{t\in Q}\mu_t\) such that the background constraints, \(P^\star\), the monotonicity conditions, and every constraint \(T(P_t,\hat u)\ge 0\) for \(t\in Q\) are infeasible.

The model it lives in

Types are standardized reports that induce the same GAI constraint; \(\mu_t\) is their population mass. Binary decisions select whole report types, the objective minimizes selected mass, and infeasibility can be certified by Farkas multipliers.

The objection that survived

Feasibility depends on which report types have positive mass, so minimizing constituency mass adds an objective not intrinsic to the paper’s Farkas explanation; the proposal motivates it through consultation but does not fully establish why that weighting is the right explanation criterion.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 5’s inconsistency-explanation formulation; it leaves the representation result, repair-generation method, coalition reduction, and interpretation indices aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the complexity and parameterized complexity of finding a minimum-mass infeasible report set as the support size \(\tau\) varies?
  • How does the minimum-mass explanation relate to the paper’s minimum-cardinality explanation when identical reports are duplicated?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is Minimum-Mass GAI Conflict Explanation. Imagine a large public consultation about the same set of policy alternatives, evaluated on the same criteria. Each participant submits one standardized judgment, such as \(x \succeq y\) or \(x \unrhd \alpha\). A type is the complete report relevant to this diagnostic question, including its flexibility status; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants with report type \(t\). Many participants may share a report, so the population can be large while the observed support \(\tau\) is modest. The group is trying to fit one common GAI model to the consultation data.

My anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper. After a repair has been chosen, the authors take a rejected flexible constraint \(P^\star\) and a set of retained constraints \(P_C \cup P_{NF}\). They seek a smallest subset \(Q \subseteq P_C \cup P_{NF}\) such that no GAI parameter vector \(\hat u\) satisfies \(P^\star\), every constraint in \(Q\), and the monotonicity conditions (25)–(27). Proposition 5 gives a MILP for finding that explanation: it uses nonnegative multipliers for the learning constraints, multipliers for the monotonicity inequalities and equalities, coefficient-balance equations for every utility variable, and a negative combined constant as the Farkas infeasibility certificate. Binary variables mark which retained constraints have positive multipliers, and the MILP minimizes their sum; the resulting \(Q\) is the set with positive multipliers. This is a computational formulation, rather than a result placing the problem in a standard complexity class.

For the mirror, the instance consists of the GAI attribute domains, interaction structure, and monotonicity system; mandatory background constraints; a focal report \(P^\star\); and a finite support \(T\) of optional report types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. Each type \(t\) contributes its linear constraint \(T(P_t,\hat u)\ge 0\). The question is to find a set \(Q\subseteq T\) of minimum mass,
\[ \min \sum_{t\in Q}\mu_t, \]
such that the background constraints, \(P^\star\), the monotonicity system, and all constraints \(T(P_t,\hat u)\ge 0\) for \(t\in Q\) are infeasible. A solution is the minimum-mass set of report types, optionally accompanied by a Farkas certificate verifying infeasibility. If no such \(Q\) exists, the focal report has no conflict explanation in the available reports. The objective asks how much of the consultation population is represented by a contradictory explanation, rather than how many distinct constraints appear in it.

I think the authors could recognize this as their GAI inconsistency-explanation problem in a group-elicitation setting: the GAI model, learning constraints, and Farkas explanation are all retained. I would provisionally expect the mirror to be continuum-specifically hard when \(\tau\) is part of the input, because choosing a minimum-mass infeasible support remains a combinatorial selection problem. Proposition 5 does not prove hardness, though; a polynomial algorithm or a useful parameterized result in \(\tau\) would be open questions. A further question is how this weighted explanation relates to minimum-cardinality explanations when discrete reports are duplicated.

The weakest point is the population interpretation. The paper studies one decision maker’s data, not a shared model fitted to a mass of stakeholders. Also, selecting an entire report type as part of an explanation is a deliberate modeling choice: if an explanation could implicate an arbitrary fraction of people holding that report, any positive remainder would impose the same hard constraint, making “mass of an explanation” ill-defined. The mirror therefore fits best when explanations are intended to identify whole constituencies of respondents. It covers Proposition 5’s inconsistency-explanation result, not the paper’s representation results or its interpretation indices.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror has a real modeling gap: Proposition 5 explains a contradiction among constraints, and in the paper those constraints are elicitation data from one decision maker. Repeating the same judgment does not change whether the system is feasible or which constraints form an explanation. The paper gives no population quantity that needs to be preserved.

In the consultation scenario, if every report held by a positive-mass group is a hard constraint, the fitted model depends on which report types have positive mass, not on their masses. A type with mass \(0.01\) imposes the same constraint as one with mass \(0.8\). The proposed minimum-mass explanation makes the distribution matter by adding a new objective: pay for the population share represented by each selected constraint. That is a coherent weighted unsatisfiable-core problem, but the mass weighting does not follow from the paper’s Farkas explanation. It changes “which few premises explain the conflict?” into “what is the smallest constituency whose reports create a conflict?” That might be useful for consultation analysis, but it needs its own justification.

A stronger candidate would mirror the paper’s repair algorithm: find the minimum population mass of reports to discard so the remaining judgments fit one GAI model. This has a clearer population interpretation—how much consultation input must be set aside?—but it still depends on treating reports as hard constraints and deleting whole types. If arbitrary fractions of a type can be discarded, any positive remainder imposes the same constraint, so partial deletion does not help. Whole-type deletion avoids that collapse, but makes the result depend on a chosen cohort-level deletion cost. A soft-data model could make mass affect fitting continuously, but it would replace exact inconsistency and Farkas explanations with a loss or tolerance model absent from the paper.

There is also a substantive assumption in both versions: many stakeholders’ judgments are being fitted to one common utility function. That is plausible for some consultation settings, but heterogeneity may be the phenomenon of interest rather than noise to repair. The paper does not tell us which interpretation to use.

This is not an airtight negative case. Proposition 5 is a named computational formulation, even though it gives no complexity classification, and a weighted repair or explanation problem could be worthwhile in a carefully motivated group-elicitation setting. I can argue that the proponent’s mirror is underdetermined and adds a population-weighted objective that is not intrinsic to the paper. I cannot honestly conclude that every stronger version fails in every scenario.
