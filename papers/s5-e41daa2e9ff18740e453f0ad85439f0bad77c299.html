What the mirror covers

The candidate targets only Theorem 1's stabilization and separability-based aggregation claims; it does not turn the awareness-update characterizations into computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational complexity result in this paper that can serve as an eligible anchor. The nearest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here. Under minimal awareness updating, it says that for every full state \(s^*\), announcements eventually stabilize: some finite \(t_0\) satisfies \(y_t=y_{t_0}\) for all \(t\ge t_0\). If the security \(X\) is separable under the terminal structure \(U_{t_0}\), that agreement is on the true value, so information aggregates. The theorem also says that trader \(i\ne j\) raises her awareness after \(j\)'s announcement exactly when \(S^i_{t-1}(s^*)\wedge S^j_{t-1}(s^*)\prec S^i_t(s^*)\wedge S^j_{t-1}(s^*)\); and, if every \(S\succeq S^i_{t-1}(s^*)\) can rationalize the preceding public history, \(i\)'s update does not increase the join of her and \(j\)'s awareness. These are convergence and information-aggregation claims for a finite-trader model, not a stated classification such as membership in P: the theorem gives no running-time bound in an encoded input size. I would not call it a complexity result.

The best positive extension I can make is a continuous type-cohort MSR question. A plausible regime is a large prediction market in a firm or public institution, where many participants share a small number of informational roles and awareness profiles. A type would specify a participant’s complete information and awareness structure, update rule, and payoff-relevant characteristics; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the market with type \(t\), with rational masses summing to one and support size \(\tau\ll n\). Give the instance as explicit finite state spaces and projections, a rational common prior, a security \(X\), the supported types and their masses, an initial price, and a fixed order in which type cohorts announce. Each cohort submits its shared posterior expectation under the proper scoring rule and minimal awareness updating; its total score is its mass times its per-capita score.

Call the resulting problem Continuous Type-Cohort Aggregation: compute the first round \(R\) after which all cohort announcements are constant and their common value \(y^*\), then report whether \(y^*=X(s^*_{S_0})\) for every full state with positive prior probability. A solution is \(R\), \(y^*\), and that aggregation answer. The theorem makes tractability the plausible expectation for an explicit finite-state encoding, though the complexity would need to be established for a specified representation; compact encodings could raise different questions. Natural follow-ups are a tight bound in \(|\Sigma|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length, and whether meaningful mass-weighted participation changes the answer.

This is a narrow mirror of the information-aggregation part of Theorem 1 only. Its weakest point is that cohort announcements change the paper’s sequential mechanism: mass affects total scores, but the information dynamics run through one speaker per type. The authors may recognize the same updating and aggregation question, but the mirror does not preserve the paper’s individual turn-taking exactly. And even if accepted, it would be an analytical convergence problem rather than the computational-complexity result the ChoCo programme is looking for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor, and it is not a computational result: it proves eventual stabilization and, under separability, information aggregation in a finite epistemic model. It states no decision problem, algorithm, complexity bound, or optimization task. Asking an algorithm to compute the stabilization round would be a new computational question, rather than a mirror of a computational result in the paper. The theorem’s awareness-update claims are also structural statements, not complexity claims.

A large prediction market is a plausible setting for repeated trader types, but the proposed type-cohort version gives mass no real job. Each myopic trader maximizes an expected proper score, so multiplying that score by a cohort’s positive mass does not change its chosen announcement. With one speaker per type in a fixed order, the process depends on the supported types and their order, not their masses. It is therefore a finite-type process dressed with continuous weights.

The stronger repair—keep every trader’s turn and let type masses determine how often each type speaks—runs into the mechanism’s sequential structure. The order of announcements matters to the public history traders interpret. A distribution over information and awareness types does not specify a sequence of individual turns. Include turn positions in the type, and the proposed compression loses its point; replace individual turns with mass-weighted or simultaneous updates, and the objects that drive the theorem—individual posterior announcements and public events that rationalize each announcement—have changed. That could be a worthwhile model of its own, but Theorem 1 would no longer be its mirror.

So I would reject the proposed Continuous Type-Cohort Aggregation as a useful continuization of this paper’s result. The strongest objection is that the theorem concerns finite-agent information dynamics, while population mass either disappears from the exact mechanism or requires new dynamics. This is not an airtight case against every possible continuous-market model: a large-market information-aggregation project could be valuable. But it would be a new computational question in an adjacent market setting, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
