The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types that is realizable on a line, find a set \(S\) of at most two candidates such that, for every line placement of the candidates and every finite-support voter-location measure inducing \(\mu\), \(S\) contains a candidate minimizing \(\int \min_{c\in S'} |x-c|\,d\nu(x)\) over all single candidates \(S'=\{c\}\).

The model it lives in

The population is a finite-support measure \(\nu\) over complete voter metric types (locations on the line); candidate locations and voter locations remain hidden, as in the paper’s distortion model. The input exposes only the induced rational ranking mass \(\mu\), and the decision variable is a two-candidate shortlist \(S\) that must contain a single-candidate optimum for every compatible realization.

What the mirror covers

Covers the line-metric utilitarian exact two-candidate shortlisting result in Theorem 1. It leaves the plane-metric distortion lower bounds in Theorems 31–32 and the paper’s other sum-cost and max-cost committee guarantees unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the shortlist be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass encoding length \(L\), without expanding rational masses into individual voters?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a mirror of the paper’s ordinal metric committee selection problem: replace the finite ballot profile by a rational distribution over ballot types, while keeping the committee discrete and the metric hidden. One caveat up front: the paper proves no NP-hardness, polynomial-time classification, or parameterized-complexity result. Its best anchors are named algorithmic distortion bounds and impossibility results.

Here is a precise version. Let \(C\) be \(m\) candidates and let \(\mathcal T\) be the set of rankings over \(C\). The public profile is \(\mu\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}^{\mathcal T}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and support size \(\tau\). The actual population has mass over complete metric types—voter locations, which determine distances to every candidate—but the mechanism sees only the ranking distribution induced by that population. For a metric class \(X\), let \(\mathcal R_X(\mu)\) be all candidate placements and rational finite-support population measures consistent with \(\mu\).

For a committee \(S\), define sum cost as \(\int \min_{c\in S} d(x,c)\,d\nu(x)\), and max cost as \(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\sim\nu}\min_{c\in S}d(x,c)\). In either case, \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is the cost of the best single candidate. The continuous problem takes \((C,k,X,\mu)\) as input and asks for a committee \(S\), \(|S|\le k\), minimizing its worst-case ratio to \(\mathrm{OPT}\) over all realizations in \(\mathcal R_X(\mu)\). This preserves the paper’s information constraint: the choice uses rankings, while the costs come from an unknown compatible metric.

A plausible regime is a large electorate choosing among a modest number of service sites or representatives. For example, with \(N=qm\) residents, there could be \(m\) observed ranking types and \(q\) residents per type. The coordinates or true distances remain hidden, as in the paper. The type-support size is then \(\tau=m\), while \(N\) can be much larger. The mirror leaves the paper’s candidates, committee choices, and distortion benchmark intact; it changes voter multiplicities into masses.

Theorem 32 is my lead anchor (proved in this paper). It says that for max cost, with candidates and voters in the \(2\)-D Euclidean plane, any deterministic algorithm selecting at most \(k<m\) candidates has \(1\)-distortion at least \(3-\epsilon\), for every \(\epsilon>0\), relative to the optimal single candidate. Its continuous counterpart is Mass-Max Robust Committee Selection: given \(m\), \(k<m\), and rational ranking mass \(\mu\), return a committee minimizing worst-case max-cost distortion over compatible plane realizations. The theorem’s indistinguishable-realization construction gives one ranking distribution \(\mu\) on which every committee of size at most \(k\) has a realization with ratio at least \(3-\epsilon\). Replicate each voter \(q\) times: \(N=qm\), \(\tau=m\), and the ratio is unchanged. I expect the impossibility bound to transfer—Class B-style—because the discrete instance embeds directly. That is an approximation barrier, not an NP-hardness result. The continuous problem still raises a separate question: how hard is it to compute the pointwise best robust committee from \((m,k,\tau,L)\)?

Theorem 31 (also proved here) gives a second, distinct anchor. For sum cost in \(2\)-D Euclidean space, any deterministic algorithm choosing at most \(m-1\) candidates out of \(m\) has \(1\)-distortion at least \(1+\frac{2}{m-1}-\epsilon\), for every \(\epsilon>0\), relative to the optimal single candidate. Its mirror, Mass-Sum Robust \((m-1)\)-Committee Selection, takes \((C,\mu)\) and asks for the \((m-1)\)-candidate committee minimizing worst-case sum-cost distortion over compatible plane realizations. Again, the paper’s same-profile instances survive arbitrary equal replication, so the stated lower bound carries over. This gives a second test of whether changing population scale helps when the obstacle is hidden geometry rather than the number of voters. Further questions include the exact robust value for general \(k\) and how it changes with the metric space.

There is also a useful positive boundary. Theorem 1 (proved here) says that on a line, an algorithm for the \(2\)-committee utilitarian election can always select a committee containing an optimal single candidate, and hence achieve \(1\)-distortion. Its mirror, Line-Sum Exact Mass Shortlisting, takes a line-realizable rational ranking distribution \(\mu\) and asks for two candidates such that every compatible line realization has an optimal single candidate among them. I expect this version to be tractable via a weighted implementation of the paper’s order-recovery and median ideas. The theorem itself does not establish running time polynomial in the binary encoding length of the masses, so that bit-complexity claim needs proof.

These anchors cover metric-distortion committee selection with a single-candidate benchmark, for both sum and max costs, and show a useful contrast: a sharp plane-metric impossibility alongside an exact line-metric result. The authors should recognize the mirror because the alternatives, ordinal information, hidden metric, and optimization objective are the same; the population is the object being aggregated.

The weakest point is that the paper’s metric costs are hidden and individual voters with the same ranking may have different locations. To respect the programme’s complete-type standard, the underlying continuous society must be a distribution over full metric types; \(\mu\) is only the observable ranking marginal. That makes this a continuous partial-information model, not the canonical problem where the algorithm receives masses of fully specified types. A second limit is that the paper’s contribution is about distortion guarantees, not the computational-complexity landscape ChoCo most wants to chart. The mirror is still a credible one: it carries named results into a high-multiplicity regime and makes clear which impossibility bounds persist when population size is no longer the relevant scale.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that none is a complexity result. Theorem 1 proves that a deterministic rule can shortlist two candidates while including an optimum; Theorems 31 and 32 bound the distortion any deterministic rule can achieve. They say nothing about running time, computational hardness, or an algorithm for optimizing a committee on a supplied continuous society. A slow rule satisfies the existence claim in Theorem 1 just as well as a fast one. So carrying these conclusions over to masses would extend distortion analysis, not chart the computational landscape ChoCo targets.

For Theorems 31 and 32, replication preserves the constructed lower bounds: give each voter’s full metric type \(q\) copies, and the cost ratio stays the same. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity instance, and high-multiplicity work supports the regime. But the transferred conclusion remains an information-theoretic distortion barrier. The stronger proposal—given ranking masses, compute the committee minimizing worst-case distortion over compatible metric placements—is a real computational question. It is also a new robust optimization problem. The paper’s theorems do not address its running time or complexity; their lower bounds only certify bad instances.

That stronger proposal also exposes a type problem. A ranking does not determine a voter’s distances, and the costs in the objective depend on those distances. Treating ranking as the complete type loses information the objective uses. Treating location or the full distance vector as the type restores completeness, but the paper withholds that information from the mechanism. The proposal handles this by making the full-type population an uncertainty set and supplying only its ranking marginal. That is a coherent partial-information model, but it changes the object being computed from a society of known type masses to a robust choice over hidden societies. The authors might recognize it as a new metric-distortion problem; it is not a direct continuization of the paper’s stated results.

Theorem 1 is the best candidate for a more direct mirror. One could give a line election rational ranking masses and ask for a two-candidate shortlist in time polynomial in the masses’ bit length. A weighted version of the median and ordering arguments may yield such a result. But the theorem establishes existence, not a runtime bound, so this would require new analysis. It is a plausible high-multiplicity algorithmic question; it does not make the paper’s existing theorem a computational-complexity result.

I would therefore reject these anchors as evidence that this paper itself fills ChoCo’s computational gap. The case is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the weighted shortlisting problem remains credible, and the robust metric version is well-posed. I do not see an honest basis for claiming that every scenario for those questions fails.
