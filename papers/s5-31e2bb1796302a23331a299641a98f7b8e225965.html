The continuous mirror question

Given a finite FEN role template \(R\) with the paper’s preference ballots and within-cell dependency links, consider a nonatomic unit mass of cells, each containing one agent of every role in \(R\), with agents in other cells neutral. For each partition \(P\) of \(R\), let \(\lambda_P\) be the mass of cells assigned \(P\). Does there exist \(\lambda_P \ge 0\), with \(\sum_P \lambda_P=1\), such that every partition with \(\lambda_P>0\) is Nash-stable under the paper’s preferences?

The model it lives in

A continuum of exchangeable finite cells with a fixed within-cell FEN interaction template. The decision variable is the mass \(\lambda_P\) assigned to each role partition \(P\); stability is checked within each cell, and cross-cell members are neutral to a deviator.

The objection that survived

Role-type masses alone do not record which agents are linked as friends or enemies; the mirror must carry the local cell structure as additional input.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 21’s Nash-stability existence question. It leaves the axiomatic results, the other stability notions, and their verification or existence results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Nash-stable existence remain hard in a richer nonatomic model where local links vary across cells or cells can interact?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead I find credible is Replicated-FEN Nash-Stability. Imagine a large organization with many exchangeable local teams: each team has one position of each role, and its members know only a bounded number of other roles. A role is a complete type: agents in that role share the same friend/enemy relations and rankings over the local roles. This gives a genuine high-multiplicity regime: with \(q\) roles and \(K\) copies of the team, there are \(Kq\) agents but only \(q\) types; in the continuum limit, each role has mass \(1/q\). The names of agents differ across teams, but the preference structure is the same up to renaming.

The anchor is Theorem 21, proved in this paper. It states that, given a hedonic game with distance-based preferences, deciding whether a Nash-stable coalition structure exists is NP-complete. The authors prove this by reduction from Exact Cover by Three Sets: for an instance with \(n\) elements and \(m\) sets, they construct a game with \(2n+4m\) players. The reduction is adapted to their preference model and has dependency-graph degree at most \(6\); membership in NP follows from polynomial-time verification of a proposed structure. This is a hardness result established here, not merely cited.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance is a finite role set \(R\), with the friend/enemy partition and ordinal ballots of a FEN distance-based hedonic game on \(R\). The society is a continuum of copies of this local role system. Each cell has one agent per role; an agent’s known neighbors are the corresponding roles in that cell, and agents in other cells are neutral. Let \(q=|R|\). Normalize the population so each role type has mass \(1/q\). A cell’s decision is to partition its roles into coalitions. Equivalently, let \(\lambda_P\) be the mass of cells assigned role-partition \(P\), for each partition \(P\) of \(R\). Find nonnegative \(\lambda_P\) summing to \(1\), with every partition assigned positive mass Nash-stable: no role in any block prefers leaving it for another block or for the singleton outcome. Ask whether such a mass assignment exists. This is Replicated-FEN Nash-Stability, and it mirrors Theorem 21.

The definition uses the paper’s own distance-based preferences and unilateral deviations. A deviation to a coalition made entirely of other cells is equivalent for the deviator to becoming a singleton, since those other agents are neutral. So cross-cell grouping does not change the stability test, and any feasible \(\lambda\) can be realized by assigning cells to their chosen partitions. There is no scalar objective here: as in Theorem 21, the question is whether a stable outcome exists.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. A template with a stable partition yields a continuous solution by assigning all cell mass to that partition; a continuous solution must put positive mass on some stable partition. Thus the continuous decision question is exactly the original existence question on the role template, and Theorem 21’s NP-hardness carries over. Under this finite template input representation, the mirror is NP-complete as well: a yes-instance has a finite partition as a certificate. The hardness comes from the number and interaction structure of roles, not from the number of repeated cells, so this mirror does not claim that every form of population continuization dissolves the hardness.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it covers Theorem 21’s Nash-stability existence result, not the paper’s axiomatic results or its other stability notions. It also suggests a further question: if one allows mass to split across role configurations without keeping agents in coherent local cells, does the resulting nonatomic stability problem remain hard, or does that different model admit tractable fractional structure?

The weakest point is the repeated-cell architecture. The paper studies coalitions of named players, while this mirror treats copies of a role as identical up to cell renaming and preserves their local relationships through the shared cell structure. That is a more specific regime than arbitrary FEN games. I think authors would recognize it as their problem because it retains their preference model, bounded neighborhoods, and Nash deviations, while putting the population itself in a many-copy regime. But the result should be read as a credible narrow mirror, not evidence that the paper’s entire model has a canonical continuum formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 21 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper proves NP-completeness for Nash-stable outcome existence. The strongest objection is to whether its object has a useful population-continuous form. Here preferences depend on membership of particular friends and enemies in a coalition. A distribution over role types records how much mass has each preference pattern, but not which copies of those roles are linked as neighbors.

The replicated-team construction preserves those links by adding a cell structure. If types include the identities of their cell-mates, copies across cells are different types; if types are just roles, the cell structure is extra information that the type distribution does not contain. In an atomless limit, each cell and each bounded-degree neighborhood has zero population mass. A coalition made of one cell’s roles is likewise invisible to a mass-based description, even though it can determine whether a player wants to deviate. So the ordinary continuous FEN model cannot evaluate the paper’s Nash-stability condition from its population distribution alone.

The proposed \(\lambda_P\) repairs that loss by retaining a finite cell template and recording how much cell mass receives each partition. But then the relevant state is a distribution over local outcomes, with stability checked inside each finite cell. A graphing or another explicit model of the local links could make this a coherent extension, and might be worth studying; it would require a richer input than a distribution over types. A dense-network version could make neighborhoods visible as mass, but would change the paper’s bounded-neighborhood setting and its preference model.

This does not defeat the anchor cleanly. Repeated bounded-size teams are plausible, and the programme permits co-modifications when population continuization requires them. The honest negative case is narrower: the paper itself supplies no canonical finite-type mirror, and its strongest proposed mirror works only by carrying the local graph structure alongside the continuum. I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists.
