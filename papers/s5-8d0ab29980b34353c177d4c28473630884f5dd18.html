The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), common per-capita convex piecewise-linear bid costs and feasible sets, and rational polyhedral aggregate market constraints, define \(J_\mu(\alpha)\) as the minimum dispatch cost when only coalition mass \(\alpha_t\) of type \(t\) may participate. Compute the utility densities \((u_0,(u_t)_t)\) satisfying \(u_t\ge 0\), \(u_0+\sum_t\mu_tu_t=-J_\mu(\mu)\), and \(u_0+\sum_t\alpha_tu_t\ge -J_\mu(\alpha)\) for every proper \(\alpha\in[0,\mu]\), lexicographically maximizing \((\sum_t\mu_tu_t,-\sum_t\mu_t(u_t-v_t)^2)\), where \(v_t=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}[J_\mu(\mu-\delta e_t)-J_\mu(\mu)]/\delta\).

The model it lives in

Types carry masses \(\mu_t\) and share per-capita costs and feasible sets; coalition costs are \(J_\mu(\alpha)\). Choose utilities in the mass-weighted core, first maximizing total bidder utility \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t\), then minimizing the mass-weighted squared distance to the marginal-removal VCG utility densities \(v_t\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a continuous version of computing the MPCS mechanism. One caveat sets its limits: this paper has no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest computational anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper in Appendix E. It says that the MPCS mechanism minimizes, among all core-selecting mechanisms, the sum of bidders’ maximum profits from unilateral deviations from truthful bids. The paper defines that mechanism by an optimization over the core, identifies it as a convex quadratic program, and computes it in its case studies. The theorem is an optimization result, though, not a complexity classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large fleet of modular generators, batteries, or reserve providers. A type records everything the market model uses: network location, service, feasible supply set, and bid or cost function. Many physical units share each type, with a fixed, relatively small number \(\tau\) of types and a large population \(N\). Normalize each unit’s capacity and cost by \(1/N\), so the aggregate market remains finite. Then \(\mu_t\) is type \(t\)’s fraction of the fleet, and an individual’s utility is of order \(1/N\). This is a meaningful population regime for the paper’s electricity markets, and the type definition preserves the paper’s assumption that identical bidders are interchangeable.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Consider a one-sided procurement market with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For clarity, take per-capita feasible sets \(X_t\), convex piecewise-linear bid costs \(b_t\), and rational polyhedral network and procurement constraints. A coalition is described by masses \(\alpha_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). If its members of type \(t\) supply \(q_t\in X_t\) per unit mass, let \(J_\mu(\alpha)\) be the minimum procurement and second-stage cost achievable by that coalition, subject to the market constraints. The type’s complete description includes any parameter needed to determine its costs, feasible supplies, or network role.

Let \(u_t\) be the revealed utility per unit mass of type \(t\), and \(u_0\) the operator’s utility. The continuous core is the set satisfying

\[ u_t\ge 0,\qquad u_0+\sum_t\mu_tu_t=-J_\mu(\mu), \]

and, for every proper coalition mass vector \(\alpha\in[0,\mu]\),

\[ u_0+\sum_t\alpha_tu_t\ge -J_\mu(\alpha). \]

For each type, define the VCG utility density by the marginal cost increase from removing a small amount of that type’s mass,

\[ v_t=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{J_\mu(\mu-\delta e_t)-J_\mu(\mu)}{\delta}. \]

The continuous MPCS question is: given this market and its type masses, compute a core utility vector maximizing

\[ \sum_t\mu_tu_t-\epsilon\sum_t\mu_t(u_t-v_t)^2, \]

with positive \(\epsilon\) serving as the tie-breaker, and return the corresponding per-mass payments. The first term maximizes total bidder utility; the second selects utilities close to the VCG benchmark. This is the mass-weighted analogue of the paper’s MPCS choice. It asks for their mechanism’s utility assignment in a many-unit regime, rather than for a relaxation of the market’s outcomes.

The natural prediction for this polyhedral version is Class A. The core has a continuum of coalition constraints, but a violated constraint can be sought by minimizing

\[ J_\mu(\alpha)-J_\mu(\mu)-\sum_t(\mu_t-\alpha_t)u_t \]

over coalition masses and their dispatch. With convex piecewise-linear costs and polyhedral constraints, this separation task has a linear-programming formulation; the utility vector has only \(\tau\) type coordinates. That suggests a separation-based convex optimization algorithm. The paper’s own MPCS computation already uses coalition-constraint generation, but it does not prove this polynomial-time claim. Further questions include proving the finite-population approximation and determining what happens when nonconvex bids or market constraints make separation hard.

My weakest point is the bridge from Theorem 2 to this question. The theorem optimizes over individual deviation gains. In a nonatomic limit, an individual’s effect vanishes; the derivative \(v_t\) is a proposed per-mass limit of the finite-population VCG utility, not an identity established by the authors. A proof that the rescaled discrete MPCS problem converges to this formulation is essential for the authors and a referee to accept it as their problem. Also, if “anchor” strictly requires a named complexity classification, this paper supplies no qualifying anchor: the MPCS mirror is a credible computational question suggested by their named optimization result and computations, not a complexity result they proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the MPCS anchor does not meet ChoCo’s stated entry condition. Theorem 2 proves an optimality characterization: among core-selecting mechanisms, MPCS minimizes the sum of bidders’ maximum unilateral deviation gains. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee. The paper identifies a convex quadratic program and reports numerical computation, including constraint generation, but does not establish a running time or solve a named separation problem. So a continuous MPCS algorithm would be a new computational question suggested by the paper, rather than a continuization of a computational result it proves.

The proposed mirror also needs a limit argument to preserve what Theorem 2 measures. With \(N\) copies of each type and per-person quantities and costs scaled by \(1/N\), individual utilities and VCG gains are typically of order \(1/N\). The sum of gains can converge to a meaningful mass-weighted quantity, but an individual’s deviation gain vanishes. The mirror therefore needs to justify why its aggregate first-order measure is the right continuization of the paper’s individual DSIC criterion. Its proposed density-level quadratic tie-break also does not follow directly from the finite objective: under this scaling, the paper’s squared-distance term is of order \(1/N\). A lexicographic tie-break could repair that mismatch, but it would be an additional modeling choice.

These objections do not establish that no useful mirror exists. A large fleet of genuinely interchangeable small reserve providers is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and with convex piecewise-linear costs the coalition-mass separation problem may admit the LP formulation the proponent sketches. That makes the universal negative claim weak: the mirror could be a worthwhile new ChoCo problem if the authors accept the aggregate deviation criterion and the finite-to-continuous bridge.

So the negative case is narrow but firm on the anchor: Theorem 2 is a mechanism-selection theorem, not a result about computational complexity. If “mirror” must continue a computational result of this paper, the proposed case fails. If a new algorithmic question based on the paper’s optimization model counts, I cannot honestly rule it out.
