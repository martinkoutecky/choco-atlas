The continuous mirror question

Given \(q\) and an explicitly listed set of types \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \{-1,1\}^q\), decide whether every rational probability distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal{T}\) with \(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{T}} \mu_x x_j \ne 0\) for every issue \(j\) has its strict issue-wise majority vector \(M(\mu)_j = \operatorname{sgn}(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{T}} \mu_x x_j)\) in \(\mathcal{T}\).

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete yes-no ballot bundle \(x \in \mathcal{T}\), and a society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types. The input is \(q\) and the explicit type catalogue \(\mathcal{T}\); the task is to decide universal closure under strict issue-wise majority, which Theorem 2 reduces to checking all triples in \(\mathcal{T}\). There are no mass-transfer decisions or optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's majority-stability test over domains. It leaves aside Theorem 3's aggregation-rule characterization and the paper's other results and interpretations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the triple-checking algorithm be improved beyond \(O(|\mathcal{T}|^3 q)\) for relevant domain representations?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Majority-Stability Recognition, mirroring Theorem 2. The theorem, proved in this paper, says that a domain \(\mathcal{D}\) is stable under issue-wise majority across every admissible odd-sized profile if and only if every triple of types from \(\mathcal{D}\) has its issue-wise majority outcome in \(\mathcal{D}\). The paper calls this a computationally simple test; with the domain explicitly listed, checking all triples gives a polynomial-time algorithm. The theorem does not state a complexity class, but its finite test puts this recognition problem in P for that representation.

An instance is a number \(q\) of yes-no issues and an explicit list \(\mathcal{T}\subseteq\{-1,1\}^q\) of \(\tau\) allowed complete ballots or programs. A type is one whole vector: two people have the same type exactly when they agree on every issue used by the problem. A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal{T}\), with \(\mu_x\) the fraction holding type \(x\). For societies with no exact \(50\)-\(50\) tie on any issue, define the majority outcome by
\[ M_j(\mu)= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if }\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}\mu_x x_j>0,\\ -1 & \text{if }\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}\mu_x x_j<0. \end{cases} \]
The question is whether \(M(\mu)\in\mathcal{T}\) for every such society. A solution is a yes-or-no answer; a no answer can be witnessed by a violating society. There is no optimization objective in this anchor: it is a universal feasibility question about whether majority aggregation preserves the allowed domain.

A plausible regime is a very large federation holding several linked referenda, where members choose among a limited menu of complete ballot bundles. There may be millions of members but only dozens or hundreds of distinct bundles, so mass is naturally reported as a share of members. The property asks whether *any* population mix over those allowed bundles keeps the collective outcome allowed. This matches the paper’s own multiple-elections and domain-stability settings, while making the electorate a continuous distribution.

The continuous and discrete questions coincide for this tie-free stability test. Any triple with an outcome outside \(\mathcal{T}\) gives a violating society by assigning its three members’ types mass \(1/3\) each, including repetitions. Conversely, if a continuous society has a strict-majority outcome outside \(\mathcal{T}\), its strict issue margins persist under a sufficiently close rational approximation with a common odd denominator. That approximation is an odd-sized discrete profile with the same violating outcome. Theorem 2 then guarantees a violating triple. So the continuous problem has a \(\tau^3\)-triple test, taking \(O(\tau^3q)\) time apart from membership lookup. I expect this mirror to be tractable. The paper proves the key compression from arbitrary population sizes to triples.

The scope is deliberately just Theorem 2’s majority-stability result. I would not stretch this case to Theorem 3’s characterization of aggregation rules: the paper’s strongest computational anchor here is the triple test.

The weakest point is that the theorem already handles every odd electorate size. Passing to masses does not open a new algorithmic frontier; it gives the same stability question a continuous-population formulation. Also, the mirror concerns a whole domain of allowable types, rather than testing the outcome for one supplied society. Those are real limits. Still, the type distribution is the object being quantified over, and the paper explicitly studies whether a domain remains stable under majority, so this is a recognizable continuous form of its problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The federation with repeated referendum bundles is a credible high-multiplicity setting. A complete bundle is a sensible type, and a population share is a sensible way to describe how common it is. So I can’t defeat this mirror by arguing that the scenario or its types are artificial.

The stronger objection is that the proposed recognition problem takes the allowed type set \(\mathcal T\) as input and quantifies over every distribution \(\mu\) on it. It does not take a particular continuous society as input. If the question instead concerns a supplied \(\mu\), computing its majority vector and checking membership in \(\mathcal T\) is direct; that is no longer Theorem 2’s stability question. If it retains the universal quantifier, the input is the catalogue of allowed bundles, and the mass vector is a quantified witness. The theorem already compresses that universal claim to checking triples, in \(O(\tau^3q)\) time. No mass-transfer choice, objective, or society-specific computational task emerges.

That distinction matters for ChoCo’s stated target: computational questions about interventions and robustness when a society is given as a distribution. This paper’s result concerns closure of a finite domain under an aggregation rule. Recasting its profiles as points in a probability simplex is mathematically sound, but the simplex does not become an instance on which the algorithm operates. The theorem provides a finite certificate for the domain property; it does not open a continuous-optimization question.

A stronger-sounding version might ask how much mass must move from a particular society before its majority outcome leaves the allowed domain. That would make the distribution operational, but it adds a baseline society, a distance or cost for moving mass, and an optimization objective absent from the paper. It could be a worthwhile new robustness problem, but it is not a mirror of Theorem 2. The same issue applies to the paper’s other interpretations: adding a mass-based intervention may produce a new problem, but it does not follow from the result being mirrored.

This is the strongest negative case I can make, and it has a real limit: the universal distributional stability question is well-posed, and the authors could recognize it as their domain-stability question. I can argue that it is too thin to count as a new continuous computational contribution; I cannot honestly claim that no continuous mirror survives in any scenario.
