The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchored positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification.

The closest results are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in the paper. Proposition 1 says the single-agent equilibrium acceptance threshold decreases as the agent’s private value rises. Proposition 2 says the pivotal voter’s threshold in committee search matches that voter’s single-agent threshold, while nonpivotal voters’ thresholds are lower. These are equilibrium characterizations and comparative statics, not computational results. The experimental findings are not named complexity results either.

A continuous population of committee members could be imagined, with masses for types distinguished by private value and a quota for accepting an option. But that would change the paper’s central mechanism: its unique pivotal voter is an individual in a finite committee. A continuum version has no such individual pivot, so it would not be an honest mirror of Proposition 2. A continuum of independent committees, meanwhile, would merely replicate the original problem.

So there is no qualifying anchor here, and no continuous problem “per anchor” to state without inventing one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The absence of a complexity theorem is the strongest point against a mirror, but it does not rule one out. Propositions 1 and 2 give a usable structure for a new computational question: Proposition 1 makes the reservation threshold monotone in private value; Proposition 2 identifies the member at the quota’s pivotal rank and equates that member’s committee threshold with their single-agent threshold.

Consider a large cooperative voting on whether to accept a common supplier offer or keep searching. Many members may share the same private bonus from accepting. Represent the population by rational masses \(\mu_t\) over bonus types, and let the acceptance quota be a fraction \(\alpha\). In finite committees with these type proportions, Proposition 2 makes the pivotal member’s type an order statistic. As committee size grows, that type approaches the corresponding quantile of \(\mu\). Thus the limiting cutoff can be defined through the pivotal *type* even though no individual has positive mass. This is a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s model; it does not rely on treating an atomless voter’s unilateral action as pivotal.

That gives a concrete continuous computational problem: given \(\mu\), the quota, the search parameters, and per-unit costs \(\gamma(t,t')\) for changing members’ bonus types through compensation, find the least-cost mass transfer that moves the equilibrium acceptance cutoff past a specified target. The type space is the population distribution, and the intervention acts on type mass. Proposition 1 translates the target cutoff into a required bonus level; Proposition 2 translates that level into a quantile constraint. The resulting problem is a natural high-multiplicity control or bribery question, and it asks for an algorithm in support size and input bit length.

The paper does not already answer that computational question, and the exact atomless voting game would need care because individual pivotality disappears. Those are real limitations. But the authors’ focus on bonus heterogeneity and voting rules makes the population and quota formulation recognizable, while the finite-committee limit supplies a defensible bridge. I would therefore reject the categorical “no worthwhile mirror” verdict, while treating this as a promising computational extension rather than a mirror of a complexity result the paper actually proves.
