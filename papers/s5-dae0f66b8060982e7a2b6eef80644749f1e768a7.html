The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a large LLM user population divided into a modest number of preference segments. A type is a user’s complete utility vector over the \(m\) candidate completions; its mass is that segment’s share of users. The decision is a generation policy \(\pi\), and welfare is average user utility. A regime with millions of users and relatively few utility types is plausible, and fractional policy mass is natural even though each individual receives one completion.

There is a catch: the paper already represents users by a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over utility vectors and analyzes expected welfare under that distribution. Its population is already continuous in essentially this sense. The potential contribution would therefore be a complexity analysis of that model, rather than making a previously finite population continuous.

The closest named results are not valid anchors under your stated rule. Theorem 4.1, proved here, bounds RLHF distortion under a bounded mismatch \(B\) and the paper’s specified reward clipping by
\[ C_2\bigl(\min\{e^B\tau,B,B\tau^{-1}\}+1\bigr)\beta+4. \]
Theorem 5.2, proved here, gives an algorithm-independent lower bound: when the comparison distribution equals the reference policy, for every KL budget \(\tau>0\) and temperature \(\beta>0\), there is a family of user-utility distributions such that any algorithm has expected distortion at least
\[ \frac{\beta}{2}\frac{1+e^{-\beta}}{1-e^{-\beta}}-\epsilon \]
on a uniformly chosen instance. These are distortion guarantees and an information-theoretic performance lower bound; neither classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. So the paper supplies no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror to.

If the anchor requirement were relaxed, one precise follow-up would be Finite-Support RLHF Distortion Approximation. Its input is a finite alternative set \(A\), rational utility types \(u^1,\ldots,u^s\in[0,1]^m\) with rational masses \(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_s\), a positive rational comparison distribution \(q\), a positive rational reference policy \(\pi_0\), rational \(\beta\) and KL radius \(K\), clipping thresholds, and an error tolerance \(\epsilon\). Fit the population Bradley–Terry reward using the type masses and pairwise sampling law \(q\); then form the clipped-reward maximizing policy in the KL ball around \(\pi_0\). The task is to return that policy and its distortion to additive error \(\epsilon\), with distortion measured against the best welfare achievable in the same KL ball. The type masses, reward-fitting objective, policy constraint, and welfare objective are all specified; this is not a question about a fractional outcome space.

I would expect this follow-up to be Class A, since the fitted reward and policy selection have finite-dimensional convex formulations. That expectation is not established by the paper, and it does not turn Theorem 4.1 or 5.2 into complexity anchors. My strongest positive claim is therefore limited: the user-segment regime is credible and computational questions about it are natural, but this paper already uses the continuous population model and provides no qualifying named complexity result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 bounds RLHF’s distortion, while Theorem 5.2 gives an algorithm-independent distortion lower bound. Neither classifies a computational problem or analyzes its running time. The latter is an information-theoretic limitation: it applies regardless of computational power.

There is also a deeper issue for the proposed follow-up: the paper already models the population as a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over complete utility vectors. Its welfare and population preference likelihood are expectations over that distribution. A scenario with millions of users divided into a modest number of utility types is plausible, and those types fit the paper’s model directly. But making the population continuous would not transform the model; it would specialize an already continuous one. Under ChoCo’s collision test, that is the central objection.

The strongest repaired version of the proposed finite-support problem would give rational masses and utility vectors explicitly, then ask for the fitted reward, the KL-constrained policy, and its distortion. That is a computational question, but the population is still the paper’s \(\mathcal D\). With an explicit alternative set, the main steps are finite-dimensional convex optimizations. Whether exact bit complexity or numerical conditioning raises a substantial problem would need analysis, but any result would be about computing RLHF in this already continuous model, not about continuization. A version with an implicit space of completions might be more challenging, but it needs a specified encoding or oracle for candidates and utilities; its computational content would then depend on that representation.

The same distinction disposes of Theorem 5.2 as a mirror anchor. Recasting its utility distribution using type masses does not create a new computational question: the theorem already ranges over distributions of utilities, and its lower bound concerns indistinguishability rather than running time.

So I would reject the proposed *continuous mirror* as a ChoCo contribution, while accepting that a separate computational study of RLHF—especially with a precise implicit representation of completions—could be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest on scope and weakest as a universal claim: the paper’s continuous population model rules out a genuine continuization step, but it cannot rule out every valuable complexity question someone might formulate about RLHF.
