The continuous mirror question

Given \(g\) commodities, \(\tau\) complete economic types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational linear utilities \(u_{tj}\), and rational per-capita endowments \(e_{tj}\), compute a normalized exact equilibrium \(p\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^g\) and aggregate money flows \(f_{tj}\), where \(Q_j=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tj}\), \(\sum_t f_{tj}=p_jQ_j\), \(\sum_j f_{tj}=\mu_t\sum_j e_{tj}p_j\), and \(f_{tj}>0\) only when \(u_{tj}/p_j\) is maximal for type \(t\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type high-multiplicity linear exchange economy: type \(t\) carries mass \(\mu_t\), has common utility \(u_t\) and endowment \(e_t\), and the variables are normalized prices \(p\) and mass money flows \(f\) satisfying market-clearing and bang-per-buck constraints.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a Class A mirror for exact equilibrium computation. The paper contains no hardness theorem, so I would not pretend it can anchor a question about discrete hardness disappearing. Its contribution is instead an unusually natural high-multiplicity version of the problem it actually solves.

My lead anchor is Theorem 7.2, proved in this paper. In the general, possibly degenerate linear Arrow–Debreu market, the algorithm computes equilibrium prices using \(O(n^6\log^2(nU))\) arithmetic operations on integers of bitlength \(O(n\log(nU))\), and therefore has running time \(O(n^7\log^3(nU))\). The theorem includes the perturbation machinery that removes degeneracies; it is not merely the earlier nondegenerate result.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Type-Compressed Linear Arrow–Debreu Equilibrium\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite commodity set \(G=\{1,\dots,g\}\), a finite set of economic-agent types \(T=\{1,\dots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a linear utility vector \(u_t=(u_{tj})_{j\in G}\) for each type, and a rational per-capita endowment vector \(e_t=(e_{tj})_{j\in G}\). A type is complete: agents of type \(t\) have the same utilities and the same endowments. Let

\[ Q_j=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_{tj} \]

be the per-capita supply of commodity \(j\). The task is to compute an exact normalized equilibrium price vector \(p\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^g\), together with a certificate of the corresponding mass allocation.

For a type \(t\), its per-agent budget at prices \(p\) is

\[ b_t(p)=\sum_{j\in G}e_{tj}p_j. \]

A solution may be represented by a per-agent allocation \(x_{tj}\), or equivalently by the aggregate money flow

\[ f_{tj}=\mu_t p_jx_{tj}. \]

It must satisfy

\[ \sum_t f_{tj}=p_jQ_j \]

for every good \(j\), meaning that all goods are sold,

\[ \sum_j f_{tj}=\mu_tb_t(p) \]

for every type \(t\), meaning that all money is spent, and

\[ f_{tj}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{u_{tj}}{p_j} = \max_{k\in G}\frac{u_{tk}}{p_k}, \]

meaning that each type buys only goods maximizing utility per unit of money. Prices are normalized, for example by \(\min_jp_j=1\). The usual positivity and connectivity assumptions from the paper are imposed on the active types and goods.

This is genuinely a continuous-population question. The commodity set \(G\) remains finite; the continuous object is the society \(\mu\). A mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction of a very large population having the complete economic profile \(t\). The goods remain divisible exactly as in the paper, but that is not what is being continuized.

A convincing regime is a recurring regional or national exchange market with \(N\) millions of households, firms, or production units and a relatively small catalogue of standardized economic profiles. A profile might specify a linear valuation of \(g\) commodity classes together with a standard endowment or ownership portfolio. Thus \(N\gg\tau,g\), with \(\tau\) perhaps dozens or hundreds rather than millions. Individual names, locations, and irrelevant histories are not part of the market model; agents sharing utilities, endowments, and budgets are genuinely indistinguishable for the problem.

The high-multiplicity bridge is exact. If \(N_t=N\mu_t\) agents have type \(t\), then the finite market is obtained by expanding each type into \(N_t\) identical copies. Conversely, every rational \(\mu\) can be represented by such a finite clone market. The total supplies and budgets scale with \(N\), but after dividing by \(N\), the equilibrium price problem is exactly the mass formulation above. This is not merely an analogy between a finite and continuous model.

The paper’s own machinery makes the mirror especially plausible. Its equality network has buyer vertices, good vertices, source capacities, sink capacities, and edges for maximum bang-per-buck purchases. In the mirror, the source capacity for type \(t\) becomes \(\mu_tb_t(p)\), while the sink capacity for good \(j\) becomes \(p_jQ_j\). The equality edges are now between types and goods. The balanced-flow potential naturally becomes a weighted surplus norm such as

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t r_t^2, \]

because \(\mu_tN\) identical copies contribute that many equal surplus terms. Price updates still operate on neighborhoods in the equality graph, and the perturbation idea still has a type-level interpretation. The underlying question remains exactly “find equilibrium prices in a linear exchange market,” rather than a simpler allocation or welfare problem.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable, probably in Class A. The natural conjecture is an algorithm polynomial in \(g\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of the rational data, independent of the possibly enormous common denominator representing \(N\). The weighted balanced-flow algorithm is the most direct route. A continuous optimization formulation could also exploit the same convex or flow structure. The paper does not prove this compressed bound, so it would be a new result rather than a restatement of Theorem 7.2.

The main follow-up questions are whether the balanced-flow analysis survives type aggregation without expanding the denominator of \(\mu\), whether the forest argument and perturbation scheme behave cleanly on the quotient type–commodity graph, and how the complexity changes when the number of distinct economic types grows with the number of commodities. A genuinely uncountable type space would be a different and harder extension; the proposed mirror stays within the programme’s finite-type continuous-society model.

The weakest point is that Arrow–Debreu markets already contain divisible goods and fractional allocations. A sceptic could therefore mistake this for outcome-space continuity. The defence is that the proposed mirror keeps the commodity space finite and changes the population itself: the inputs are masses of complete agent types, and all market capacities are aggregated over those masses. The mirror is credible only for the standardized high-multiplicity regime described above. If every agent has a unique endowment or idiosyncratic utility vector, then this particular mirror is not persuasive. There is also a technical gap: Theorem 7.2 does not itself establish that its \(n\)-agent implementation compresses to \(\tau\) weighted types. That compression is the new theorem to prove, not something I would claim for free.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 7.2 does not actually concern a population with repeated economic types. Its \(n\) agents are also \(n\) individually owned goods: buyer \(b_i\) owns \(c_i\), has budget \(p_i\), and the algorithm repeatedly exploits that pairing. The equality graph, the surplus analysis, the price product, and the perturbation argument are all built around it.

If we replicate an agent while preserving the paper’s model, the replica owns a distinct good. Its complete type therefore includes an identity-specific endowment, so the number of types grows with the population. No finite-type continuum results. If we instead identify those goods into commodity classes, we have changed the problem to a fixed-commodity exchange economy with vector endowments. That may be a sensible Arrow–Debreu model, but it is not a quotient of the theorem’s instance.

The proposed rescue also has less computational content than claimed. For a type \(t\), replace its mass \(\mu_t\) and per-capita endowment \(e_t\) by one representative agent with aggregate endowment \(\mu_t e_t\). Because utility is linear, every mass allocation is equivalent to an allocation for these \(\tau\) representative agents, and vice versa. Thus

\[ \mu \longmapsto \{\,\mu_t e_t\,\}_{t\in T} \]

eliminates the population entirely. The proposed “continuous” market is exactly a finite linear exchange market with \(\tau\) agents and \(g\) goods. The distribution is not an object that the equilibrium computation reasons about; it is merely a collection of coefficients in a smaller ordinary market. Existing finite-market convex-programming algorithms already address that reduced object.

Nor does Theorem 7.2 establish the advertised weighted-flow extension. In the paper, source and sink capacities are paired as \(p_i\), every buyer has a distinguished owned good \(c_i\), and several phase arguments use that correspondence. With general \(e_t\), a type has no distinguished good and its budget is \(e_t\cdot p\); the forest and surplus lemmas do not simply survive after replacing counts by \(\mu_t\)-weights. A weighted balanced-flow conjecture is a new theorem, not an implication of the paper.

One can restrict the mirror to \(e_t\) being one unit of a type-specific good. That preserves the buyer–owner pairing and yields a legitimate weighted-network problem, but then the “continuization” is only a routine insertion of rational vertex weights into the paper’s finite algorithm. It does not supply the proponent’s broader economic scenario, and its polynomial compression still has to be proved.

This is nevertheless a weak universal negative. A large economy with finitely many standardized household portfolios is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and proving the weighted algorithm could be worthwhile precisely because it would give a compressed exact solver. The objections decisively undermine the claim that Theorem 7.2 already supplies that mirror; they do not honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror can be formulated.
