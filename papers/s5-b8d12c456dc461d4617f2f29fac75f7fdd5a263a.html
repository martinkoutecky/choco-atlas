The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, approval-ballot types \(p^1,\ldots,p^\tau\in\{0,1\}^m\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), find a committee \(x\in\{0,1\}^m\) minimizing \(\max\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tau}q_\ell\|p^\ell-x\|_1:0\le q_\ell\le\mu_\ell,\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{\tau}q_\ell=\alpha\}\).

The model it lives in

The population consists of approval-ballot types carrying mass \(\mu_\ell\); the committee remains binary, and the objective is the total Hamming distance within the farthest \(\alpha\) share of the population, allowing a type’s mass to be split at the tail boundary.

What the mirror covers

Covers the \(k\)-sum approval committee problem and Theorem 3’s formulation; leaves the separate objective based on the \(h\) smallest distances alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the general problem in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • Does fixing \(\alpha\), or parameterizing by \(\tau\), yield tractable cases?
  • What approximation guarantees are achievable in terms of \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one mirror, for the paper’s \(k\)-sum approval committee problem. Its natural regime is a large association or electorate choosing a committee from a short candidate slate. If there are \(m=12\) candidates, for example, there are at most \(2^{12}\) approval ballots, even if millions of members vote. A type is one complete approval ballot \(p\in\{0,1\}^m\); its mass \(\mu_p\) is the fraction of members using it. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: the number of voters can greatly exceed the number of distinct ballot types.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It gives an exact mixed-integer linear formulation for minimizing the sum of the \(k\) largest Hamming distances from voters’ ballots to a committee. Writing \(d_i(x)=\sum_j|p_{ij}-x_j|\), the formulation is \(\min kt+\sum_i v_i\), subject to \(v_i\ge d_i-t\), \(d_i\ge\sum_j z_{ij}\), \(z_{ij}\ge x_j(1-p_{ij})+p_{ij}(1-x_j)\), \(x_j\in\{0,1\}\), \(t\ge0\), and \(v_i\ge0\), for all voters \(i\) and candidates \(j\). The paper uses this formulation in its computational study. The result is about the committee optimization problem; the formulation is the anchor, not the mirror question.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Upper-\(\alpha\)-Tail Approval Committee. An instance consists of \(m\) candidates, a finite support of distinct approval ballots \(p^1,\ldots,p^\tau\in\{0,1\}^m\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and a binary-rational tail share \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Find a committee \(x\in\{0,1\}^m\) minimizing
\[ F_{\mu,\alpha}(x)= \max\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tau}q_\ell\|p^\ell-x\|_1: 0\le q_\ell\le\mu_\ell,\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{\tau}q_\ell=\alpha\right\}. \]
Here \(q_\ell\) is the mass of type \(\ell\) included in the farthest \(\alpha\) share of the population. The decision variable remains the paper’s committee, and the loss remains its Hamming distance. The problem asks which committee minimizes the total distance of that worst-off share.

This is a direct population continuization of the \(k\)-sum objective. For a discrete electorate, set \(\mu_p=n_p/n\), where \(n_p\) voters cast ballot \(p\), and set \(\alpha=k/n\). Then \(F_{\mu,\alpha}(x)\) is exactly \(1/n\) times the sum of the \(k\) largest voter-to-committee distances. Ties can be split fractionally without changing that value. Thus the mirror preserves the committee and the paper’s objective; it expresses \(k\) as a share of the population and normalizes the total.

The continuum problem also has the natural type-level version of Theorem 3’s formulation: minimize \(\alpha t+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tau}\mu_\ell v_\ell\), subject to \(v_\ell\ge\|p^\ell-x\|_1-t\), \(v_\ell\ge0\), \(t\ge0\), and \(x\in\{0,1\}^m\). This makes the type support, rather than a list of named voters, the input scale.

For the general problem with \(\alpha\) as input, I expect hardness to transfer. The paper states in Section 2 that the discrete \(k\)-sum problem for \(1\le k<n\) is NP-hard, citing earlier work. Any explicit discrete instance maps to the continuous one in polynomial time by grouping equal ballots and setting \(\alpha=k/n\); the optimal committees are identical. So an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length would solve those discrete instances too. This says nothing by itself about fixed-\(\alpha\) slices or small-\(\tau\) regimes, which are worthwhile separate questions. It does establish that continuization does not erase the general problem’s hardness.

The mirror covers the paper’s \(k\)-sum approval optimization and Theorem 3’s computed problem. It does not cover the paper’s separate objective based on the \(h\) smallest distances. Further questions include whether the problem is tractable or fixed-parameter tractable in support size \(\tau\), how complexity changes when \(\alpha\) is fixed, and what approximation guarantees are possible in terms of \(m,\tau\), and input bit length.

The weakest point is that the paper’s \(k\) is a voter count, while this mirror takes \(k/n\) to a population share. That is a regime choice: it follows the paper’s stated family \(1\le k<n\), but authors focused on fixed \(k\) might not regard it as their primary limit. Also, the NP-hardness statement is not a numbered result of this paper; it appears in prose and cites earlier work. The numbered anchor here is the computed formulation theorem. Still, the underlying question keeps their ballots, Hamming loss, and committee decision intact, and the high-multiplicity electorate makes the share-based objective a natural version of their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the anchor is narrower than the proponent makes it sound. Theorem 3 proves an exact mixed integer formulation; it does not prove a complexity classification. The NP-hardness discussed in Section 2 is attributed to earlier work, and the experiments cover finite instances of bounded size. So this paper does not itself establish the kind of complexity result the continuous question would classify.

That limits the anchor, but it does not defeat it. Theorem 3 is still a named computational result about the committee optimization problem. Its type-level version follows naturally: give each approval ballot \(p^\ell\) mass \(\mu_\ell\), and minimize the sum of the farthest \(\alpha\) share’s Hamming distances. The same threshold formulation becomes \(\min \alpha t+\sum_\ell \mu_\ell v_\ell\), with \(v_\ell\ge \|p^\ell-x\|_1-t\). This uses the support of the population rather than a list of voters.

The best case against the mirror, then, is that it appears to be a direct high-multiplicity reformulation of the paper’s model. The type-level formulation is compact and follows from the same order-statistic duality as Theorem 3; the main change is replacing voter counts by rational masses and \(k\) by a share \(\alpha\). That may make this a modest extension rather than a new optimization structure. But modestness is not enough to show the question is unworthy: the programme expressly treats the high-multiplicity version as a legitimate object, and asks whether it is tractable, hard, or parameterized.

There is also a real regime choice. If \(k\) stays fixed as the population grows, then \(k/n\) tends to zero, so the normalized tail objective vanishes. The proposed mirror instead studies \(k/n\to\alpha>0\): the farthest fixed share of the population. That is not the only possible limit, but it is a coherent one, and it fits the paper’s variable-\(k\) problem and its discussion of representing a population’s preferences.

I cannot make a strong case that this anchor fails in every scenario. Approval ballots are complete types for the paper’s distance objective, and many voters can share each type. The tail objective remains nontrivial for \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Grouping discrete ballots gives a clean bridge, while the paper’s cited NP-hardness supplies a meaningful hardness question. High-multiplicity work supports the regime rather than colliding with it. The honest negative case is therefore weak: the paper’s own theorem is a formulation result, and the continuous extension may be straightforward, but the proposed mirror still looks worthwhile under the programme’s criteria.
