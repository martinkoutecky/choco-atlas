The continuous mirror question

Given a finite state set Θ with rational prior π, districts \(d=1,…,D\), and rational masses μ_dt over supported voter types with utility differences \(u_{dt}(θ)\), find an optimal private signaling scheme. Represent the scheme, conditional on each state θ, by a probability law Λ_θ over recommendation shares \(x_{dt}∈[0,1]\), where \(x_{dt}\) is the fraction of type \(t\)'s mass in district \(d\) recommended \(c_0\). With \(q_{dtθ}=ᵓ_{Λ_θ}[x_{dt}]\), require \(∑_θ π_θ q_{dtθ}u_{dt}(θ)≥0\) and \(∑_θ π_θ(1-q_{dtθ})u_{dt}(θ)≤0\) for every type. Maximize the expected probability that \(c_0\) wins: in each realized allocation, district \(d\)'s \(c_0\) vote share is \(∑_t μ_{dt}x_{dt}\), and \(c_0\) wins the election if at least \(\lceil D/2\rceil\) districts have a share of at least \(1/2\).

The model it lives in

A finite-state, district-based electorate with rational mass μ_dt on each complete utility type. A private scheme selects, conditional on each state, a joint distribution over the fractions \(x_{dt}∈[0,1]\) receiving a \(c_0\) recommendation; its marginals enforce persuasion for both recommendations, while its joint outcomes determine district vote shares and the probability of winning the nested-majority election.

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers optimal private signaling in district-based elections. It leaves aside the public and semi-public hardness discussion, the relaxed PTAS results, and the private-versus-public efficiency comparison.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does optimization over the conditional laws Λ_θ admit an exact polynomial-time formulation or separation algorithm in \(D\), the number of supported types τ, \(|Θ|\), and the rational input bit length?
  • What two-way correspondence, if any, relates this mass model to finite private-signaling instances with repeated voter types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large electorate inside a fixed set of districts. Keep the paper’s finite states, two candidates, voter incentives, and district aggregation; replace the named voters in each district with a distribution over complete voter types. A type \(t\) in district \(d\) has a utility difference \(u_{dt}(\theta)=u_{dt}(\theta,c_0)-u_{dt}(\theta,c_1)\) for every state \(\theta\), and mass \(\mu_{dt}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_{dt}=1\). District membership belongs in the type because it determines which local signal a voter receives and how their vote is counted. A plausible regime is a few hundred districts, each with a large electorate and a modest number of recurring utility profiles: \(N\) voters can be very large while the total number of supported district-type pairs \(\tau\) stays moderate. The final election remains binary; fractional quantities describe shares of voters.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that for \(\epsilon>0\), \(\delta\in(0,1)\), and \(\eta\in(0,1]\), there is an algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(|R||\Theta|^{\log(1/(\eta\delta))/\epsilon^2})\) time that outputs an \(\epsilon\)-persuasive semi-public signaling scheme \(\phi^\epsilon\) with
\[ W_\delta(\phi^\epsilon)\ge (1-\eta)\max_{\phi\in\Phi}W(\phi), \]
where \(\Phi\) is the set of persuasive schemes. Here \(W_\delta\) relaxes the number of districts needed for \(c_0\) to win; the vote threshold inside each district is unchanged.

The corresponding continuous question is Continuous Semi-public District Persuasion. An instance gives a finite state set \(\Theta\), rational prior \(\pi\), districts \(d=1,\ldots,D\), rational type masses \(\mu_{dt}\), and rational utility vectors \(u_{dt}(\cdot)\). A scheme specifies, conditional on each state, a distribution over tuples of district signals. Every voter in district \(d\) sees the same component of that tuple. Given a district signal, let \(p\) be its posterior over states. A type with \(\sum_\theta p_\theta u_{dt}(\theta)>\epsilon\) votes for \(c_0\); one with value below \(-\epsilon\) votes for \(c_1\); and a type inside that band may split its mass between the two actions. Thus the \(c_0\) vote share in district \(d\) is \(v_d=\sum_t\mu_{dt}z_{dt}\), where \(z_{dt}\in[0,1]\) is that type’s share voting for \(c_0\).

Write \(K_D=\lceil D/2\rceil\). The exact election utility is \(W(v)=1\) when at least \(K_D\) districts have \(v_d\ge 1/2\), and zero otherwise. The relaxed utility \(W_\delta(v)\) uses the paper’s threshold \(\lceil(1-\delta)K_D\rceil\), keeping each local threshold at \(1/2\). The problem is to output an \(\epsilon\)-persuasive scheme whose expected \(W_\delta\) is at least \(1-\eta\) times the best expected \(W\) over exactly persuasive continuous schemes. This specifies the instance, the signal structure, the objective, and what counts as a solution. I expect a Class A result for fixed \(\epsilon,\delta,\eta\), with complexity polynomial in \(D,\tau,|\Theta|\), and the input bit length, and with dependence on the tolerances resembling the paper’s bound. The paper’s posterior discretization and LP ideas give a credible route, though the type-mass version still needs proving.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here: LP (1) computes an optimal solution to PRIVATE-DBE in polynomial time. Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Private District Persuasion on the same mass-and-type instance. A private scheme can be represented by, for each state \(\theta\), a probability law \(\Lambda_\theta\) over arrays \(x=(x_{dt})\in[0,1]^\tau\), where \(x_{dt}\) is the fraction of type \(t\)’s mass in district \(d\) receiving a \(c_0\) recommendation in that realized recommendation profile. Set \(q_{dt\theta}=\mathbb E_{\Lambda_\theta}[x_{dt}]\). Persuasiveness requires, for each type,
\[ \sum_\theta\pi_\theta q_{dt\theta}u_{dt}(\theta)\ge 0,\qquad \sum_\theta\pi_\theta(1-q_{dt\theta})u_{dt}(\theta)\le 0. \]
For each realized array, district \(d\)’s \(c_0\) vote share is \(\sum_t\mu_{dt}x_{dt}\); the objective is to maximize the expected probability that \(c_0\) wins the exact nested-majority election. The output is an optimal private signaling plan, including its randomization over mass allocations. I would also expect Class A, via a finite-dimensional LP and threshold pricing over type masses, but Theorem 1 by itself does not establish that bit-complexity bound: its runtime is polynomial in the number of named voters.

These are computational mirrors of the paper’s problems, not continuized outcomes or states. They retain the paper’s information advantage, receiver incentives, channel restrictions, and election rule. The public or district-level broadcast settings are especially plausible at high multiplicity: mass by utility type is the natural representation of a large electorate reached through shared communication.

The weakness is that the paper’s most directly relevant named results are algorithmic. It has no numbered hardness theorem to anchor a claim that discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum. Its public-hardness discussion invokes a result from Castiglioni, Celli, and Gatti (2020a) in prose; I would not present that as a numbered anchor from this paper. It does suggest a useful next question: does exact public-signaling hardness survive when the electorate is given by rational masses over a moderate number of complete utility types? The other main limitation is that Theorem 3’s guarantee deliberately relaxes the outer majority and allows \(\epsilon\)-persuasiveness. Whether the same high-multiplicity regime admits an exact result, or whether hardness remains because of the state and district structure, is open in this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed mirror of Theorem 3. Semi-public signals in the paper are district-wide messages that can encode a different recommendation for each named voter. A continuous distribution over payoff types does not say which members of one type receive which recommendation. If every member of a type must respond alike, the mirror removes recommendation patterns available in the paper. If the message can select an arbitrary fraction of that type, the model needs a way for members to identify themselves as selected—effectively adding an agent-level coordinate. That makes the claimed compression to district-type masses incomplete, and the theorem’s posterior-discretization argument does not by itself establish the proposed continuous guarantee.

A stronger mirror could add an atomless public label and let a district’s common message specify a measurable recommendation function over those labels. That is mathematically coherent, but it changes the input and signal model: one must explain how labels are supplied and represented, and why a message that targets label-specific groups still counts as semi-public. This is a real modeling and algorithmic obligation. It is not, however, decisive: an anonymous random partition of each type’s mass may provide a natural implementation, and the resulting aggregate allocations might still admit a finite description. So this objection weakens the stated mirror more than it rules out a worthwhile one.

It does not dispose of Theorem 1. For private signaling, a distribution over type-level recommendation shares can preserve the relevant incentives and election lottery: persuasion constraints depend on each type’s marginal recommendation probability, while the outcome depends on the district vote shares. A law over those shares can retain correlations between recommendations that affect the probability of winning. This is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and the paper’s named-voter LP does not prove its complexity bound—but neither does that gap make the question ill-posed. A weighted analogue may be straightforward; that would limit the likely novelty of the result, not defeat the mirror.

So the negative case has one substantial boundary question for semi-public signaling, but it cannot honestly eliminate the private mirror. The claim that *no scenario* yields a worthwhile continuous mirror is weaker than the case for at least one: large electorates with recurring payoff types fit the paper’s setting, and private mass-level persuasion remains a well-posed computational problem.
