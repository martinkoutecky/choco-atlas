The continuous mirror question

Given fixed districts, additional candidates \(A\), placement prices \(\pi(i,a)\), a budget \(B\), and for each district \(i\) a binary-rational distribution \(\mu_i\) over supported rankings of \(C_i \cup A\), is there a partition \(A_1,\ldots,A_k\) of \(A\) with total placement cost at most \(B\) such that every \(a\in A_i\) maximizes its \(R\)-score in \(C_i\cup A_i\), and every district with \(A_i\ne\varnothing\) has at most two winners?

The model it lives in

Each type is a district and a complete ranking of its candidates and \(A\), carrying rational voter mass \(\mu_{i,\succ}\). The decision variables are binary placements \(y_{ia}\in\{0,1\}\), with \(\sum_i y_{ia}=1\); the objective is to minimize \(\sum_{i,a}\pi(i,a)y_{ia}\), subject to the budget and winner constraints. District scores are the mass-weighted \(R\)-scores of rankings restricted to the candidates present after placement.

The objection that survived

The hardness rests on indivisible candidate-to-district assignments, so this anchor does not expose a continuous-optimization advantage; the proponent acknowledges the answer-preserving normalization but does not answer that contribution limitation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.10’s two-winner hardness for nontrivial pure scoring rules and Theorem 5.4’s unbounded \(t\)-approval hardness. It leaves the paper’s other worst-case, parameterized, and model-relationship results without separate mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed or parameterized total support size \(\tau\), what is the complexity of priced two-winner recampaigning under nontrivial pure scoring rules?
  • Do restricted ranking domains or bounded support yield algorithms parameterized by \(\tau\) and the number of candidates?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror I believe in is priced two-winner recampaigning over continuous district populations, for a nontrivial pure scoring rule \(R\). In district \(i\), a type is the district together with a complete ranking \(\succ\) of \(C_i\cup A\). Its mass \(\mu_{i,\succ}\) is the fraction of that district’s voters with that ranking; the masses sum to \(1\), except that an empty district can be represented by an empty electorate. The input gives each district’s fixed candidates, the supports and binary-rational masses, the additional candidates \(A\), placement prices \(\pi(i,a)\), and a budget \(B\).

A solution is a partition \(A_1,\ldots,A_k\) of \(A\): candidate \(a\) is placed in the district whose part contains it. For each district, score candidates in \(C_i\cup A_i\) using the ranking types restricted to that candidate set. In particular,
\[ \mathrm{sc}_i(c;A_i) =\sum_{\succ}\mu_{i,\succ}\, s^{(|C_i|+|A_i|)}_{\operatorname{pos}_{\succ|_{C_i\cup A_i}}(c)}. \]
Let \(W_i(A_i)\) be the maximizers. The instance is yes if the total placement cost is at most \(B\), every \(a\in A_i\) belongs to \(W_i(A_i)\), and every district with \(A_i\ne\varnothing\) has \(|W_i(A_i)|\le 2\). Setting every price to \(1\) and \(B=|A|\) gives the unpriced version. Its input-size parameters include the number of candidates and districts, total support size \(\tau\), and the rational-mass bit length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a state party placing a relatively small slate of candidates across fixed, populous districts, where each district has many voters but far fewer recurring complete ballot types: for example, \(N_i\) in the hundreds of thousands or millions, with \(\tau_i\) in the hundreds or thousands. Repeated party-line rankings or other recurring ballot patterns give the types a concrete interpretation. This is a scenario, not a claim that all elections have small support. It preserves the paper’s fixed districts, its candidate-placement decision, and its winner condition. Only the electorate representation changes: each finite list of ballots becomes a distribution over ballot types.

My strongest anchor is Theorem 4.10, proved in this paper. It states that for every nontrivial pure scoring rule \(R\), both priced and unpriced \(R\)-Constructive \(2\)-Recampaigning are NP-complete. The continuous question is the priced and unpriced problem just defined with \(R\)’s scores computed from the district distributions. I expect hardness to transfer, so this is a Class B mirror. Given a finite district with \(n_i>0\) votes, set \(\mu_{i,\succ}\) to the number of votes of type \(\succ\), divided by \(n_i\). Every candidate’s score is then the original score divided by \(n_i\), so the winner set is unchanged. The reduction in Theorem 4.10 therefore gives continuous instances in polynomial time. Conversely, a proposed placement is a polynomial-size certificate, and its rational scores and winner sets can be checked in polynomial time. The mirror remains NP-complete. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in assigning candidates to districts, rather than in distinguishing individual voters.

A second, complementary anchor is Theorem 5.4, also proved in this paper. It states that for every \(t\ge 1\), unbounded constructive recampaigning under \(t\)-approval is para-NP-hard with respect to the number of districts, in both priced and unpriced settings. The paper’s reduction gives NP-hardness with just two districts. The corresponding continuous question uses the same district distributions, placements, and prices, but removes the cap on the number of winners in a district receiving candidates. I expect this hardness to transfer by the same ballot-to-mass construction; the reduction’s two-district bound means the continuous unbounded problem is para-NP-hard as well. I consider Theorem 4.10 the stronger lead because it covers every nontrivial pure scoring rule in the bounded case; Theorem 5.4 shows the same population mirror reaches a distinct, unbounded regime.

I think the authors would recognize both questions as their problems: candidate identities remain indivisible, candidates are still assigned to predetermined districts, and a placed candidate must still win under the same voting rule. This does not make outcomes or campaign effort continuous. It makes the electorate continuous, while expected per-capita scores follow directly from the paper’s scoring model. The scope here is these two hardness results, not a claim that every relationship or classification in the paper needs a separate mirror.

The weakest point is also the clearest high-multiplicity connection: every binary-rational distribution can be scaled to a finite electorate with integer vote counts. So this is not a new voting semantics; it is a succinct population representation of the same score arithmetic. That limits any claim that the continuous model changes the answer. It does not undercut the mirror: the paper lists ballots explicitly, whereas the mirror measures complexity in support size and mass bit length, and the hardness reductions still fit that encoding. The expected outcome is a useful boundary result: continuous population does not dissolve hardness rooted in discrete candidate-to-district assignments.

For the bounded mirror, a natural next question is whether small ballot support, restricted rankings, or other scoring structure yields algorithms in terms of \(\tau\) and \(L\). For the unbounded mirror, it is whether the two-district hardness persists under bounded support or structured preference domains.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both anchors continuize only the representation of the voters. Once a candidate placement is fixed, each district’s outcome is determined by a finite vector of rational scores. There is no decision variable that transfers or allocates voter mass: the choices remain which district receives each indivisible candidate.

This applies equally to the stronger versions of both mirrors. For Theorem 4.10, take the ballot frequencies in each district and normalize them to rational masses. Every score is scaled by the same positive factor within that district, so the winner set and the reduction are preserved. For Theorem 5.4, the same translation preserves the two-district reduction and its parameterized hardness. Conversely, any rational type distribution can be scaled by a common denominator into a finite electorate with exactly the same winners. That denominator may be exponentially large in the mass bit length, so the encodings can have different complexity; still, the voting semantics and the candidate-placement problem are identical.

That gives the negative case its narrow force: the proposed mirrors appear to yield an immediate encoding transfer, not a new continuous optimization problem. There is no fractional campaign action, mass transfer, or pricing problem over voter types for continuous-optimization methods to exploit. Theorem 5.4 makes the point especially sharply: hardness already persists with two districts, while the combinatorial choices concern candidate identities. Repeating the same ballot types to represent millions of voters changes the population scale but not the assignment problem.

This does not make the anchors invalid. The paper has explicit computational theorems, and its results do not already model a continuous population. Nor is high multiplicity a bad scenario here: large districts with recurring ballot types are plausible. A question about support-restricted or \(\tau\)-parameterized recampaigning could be worthwhile, but it would need its own result; these theorems alone do not establish that such restrictions help.

So the case against is a contribution argument, not a modeling objection: the unrestricted mirrors may amount to routine normalization plus transferred hardness, with little new structure to chart. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, that is weak. In particular, the argument does not defeat either anchor as a well-posed mirror, and I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no scenario makes this worth pursuing.
