The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Sections 6–7 report computed Banzhaf and Coleman indices; §6 relies on an algorithm cited as Leech [22]. Section 8 proposes an iterative weight adjustment, but says only that *if* it converges, the resulting weights have the desired property. I can’t honestly promote any of these into a named anchor. So the paper offers no eligible anchor for the requested case.

There is still a plausible mirror of the computations in §§6–7. Imagine a large federation of local cooperatives voting in a weighted congress. Many cooperatives share the same charter tier and therefore the same vote weight. Let \(T\) be the finite set of tiers, \(\mu_t\) the fraction of cooperatives in tier \(t\), \(a_t\) its vote weight, and \(q\in[1/2,1)\) the passage quota. Mass is the fraction of member organizations in each tier; the coalition decision is which organizations vote together. The regime is \(N\) organizations over relatively few tiers, with each tier’s members interchangeable for the voting-power calculation.

A precise candidate is High-Multiplicity Banzhaf Power Limit. For admissible \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral, form a game with \(N\mu_t\) members of each tier and total weight \(W_N=\sum_t N\mu_t a_t\). For a member \(i\) of tier \(t\), let
\[ \eta_t(N)=\left|\left\{S\subseteq P_N\setminus\{i\}:\ w(S)\le qW_N<w(S)+a_t\right\}\right|, \]
the number of coalitions for which adding that member swings the outcome. Define tier \(t\)’s aggregate Banzhaf share by
\[ B_t(N)=\frac{N\mu_t\,\eta_t(N)}{\sum_{r\in T}N\mu_r\,\eta_r(N)}. \]
Given \(T,\mu,(a_t)\), and \(q\), the problem is to determine the limit of the vector \((B_t(N))_{t\in T}\) as \(N\) grows along a specified admissible sequence, or characterize its subsequential limits if it does not converge. The answer is the distribution of total swing power across tiers. This mirrors the paper’s questions about how power compares with voting weight and how changing the quota changes power. For \(q=1/2\), a natural expected limit under standard lattice conditions is \(B_t\to\mu_ta_t/\sum_r\mu_ra_r\); for supermajorities, the limit needs analysis. I would expect tractability for fixed support, using finite-type probability or exponential-tilting methods, but that is a conjecture about this proposed problem, not a result of Leech’s paper.

The strongest feature of this case is that it keeps the same formal voting game and the same notion of a swing. Its weak point is also central: an individual member’s influence vanishes in an atomless population. Defining the mirror through growing finite replicas is necessary to preserve what Banzhaf power measures, and whether the authors or a referee would accept that limit as their computational question remains uncertain. The proposal covers the power-distribution and quota analyses, not the paper’s weight-reconstruction procedure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The threshold problem is that the paper contains no named computational result to mirror. Sections 6–7 report calculated power indices and draw conclusions about them; they do not state a theorem about the complexity of computing those indices. Section 8 gives an iterative weight update and explains why a limit point would have the desired property, but does not establish convergence or a runtime bound. The algorithm for the paper’s calculations is attributed to Leech [22]. So the proposed limit is not a continuization of a computational result asserted in this paper.

The strongest rescue is the proposed federation of many cooperatives in a few vote-weight tiers. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and Banzhaf power is well-defined for its finite games. The obstacle appears when turning it into a continuous-population question. A Banzhaf swing is an individual, positive-weight member changing a losing coalition into a winning one. In an atomless population, one member has zero mass and cannot swing the outcome. Nor does the type-mass vector itself supply the finite coalition counts: under independent fair votes, coalition mass has a deterministic limit, while the swing counts depend on fluctuations at the vanishing, individual-member scale.

The proposed \(B_t(N)\) handles this by counting swings in finite replicas, aggregating by type, and normalizing before taking a limit. That may define a worthwhile asymptotic voting-power problem. But the information it preserves comes from discrete boundary effects in the replicas; the continuous society does not have those pivotal members. If the limit fails to converge, specifying subsequential limits makes the object more precise, but it still leaves the computational question centered on finite-game swing counts and their asymptotics. The paper’s computed IMF results do not pose that question.

One could also mirror the weight-reconstruction section by choosing type-level weights to realize target aggregate power shares. That is a coherent design problem, but it inherits the same issue: individual power is atomic, while aggregate cohort power requires a finite-replica normalization. It also replaces the paper’s target—power shares for named countries and directors—with a new target over replicated types.

So the negative case is strong under the requested “named computational result” standard, and the atomless limit gives a substantive objection to treating the proposed \(B_t(N)\) as a direct continuous mirror. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile adjacent project exists: the high-multiplicity Banzhaf limit could interest the authors. I would describe it as a possible asymptotic extension, not a continuous computational result of this paper.
