What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that Proposition 1 has a natural population-continuous analogue. But it is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a computational anchor: the paper contains no named result about complexity, algorithms, or parameterized tractability. So this is a plausible mirror of one result, but it does not make a positive case within ChoCo’s computational scope.

Proposition 1, proved in the paper, says: if \(n\) is odd and the domain \(\Omega\) contains every profile of strict rankings, then no choice correspondence on \(\Omega\) satisfies Weak Pareto, Majority, and no-spoiler together. The setting has a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large electorate can contain millions of voters but only a few distinct ballots. A voter type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate holding that ranking.

One possible continuous analogue is Continuous No-Spoiler Existence. Fix a finite alternative set \(X\) and let a society be a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(X\). Ask whether there is a map \(F\), defined on all such societies, that assigns each one a nonempty subset of \(X\) and satisfies the mass versions of Weak Pareto and Majority, plus this no-spoiler condition: if \(F(\mu)\ne\{a\}\), move \(a\) to the bottom of every ranking to obtain \(\mu^a\), and require \(F(\mu^a)=F(\mu)\setminus\{a\}\).

The analogy is concrete, and the continuous version is already impossible on a society with four types:

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{ranking} & \text{mass}\\ x\succ y\succ z & 0.4\\ z\succ x\succ y & 0.2\\ y\succ z\succ x & 0.2\\ z\succ y\succ x & 0.2 \end{array} \]

Place any other alternatives below \(x,y,z\), so Weak Pareto excludes them. Demoting \(z\) makes \(x\) uniquely first for mass \(0.6\), demoting \(x\) makes \(y\) uniquely first for mass \(0.6\), and demoting \(y\) makes \(z\) uniquely first for mass \(0.6\). Majority therefore forces the corresponding singleton after each demotion. For each possible nonempty \(F(\mu)\subseteq\{x,y,z\}\), one of those demotions contradicts no-spoiler. This gives the authors a recognizable population-level version of their question, with an explicit society whose support stays tiny as the electorate grows.

The weakness is also decisive: this asks whether an axiomatic choice rule exists, not for an algorithm or a complexity classification. It has no natural A/B/C status in the programme’s trichotomy. The mass translation is a strong continuous-population mirror of Proposition 1, but the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Its propositions and corollaries are axiomatic existence and incompatibility claims: they ask whether a choice correspondence can satisfy specified properties on a domain of profiles. They do not ask for an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization method. That is a decisive mismatch with ChoCo’s computational programme.

The proponent’s mirror of Proposition 1 is nevertheless a good continuous *axiomatic* mirror. A large electorate with repeated rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the proposed four-type society is a clear mass analogue: demoting each of \(x,y,z\) makes another alternative uniquely top for mass \(0.6\). The contradiction with no-spoiler is concrete. I would not reject this mirror on population grounds, or because its answer is immediate.

The best attempt to make it computational would be: given a rational finite-support society, choose a set that can be extended to a global correspondence satisfying the mass versions of Weak Pareto, Majority, and no-spoiler. But the global extension requirement is the paper’s original axiomatic existence question; if it holds across the whole domain, the task is already settled by an impossibility argument. If instead the task asks for a locally permissible set at just the input society, the paper supplies no such algorithmic problem or rule representation. Defining one would add a new computational question rather than mirror a named result.

The same issue applies to the paper’s other results, including the strengthened Pareto, no-loser spoiler, and Point Majority claims: they are all about compatibility of axioms. Treating criteria or states as populations would be a weaker fit than the voter interpretation, but that is not needed for the negative case. The honest limit is that this paper does have a worthwhile continuous-population analogue for axiomatic social choice; the case against is specifically that it offers no worthwhile mirror *for ChoCo’s computational complexity programme*.
