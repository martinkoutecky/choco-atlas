The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(X\), rational masses \(\nu_{\pi,a}\) of fixed voters of ranking \(\pi\) and per-unit weight \(a\), and rational masses \(\rho_a\) of strategic voters of weight \(a\), with all strategic voters sharing target \(c\) and allowed every ranking, does there exist a rational allocation \(q_{a,\pi}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{\pi}q_{a,\pi}=\rho_a\), that makes \(c\) the unique Schulze winner? The masses sum to \(1\); the resulting pairwise margin is \(\omega_q(x,y)=\sum_{\pi,a}\nu_{\pi,a}a\,\sigma_\pi(x,y)+\sum_a a\sum_\pi q_{a,\pi}\sigma_\pi(x,y)\), where \(\sigma_\pi(x,y)=1\) if \(\pi\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\), and \(-1\) otherwise.

The model it lives in

Use voter types \((\pi,a)\) for fixed voters and \((a,c,\mathcal R)\) for strategic voters, where \(\mathcal R\) is their shared set of allowed rankings. Input their rational masses and per-unit weights; choose mass variables \(q_{a,\pi}\) summing to each strategic class’s mass, and ask whether the resulting Schulze margins make \(c\) the unique winner. Theorem 2’s aggregate-margin criterion and common-ballot construction predict a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\), support sizes, and rational input bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

Theorems 2 and 10 cover constructive weighted coalitional manipulation with unique-winner and nonunique-winner goals. The mirror leaves aside the destructive WCM adaptation mentioned in the conclusion and other Schulze problems such as bribery, control, and margin of victory.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the aggregate-margin criterion retain polynomial bit complexity in \(m\), support sizes, and \(L\) after clearing rational denominators?
  • Does tractability persist when strategic types have different allowed ballot sets, or when the task is to minimize the strategic mass needed for success?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the unique-winner result. The paper’s formal input is finite, but its weighted voters already represent coalitions of voters, and the algorithm works for an unbounded number of manipulators. That makes a large population with repeated task-relevant types a natural regime for the continuous version.

Take a population of total mass \(1\). A fixed voter type is a ranking \(\pi\) together with a per-person voting weight \(a\); the input gives its rational mass \(\nu_{\pi,a}\). The strategic population is divided into weight classes \(a\), with rational mass \(\rho_a\) in each class. Its members share the target candidate \(c\) and the same unrestricted set of possible ballots. The full type therefore includes the ranking and weight for fixed voters, or the target, weight, and available actions for strategic voters. A regime with millions of voters but only \(\tau\) fixed ranking-and-weight types and \(\kappa\) strategic weight classes is plausible, for example, when an organization coordinates a large bloc of voters to elect a shared target.

For each strategic weight class, let \(q_{a,\pi}\) be the mass assigned to ranking \(\pi\), with \(q_{a,\pi}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_\pi q_{a,\pi}=\rho_a\). The resulting pairwise margin is
\[ \omega_q(x,y) = \sum_{(\pi,a)} \nu_{\pi,a}a\,\sigma_\pi(x,y) + \sum_a a\sum_\pi q_{a,\pi}\sigma_\pi(x,y), \]
where \(\sigma_\pi(x,y)=1\) if \(\pi\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\), and \(-1\) otherwise. A solution is an exact rational allocation \(q\) for which \(c\) wins under Schulze’s path-strength rule, or a report that no such allocation exists. The question is about manipulating a continuous population; \(q\) is a distribution of integral ballots across mass, not a lottery over election outcomes.

Lead anchor — Theorem 2, proved in this paper. Given a weighted profile of nonmanipulators, weights for the manipulators, and a target \(c\), the authors compute a function \(U:X\to\mathbb Z\) in polynomial time. A successful manipulation making \(c\) the *unique* Schulze winner exists if and only if, for every \(x\ne c\), \(U(x)>\omega_{\text{nonmanip}}(x,c)-W\), where \(W\) is the manipulators’ total weight. The paper also constructs successful votes in polynomial time. Its continuous mirror is Continuous Unique-Winner Schulze Coalition Manipulation: given the rational masses and weight classes above, determine whether an allocation \(q\) makes \(c\) the unique winner and return one if it exists. I expect this problem to be in P in \(m,\tau,\kappa,L\), where \(L\) is the rational input bit length. The theorem’s computation depends on the aggregate weighted-majority graph and \(W\); those can be computed from the compressed support without expanding the population. Schulze margins and path strengths scale together by any positive factor, so clearing rational denominators also connects this mirror to the paper’s integer-weight instances.

A second, distinct anchor — Theorem 10, proved in this paper. For the same weighted input, the paper computes a polynomial-time function \(U\) such that a manipulation making \(c\) a (not necessarily unique) Schulze winner exists if and only if \(U(x)\ge\omega_{\text{nonmanip}}(x,c)-W\) for every \(x\ne c\). It too yields a polynomial-time decision and construction algorithm. Its mirror, Continuous Nonunique-Winner Schulze Coalition Manipulation, asks for a mass allocation \(q\) making \(c\) a Schulze winner, with ties permitted. I also expect this problem to be in P, by the same aggregate-margin and denominator-scaling argument, with the paper’s weak inequalities preserved.

The two anchors cover the paper’s constructive weighted coalitional manipulation results. Theorem 2 is the stronger lead because it handles the stricter unique-winner requirement; Theorem 10 earns its place as the corresponding nonunique-winner problem with a genuinely different success condition. The paper proves no hardness result, so these are necessarily algorithmic anchors; neither should be presented as showing that continuous populations dissolve discrete hardness.

This mirror is plausible because it preserves the paper’s central question: can a coalition with a shared target choose ballots that make that candidate win under Schulze? The continuum changes how the electorate is represented and lets strategic mass be divided among ballots. It does not weaken the winner condition. In fact, the paper’s construction says all manipulators can vote the same way, so in these mirrors a successful allocation can be represented compactly by one ranking used by every strategic weight class. That structural collapse is part of the result, not a simplification imposed on the question.

The useful follow-up questions are whether the characterization still depends only on total strategic voting weight when strategic types have different allowed ballot sets, and whether minimizing the mass of manipulators needed for success remains tractable. The mirror here covers the paper’s two constructive weighted manipulation variants; it does not claim results about bribery, control, or margin of victory.

My weakest point is that the mirror inherits so much of the finite theorem that the continuous model may reveal little new structure: exact rational scaling and the common-ballot construction make the algorithmic answer unusually direct. Still, the population representation is a sensible high-multiplicity instance regime, and the mirror asks the authors’ actual manipulation question over that regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat either anchor on whether it is a sensible continuous-population question. Theorem 2 and Theorem 10 are computational results about manipulation, and a large bloc sharing a target is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Unique-winner and nonunique-winner conditions both survive normalization: positive rescaling preserves the path-strength comparisons, including ties.

The strongest case against is about priority, not validity. The paper already represents coalitions through voter weights, and its algorithms work from aggregate pairwise margins and total manipulator weight. For rational masses, scaling gives a finite weighted instance; moreover, the construction says all strategic mass can use one ballot. So these mirrors appear to add little new algorithmic structure.

But under the programme’s stated standard, that is not enough to reject them: a known or immediate answer does not make a well-posed mirror unworthy, and weighted or high-multiplicity structure supports the regime rather than colliding with it. The honest negative case is therefore weak; I see no defensible fundamental objection to either anchor.
