The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\) with \(d\)-attribute vectors, committee size \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of complete attribute-approval ballots with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether there is a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) with \(\operatorname{AV}_{\mu}(W)\ge q\) that satisfies mass-SJR. Mass-SJR requires that for every subpopulation \(\nu\) with \(0\le \nu_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\nu_t\ge 1/k\), if all types with \(\nu_t>0\) approve a common attribute value in some dimension, then the union of those types’ approved attribute values intersects \(\{c[j]:c\in W,\ j\in[d]\}\).

The model it lives in

The input is a candidate slate with its attribute vectors, a fixed committee size, and a distribution over supported complete approval-ballot types. The decision variable is the discrete committee \(W\); its per-capita objective is \(\operatorname{AV}_{\mu}(W)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{c\in W}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}[c[j]\in A_t^j]\), subject to the mass-SJR condition and the threshold \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 8’s highest-AV justified-committee problem; Lemma 7’s CJR-feasibility result also has a plausible mass analogue. It leaves the paper’s GAV properties, standard aggregation-rule analyses, and other representation results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of AV-optimal SJR over a continuous society when parameterized by support size \(\tau\), committee size \(k\), or attribute dimension \(d\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s “justified committee with highest AV” problem. It already asks whether a committee can score well while representing sufficiently large groups of voters. Replacing voter counts by population masses preserves that question’s structure.

Take a finite candidate set \(C\), with each candidate \(c\) having attributes \(c[j]\in D^j\) for \(j\in[d]\), and fix committee size \(k\). A voter type \(t\) is a complete attribute approval ballot \((A_t^1,\ldots,A_t^d)\), where \(A_t^j\subseteq D^j\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the supported types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Its support size is \(\tau\); record running time in \(m,d,k,\tau\), the encoding length \(L\), and the input description of the attributes and ballots.

For a committee \(W\), define its per-capita AV score by
\[ \operatorname{AV}_{\mu}(W)= \frac{1}{d}\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{c\in W}\sum_{j=1}^d \mathbf{1}[c[j]\in A_t^j]. \]
To state simple justified representation (SJR), let \(\nu_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) describe a subpopulation, with \(\sum_t\nu_t\ge 1/k\). If the types with \(\nu_t>0\) share an approved value in some dimension \(j\), then their union of approvals must contain an attribute of some selected candidate, possibly in another dimension. The committee is SJR if this holds for every such subpopulation.

Call the resulting decision problem AV-optimal SJR\(_\infty\): given \((C,d,k,T,\mu)\) and a rational threshold \(q\), is there a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) that is SJR and has \(\operatorname{AV}_{\mu}(W)\ge q\)? A yes-solution is the committee itself. This keeps the committee and its candidates discrete; the population is the continuous object.

The anchor is Lemma 8, which states that the justified committee with highest AV problem is NP-complete for \(k\ge2,d\ge2\), and for \(k\ge3,d=1\). The paper gives a Set Cover reduction for the attribute case \(d\ge2\); it also says the one-dimensional hardness was obtained simultaneously and appeared in Bredereck et al. (2019). In its decision formulation, the paper asks whether there is a size-\(k\) justified committee whose approval count reaches a threshold while the specified residual approval counts for unselected candidate attributes are each below \(n/k\). The continuous question above uses the same underlying problem: the score threshold is normalized per capita, and the group quota \(n/k\) becomes mass \(1/k\). I consider this the lead anchor.

There is a natural second mirror, CJR-feasibility\(_\infty\). Use the same instance, and ask whether any size-\(k\) committee satisfies compound justified representation: for every subpopulation of mass at least \(1/k\) and every dimension \(j\), if its types share an approved value in dimension \(j\), then its union of approvals in that *same* dimension intersects \(W^j\). The anchor is Lemma 7, which states that checking whether a CJR committee exists is NP-complete for \(k\ge2,d\ge2\). The paper presents a Set Cover reduction for this result. This is a feasibility question, not a score-maximization question, so it supports the case independently of Lemma 8.

Both mirrors have a credible large-population setting: a city or regional electorate chooses a small committee from a fixed slate of candidates described by standardized attributes, and many residents submit the same complete approval ballot. The types are those repeated ballots; their masses are population shares. This is plausible when the slate and ballot format are fixed and recurring coalitions produce a moderate number of ballot patterns. The \(1/k\) quota then says directly that a group comprising at least that fraction of the electorate deserves representation.

The hardness transfer is especially clean. Given a discrete profile of \(n\) voters, group identical ballots into types and set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). AV scores then agree after normalization. For SJR and CJR, a group’s common approvals and union depend on which types it contains, not on how much of each type is selected. If a continuous group violates representation, taking all discrete voters of its positive-mass types preserves those approvals and unions and only increases its size; conversely, any discrete violating group induces a continuous one. Thus the paper’s discrete hardness instances embed in these continuous questions. I expect hardness transfers for both anchors, rather than hardness caused specifically by the continuum. For any proposed committee, the continuous representation conditions can also be checked by summing masses of the types that share each attribute and remain unrepresented.

The paper’s high-level formulation makes these mirrors recognizable as the authors’ problems: it already studies group representation, attribute approvals, committee size, and approval score. In particular, changing \(n/k\) to \(1/k\) preserves the meaning of justified representation rather than replacing it with a smoother but different welfare goal.

The main limitation is that the hardness results do not establish hardness in the especially compressed regime \(\tau\ll n\), and they do not rule out fixed-parameter algorithms in support size \(\tau\). The ballot support may grow with the reduction, so the large-electorate scenario is plausible but not itself certified by those theorems. Lemma 7 is also a less secure anchor: its reduction sketch is terse and has inconsistent terminology, so I would want it checked carefully. If that claim does not withstand review, AV-optimal SJR\(_\infty\) remains a concrete mirror anchored by Lemma 8. The next questions are whether either problem becomes tractable for small \(\tau\), and how its complexity varies with \(k\), \(d\), and the candidate attribute structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat both anchors. The high-multiplicity setting is credible: a large electorate choosing a small committee from a fixed slate can have many residents with the same complete approval ballot over candidate attributes. The population is the continuous object; the candidates and committee remain discrete.

For a committee \(W\), SJR has a natural mass version. For each attribute \(a\), consider the mass of voters who approve \(a\) and approve none of the attributes in \(W\). SJR requires that mass to be less than \(1/k\). CJR has the analogous condition within each dimension. These are well-defined threshold conditions on a distribution over ballot types, and the per-capita AV score is a direct normalization of the paper’s score.

The discrete instances also embed cleanly: group identical ballots and assign type \(t\) mass \(n_t/n\). A violating discrete group yields a violating mass group, and a violating mass group can be expanded to all voters of its types. Thus Lemmas 7 and 8 suggest genuine continuous computational questions, with hardness transfer as a plausible outcome. Lemma 8 alone is enough to sustain the case even if Lemma 7’s terse reduction deserves closer checking.

The strongest negative observation is that these mirrors may mostly expose candidate-selection combinatorics, rather than create an obvious column-generation or continuous-optimization opportunity. But that predicts a less tractable result; it does not make the question unworthy, especially since mapping where continuization stops helping is part of the programme. I therefore don’t have a sound case that no worthwhile mirror exists.
