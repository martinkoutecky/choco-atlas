The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational-complexity result of the kind the prompt requires as an anchor. Theorem 7 is the closest candidate, but it is a learning theorem, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Proved in the paper, it says that with Gaussian signals, power precision costs, vanishing entry fees, and diminishing nonsummable popularity updates, complete learning from every interior prior and initial popularity index occurs exactly when \(k\geq1\) and \(\beta>\tfrac12\). It also gives a positive lower bound on purchased precision, and hence a finite expected purchase count, when \(k<1\) and \(\beta>\tfrac12\). Theorem 13 similarly gives formulas for terminal error and expected purchases in a binary-signal model; those formulas are not a complexity classification.

There is a potentially relevant population story in the analyst interpretation: many analysts make binary recommendations, and one could describe fractions sharing the same information technology and payoff parameters. But the paper’s results concern agents arriving one at a time, whose actions update a common belief and popularity index. Replacing them with a mass acting together changes what their aggregate actions reveal and how popularity evolves. The paper’s popularity statistic \(p_t\) is a frequency over past discrete actions, not a society supplied as a distribution over voter types for a computational problem. Its continuous objects are beliefs, signal precision, and time; those do not establish a continuous-population mirror under ChoCo’s scope.

So the honest positive-side conclusion is limited: the model offers primitives from which a future computational problem over a distribution of analyst types might be built, but that would be a new extension. There is no named computational anchor here to mirror, and I would not present one as though the paper supplied it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “CASE FOR” gives no affirmative anchor: it explicitly declines to identify one. It names Theorem 7 and Theorem 13 as the closest candidates, then explains why neither is a computational result. That is the strongest objection to a mirror here. Theorem 7 characterizes when a sequential learning process reaches the true state; Theorem 13 computes error and purchase counts for a random walk. Neither asks for an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization over societies. The paper supplies no named computational result for a continuous-population version to inherit.

A stronger candidate would take a distribution over analyst types—say, different information technologies, costs, and contrarian incentives—and ask whether the society learns the state. But the paper’s mechanism depends on agents arriving one at a time: each action changes the next agent’s popularity incentive, while public beliefs update from that individual action. A distribution of types alone does not specify the order that drives those updates. If instead a continuum of identical analysts acts as a cohort, aggregation changes the learning mechanism. For Gaussian signals with precision \(\rho>0\), an action is determined by whether the signal exceeds
\[ s^*=\frac12+\frac{\logit c-\logit\mu}{\rho}. \]
The cohort’s exact action share differs across the two states, since its conditional share is \(1-\Phi(\sqrt{\rho}(s^*-\theta))\). Observing that share therefore reveals the state immediately. The gradual learning threshold in Theorem 7 has disappeared. Preserving noisy, gradual updating would require finite cohorts or added common shocks, which changes the model.

Theorem 13’s apparent computational hook fares similarly. Its terminal-error formulas rely on a sequence of individual signal realizations producing a nearest-neighbor random walk in log odds. With continuum cohorts, signal realizations aggregate into deterministic shares, so that walk no longer describes the process. A continuum of independent economies could preserve a distribution of random walks, but that continuizes the uncertainty across economies, not the population within one society. A planner’s problem—such as allocating research subsidies across analyst types—could be studied, but the paper has no planner or welfare objective; its conclusion explicitly leaves welfare comparisons open. That would be a new research question, not a mirror of Theorem 13.

The negative case is strongest for this paper’s actual results: its continuous objects are beliefs, experiment precision, popularity, and time, while its agents remain sequential and consequential one by one. A cohort or planner model may be worth pursuing on its own. The case is weaker against the absolute claim that no useful computational question could ever be built from these primitives; one can always add a population and an objective. But that possibility does not supply a worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result this paper actually proves.
