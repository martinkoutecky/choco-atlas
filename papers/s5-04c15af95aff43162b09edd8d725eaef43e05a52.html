The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) ordered reach types with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\), \(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i=1\), and per-unit-mass cleanup costs \(q_i\), compute each type's per-capita HLRS charge. Let \(M_{<k}=\sum_{j<k}\mu_j\), \(\alpha_1=1\), and \(\alpha_k=1/2\) for \(k\ge2\). The charge is \(y_i=\alpha_iq_i+\sum_{k=i+1}^{\tau}(1-\alpha_k)\mu_kq_k/M_{<k}\); the aggregate charges should satisfy \(\sum_i\mu_i y_i=\sum_i\mu_iq_i\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over ordered reach types, each type carrying mass \(\mu_i\) and per-unit-mass cleanup cost \(q_i\). The output is the charge vector \(y\) under mass-based HLRS sharing: each reach pays its prescribed fraction of its own per-capita cost, while each downstream reach's residual cost is shared equally per unit of upstream mass. There is no optimization objective; the task is exact rational evaluation of the allocation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are axiomatizations and game-theoretic identities: Theorems 3.1–3.4 characterize cost-sharing rules, Lemma 4.1 establishes a structural property of a game, and Corollary 4.2 and Theorem 4.3 identify solution concepts. None asserts a complexity result such as membership in P or hardness.

The closest foothold is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. It says that for every pollution cost-sharing problem \((N,c)\), the Shapley value, nucleolus, and \(\tau\)-value of the paper’s pollution cost-sharing game all give the Half Local Responsibility Sharing allocation: \(x_1=c_1+\sum_{k=2}^n c_k/[2(k-1)]\), and \(x_i=c_i/2+\sum_{k=i+1}^n c_k/[2(k-1)]\) for \(i\ge2\). The proof computes the Shapley allocation; the equality with the other two values uses the paper’s Corollary 4.2 and cited results about additive and 2-additive games. This is a substantial named result, but it is not a computational-complexity result under the requested standard.

If a weaker, non-complexity foothold is acceptable, I would propose Continuous HLRS Evaluation as the lead mirror. Imagine a river basin with many farms or discharge sites, grouped into \(\tau\) ordered reaches. Agents in reach \(i\) share a location, per-unit-mass cleanup cost \(q_i\), and responsibility level; the number of agents \(N\) is large compared with \(\tau\). Let \(\mu_i>0\) be reach \(i\)’s population mass, with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and let \(M_{<k}=\sum_{j<k}\mu_j\). The total cleanup cost at reach \(k\) is \(\mu_kq_k\). Given \(\tau\), the rational masses and costs, and HLRS responsibility levels \(\alpha_1=1\) and \(\alpha_k=1/2\) for \(k\ge2\), compute the exact per-unit-mass charge \(y_i\) for each reach, where \(y_i=\alpha_iq_i+\sum_{k=i+1}^{\tau}(1-\alpha_k)\mu_kq_k/M_{<k}\). Each reach pays its prescribed fraction of its own cost, and the remaining cost from a downstream reach is shared evenly across upstream mass. The resulting aggregate charges conserve total cost: \(\sum_i\mu_i y_i=\sum_i\mu_iq_i\).

That regime has a plausible high-multiplicity story: many emitters fall into relatively few reach-and-cost classes, and mass replaces the number of upstream agents in the sharing denominator. It is also close to the paper’s rule: the continuous model makes a necessary co-modification from counting upstream individuals to measuring upstream population mass. The evaluation problem is tractable by direct rational arithmetic, with \(O(\tau^2)\) operations and polynomial bit complexity. A natural follow-up would study convergence as the reaches become finer, or whether values of finite games converge to this allocation under replication.

The weakness is decisive for a strict ChoCo assessment: this candidate computes an explicit cost-sharing rule; it does not turn a paper result about discrete hardness into a continuous computational question. The paper’s Theorem 4.3 motivates the HLRS choice, but does not prove this grouped-population extension or a continuum identity among Shapley value, nucleolus, and \(\tau\)-value. I’d present it as a plausible adjacent mirror, not as an eligible anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.3 is the strongest possible foothold, and the proposed mass version is coherent: group emitters by reach and cost, then replace counts of upstream agents with upstream mass. But it does not supply a computational problem from this paper to continuize. The theorem identifies three game solutions with the explicit HLRS formula; it asserts no complexity result. The same is true of the α-LRS axiomatizations. The paper studies which allocation rules satisfy which properties, not the computational difficulty of finding an allocation.

In the proposed mirror, “compute the per-capita charge” means evaluate the paper’s formula after adding a population model and scaling costs by mass. That may be a useful extension, but it is not a complexity question exposed by the paper. A stronger candidate might ask for continuous Shapley or nucleolus values of a game over a continuum of emitters; that would require constructing a new game and its solution concept, while the paper’s finite game already yields HLRS directly. Asking how to choose responsibility levels or cleanup actions would add a design or optimization problem the authors did not study. Those could be worthwhile questions on their own, but they do not make a continuous computational mirror of this paper.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here: cohorts of emitters at a small number of reaches are a sensible regime. The negative case is narrower. Even granting that regime and the mass-based reformulation, the paper offers no named computational result whose complexity landscape the ChoCo programme could chart. The proposed mirror is adjacent to its allocation results, but it does not meet the programme’s computational brief.
