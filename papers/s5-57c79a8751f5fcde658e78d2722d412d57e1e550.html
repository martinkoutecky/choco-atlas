But the paper contains no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish convergence and ergodic averages; Theorem 2 establishes persistent disagreement; Theorem 3 characterizes moments; and Theorems 4–5 give bounds for large networks. None asks for the complexity of an algorithmic problem. A continuous computational question could be added to the paper’s subject, but it would not be a mirror of one of its results.

The strongest proposed anchor is Theorem 5’s large-population limit. But \(n\to\infty\) there is an analytic limit over families of finite graphs, not a society represented by a distribution over agent types. The theorem assumes graph-specific mixing and interaction structure, and studies how belief moments behave under those assumptions. Recasting it with a continuum of agents and an interaction kernel could be a sensible analytic model. It still would not supply the missing computational question.

The same holds for a stronger reconstruction from Theorem 3: ask for an algorithm to compute ergodic moments from type masses and an interaction kernel. That is a possible follow-on project, but the paper’s result is a characterization by hitting probabilities, not a complexity claim about computing them. Choosing a finite representation for the kernel, or asking how to optimize network structure or stubborn-agent influence, supplies the algorithmic problem the paper did not pose. Those choices may lead somewhere worthwhile; they do not make a computational mirror of this paper.

There is a further modeling obstacle, though it is not decisive on its own. An agent’s role depends on its position in the graph, its stubbornness and belief, and its interactions with other agents. Treating the full role as a type can leave nearly every agent in a different type. A continuum interaction kernel can preserve the pairwise structure, but that changes the object being modeled and still leaves the computational task to be specified.

So the negative case is strong for the requested programme: no named result here is an eligible computational anchor, including the best candidate, Theorem 5. The limit results and network structure make continuous *analysis* plausible; they do not establish a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror. The limit of the case is the universal claim: one can invent follow-on algorithmic questions, but their possible value would be evidence for a new project, not for continuizing a computational result in this paper.
