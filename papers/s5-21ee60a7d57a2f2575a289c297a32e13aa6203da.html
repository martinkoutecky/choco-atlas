The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a defensible computational mirror under this brief. The paper contains no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified by computational complexity.

The closest result is Theorem 6, proved here: in the strict, three-alternative case, Arrow’s impossibility statement is equivalent to saying that no social welfare function satisfying unanimity, IIA, and non-dictatorship has orientable social choices. Theorem A.3 gives the corresponding reformulation for three or more alternatives with indifference. These are axiomatic and topological results, not computational-complexity results. Theorem 4 and Theorem 5 classify topological models as a Klein bottle and a real projective plane, respectively.

One could imagine a large population represented by a distribution over rankings, with mass denoting the fraction holding each ranking. But asking whether an aggregator on that distribution satisfies Arrow-style axioms would be an extrapolation, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. The paper’s continuity concerns the topology of preference spaces; it does not make the society itself continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no computational anchor to defend. Theorem 4 and Theorem 5 classify topological models as a Klein bottle or a real projective plane; Theorem 6 and Theorem A.3 reformulate Arrow’s impossibility result; Theorem 7 and Theorem A.4 connect non-dictatorship to non-orientability. These are topological and axiomatic results. None states a complexity result for an algorithmic problem. The paper’s topology is not a continuous population model, but that alone is not the objection: the decisive gap is that there is no computational result here to mirror.

The best attempt would be to give voters’ rankings as a rational distribution \(\mu\) and ask whether an aggregator produces a cycle or satisfies Arrow-style conditions. But that requires specifying a new, effectively represented aggregator and a computational task; the theorems specify neither. Worse, the original dictatorship condition refers to a named individual, information a distribution over rankings discards. Add identities to voter types and the multiplicity disappears; discard them and dictatorship is no longer a property of the input. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so a dictator condition based on one person’s preferences loses its force. Replacing it with a condition about a positive-mass bloc could be interesting, but it would be a new axiom and a new problem.

One could still invent worthwhile computational questions about distribution-valued social choice rules, or study a particular rule such as pairwise majority on continuous societies. Those would be adjacent research, not mirrors of the results in this paper. The honest limit of the negative case is that it cannot rule out every such invention; it can say that none is grounded by the paper’s named results. Under this programme’s scope, that is enough to defeat the proposed anchors.
