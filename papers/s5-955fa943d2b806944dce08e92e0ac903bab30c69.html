The continuous mirror question

Given supported types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, house categories \(H\) with rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_h\) totaling at least one, and binary utilities \(u_t(h)\), choose nonnegative flows \(x_{t,h}\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le\sigma_h\). Is there a feasible flow assigning at least \(q\) total mass to envy-free units, where an assignment of type \(t\) to category \(h\) is envy-free exactly when \(u_t(h)\ge u_t(h')\) for every category \(h'\) receiving positive mass?

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete binary utility vector over interchangeable house categories and has population mass \(\mu_t\); each category has per-capita supply \(\sigma_h\). The flow \(x_{t,h}\) represents the fraction of a type cohort assigned to a category, while each individual still receives one whole house. Envy is measured against every category receiving positive assigned mass, as in the paper’s comparison with all assigned houses.

The objection that survived

The discrete hardness reduction may depend on indivisible assignments, and a fractional flow could make the continuous problem much easier; the proponent acknowledges this but does not resolve it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors address the maximum envy-free objective behind Theorem 3.5 and proportional assignment existence in Theorem 4.1. They leave the paper’s approximation guarantees and hardness in Theorem 3.1 and Corollaries 3.2–3.3 unestablished in the continuous setting, and do not address equitable existence in Theorem 5.1.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is threshold feasibility for maximum envy-free mass polynomial-time solvable in \(|T|\), \(|H|\), and bit length \(L\), or is occupied-category selection hard?
  • Does proportional assignment existence remain hard when type mass can split fractionally among house categories?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make the positive case around two fairness problems, with Maximum Envy-Free Mass Allocation as the lead. Both retain the paper’s underlying question: how should people be assigned one house each when fairness is measured against the other assignments?

A plausible regime is a large public or university housing allocation. There may be tens of thousands of households but only a modest number of recurring preference types and standardized housing categories, such as district-by-size combinations. A type is a household’s complete utility vector over those categories. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of households of type \(t\), and let \(\sigma_h\) be the available supply of category \(h\), also measured per capita, with \(\sum_h \sigma_h\ge 1\). The decision variable \(x_{t,h}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to category \(h\), subject to
\[ \sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,h}\le \sigma_h,\qquad x_{t,h}\ge 0. \]
Each household still receives one whole unit. Only the aggregate assignment of a cohort can be fractional. Grouping houses with identical utility vectors into a category is exact when those houses are interchangeable.

Lead: Maximum Envy-Free Mass Allocation. Given rational \(\mu,\sigma\), a binary utility matrix \(u_t(h)\in\{0,1\}\), and a threshold \(q\in[0,1]\), ask whether a feasible \(x\) exists for which at least \(q\) of the population is envy-free. Write \(y_h=\sum_t x_{t,h}\) for the assigned mass of category \(h\). The envy-free mass is
\[ E(x)= \sum_{t,h:\,u_t(h)\ge u_t(h')\ \text{for every }h'\text{ with }y_{h'}>0} x_{t,h}. \]
Thus an assigned household is counted precisely when its house is at least as good for its type as every category with positive assigned mass. Ties count as envy-free. This gives a decision problem, or equivalently the optimization problem of maximizing \(E(x)\), with input size measured in the number of supported types, housing categories, and bit length.

The anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper: given a positive integer \(k\), deciding whether an injective assignment has at least \(k\) envy-free agents is NP-complete, even with binary utilities. The paper proves this by reduction from Minimum Coverage. The mirror asks what becomes of that same maximum-envy-free objective when repeated agents and interchangeable houses are represented by masses and supplies.

My working prediction is Class C, though the theorem does not establish that classification. Envy-freeness here depends on which house categories receive any positive mass. Choosing that occupied support is a discrete decision that remains present even when cohort assignments are divisible. But the paper’s reduction does not directly prove hardness for this mirror: splitting a type across houses could destroy the reduction’s integral structure. The resulting questions are whether the problem is tractable in \((|T|,|H|,L)\), whether support selection causes hardness in its own right, and how its approximation behavior depends on type support.

A second mirror is Proportional Allocation Existence. Use the same mass-assignment model, now with nonnegative rational utilities. For each type \(t\), define its average utility over all assigned population mass as
\[ \bar u_t(x)=\sum_{s,h}x_{s,h}u_t(h). \]
This is the continuum counterpart of the paper’s average over the \(n\) assigned houses, since total population mass is normalized to one. Ask whether there is a feasible \(x\) such that every positive assignment \(x_{t,h}>0\) satisfies
\[ u_t(h)\ge \bar u_t(x). \]
The solution is the mass assignment itself; no unassigned house contributes to the average, matching the paper’s definition.

Its anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here: deciding whether any proportional assignment exists is NP-complete, by reduction from Exact 3-Set Cover. There is a particularly suggestive high-multiplicity feature in that reduction: it uses \(T=100(M+N)\) dummy agents with the same utility vector, alongside \(T\) houses that are identical in their utility vector across agent types. Those can be represented as a repeated cohort and a house category with supply \(T\). That feature supports the plausibility of this mirror, although it does not prove the reduction survives fractional assignments.

I would again tentatively expect Class C, with less confidence. The type-specific average depends on the allocation being sought, so proportionality couples the assignment flows through endogenous utility thresholds. That may preserve meaningful combinatorial difficulty, but it may also expose a continuous optimization formulation that the discrete problem obscures. Useful next questions are whether the thresholds can be handled by an LP or another continuous method, and whether hardness persists when population mass is divisible.

These mirrors cover the paper’s envy-freeness and proportionality results. They do not claim a continuous analogue of Theorem 5.1 on equitability. The weakest point is also the main open issue: the discrete hardness results may rely on indivisible agent-level choices, while these mirrors let a type’s mass split among houses. That could make either problem easier. Still, the models preserve the paper’s fairness tests, one-house-per-person semantics, and feasible assignment constraints in a plausible high-multiplicity regime. That makes them credible questions for the authors’ problem, even before their complexity is known.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither paper reduction gives evidence that its central combinatorics survives a continuous population. Theorem 3.5 encodes Minimum Coverage in agents’ utility vectors and relies on an injective, indivisible assignment. Splitting a repeated type’s mass across house categories can break that encoding. Theorem 4.1 likewise builds an exact-cover gadget around indivisible assignments; its repeated dummy agents make the high-multiplicity regime plausible, but do not show that the reduction survives fractional flows. So the proponent’s tentative Class C predictions are unsupported.

That does not defeat the mirrors. For envy-freeness, the proposed occupied-support definition is a coherent way to ask how much of a population can be envy-free. It has a real modeling wrinkle: an arbitrarily small positive mass assigned to a category can affect envy for a much larger group. But that is a feature of support-based envy, not a reason the question is ill-posed; vanishing mass and positive mass have different meanings in the continuum. A better version could make the scope of comparison explicit, but the paper’s own test—comparison with other assigned houses—already supplies a natural choice.

The proportionality mirror is also faithful to the paper’s definition: it averages each type’s utility over the assigned population, then asks whether every assigned unit meets that type’s threshold. Fractional assignment may make the problem easier, and its endogenous thresholds may make it harder. Neither possibility is an objection to asking the question. The paper has named computational results for both anchors, and the proposed public-housing regime supplies a plausible repeated-type setting with scalable supplies. The agents remain complete types, and the mass constraints preserve one house per person.

I therefore cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. The real weakness is evidential: the paper gives no result showing that either continuous problem has a worthwhile complexity landscape, and the discrete hardness proofs should not be presented as evidence that it does. But under ChoCo’s stated standard, a well-posed computational question can be worth asking even if its answer is easy or its hardness disappears. Both anchors survive that standard; Theorem 5.1 need not be mirrored for either one to do so.
