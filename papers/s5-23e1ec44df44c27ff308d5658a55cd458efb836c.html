The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is a logical claim about whether decisions obey axioms; Theorem 2 and Proposition 1 concern how axioms behave under the framework’s definitions. None analyzes the complexity of a computational problem. Section 7.1 defines acceptance-maximizing procedures, but proves no algorithmic result about them. The paper explicitly leaves the complexity of domain-specific decision-making and evaluation to future work. So there is no named result here whose computational landscape a continuous-population analysis could chart.

The proposed stakeholder-types model is coherent: let each type be a normative axiom with mass \(\mu_t\), then at profile \(x\) choose a decision maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t A_t(x,f,f(x))\). But that extends Section 7.1; it does not continue a result of this paper. With an explicit finite set of rules or outcomes, it is a weighted version of the acceptance objective already written there. To pose a nontrivial algorithmic question, one must specify how rules and axioms are represented, what decision domain is used, and which structured family is being optimized over. In this paper, \(\mathcal X\), \(\mathcal Y\), \(\mathcal F\), and the axioms are deliberately unrestricted, so there is no finite input model or pricing structure from which to derive a complexity question. Any such structure would be a new, domain-specific contribution.

The conceptual results do not supply another anchor. A decision either satisfies an axiom or does not; putting mass on stakeholder types can measure how many types accept it, but does not make the Decision-Evaluation Paradox a population-dependent phenomenon. Treating the set of profiles or rules as the continuum would continuize something other than the population. A population of stakeholders with shared normative positions is the best population interpretation, but it leads back to the unanalysed acceptance-maximization extension.

That makes the negative case strong against a mirror of this paper’s results. It is weaker against the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario: the stakeholder population is plausible, and the authors themselves identify computational analysis as future work. I would reject the proposed mirror as unanchored to a computational result in this paper, while treating that follow-on question as potentially worthwhile research.
