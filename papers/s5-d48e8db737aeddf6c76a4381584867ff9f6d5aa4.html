The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper contains a very natural continuous-population model, but it does not contain a qualifying named computational anchor under your rule. The text has numbered Definitions 1–13, formulations \((\mathrm{HPIEF})\), \((P_{\mathrm{Rawls}})\), \((P_{\mathrm{Nash}})\), and Algorithm 1, but no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Thus I cannot truthfully present “Theorem X” as the anchor. The paper’s claim that its schemes can be formulated as conic programs and solved by column generation is a computational contribution, but not a proved complexity classification.

If the paper’s named formulation is accepted as a permissive anchor, my lead mirror would be Continuous Typed Rawlsian Kidney-Exchange Policy.

A type is a complete clinical and operational signature of a patient–donor pair: patient compatibility characteristics, donor compatibility characteristics, PRA or other priority class, transplant utility, and every feature used by the exchange model. A separate type describes a non-directed donor. Two agents have the same type exactly when they induce the same compatibility arcs, utilities, fairness category, and exchange eligibility. The society is a mass vector \(\mu\) over finitely many pair and donor types, with many individual pairs represented by each positive \(\mu_t\). Compatibility is a relation between donor types and patient types.

Fix cycle and chain caps \(K\) and \(K'\). A feasible exchange pattern \(q\) is either a compatible directed cycle of pair types of length at most \(K\), or a compatible chain beginning with a non-directed-donor type and containing at most \(K'\) patients. Let \(x_q\ge 0\) be the mass assigned to pattern \(q\). If \(a_{tq}\) is the number of agents of pair type \(t\) used by \(q\), feasibility is \(\sum_q a_{tq}x_q\le \mu_t\) for every type \(t\). Thus each infinitesimal agent still receives an integral kidney-exchange bundle; only the population mass is divisible.

The selected mass of pair type \(t\) is \(p_t=\sum_q a_{tq}x_q\), and its selection probability is \(\rho_t=p_t/\mu_t\). Let \(U(x)=\sum_q w_qx_q\) be expected transplant utility, where \(w_q\) is the total utility of the transplants in pattern \(q\), and let \(R(x)=\min_t\rho_t\) be the Rawlsian fairness score. Given the same kind of reference point \((d_U,d_R)\) used in the paper’s NSWP, the continuous problem is to choose \(x\) and \(\theta\) maximizing \((U(x)-d_U)(\theta-d_R)\), subject to the capacity constraints, \(\theta\le \rho_t\) for every pair type, and \(U(x)\ge d_U,\theta\ge d_R\). Equivalently, it is the rotated-second-order-cone version of this product maximization.

This directly mirrors the paper’s \((P_{\mathrm{Rawls}})\): its \(\delta_S\) variables assign probability to feasible exchange plans, its \(y_1\) measures expected transplant utility above a reference point, its \(T\) is the minimum patient-selection probability, and its rotated cone maximizes the product of the two surpluses. In the continuum, \(\rho_t\) is the probability that a uniformly selected member of type \(t\) is transplanted. A finite high-multiplicity implementation can realize rational \(x_q\) by assigning distinct individuals of each type to the prescribed cycles and chains, so this is not fractional kidneys or fractional cycles.

The high-multiplicity regime is a national or regional clearinghouse with thousands of patient–donor pairs but a much smaller number of repeated compatibility and priority signatures—for example, standardized blood/tissue compatibility classes crossed with a modest number of PRA and utility categories. The paper’s finite graph remains intact at type level; only repeated vertices with identical behavior are aggregated. Its original lottery over named exchange plans becomes a type-level selection-rate policy, which is exactly the population-side analogue of its fairness objective.

The paper’s closest computational artifact is Algorithm 1. It repeatedly solves a restricted master/dual conic program, uses the dual weights to assign arc weights, solves a feasible-exchange subproblem, and adds the resulting exchange plan as a new column until no negative reduced-cost column remains. In the typed mirror, the pricing problem searches for a compatible cycle or chain whose type-pattern column has maximum reduced value. For fixed \(K\) and \(K'\), all type patterns can be enumerated in \(\tau^{O(K+K')}\) time, so the mirror is plausibly Class A: the population size disappears from the formulation, and the remaining optimization is a finite conic program over type patterns. A linear Social Welfare variant would even reduce to an LP. If \(K\) is part of the input, pricing may inherit hard longest-cycle or longest-path structure; that is a boundary question rather than evidence that the population continuization itself is useless.

This mirror covers the paper’s Rawlsian fairness scheme, NSWP tradeoff, feasible exchange plans, and column-generation methodology. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s dynamic-K​​EP discussion or every fairness score. The natural follow-up questions are whether type-level policies round to finite elections with bounded additive loss, whether the continuous and lottery interpretations coincide exactly, and how pricing behaves when cycle lengths are unbounded.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper never proves a named complexity or algorithmic theorem, so this cannot satisfy the programme’s strict anchor requirement. The second weakness is empirical plausibility: real HLA compatibility patterns may be so individualized that \(\tau\) is close to the number of patients. The case survives only in a genuine cohort regime where the stated clinical features completely determine the exchange model. Under that assumption, however, the continuous question is recognizably the paper’s problem—not merely fractional outcome allocation—and is a credible new Class A mirror rather than a restatement of its existing finite lottery model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. It contains numbered definitions, conic formulations, an algorithm, and experiments, but no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. “The schemes can be formulated as conic programs” is a modelling observation, not a complexity theorem; Algorithm 1 is a computational procedure, not a proved polynomial, approximation, or parameterized guarantee. Its scalability claims are empirical, restricted to synthetic instances with \(K=3\), and include timeouts. Under the programme’s stated gate, there is therefore no result to continuize.

Even granting the proponent’s permissive use of Algorithm 1, their construction changes the paper’s central object. The paper chooses a lottery \(\delta\) over complete exchange plans \(S\in\mathcal F_G\), with individual patient probability

\[ \delta_v=\sum_{S\ni v}\delta(S). \]

The proposed model instead chooses masses \(x_q\) of local type-level cycles and chains, subject to

\[ \sum_q a_{tq}x_q\le \mu_t, \]

and defines \(\rho_t\) as the fraction of type \(t\) sent through an exchange. That is an anonymous fractional exchange-flow problem. It is not a probability distribution over the paper’s exchange plans: \(q\) is a local pattern, not a complete plan, and \(\rho_t\) is a type-level participation rate, not the probability of a named patient.

This is not merely an integrality issue. The article’s policy question is how to randomize among mutually feasible plans for identifiable patients. The proposed question is how to divide a mass of interchangeable agents among compatible patterns. The latter may be a sensible new model, but it has replaced the paper’s individual-procedural fairness problem with a typed allocation problem. Calling \(\rho_t\) “the probability of a uniformly selected type-\(t\) patient” does not restore the lost individual object; it introduces a new anonymity assumption and a new fairness criterion.

There is also a serious high-multiplicity obstruction. In the paper, feasibility is determined by the entire compatibility graph. For two patient–donor pairs to be the same type in the programme’s precise sense, they must have the same incoming and outgoing compatibility relations, utilities, PRA category, and every other relevant attribute. In an arbitrary KEP graph, that type is essentially an adjacency-and-weight signature. Such signatures can be unique, so \(\tau\) grows with the number of pairs and the supposed population compression disappears. If one instead uses coarse clinical categories, those categories generally do not determine the graph and therefore do not define complete types.

The best rescue is to posit a genuinely repeated block-structured clearinghouse: many pairs with exactly identical compatibility signatures and a fixed finite catalogue of donor, patient, utility, and priority types. That is coherent, and it produces a legitimate high-multiplicity exchange-flow model. But it is a stylized typed KEP rather than the irregular graph problem studied in the paper; its conic program and pricing routine are consequences of the new block model, not a mirror of a named result in this article.

So the strict negative verdict is strong: there is no qualifying computational result here to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The broader universal claim—that no repeated-signature KEP scenario could ever yield a worthwhile continuous-population problem—is weaker and cannot honestly be proved. A typed KEP extension may be interesting research, but the proponent has not shown that it mirrors this paper’s computational contribution rather than reformulating its fairness objectives into a different anonymous mass-allocation problem.
