The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), capacities \(\omega_t\), good classes \(J\) with rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and rational values \(v_{tj}\), define coalition welfare \(W(x)=\max\{\sum_{t,j}v_{tj}y_{tj}:\sum_j y_{tj}\leq\omega_t x_t,\ \sum_t y_{tj}\leq b_j,\ y_{tj}\geq0\}\). For each \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), let \(g(\lambda)\) be the minimum-Euclidean-norm supergradient of \(W\) at \(\lambda\mu\). Determine the exact computational complexity, in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length, of computing the type shares \(A_t=\mu_t\int_0^1g_t(\lambda)\,d\lambda\) for all \(t\in T\).

The model it lives in

A continuous allocation game with a finite support of complete agent types, rational type masses, type capacities, rational per-capita supplies of indivisible good classes, and rational valuations. Coalition welfare is the aggregate-allocation LP value; the output is each type’s mass-based Shapley counterpart, defined by integrating a selected supergradient along the radial path from zero population to the full society.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.1’s Shapley-value computation for allocation games, linked to the paper’s truthful utility interpretation in Theorem 5.4. It leaves aside truthfulness, no-punishment guarantees, and the other mechanism properties, which do not automatically transfer to a population continuum.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact shares be computed in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length, or can their computation be proved hard?
  • Do these shares coincide with limits of exact finite Shapley shares under replicated populations, including at nondifferentiable points?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the paper’s Shapley-value computation for allocation games. It targets the paper’s computational bottleneck: maximizing welfare is a matching problem, but computing the fair utility shares can be much harder.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large organization allocating standardized benefits—such as parking permits by zone—to many employees. Employees with the same valuations for each benefit class, eligibility, and allocation capacity form one type. The population might contain tens of thousands of employees but only dozens of distinct types. Each permit remains indivisible; as the organization grows, the supply of each class scales with the population. That scaling is part of the mirror, since a fixed finite stock of goods would make most agents irrelevant in the limit.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Allocation-Game Shapley Shares. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), capacities \(\omega_t\), a finite set \(J\) of good classes with rational supply \(b_j\) per capita, and rational values \(v_{tj}\). A type is the agents’ complete description for this allocation problem. For any coalition mass vector \(x\), define its best attainable welfare by

\[ W(x)=\max \left\{ \sum_{t\in T,j\in J}v_{tj}y_{tj}: \sum_j y_{tj}\leq \omega_t x_t,\ \sum_t y_{tj}\leq b_j,\ y_{tj}\geq 0 \right\}. \]

The variables \(y_{tj}\) describe aggregate assignments: they may be fractional because they measure mass receiving a good class, though each actual good and each individual bundle remain integral. Let \(g(\lambda)\) be the minimum-norm supergradient of \(W\) at \(\lambda\mu\), and ask for the exact type shares

\[ A_t=\mu_t\int_0^1 g_t(\lambda)\,d\lambda. \]

The solution is the vector \((A_t)_{t\in T}\), or the per-agent type utility \(A_t/\mu_t\). The shares sum to \(W(\mu)\): along the path from no agents to the full population, the integral distributes the change in coalition welfare. Where \(W\) is differentiable, this is the usual radial marginal-value calculation; the minimum-norm rule makes the question definite at ties.

The named hardness anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It says that computing the Shapley value of the coalitional games associated with allocation problems in Definition 5.1 is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-complete. Those games assign each coalition either its best achievable allocation value or its marginal contribution to the grand coalition; the paper shows their Shapley values coincide. The authors prove hardness by a reduction from counting perfect matchings in a bipartite graph, encoding matching counts in Shapley-value terms. They cite prior work for the counting problem and the membership argument, while giving the reduction here. Theorem 5.4 connects the mechanism’s truthful utilities to these Shapley values, so this is the computation behind the paper’s fairness payments.

The continuous question is whether that coalition-by-coalition computation becomes tractable when coalition composition is represented by type masses. In a \(q\)-fold replication, a random predecessor coalition at rank \(\lambda\) has mass close to \(\lambda\mu\); when the welfare function is differentiable along that path, its marginal contributions motivate the integral above. I would tentatively expect Class A: each welfare query is a matching LP, and the continuous calculation follows a one-dimensional path instead of summing over all subsets. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of the paper. Exact parametric LP behavior is the obstacle: a polynomial method for finding and integrating the relevant regions is the central algorithmic question. Follow-ups include bounding those regions, approximating the continuous shares from replicated finite markets, and determining whether the payment interpretation can retain the paper’s no-punishment properties.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous analogue of their *fairness computation*: it keeps their coalition worth—optimal allocation among a coalition—and asks for its mass-based Shapley counterpart. It does not claim to continuize their entire mechanism. In particular, a single atomless agent cannot affect the population distribution, so the paper’s dominant-strategy truthfulness result does not carry over automatically. Payments can still be interpreted as type-level accounting shares, but that is a narrower target than reproducing the mechanism.

The weakest point is the change from a finite Shapley value over named agents to a mass-based value with a tie rule. The integral has a natural finite-replication motivation in regular cases, but the paper does not prove that it is the limit of its Shapley payments in degenerate cases. There is a related limitation in the hardness anchor: its reduction creates only a constant number of identical agents per graph edge, so it does not itself demonstrate a regime with many agents and few types. Those caveats narrow the claim; they do not erase the mirror of the paper’s central computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that Theorem 6.1’s hardness belongs to a finite, identity-based Shapley calculation inside a mechanism whose truthfulness is defined by individual deviations. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass, so that incentive guarantee has no direct counterpart. The proposed mirror narrows the target to type-level fair shares. That is a legitimate computational question, but it no longer captures the mechanism’s combined contribution.

The formula offered for those shares also does not establish the needed bridge. The paper averages marginal contributions over random orders of named agents; selecting a minimum-norm supergradient at a kink is a separate tie rule, with no argument that it matches the limit of those finite Shapley values. A stronger mirror would define shares as the limit of exact Shapley values in replicated markets, with supplies scaled alongside the population. That would avoid the arbitrary tie rule, but it would still leave the authors with a new limiting-value problem rather than a direct continuization of their payment computation.

There is less novelty in the computational machinery than the proposal suggests. Aumann–Shapley shares for piecewise-linear cost functions have been computed by tracing radial paths with parametric linear programming, and Shapley computation for matching and allocation games already has a complexity literature with both hardness and tractable cases ([parametric-LP cost allocation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221716302429); [matching and allocation games](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370219301833)). These works do not settle the replicated-market limit, so they are not a novelty collision under ChoCo’s standard. They do, however, make the proposed mirror look more like a targeted extension of existing cost-allocation work than a new computational question opened by this paper.

That is the strongest case against the anchor, but it does not defeat its best version. Replicated populations with a small support of complete allocation types and per-capita-scaled supplies are sensible here. The limiting Shapley-share problem is recognizable as the authors’ coalition-worth computation, and the adjacent literature does not answer its exact complexity. I would therefore call the negative case weak: it shows that the proponent’s particular formula needs a sound finite-replication justification, not that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
