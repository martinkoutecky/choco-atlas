The case FOR (proponent)

The honest limit is that this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. Its numbered Propositions concern convergence, variance, and equilibrium behavior; they do not claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The closest is Proposition 6: for fixed choices by the other agents, it characterizes agent \(i\)’s optimal \(m_i\) by
\[ \frac{m_i}{1-m_i}=\frac{W_i}{h_i\sigma_i^2}, \qquad L_i=\sigma_i^2p_i=\frac{W_i}{1+W_i/\sigma_i^2}, \]
where \(W_i\) is the variance of the effective information from others, including accumulated error, and \(h_i\) relates \(m_i\) to the weight \(p_i\) on agent \(i\)’s own signal. That is an analytic best-response characterization, not a complexity result. So I can’t honestly provide an anchor-specific mirror of the kind requested.

The strongest positive lead, explicitly unanchored, is a continuous-population version of the paper’s rule-choice equilibrium. Let \(T\) be a finite set of communication roles, with rational masses \(\mu_a\). A role \(a\) specifies its interaction row \(K_{ab}\), signal precision, and transmission-error law; \(K\) is row-stochastic. All agents in a role choose the same stationary rule \((m_a,\gamma_a)\) at equilibrium. If \(\bar y_b^k\) is the mean opinion in role \(b\), their update is
\[ y_i^k=(1-\gamma_a)y_i^{k-1} +\gamma_a\!\left[m_a x_i+(1-m_a) \left(\sum_{b\in T}K_{ab}\bar y_b^{k-1}+\xi_i+\nu_i^k\right)\right]. \]
The computational question would be: given \(T,\mu,K\), the signal and error laws, and a rational tolerance \(\varepsilon\), compute a type-symmetric \(\varepsilon\)-Nash equilibrium of stationary rules, where each agent minimizes \(\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb E[(y_i^k-\theta)^2]\). A solution is a policy vector for which no individual can lower that limiting loss by more than \(\varepsilon\) through a unilateral change in \(m_a\) or \(\gamma_a\).

There is a plausible regime for this model: a very large online or workplace network with many agents but a small number of recurring communication roles, so \(N\gg |T|\). Role members share their interaction pattern and the distributions relevant to their ex ante rule choice. This preserves the paper’s central ingredients—private signals, noisy communication, FJ/DG updating, and individual loss—while making population mass the input rather than a named roster. The paper’s Propositions 1–8 and Results 1–6 would motivate this lead, but none is a qualifying complexity anchor. For unrestricted interaction kernels, my tentative expectation would be continuum-specific difficulty from the coupled equilibrium conditions; homogeneous or low-rank kernels could admit tractable cases. The paper supplies no complexity evidence for either expectation.

The main weakness is that the atomless limit changes incentives: one person’s rule no longer affects the population aggregate, whereas feedback and “echo” effects are central to the finite-network analysis. Also, this is a computational question about social learning, not clearly a computational social-choice problem: the paper studies individuals’ beliefs and rules, not a collective choice or winner. So the population continuization is natural, but this paper does not provide a named computational result that would ground the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no qualifying anchor. That is the strongest objection: the numbered propositions and results characterize convergence, losses, and equilibrium behavior, but none states a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Proposition 6 gives a best-response formula,
\[ \frac{m_i}{1-m_i}=\frac{W_i}{h_i\sigma_i^2}, \]
not a complexity question. Turning the paper’s equilibrium analysis into “compute an \(\varepsilon\)-Nash equilibrium” proposes a new computational problem; it does not supply a computational result to mirror.

The proposed role model is a plausible extension, but as written its masses \(\mu_a\) do not affect the update equations: the interaction matrix \(K\) already supplies the weights on role averages. Changing a role’s mass while holding \(K\) fixed leaves the dynamics unchanged. Making \(K\) depend on the masses could fix that, but it is an added modeling choice.

More fundamentally, the extension changes the feedback the paper studies. In the finite network, an agent’s signal and persistent error can travel through paths and return as echoes. In an atomless role population, one agent’s deviation cannot affect a role average. Independent errors also average away at the population level; preserving aggregate error requires a common or correlated shock. The best response and its external effects therefore differ from the paper’s finite-network rule-choice game. A block model could make this a sensible mean-field learning project, but the paper supplies no computational result or complexity evidence for it.

There is a costly alternative: retain enough network structure to capture paths, cycles, and error correlations. Then the type space must carry topology or individual identities, so the role masses alone no longer describe the relevant input. In the atomless version, unilateral influence still disappears; retaining finitely influential agents moves away from the continuous-population model. Either route can motivate new work, but neither yields a faithful computational mirror of a named result here.

Finally, the paper’s object is equilibrium choice of private opinion-updating rules. It has no collective choice rule, winner, or allocation whose computation grounds a ComSoC problem. Adding one would create another extension. On this paper, the negative case is strong at the programme’s source-eligibility gate. It is weaker as a universal claim that continuous computation around social learning could never be worthwhile; the proposed model might merit study independently, but this paper does not establish that it is a ChoCo mirror.
