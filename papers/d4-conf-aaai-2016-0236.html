What the mirror covers

Theorem 2 is the only proposed anchor. The proposed block-trading model would address its MAX-WELFARE hardness claim; the paper’s other results are not anchored.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would anchor the case on Theorem 2, proved in this paper: MAX-WELFARE is MAX SNP-hard. The problem is to choose feasible trades on the market’s compatibility graph to maximize \(\sum_{sb\in E} f_{sb}(p_b-p_s)\), subject to each seller’s and buyer’s capacity and the rule that a trade on an edge is either zero or at least its transaction threshold. The proof reduces from MAX IS-3, the maximum independent-set problem on graphs of maximum degree three, which the paper cites as MAX SNP-complete. For each graph vertex it creates a seller and buyer joined by a thresholded edge; for each graph edge it creates a buyer that can trade with the sellers for its endpoints. The construction gives \(\mathrm{OPT}(I')=|E|+\mathrm{OPT}(I)\), and the paper proves an L-reduction. The MAX IS-3 hardness is cited; the reduction establishing Theorem 2 is proved here.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a regional water exchange with many irrigation cooperatives submitting standardized orders. Cooperatives share a type when they have the same buyer or seller role, per-capita capacity and price, and the same local trading and threshold relationships to every other type. Repeating districts or standardized network zones could yield thousands of cooperatives but only dozens of distinct types. This needs an institutional feature the paper’s current market does not assume: an umbrella or clearinghouse pools orders of a type and can sign a block contract with another type. That makes the minimum threshold apply to the pooled type-to-type trade.

Continuous Maximum-Welfare Trading. The input is buyer and seller type sets \(B,S\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-capita capacities \(q_t\), prices \(p_t\), a compatibility graph \(E\subseteq S\times B\), and a rational threshold \(h_{sb}\) for each compatible pair. Choose rational type-to-type trade volumes \(x_{sb}\ge 0\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{sb\in E}(p_b-p_s)x_{sb}, \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{b:sb\in E}x_{sb}\le \mu_s q_s,\qquad \sum_{s:sb\in E}x_{sb}\le \mu_b q_b, \]
and \(x_{sb}=0\) or \(x_{sb}\ge h_{sb}\) for every edge. A solution is an optimal trade-volume vector \(x\). Its inputs and outputs keep the paper’s welfare objective, locality, capacities, prices, and transaction-threshold discontinuity; the population is represented by type masses.

For unrestricted support size \(\tau\), I expect hardness to transfer: the type graph can encode the paper’s hard instances, with one type per original agent. That does not settle the more interesting high-multiplicity slice, where many agents share each type. For fixed \(\tau\), one can enumerate active type-pair trades and solve an LP for each choice, giving an elementary FPT baseline in \(\tau\). The open algorithmic question is whether stronger algorithms are possible when \(\tau\) is moderate, and where threshold choices still make the problem hard. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s MAX-WELFARE result only; it does not claim a mirror for every result in the paper.

The weak point is the pooled-contract assumption. In the paper, thresholds apply to trades between individual villages; in this mirror, identical villages’ orders are bundled before the threshold is applied. If regional markets cannot plausibly aggregate those orders, that changes the operational question enough to undermine the mirror. If they can, the authors should recognize the result as their same welfare-maximization problem on a high-multiplicity market, with a clear parameter \(\tau\) for studying when population repetition helps.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational result, so the case against cannot rest on there being nothing to mirror. The stronger objection is that its transaction threshold belongs to a bilateral contract: each edge trade is either zero or at least \(h_{ij}\). The paper’s graph also records which particular villages can trade. Those two features make a distribution over agent types an incomplete description of a high-multiplicity market.

Consider the proponent’s best scenario: many standardized irrigation cooperatives arranged in repeated local networks. If their trades remain bilateral, aggregating by type loses how many compatible pairs there are and how those pairs are arranged. More fundamentally, \(N\) copies of a local network have the aggregate feasible set obtained by combining \(N\) separate thresholded markets; that is generally not the feasible set of one trade between two pooled types. With population mass normalized to one, a fixed per-contract threshold becomes \(h_{ij}/N\). In the limit, it no longer rules out small positive per-capita trades. Keeping a fixed threshold on the pooled type-to-type trade preserves a discontinuity, but changes the institution: the contract is now between cohorts, not villages. It amortizes the very transaction barrier the paper models.

A richer mirror could track densities of active contracts and preserve some of the repeated network structure. But for arbitrary locality, that structure is itself the compatibility graph. Encoding it as neighborhoods makes villages with different neighborhoods different types; encoding it separately means the population distribution alone no longer describes the instance. A continuum of locations with a compatibility relation faces the same threshold problem: either individual contract sizes vanish in normalized units, or the model must retain atomic contracts and their incidence structure. The latter may be a useful continuous network-optimization project, but it is not a clean high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 2.

So I would reject the proposed pooled-threshold mirror as a faithful continuization of the paper’s central result. The negative case is not airtight: if the authors want to study a redesigned market in which districts actually pool orders and trade by type-level blocks, that could be a worthwhile computational question. But it changes the transaction unit and therefore the market mechanism; on the paper’s stated bilateral-trading model, I do not see a nondegenerate continuous population mirror that preserves what Theorem 2 is about.
