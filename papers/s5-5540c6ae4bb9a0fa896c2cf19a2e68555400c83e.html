The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of resource classes \(J\), bundles \(\mathcal B\), complete agent types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), lift a \(c\)-approximate monotone strongly loser-independent greedy allocation rule to a fluid allocation that may split type mass at capacity boundaries while each individual receives an integral bundle. In the resulting nonatomic critical-price auction, does every equilibrium in which agents do not overbid achieve expected per-capita welfare at least \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty(\mu,q)/(c+1)\)? The equilibrium is over measurable type-dependent bidding strategies, with each infinitesimal agent best responding while taking aggregate bid mass as fixed.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with finite type support \(\mathcal T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and resource \(j\) supplied at rate \(q_j\) per capita. The fluid greedy rule assigns mass of each type to integral bundles, splitting mass at ties or capacity cutoffs; agents pay critical prices and negligible individual deviations do not change aggregate allocation. The target is the welfare guarantee for all no-overbidding nonatomic equilibria.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper. It says that if \(A\) is a \(c\)-approximate, monotone, strongly loser-independent allocation rule, then every Bayes–Nash equilibrium of its critical-price mechanism \(M_2(A)\) in which agents do not overbid achieves expected welfare at least \(\operatorname{OPT}/(c+1)\). The paper’s named results are welfare-performance theorems, not P/NP classifications; this is the closest anchor, and I would not present it as a complexity result.

A plausible regime is a large market allocating bundles of cloud capacity to many small users or firms. Let \(J\) be a finite set of resource classes and \(\mathcal B\subseteq 2^J\) the available bundles. The population has unit mass and a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) complete types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type specifies the user’s values for bundles, eligibility, and any other feature the mechanism uses. Resource \(j\) has supply \(q_j\) per unit of population, so a finite market with \(N\) users has \(Nq_j\) copies. Here \(N\gg\tau\): many users share each full type.

Each user still receives one whole bundle; fractional quantities arise only in the aggregate assignment of mass. The optimal per-capita allocation is the LP
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_\infty(\mu,q)= \max_{x\ge 0}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{S\in\mathcal B}v_t(S)x_{t,S} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal B}x_{t,S}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j. \]
Run the paper’s priority-greedy rule on this population, splitting tied mass at a capacity boundary, and charge each user the critical price for their bundle. A continuum equilibrium is a type-contingent bidding strategy in which each infinitesimal user best responds to the aggregate bids of everyone else. No-overbidding means \(b(S)\le v_t(S)\) for every type \(t\) and bundle \(S\).

I would give a prover this question, called Fluid Critical-Price Greedy PoA: for a rational finite-support instance \((J,\mathcal B,\mathcal T,\mu,q,A)\), compute the worst equilibrium welfare ratio
\[ \rho_\infty=\sup_{\sigma\in\mathrm{BNE},\,\text{no overbidding}} \frac{\operatorname{OPT}_\infty(\mu,q)} {\operatorname{SW}_\infty(\sigma)}. \]
Equivalently, for the anchor’s bound, determine whether any such equilibrium has welfare below \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty/(c+1)\). A counterexample is an equilibrium and its welfare; a positive answer is a proof that all equilibria meet the bound. The same \(c+1\) bound is my expected answer. The likely route is an integrated version of the paper’s equilibrium inequalities: replace sums over agents by integrals over type mass, with the per-capita optimum given by the LP above. For this bound question, I expect a tractable, Class A style result. Computing the exact worst equilibrium for each instance is a further question, and Theorem 5.1 alone does not establish its complexity.

This covers the critical-price welfare guarantee, not the paper’s first-price results or every allocation problem it considers. The co-scaling of supply is part of the mirror: without it, welfare per capita vanishes as the population grows. The individual bundles remain integral, while the type-level assignment is fractional.

My weakest point is the equilibrium change at the limit. A single user has zero mass and cannot alter aggregate bids or allocations, although their own bid can still determine whether they receive a bundle and what critical threshold they face. The paper’s theorem does not automatically survive that change; the continuum model needs a precise tie and threshold semantics, followed by a proof of the integrated bound. The positive case is that this remains the authors’ central question—welfare at equilibrium for a greedy combinatorial-auction mechanism—in a credible large-market regime. Useful follow-ups are whether \(c+1\) is tight in the fluid model, how the worst equilibrium compares with large finite replications, and whether the exact \(\rho_\infty\) is computable from \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 5.1 is not a computational result. It assumes an allocation rule \(A\) with approximation factor \(c\), then proves a welfare guarantee for equilibria of the critical-price mechanism built from \(A\). The other central results follow the same pattern: price-of-anarchy bounds, incentive claims, and tightness examples. The paper establishes no complexity classification or algorithm for computing a strategic intervention. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, this leaves no named computational result for the proposed fluid model to mirror.

The proposed \(\rho_\infty\) question repairs that gap by asking for the exact worst equilibrium welfare ratio. As stated, though, it needs a computational model before it is a finite-input problem: a representation of \(A\), a specified class of bid strategies, and precise allocation, tie, critical-price, and equilibrium semantics for the continuum. Restricting strategies to type-contingent bids may omit equilibria; allowing arbitrary measurable bid distributions leads to a measure-valued game. A carefully specified version could be a serious project, but it would be a new equilibrium-computation problem rather than a computational question the paper itself develops.

There is a credible large-market auction story, and an atomless model can retain meaningful threshold prices: an individual cannot move the aggregate cutoff, but can choose whether to bid above it. So I cannot honestly argue that every continuous version is incoherent. The negative case is that the anchor’s natural fluid continuation is mean-field equilibrium analysis, while the exact-ratio proposal changes the target into computing equilibria. Neither supplies the computational mirror ChoCo is seeking.

The universal claim is therefore weak. A continuous critical-price price-of-anarchy study may be worthwhile in algorithmic game theory; the case against is strongest only when the question is whether this paper offers a computational-social-choice result for ChoCo to continuize.
