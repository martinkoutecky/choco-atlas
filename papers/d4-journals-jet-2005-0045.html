The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible population-continuum mirror here, but the paper gives me no qualifying computational anchor under your rule. Its numbered results establish matching structure, fairness, and incentives; none classifies a named problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise. The paper says efficient exchange reduces to maximum-cardinality matching, and notes Edmonds’s polynomial-time algorithm, but that computational claim is not itself a numbered result of this paper.

The closest results for a positive case are Theorems 2 and 3, both proved here. Theorem 2 says the recursively constructed egalitarian utility vector \(u^E\) is feasible. Theorem 3 says \(u^E\) Lorenz-dominates every feasible utility profile: when utilities are sorted from lowest to highest, every initial sum for \(u^E\) is at least the corresponding sum for any other feasible profile, with a strict inequality for at least one initial sum. The paper constructs \(u_i^E=1\) for patients outside the underdemanded set and assigns the recursively calculated rate \(f(D_{k(i)},N^O_{k(i)})\) to each underdemanded patient. These are substantive anchors for an egalitarian analogue, but they are not computational-complexity anchors. Lemma 1, also proved here from the cited Gallai–Edmonds decomposition, says all Pareto-efficient matchings match the same number of patients; it likewise does not state a complexity classification.

A plausible mirror is Mass-Egalitarian Pairwise Kidney Exchange. Consider a large regional or national registry in which many patient–donor pairs share one of finitely many complete compatibility types—for example, the same compatibility pattern with every other type. Let \(T\) be those types, \(E\) the undirected compatibility relation between them, and \(\mu_t>0\) the fraction of patients of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The intended regime has many patients and a comparatively small number \(\tau=|T|\) of types.

For each compatible pair of types \(t,u\), choose mass \(x_{tu}\) to exchange. A same-type exchange \(x_{tt}\) uses two units of type-\(t\) mass, so the capacity constraints are
\[ 2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}\le \mu_t. \]
The matched rate for type \(t\) is
\[ q_t=\frac{2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}}{\mu_t}, \]
and the total matched mass is \(2\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}\), counting same-type edges as well. The problem is to maximize total matched mass, then choose a maximum-mass assignment whose type rates are weighted leximin: sort the rates across the population, with type \(t\)’s rate represented by mass \(\mu_t\), and improve the least-served mass first. A solution is a rational assignment \(x\) and its rates \(q_t\) witnessing both maximum volume and that fairness criterion.

I would expect this proposed problem to be tractable: the mass-assignment constraints are linear, and the weighted leximin objective can be handled by successive optimization over the finite set of types. That expectation is my proposed mirror’s algorithmic claim, not a result of the paper. A further question is whether the resulting weighted leximin profile always has the stronger Lorenz-dominance property proved in Theorem 3. Other natural questions compare the mass optimum with large finite registries: how quickly do normalized match volumes and fair rates converge, and what rounding guarantees hold as the population grows with \(\tau\) fixed?

The scenario is plausible because compatibility is determined by type-level medical information, and the paper’s problem already concerns pairwise exchanges among incompatible patient–donor pairs. Treating \(x_{tu}\) as exchange mass is population continuization; fractional rates follow from assigning a continuum of patients to pairwise exchanges. This mirror covers the paper’s efficient-exchange and egalitarian results, especially Theorems 2 and 3.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s patients are indivisible, and its Theorem 3 concerns lotteries over discrete matchings. The mass model changes the feasible set to fractional matchings. Its incentive theorems are also poor anchors for an atomless population, where a single patient’s report has zero mass and may have no effect. So I would offer this as a defensible mirror proposal, while being explicit that it does not satisfy the requested named-complexity-anchor condition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is that it has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 proves feasibility of the egalitarian utility profile; Theorem 3 proves its Lorenz dominance; Lemma 1 says efficient matchings all serve the same number of patients. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper notes that efficient exchange reduces to maximum-cardinality matching, but that observation is not a numbered complexity result of its own. Theorems 1 and 4 concern truthful individual reports, whose effect disappears when one patient has zero mass.

That leaves the proponent’s mass-egalitarian exchange as a new computational follow-up to Theorems 2 and 3, rather than a mirror of a computational result in the paper. Its formulation is coherent: with a fixed set of exact compatibility types, exchange mass on type pairs and matched rates by type preserve the population axis. Fractional rates can represent aggregate shares while every individual receives an integral outcome. So indivisible surgeries do not defeat this model.

I also cannot defeat the fairness anchors on modeling grounds. A weighted Lorenz or leximin analogue is a reasonable continuum counterpart to Theorem 3, and Theorem 2’s feasibility claim has a natural mass version. Theorem 3’s individual lotteries need careful translation into type rates, but the proposed model makes that translation explicit. Saying that the resulting optimization may be easy would not refute it: tractability is one of the programme’s desired outcomes.

The scenario is less persuasive than the mathematics. Exact type aggregation must preserve all medically relevant compatibility relations, and kidney compatibility can be highly patient-specific; the number of distinct types may therefore grow with the registry. A fixed compatibility-type graph with large cohorts is possible, though, and the programme allows an instance regime different from the paper’s motivating registry. This weakens the proposal’s practical case without ruling out a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

So I can make a strong case that this paper lacks the required computational anchor, and that its strategy-proofness results do not carry over as individual-incentive claims. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the mass version of egalitarian pairwise exchange survives as a plausible computational question if the programme accepts follow-ups to theorems about feasibility and fairness.
