The continuous mirror question

Given worker types \(\mathcal A=\{A_1,\ldots,A_p\}\) and opening types \(\mathcal B=\{B_1,\ldots,B_q\}\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, eligibility edges \(E\), strict rankings over eligible opposite-side types with \(\bot\) last, and weights \(w_{ij}\in\{1,2\}\), find a maximum-weight feasible mass flow \(x\). The flow has \(x_{ij},x_{i\bot},x_{\bot j}\ge0\), \(\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}+x_{i\bot}=\mu_i\), and \(\sum_{i:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}+x_{\bot j}=\nu_j\); its objective is \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\). Each unit of mass receives one counterpart type or is unmatched, while the mass assigned to each option may be fractional. For a type \(A_i\), let \(\sigma_i(k,\ell)\) be \(1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) according as it prefers \(k\) to \(\ell\), prefers \(\ell\) to \(k\), or is indifferent, and set \(v_i(k\mid x)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal B\cup\{\bot\}}(x_{i\ell}/\mu_i)\sigma_i(k,\ell)\); define \(v_j(k\mid x)\) analogously for each \(B_j\). Require every feasible challenger flow \(y\) to have margin \(D(y,x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}\sum_{j\in\mathcal B\cup\{\bot\}}y_{ij}v_i(j\mid x)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal A\cup\{\bot\}}y_{ij}v_j(i\mid x)\le0\). Masses are encoded in binary.

The model it lives in

A bipartite nonatomic market with worker and opening types carrying rational masses, strict rankings over counterpart types, and type-pair weights. Choose a maximum-weight mass flow \(x\) whose expected pairwise vote margin against every feasible flow is nonpositive.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the paper’s Theorem 3, proved here in Section 5 by reduction from Vertex Cover. It says that, in a bipartite graph with strict preference lists and edge weights in \(\{1,2\}\), finding a maximum-weight popular matching is NP-hard. The paper’s own discussion of popular fractional matchings gives a credible route to a population version of this hardness result.

Consider a large two-sided labor market: workers on one side, openings on the other. Millions of participants may fall into a moderate number \(\tau\) of exchangeable types—say, by qualification or job class. A type specifies the agent’s strict ranking of counterpart types, eligibility, and all other properties relevant to matching. Copies of a type are indistinguishable; they rank counterpart *types*, with copies within a type treated as interchangeable.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Max-Weight Popular Type-Mass Matching. The instance has worker types \(A_1,\ldots,A_p\) and opening types \(B_1,\ldots,B_q\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side; an eligibility graph \(E\); strict rankings over eligible counterpart types, with being unmatched last; and weights \(w_{ij}\in\{1,2\}\) on eligible pairs. Masses and other rational input are encoded in binary.

A matching is a mass flow \(x\): \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) is the mass of type \(A_i\) matched to type \(B_j\), and \(x_{i\bot}\), \(x_{\bot j}\) are unmatched mass. It must satisfy \(\sum_j x_{ij}+x_{i\bot}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}+x_{\bot j}=\nu_j\). The question is to find a feasible \(x\) of maximum weight \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\), subject to popularity: no other feasible mass flow \(y\) wins a positive mass-weighted pairwise vote against \(x\).

For precision, use the fractional-popularity convention studied in the paper. Under \(x\), a type-\(A_i\) participant’s current partner is distributed across \(B_j\) in proportions \(x_{ij}/\mu_i\), including unmatched mass \(x_{i\bot}/\mu_i\). Let \(v_i(k\mid x)\) be that type’s expected vote for being assigned \(k\), rather than its current assignment: the probability that \(k\) is preferred minus the probability that the current assignment is preferred. Define \(v_j(i\mid x)\) analogously for type \(B_j\). Then the vote margin of challenger \(y\) against \(x\) is the sum of these per-capita votes, weighted by the challenged masses in \(y\); for matched mass \(y_{ij}\), the contribution is \(y_{ij}\bigl(v_i(j\mid x)+v_j(i\mid x)\bigr)\), with corresponding terms for unmatched mass. Thus popularity means this margin is at most zero for every feasible \(y\). This specifies both what is being optimized and what counts as a solution.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). For fixed \(x\), a best challenger is a maximum-cost transportation matching. Its edge costs are the expected pairwise votes defined above, which are linear functions of \(x\). The paper’s LP-duality treatment of popularity suggests a direct certificate: type potentials \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) covering every challenger edge and unmatched option, with weighted dual objective \(\sum_i\mu_i\alpha_i+\sum_j\nu_j\beta_j=0\). The incumbent \(x\) itself has vote margin zero against itself, so such a certificate says no challenger can have positive margin. These conditions are linear in \(x,\alpha,\beta\), and the objective is linear too. That gives a polynomial-size LP in the number of supported types, eligible pairs, and input bit length. The likely gain is that divisible mass can split across assignments, removing the discrete choices behind the hardness result.

The authors would, I think, recognize this as their problem’s continuous form: it keeps bipartite matching, pairwise popularity, and the maximum-weight objective, and gives mass to populations that share complete types. The paper itself studies popular mixed and fractional matchings, so this extension has a close methodological precedent. Scope-wise, I am mirroring only Theorem 3’s max-weight popular matching result, not the roommates hardness or the other results.

The weakest point is the change in what is compared. Theorem 3 concerns integral matchings of named agents; this mirror compares fractional mass allocations using expected votes. Ranking types rather than named copies also makes copies within a type interchangeable. That may smooth away the very choices responsible for NP-hardness. The authors might regard it as a natural population model, given their fractional-matching work, or as a different problem. That is the main acceptance risk—not whether it is a well-defined computational question.

The next questions are whether this fractional-popularity model approximates the discrete high-multiplicity optimum, whether its optimal flow can be rounded while preserving popularity, and whether hardness returns when type masses cannot split. Those would clarify how far the tractability result extends beyond this particular continuous formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The clean objections do not work here: the paper has a named NP-hardness theorem, and a large labor market with repeated preference and weight types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I would not argue that the mirror is ill-posed or that its answer differs from the theorem. The strongest case against it is that the proposed mirror appears to be an existing popular-fractional-matching problem in type-mass notation.

Given a flow \(x\), the proposed popularity test asks whether any feasible challenger flow has positive vote margin. That is a maximum-cost capacitated matching problem. Its dual gives type potentials covering each type pair, with a weighted zero objective. Since the vote coefficients are linear in \(x\), these are linear constraints; maximizing \(\sum_{ij} w_{ij}x_{ij}\) over them is an LP. This is exactly the structure of the popular fractional matching polytope the paper discusses in Section 6.1, building on the cited work of Kavitha, Mestre, and Nasre. With homogeneous types, a continuum assignment is just a mass flow: each type’s population can be partitioned into pieces of the prescribed sizes, so individual agents can still receive single partners. The mirror does not need fractional bundles for people; its aggregate flow is the known fractional matching object.

A stronger version does not seem to escape that equivalence. Preserving an agent’s single assignment is compatible with a nonatomic population, because the mass can be partitioned. Preserving the paper’s idiosyncratic choices by requiring atomic agents or indivisible type supplies brings back the finite matching problem rather than a continuous population. Splitting types to retain individual-specific rankings or weights may also make \(\tau\) as large as the population, removing the compressed high-multiplicity regime.

That makes this a credible mirror, but a weak candidate for new ChoCo work: its natural formulation seems to restate an already-developed fractional matching polytope rather than expose a new computational landscape. The limitation in this case against is real: the paper’s cited polytope is for finite unit-capacity agents, while arbitrary binary rational type masses require a capacitated aggregation argument. If that quotient or its bit-complexity analysis has not been established, formalizing it could still be worthwhile. So I would not claim the universal negative is airtight; this is the strongest objection to the proposed anchor, and it rests on the authors recognizing the type-mass model as already covered by their fractional matching machinery.
