What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors, Propositions 1 and 2, are not computational results. Section 6's finite-grid LP and numerical findings are not a named computational result; the remaining analytical and numerical results are likewise not computational-complexity claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest pro case is very limited: the paper has a natural route toward a larger-population extension, but it contains no named computational result that can serve as an eligible anchor for a continuous mirror.

The closest named results are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in this paper. Proposition 1 says that when the welfare weight satisfies \(\lambda>0.5\), no \(\lambda\)-weighted first-best decision rule is incentive compatible. Proposition 2 says the same when \(\lambda=0.5\). These are mechanism-design impossibility results, not complexity results: neither classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard, nor gives an algorithmic result. Section 6 says that the finite-type second-best problem can be formulated as a linear program and reports numerical computations, but that is not a numbered complexity result.

The paper’s most promising extension point is its analogy with public-goods provision, which could motivate studying compromise mechanisms for many participants. But the paper’s own model has exactly two agents. Its continuous object is the agents’ private-valuation type space and prior, while its decision rule selects a lottery over three alternatives. That is continuous uncertainty and outcome randomization, not a continuous population.

A plausible larger-population scenario might involve many people on two opposing sides of a recurring arbitration problem, with a few shared valuation types. Yet the two propositions do not establish results for that model. In an atomless population, an individual report has no effect on the aggregate report distribution, so the original incentive problem may disappear; preserving its strategic content would require additional modeling choices and new results. That is the weakest point, and it prevents me from presenting this as a mirror the authors would clearly recognize as their problem.

So there is a credible research direction in extending the compromise model to many participants, but no anchored continuous-population computational question in this paper under the programme’s criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The pro case identifies no surviving anchor: it concedes that Propositions 1 and 2 are incentive-compatibility impossibility results, not complexity results. Section 6’s finite-grid linear program and numerical analysis are the closest computational material, but the paper states no numbered algorithmic or complexity result there. The paper’s continuous object is already the agents’ private valuation type space and its prior; that does not make the society continuous.

The strongest repair would be to extend the compromise problem to a large population with the same opposing rankings and valuation types, then study the complexity of choosing an incentive-compatible, welfare-maximizing lottery. But in an atomless, anonymous population, an individual’s report has zero effect on the aggregate reports and hence on the shared lottery. The incentive constraints that make the paper’s problem distinctive then degenerate. This is a structural obstacle to mirroring its impossibility results as computational questions, not an objection that the continuous version might have a different answer.

One could try to preserve strategic influence by making agents pivotal through sampling, or by treating each side as a strategic bloc. Sampling leaves only finitely many strategically relevant people; their types’ distribution is a prior, not the population being continuized. Blocs can have positive strategic weight, but then the agents are the blocs themselves. That may be a sensible new model of group bargaining, but it changes the unit of agency rather than giving the paper’s agents a high-multiplicity society.

The paper’s public-goods analogy offers another plausible extension: many participants, a shared compromise, and liquidity constraints. That could be worthwhile mechanism-design research. Yet it supplies no computational result from this paper to mirror, and the central many-participant question would need to be formulated anew. The paper itself points toward the public-goods literature; that connection does not turn its two-agent impossibility results into computational social choice results.

So the negative case is strongest on scope and degeneration, not on proving that no related population model could ever be useful. A larger-population compromise or public-goods project might be valuable. On the evidence here, though, it would be a new research direction rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
