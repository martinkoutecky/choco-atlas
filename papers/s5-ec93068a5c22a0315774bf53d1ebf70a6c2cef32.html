What the mirror covers

The proposed aggregate check would cover only a ranking derived from pooled expert judgments; it does not mirror the fixed-matrix guarantees in Theorems 1–3 or the inconsistency-index results in Lemmas 1–4.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a nearby extension, not a valid anchor under ChoCo’s rules. The paper has no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity.

Theorem 2, proved in the paper, gives sufficient conditions for order preservation for a reciprocal pairwise-comparison matrix and a priority vector: if \(c_{ij}\) and \(c_{kl}\) both exceed \(\mathscr{E}(C,w)\), and \(c_{ij}\div c_{kl}>\mathscr{E}(C,w)^2\), then the corresponding priority weights preserve both pairwise preferences and their relative intensity. Theorem 3, also proved there, specializes this to the multiplicative positive-real setting with GGMM: the thresholds can be expressed using \(1/(1-KI(C))\). These are mathematical implications, not computational-complexity results. The numerical example does not supply one either.

A plausible adjacent scenario is a large group of experts repeatedly evaluating the same alternatives. A type would be a complete additive pairwise-comparison matrix \(\ell^t\), with \(\ell^t_{ij}=-\ell^t_{ji}\); it specifies all the judgments relevant to the ranking task. If \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of experts of type \(t\), aggregate judgments as \(\bar\ell_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_t\ell^t_{ij}\), then compute the GGMM priority scores \(w_i=\frac{1}{n}\sum_j\bar\ell_{ij}\), up to a common additive normalization. The continuous question could be: given these types and masses, does the aggregate GGMM ranking satisfy POP and POIP? Its answer is yes or no. A crowd giving standardized judgments could have many experts but relatively few distinct judgment types.

That makes a coherent group-decision question, and the authors might recognize it as an application of their order-preservation analysis. But the paper does not define this population aggregation model, and its theorems do not establish a complexity result for that question. The proposed task is a direct check of ranking inequalities; it does not reveal a hardness result whose fate in a continuum would be at issue. It also makes an axiomatic property of an aggregate ranking the central question, rather than the computational population problem ChoCo is seeking.

So I would not present this as an anchored continuous mirror. There is no eligible anchor and therefore no defensible per-anchor trichotomy classification. The weakest point is decisive: the population of experts and the aggregation step must be added to the paper’s model. The paper’s stated results concern a single pairwise-comparison matrix, not a continuous society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the missing computational anchor. Theorem 2 gives sufficient conditions for POP and POIP for a fixed reciprocal matrix \(C\) and priority vector \(w\). Theorem 3 specializes those conditions to GGMM and the Koczkodaj index. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s brief computational observation is that checking COP requires calculating a ranking; it does not define a population problem or an optimization task.

The best population version would use a large panel of experts, each with a complete pairwise-comparison matrix as their type. For multiplicative judgments, take logs, average each type’s judgments by its mass, then apply GMM to the aggregate. One could ask whether the resulting ranking satisfies POP and POIP. That is a coherent high-multiplicity scenario, and it takes the paper’s references to human experts seriously.

But it still supplies a new computational problem around the paper, rather than continuizing one the paper formulates. To ask how much expert mass must change to restore order preservation, one must add the aggregation rule, permitted changes, costs, and target condition. The theorems themselves take a single matrix as input and give guarantees about its priority vector; they say nothing about an expert population or interventions on one. The continuous population model is therefore an additional group-decision model, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

I would reject the case for an anchored mirror under ChoCo’s stated gate. The weak point in that negative verdict is real: a population of experts supplying standardized judgments is plausible, and an intervention or robustness study built around it could be worthwhile. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario. The narrower claim is stronger: such a study would need to supply the computational problem that the paper itself leaves unspecified.
