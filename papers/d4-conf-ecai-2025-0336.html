The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. It is primarily an experimental comparison, so there is no qualifying anchor to quote and restate as required. The closest statements are prose claims that the Kemeny consensus is NP-hard, citing Bartholdi et al. (1989), and that the optimal bucket order problem is NP-hard, citing Lorena et al. (2021). Those are claims reported from elsewhere, not numbered results proved in this paper. On the strict anchor rule, there are therefore zero anchor-based continuous questions.

The strongest candidate mirror is the population version of the optimal bucket order problem, though it is necessarily an unanchored candidate here. Let the items be \(\mathcal I=\{1,\ldots,n\}\). A type \(t\) is a complete bucket order over \(\mathcal I\); let \(q_t(u,v)\) be \(1\) when \(t\) ranks \(u\) above \(v\), \(1/2\) when it ties them, and \(0\) when it ranks \(v\) above \(u\). The input is a support of \(\tau\) such types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Set \(C_\mu(u,v)=\sum_t\mu_tq_t(u,v)\). The task is to return a bucket order \(B\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{u,v\in\mathcal I}\left|B(u,v)-C_\mu(u,v)\right|, \]
where \(B(u,v)\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\) encodes the output bucket order. Its input size is naturally described by \(n,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large set of users in one feature-defined leaf of a partial-label-ranking model, all ranking the same modest set of labels. The types are their ranking patterns; mass is the fraction of users with each pattern. The regime is convincing when the leaf contains many users but relatively few distinct patterns. This mirrors the paper’s aggregation step: its pair-order matrix can be the mass-weighted average of those reports, and the output remains the consensus bucket order. The mirror is about the population distribution behind the pairwise probabilities, not about the fact that the paper also uses lotteries or probabilistic scores.

I would expect hardness to transfer if the cited optimal-bucket-order hardness instances can be realized by distributions over these types. Every finite ranking profile gives rational masses and embeds directly. The point that needs checking is whether the known hardness reduction stays within pair-order matrices generated by such profiles; hardness on unrestricted matrices alone does not establish hardness for this mirror. The resulting question is whether that restriction preserves hardness or permits an efficient algorithm. The mirror covers the optimal-bucket-order aggregation subproblem, not the paper’s full supervised learning framework or its comparisons of Borda, Copeland, and probabilistic methods.

The weak point is also the decisive one: the paper does not supply a numbered computational result to anchor this case, and the authors might view the population model as a reformulation of an aggregation input they already represent by \(C\), rather than as a new problem. The candidate is plausible because the paper’s examples aggregate many ranking reports, but it cannot satisfy the requested named-anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named computational result from the paper to mirror. The NP-hardness claims for Kemeny and the optimal bucket order problem are cited from other work; the paper’s own contributions are algorithm variants and experimental comparisons. Its runtime statements describe procedures, but it does not prove a numbered complexity result about them. That leaves no anchor-based continuous question to defeat.

The proposed optimal bucket order mirror is the strongest candidate, and it is well formed. In a leaf containing many users with a small number of ranking patterns, let \(\mu\) give the mass of each pattern and set \(C_\mu(u,v)=\sum_t\mu_t q_t(u,v)\). The objective then depends on \(\mu\) only through \(C_\mu\):
\[ \sum_{u,v}\left|B(u,v)-C_\mu(u,v)\right|. \]
This matters because the paper’s aggregation step already takes \(C\) as its input. If the mirror admits every matrix the paper admits, it restates that matrix-input problem. If it requires \(C\) to come from a distribution over rankings, it poses a restricted version of the problem, rather than mirroring a computational result established by this paper. A high-multiplicity classification of that restricted version could be worthwhile in its own right, but it is an unanchored candidate.

The paper’s other computational content does not supply a better anchor. Borda and Copeland compute scores from \(C\); the Markov methods compute from transition matrices; maximal lottery solves for a lottery over labels, which is outcome-space continuity. Turning the training examples into a distribution could also raise population-risk or generalization questions, but those would move the focus from the paper’s aggregation computations to statistical learning.

I cannot honestly claim that no plausible high-multiplicity scenario exists: the leaf example is credible, and a support-sensitive study of optimal bucket order may deserve attention independently. The negative case is strongest on the assignment’s narrower test: none of the paper’s own named computational results anchors it. The universal claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile is weaker than that finding; the paper alone cannot rule out the broader candidate.
