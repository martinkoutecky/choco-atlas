The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\), rational per-capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}^m\cap[0,1]^m\) with \(\sum_g q_g\geq 1\), and value-oracle access to a normalized, globally nonnegative, \(\sigma\)-subadditive valuation \(v:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{Q}\) with single-item marginals bounded by \(\Lambda\), find a distribution \(x\) over nonempty bundles such that \(\sum_S x_S=1\), \(\sum_{S:g\in S}x_S=q_g\) for every category \(g\), and \(\max_{S:x_S>0}v(S)-\min_{S:x_S>0}v(S)\leq 5\Lambda+1\), or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A society of one complete valuation type with mass \(1\); each person receives an integral bundle, while \(x_S\) records the mass receiving bundle \(S\). Category \(g\) has per-capita supply \(q_g\), and the objective is to bound the spread of \(v(S)\) across bundles assigned positive mass.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 24, proved in this paper using its cake construction and rounding machinery. The paper gives no named hardness result, so an algorithmic result is the right anchor here. Theorem 24 says that, under the paper’s standing normalization and global nonnegativity assumptions, if \(n\) agents have the same \(\sigma\)-subadditive valuation \(v\) on \(m\) indivisible items and every single-item marginal has absolute value at most \(\Lambda\), an algorithm computes in time polynomial in \(n\), \(m\), and \(\Lambda\) a partition into nonempty bundles \(A_1,\ldots,A_n\) with \(\lvert v(A_i)-v(A_j)\rvert\leq 5\Lambda+1\) for every pair. The valuation may still be nonmonotone and may be negative on some bundles. This is the paper’s result, not a cited theorem.

My lead mirror is Per-Capita Equitable Allocation. Consider a large cohort of interchangeable participants receiving bundles from a fixed menu of indivisible resource categories—for example, a standardized program assigning combinations of course or service entitlements. Each participant has the same complete valuation \(v\), including the same costs and constraints. There is one agent type, with population mass \(1\); in a cohort of \(N\) people, \(N\) is large while the number of distinct agent types is \(\tau=1\). Each category’s stock scales with the cohort, so category \(g\) has supply \(q_g\) per person, where \(q\in\mathbb{Q}^m\cap[0,1]^m\). A participant receives either all or none of a category. That last condition fits settings where each participant can receive at most one entitlement of each category.

An instance consists of \(m\), the rational supply vector \(q\) with \(\sum_g q_g\geq 1\), and value-oracle access to a normalized, globally nonnegative, \(\sigma\)-subadditive valuation \(v:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{Q}\) whose single-item marginals are bounded by \(\Lambda\). A solution is a finite list of bundles and rational masses \(x_S\geq 0\), one for each listed \(S\subseteq[m]\), such that
\[ \sum_S x_S=1,\qquad x_\emptyset=0,\qquad \sum_{S:\,g\in S}x_S=q_g\quad\text{for every }g. \]
Thus every infinitesimal participant gets an integral bundle; only the fraction of the population receiving each bundle is fractional. Measure inequity by
\[ \Delta_v(x)=\max_{S:x_S>0}v(S)-\min_{S:x_S>0}v(S). \]
The constructive question is to find a feasible \(x\) with \(\Delta_v(x)\leq 5\Lambda+1\); its natural optimization version minimizes \(\Delta_v(x)\).

I expect the fixed-bound problem to be tractable, as a Class A candidate. For a guessed value interval, its feasibility constraints are linear in the masses \(x_S\), with exponentially many bundle variables. A configuration LP and a pricing oracle therefore look like a natural route, and Theorem 24 gives evidence that this kind of additive equity guarantee can be achieved efficiently. That evidence is not a proof for arbitrary \(q\): the hard step is pricing over bundles whose values lie in a specified interval. Whether \(\sigma\)-subadditivity and bounded marginals make that pricing task efficient is a central open question for this mirror; if not, this could instead be continuum-specifically hard.

The mirror covers only Theorem 24’s identical-valuation equitable allocation result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s cake outcomes, its envy-freeness results, or its graph applications. Further questions include whether the \(5\Lambda+1\) guarantee holds for every rational supply vector, whether the optimal spread can be computed or improved, and whether the model extends to a finite set of heterogeneous valuation types with type-specific supplies.

The soft spot is the supply model. The paper partitions a finite set of distinct items among agents; this mirror introduces scalable stocks of resource categories and requires at most one item of each category per recipient. Theorem 24 does not directly establish the mirror’s guarantee for those arbitrary per-capita supplies, so translating between the two models is part of the research, not a consequence to assume. Still, the problem keeps the paper’s core question—how to distribute indivisible bundles so recipients receive nearly equal value—while making population mass the object being allocated. That makes it a plausible follow-up for the authors and a referee, even if the new supply assumption is the point they would scrutinize first.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot use the decisive objection that the paper lacks a computational result: Theorem 24 is a named algorithmic result, and it is the proponent’s strongest anchor. The paper’s other central theorems are mostly existence results, while Theorem 16 concerns continuous cake outcomes with a finite set of agents. The best case against therefore has to rest on whether Theorem 24’s proposed mirror actually makes the population the continuous object.

In the proposed one-type model, the society’s type distribution is fixed at \(\mu=\delta_v\). The variable \(x_S\) instead describes how mass is assigned across bundles, subject to exact item-supply constraints. That is a coherent problem, but its mathematical work lies in choosing a distribution over outcomes for a homogeneous population. It is not the kind of complexity landscape over a distribution of voter types that motivates ChoCo. Calling the mass “people” does not by itself show that the continuous type population is doing computational work.

The strongest defense is that those masses really can represent a large cohort of interchangeable recipients, with inventories scaled per person. But that scaling changes the model substantially. Theorem 24 partitions one finite set of distinct items; the proposed mirror has per-capita supplies of categories and limits each recipient to one unit of each category. To compare the two, one would need to explain how copies of a category become the paper’s distinct items while preserving the valuation and the per-recipient capacity. The paper’s theorem does not supply that bridge. The proposed pricing problem may be worth studying, but its relevance would come from a new supply-and-assignment model, not from a continuous relaxation already latent in Theorem 24.

A stronger population-centered variant could give several complete valuation types positive mass and scale supplies with cohort size. That removes the fixed-\(\delta_v\) concern, but it also leaves the theorem’s setting: one must specify which bundles each type may receive and what fairness requires across the support. The paper’s \(\mathrm{EF}3\) condition is pairwise over agents and their actual bundles; a condition on type-level masses does not automatically preserve it. This might lead to a good new problem, but the paper does not make it a mirror of one of its algorithmic results.

So the honest negative case is narrow: the proposed anchor risks placing all the continuity in the assignment distribution while leaving the society’s type distribution trivial, and repairing that requires substantial modeling work beyond the paper. That is a reason to doubt the fit, not a reason to conclude that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The paper’s identical-agent allocation result is unusually hospitable to high multiplicity, and I cannot responsibly rule out a convincing multi-type, per-capita formulation.
