The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), candidate centers \(M\), a metric \(d\) on \(T\cup M\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(k\), and \(\rho\ge1\), with the promise that a \(\rho\)-proportional \(k\)-center set exists, compute \(X\subseteq M\), \(|X|=k\), with \(C_\mu(X)\le8\,\mathrm{OPT}_\rho\) and proportionality \(1+27(1+\rho)\), where \(C_\mu(X)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tD_t(X)\), \(D_t(X)=\min_{x\in X}d(t,x)\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\rho\) is the minimum \(C_\mu\) over \(\rho\)-proportional sets.

The model it lives in

Public-facility siting with finitely many complete distance-profile types \(T\): type \(t\) carries mass \(\mu_t\), an integral \(k\)-set \(X\) of centers is selected, each type pays \(D_t(X)\), and \(\mu(\{t:\rho d(t,y)<D_t(X)\})<1/k\) for every \(y\in M\); the internal LP uses opening variables \(v_j\) and assignment variables \(u_{tj}\), with objective \(C_\mu(X)\).

The objection that survived

The proponent's formulation asks to report infeasibility, whereas Theorem 2 only guarantees an output under a \(\rho\)-proportionality promise; this bounds the formulation but does not invalidate the mass analogue.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 1 and 2 and their mass-normalized structural arguments. It leaves Theorem 3, Corollaries 1–3, the local-capture heuristic's convergence behavior, and the empirical results largely untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the weighted-demand rounding yield the stated \(8\)-objective and \(1+27(1+\rho)\)-proportionality guarantees in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the bit length of \(\mu\), without expanding rational masses?
  • Can the bicriteria constants or the exact \(\rho\)-proportional optimization problem be improved in the finite-support model?
  • What input model is appropriate for atomless geographic populations, and which guarantees extend beyond finite support?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has a credible continuous mirror, with Theorem 2 as the lead anchor. The mirror does not make centers or assignments fractional. It makes the population high-multiplicity.

Take a finite set \(T\) of complete demand types and a finite candidate-center set \(M\), with a metric \(d\) on \(T\cup M\). A type records the location, or equivalently the complete distance profile to candidate centers, together with every other parameter used by the problem. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents of type \(t\). For \(X\subseteq M\), \(|X|=k\), let \(D_t(X)=\min_{x\in X}d(t,x)\), and let the normalized \(k\)-median objective be \(C_\mu(X)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tD_t(X)\).

The natural continuous version of proportionality is

\(X\) is \(\rho\)-proportional if, for every \(y\in M\), \(\mu(\{t\in T:\rho d(t,y)<D_t(X)\})<1/k\).

This is exactly the paper’s blocking-coalition definition with \(\lceil n/k\rceil\) replaced by population mass \(1/k\). A violating set of types has enough mass to form a blocking coalition; conversely, any blocking coalition must lie inside such a violating set. Each unit of mass still receives an integral nearest center, and \(X\) remains an ordinary \(k\)-element set. Only the population and the objective have been normalized.

A plausible regime is public-facility siting. Millions of residents live in a few hundred or thousand neighborhood/location types, many of whom have identical distances to the candidate park or hospital sites. Thus \(n\) is very large while \(\tau=|T|\ll n\). This is not an invented interpretation alien to the paper: its own examples use many co-located points, and its motivating public-parks story already treats clustering as a collective decision over residents. The type is not merely a demographic label; it is the complete metric preference relevant to the clustering problem.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper, though its rounding machinery builds on the cited work [8]. The theorem says: if there is a \(\rho\)-proportional clustering with \(k\)-median objective \(c\), then one can compute, in polynomial time in \(m\) and \(n\), a clustering that is \(O(\rho)\)-proportional and has \(k\)-median objective at most \(8c\). The proof gives the explicit proportionality factor \(1+27(1+\rho)\), with the constant \(27\) improved to \(13\) in the special case \(N=M\).

The corresponding problem is Proportional \(k\)-Median\(_\infty\):

Given \(T,M,d,\mu,k\), and \(\rho\ge1\), either report that no \(\rho\)-proportional \(k\)-center solution exists, or output a \(k\)-center set \(X\) satisfying \(C_\mu(X)\le 8\,\mathrm{OPT}_\rho\) and being \(O(\rho)\)-proportional, where \(\mathrm{OPT}_\rho\) is the minimum \(C_\mu(X)\) over all \(\rho\)-proportional \(X\).

This is not a weakened problem. It retains the paper’s center-selection decision, its group fairness condition, its metric preferences, and its global \(k\)-median objective. The only change is replacing repeated individual demands by their masses.

The paper’s LP compresses naturally. Define \(R_q^\mu\) as the least radius containing population mass \(1/k\) around candidate center \(q\): \(R_q^\mu=\inf\{r:\sum_{t:d(t,q)\le r}\mu_t\ge1/k\}\). With \(\gamma=\rho+1\), use variables \(v_j\) for opening centers and \(u_{tj}\) for assigning type \(t\) to candidate \(j\):

\(\min \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{j\in M}d(t,j)u_{tj}\),

subject to \(\sum_j u_{tj}=1\), \(u_{tj}\le v_j\), \(\sum_jv_j\le k\), and \(\sum_{j'\in M:d(q,j')\le\gamma R_q^\mu}v_{j'}\ge1\) for every \(q\in M\).

This has size polynomial in \(m\) and \(\tau\), rather than in the potentially enormous population size \(n\). The paper’s Lemma 1—the structural fact that coverage within \(\gamma R_q\) implies \((1+\gamma)\)-proportionality, while \(\gamma\)-proportionality implies coverage within \((1+\gamma)R_q\)—continues to hold with population mass replacing cardinality. The subsequent weighted-demand rounding gives the same bicriteria guarantee. The fractional \(u\) and \(v\) variables are only an internal optimization device; the returned public-facility set is integral.

I therefore expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable by continuous optimization. The interesting high-multiplicity question is whether the guarantee can be expressed and implemented in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the bit length of the rational masses, rather than in the expanded population size. Further questions include whether the \(8\) objective factor or the \(O(\rho)\) fairness loss can be improved, and whether the exact \(\rho\)-proportional optimization problem has a sharper configuration-LP or separation formulation.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 1, also proved in this paper. It states that Greedy Capture, Algorithm 1, always returns a \((1+\sqrt{2})\)-proportional clustering, and that this bound is tight for some instance. The algorithm continuously grows balls around candidate centers, removes mass already captured by opened centers, and opens a new center whenever its ball contains at least the entitlement threshold.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Greedy Capture\(_\infty\):

Given \(T,M,d,\mu,k\), maintain a residual mass distribution. As the radius \(\delta\) grows, remove residual mass within distance \(\delta\) of already opened centers; whenever an unused \(y\in M\) has residual mass at least \(1/k\) in its radius-\(\delta\) ball, open \(y\) and remove that mass. Return \(k\) centers, adding arbitrary centers if fewer than \(k\) were opened.

The question is whether the returned \(X\) is guaranteed to be \((1+\sqrt2)\)-proportional for every finite-support distribution \(\mu\), and whether the factor is tight. The answer should again be Class A. The proof uses only triangle inequality, the \(1/k\) mass threshold, and the existence of a farthest type in the finite support, so it transfers directly. The paper’s tight six-point construction becomes a continuous-population instance simply by assigning mass \(1/6\) to each point. This mirror also preserves the paper’s boundary question: the paper’s lower-bound constructions rule out factors below \(2\), while Theorem 1 gives \(1+\sqrt2\), leaving a genuine approximation gap in the continuous formulation.

These two mirrors cover the paper’s core theoretical results—Theorems 1 and 2—not its empirical experiments or its sampling implementation results. The paper contains no named NP-hardness result that would serve as a hardness anchor, so I would not pretend that this case establishes hardness transfer. Its positive contribution is instead a plausible Class A mirror whose LP and greedy structure become more natural, not less, when the electorate is represented by mass.

The weakest point is that, for finite support, one can expand each mass \(\mu_t\) into many identical individuals and recover the paper’s finite instance. Thus the continuous theorem may initially look like bookkeeping rather than a new algorithmic result. Moreover, ordinary point-cloud clustering often has nearly as many distinct locations as individuals, making the high-multiplicity regime scenario-dependent. I would concede both points. The case survives because the paper’s public-facility interpretation supplies a credible regime with many interchangeable residents, and because the programme is asking a high-multiplicity complexity question—not claiming that every clustering dataset should be viewed as a continuum. Even if the answer is already tractable, this is a genuine continuous-population mirror and a clean Class A instance for the programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors are almost entirely invariant under population aggregation. Section 3.1 already describes the \(k\)-median rounding in terms of weighted demands \(t_i\), not intrinsically named individuals. Replacing repeated unit-demand points by one type of rational mass therefore requires no new optimization idea, separation problem, or structural lemma. The LP simply changes \(\sum_i\) into \(\sum_t \mu_t\), while the rounding proof is the same demand-consolidation argument. The proponent’s formulation also overstates what Theorem 2 supplies: the theorem is conditional on the existence of a proportional solution and does not decide infeasibility. Its repaired promise version is valid, but computationally it is a compressed weighted restatement of the paper’s own machinery.

The best rescue for Theorem 2 is the public-facility scenario with many residents sharing a finite set of distance profiles. That scenario is perfectly plausible, but it makes the objection one of yield rather than validity. The paper’s problem already depends only on aggregate demand at those profiles. The continuous formulation does not expose a population-driven obstruction, an exponential type-space pricing problem, or a new complexity boundary; it mainly records that duplicate points can be stored with weights. That may be useful implementationally, but it is a thin ChoCo case study.

Theorem 1 is even harder to use as a negative anchor. The weighted version of Greedy Capture is obtained by replacing cardinality by residual mass and opening a center once its ball contains mass \(1/k\). Its proof transfers essentially line by line: the only ingredients are triangle inequality, the threshold \(1/k\), and a farthest support type. The tight example has only six support points and therefore says nothing about a population continuum; duplicating those six types merely reproduces the same finite instance. If one instead allows an atomless geographic population, the paper no longer specifies an exact computational input model for evaluating masses of balls, so that version is underdefined rather than a stronger mirror.

But this still does not defeat the anchor. The finite-support formulation is exactly the programme’s permitted high-multiplicity model, and the gap between the lower bound \(2\) and the guarantee \(1+\sqrt{2}\) remains a legitimate continuous question. The fact that the proof is simple, or that the answer may already be apparent, is not an admissible objection under the programme’s rules.

Thus the honest negative case is weak. There is no identity dependence, no vanishing fairness condition, and no absence of a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 both admit sensible population-mass versions. One can argue that they are low-yield mirrors whose main contribution is input compression, but one cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The proponent’s case should survive, probably as a modest Class A mirror rather than a flagship ChoCo example.
