The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types on \(C\), a preferred candidate \(w\in C\), and \(k\), decide whether there exists \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{w\}\) with \(|D|\le k\) such that, after restricting every type to \(C\setminus D\), \(w\) is the unique Bucklin winner under \(\mu\).

The model it lives in

Types \(t\) are complete rankings of \(C\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); the decision variable is the candidate set \(D\), with \(|D|\le k\). Each type is restricted to \(C\setminus D\), and Bucklin winners are computed from the resulting mass-weighted level scores.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 3.5 and 3.14, predicting Class B for candidate deletion and Class A for voter-mass addition, while leaving the remaining Bucklin and fallback control results unclaimed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the Class-B hardness of Theorem 3.5 be formalized for normalized rational mass input under support-size or candidate-count parameterizations?
  • Does continuous voter-mass addition admit exact rounding or additive-error guarantees for finite high-multiplicity elections?
  • Which remaining Bucklin and fallback control modes have tractable or hard continuous-population mirrors?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper supports two faithful mirrors with opposite continuization behaviour. The lead is voter addition: the discrete problem is hard because the chair selects whole voters, while its high-multiplicity version becomes a linear feasibility problem. A second mirror shows the boundary: candidate-deletion hardness survives because the combinatorics live in the agenda, not in voter multiplicity.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.14, proved in this paper. It states that Bucklin voting is resistant to constructive control by adding voters: given registered voters, an unregistered pool, a preferred candidate, and a bound \(k\), deciding whether at most \(k\) pool voters can be added so that the preferred candidate becomes the unique Bucklin winner is NP-hard. The theorem also proves parameterized resistance: the problem is W[2]-hard when parameterized by the number of voters added. The reduction is from \(k\)-DOMINATING SET.

I would mirror it as Continuous Constructive Control by Adding Voter Mass for Bucklin. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a finite set \(T\) of complete ranking types, a rational registered-mass distribution \(\mu\), a rational unregistered-pool distribution \(\nu\), a preferred candidate \(w\), and a rational mass budget \(\beta\). The decision variable is \(z_t\), the amount of type-\(t\) pool mass recruited, with

\[ 0\le z_t\le \nu_t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{t\in T}z_t\le \beta. \]

The resulting society is \(\eta=\mu+z\). If \(M=\sum_t\eta_t\), define

\[ S_i^\eta(c)=\sum_{t:\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\le i}\eta_t. \]

Candidate \(c\)'s Bucklin level is the least \(i\) with \(S_i^\eta(c)>M/2\), and the winners at that level are the candidates with maximum \(S_i^\eta(c)\). The question is whether some feasible \(z\) makes \(w\) the unique Bucklin winner. A solution is the mass vector \(z\), not an encoding or a surrogate election.

The regime is plausible. Think of a very large software-agent electorate, platform poll, or membership organization with millions of registered and eligible-but-unregistered agents, but only a modest number of repeated preference profiles. A type includes the complete ranking and any eligibility information relevant to recruitment. Thus \(N\gg\tau\): many agents share each type, and the chair mobilizes a fraction of a type cohort. For a finite election with \(n_t\) registered and \(q_t\) unregistered agents out of a base population \(N\), take \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), \(\nu_t=q_t/N\), and \(\beta=k/N\). Clearing denominators recovers the corresponding finite instance.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For each possible Bucklin level \(r\), test whether \(w\) can be uniquely winning at level \(r\). The conditions are linear in \(z\): for every \(j<r\), every candidate must have \(S_j^\eta(c)\le M/2\); at level \(r\), \(w\) must satisfy \(S_r^\eta(w)>M/2\) and \(S_r^\eta(w)>S_r^\eta(c)\) for every \(c\ne w\). There are only \(m\) choices of \(r\), so linear programming gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length.

This is exactly the kind of transition ChoCo is meant to expose. Theorem 3.14’s discrete hardness comes from selecting indivisible voters from a pool. Once many agents of each type are represented by divisible mass, the selection becomes a continuous allocation. The theorem’s W[2]-hardness should therefore disappear in this mirror, while the underlying Bucklin control question remains recognizable to the authors.

The second anchor is Theorem 3.5, also proved in this paper. It states that Bucklin voting is resistant to constructive control by deleting candidates: deciding whether at most \(k\) candidates can be deleted so that a preferred candidate becomes the unique Bucklin winner is NP-hard. It further proves W[2]-hardness when parameterized by the number of candidates deleted, via a parameterized reduction from \(k\)-DOMINATING SET.

Its mirror is Continuous Bucklin Control by Deleting Candidates. An instance consists of \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types, a preferred candidate \(w\), and an integer \(k\). A solution is a set \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{w\}\) with \(|D|\le k\). Every type ranking is restricted to \(C\setminus D\), and Bucklin scores are computed from the resulting population masses. The question is whether \(w\) is the unique continuous Bucklin winner.

This is a natural high-multiplicity setting for a large electorate of software agents, customers, or organizational voters whose rankings repeat across many individuals, while the chair controls which alternatives remain on the ballot. The population is genuinely continuous even though the action—removing candidates—remains discrete.

Here the expected classification is Class B: hardness transfers. Take the election constructed in Theorem 3.5 and assign each listed vote mass \(1/(2n)\), aggregating identical rankings if desired. Every majority comparison and score comparison is preserved under multiplication by \(1/(2n)\). Replicating each type \(q\) times gives an arbitrarily large electorate with the same distribution, so the reduction is compatible with high multiplicity. Candidate deletion remains an integral combinatorial choice, and the parameter \(k\) is unchanged. Thus the NP-hardness and W[2]-hardness are not artifacts of named voter identities.

These two mirrors cover only Theorems 3.14 and 3.5; I would not claim to have continuized the whole \(22\)-case table. They already give a useful boundary: voter-mass addition is a genuine Class-A relaxation, while candidate control remains Class B. Natural follow-up questions include continuous fallback-control variants, optimal rather than budgeted recruitment, approximation and rounding bounds back to finite electorates, and mixed problems combining candidate deletion with voter-mass transfers.

The weakest point is the first mirror’s fractional recruitment. An opponent can say that adding \(0.37\) of a voter type is not literally the paper’s action. That criticism is valid at the finite-agent level, but it is precisely the high-multiplicity move: the action is recruiting \(37\%\) of a large cohort whose members are indistinguishable for the problem. The candidate-deletion mirror is less vulnerable on fidelity, though its population masses are passive coefficients and its continuum-specific payoff is mainly a sharp Class-B boundary. Together, however, they make a credible positive case: the paper contains a named hardness anchor whose indivisibility dissolves, and another whose agenda-level hardness survives the population continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is ultimately not strong enough: both anchors survive the programme’s gate.

Theorem 3.5 is a valid Class-B mirror. Give each ranking type mass \(\mu_t\), delete a set \(D\) of candidates, restrict every ranking to \(C\setminus D\), and recompute Bucklin scores from the masses. This is exactly a high-multiplicity version of candidate control. The reduction’s finite voters can be assigned rational masses, and repeating every type \(q\) times gives arbitrarily large cohorts without changing the outcome. The fact that the hardness survives, or that the continuous population is passive during the action, is not an objection: Class B is an intended result of the programme.

The only serious attack is on Theorem 3.14. Its original action selects indivisible, named voters from an unregistered pool. One might argue that fractional recruitment is a different problem, and that the theorem itself supplies no evidence that voters occur in interchangeable cohorts. That would make the proposed \(z_t\)-model an unjustified fractionalization.

But the universal burden defeats that objection. A large platform, membership organization, or software-agent population can contain registered and unregistered cohorts sharing the same ranking and eligibility status. Eligibility and recruitability can be included in the type. Recruiting a fraction \(z_t\) of such a cohort is then precisely the high-multiplicity interpretation of the paper’s action. Equivalently, one can use a fixed society whose types include an active/inactive status and convert inactive mass into active mass. The Bucklin conditions remain linear in the resulting masses, so the continuous problem is a genuine LP.

Insisting that recruitment must remain person-by-person merely rejects the high-multiplicity regime itself. Likewise, the disappearance of the paper’s W[2]-hardness is not a defect: that is exactly the computational phenomenon continuization is meant to expose.

Thus the negative case can fairly warn that the voter-addition mirror is unusually generic and that its continuous algorithm may be an easy baseline rather than a deep theorem. It cannot show that the question is unfaithful or uninteresting under the programme’s definition. The candidate-deletion anchor is a legitimate Class-B boundary, and the voter-addition anchor is a legitimate Class-A relaxation. I cannot honestly defeat either one, so the universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror fails.
