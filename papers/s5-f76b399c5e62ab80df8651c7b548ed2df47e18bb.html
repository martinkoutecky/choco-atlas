The continuous mirror question

Given item types \(j\in[m]\) with rational per-capita capacities \(q_j\), buyer types \(\theta\in\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) satisfying \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\), multi-unit valuations \(v_\theta\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\), and a finite-support benchmark allocation \(Y\) with masses \(y_{\theta,b}\), compute a finite package catalogue \(\mathcal P\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^m\), package masses \(z_P\), nonnegative linear package prices \(p_P\), and an allocation \(x_{\theta,r}\) of type mass to integral package bundles \(r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{\mathcal P}\). Require \(\sum_{P\in\mathcal P}z_P P_j\le q_j\), \(\sum_r x_{\theta,r}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}r_P\le z_P\), and, whenever \(x_{\theta,r}>0\), require \(r\) to maximize \(v_\theta(\sum_P r_P P)-\sum_P r_Pp_P\). The objective is to obtain \(\mathrm{SW}(x)\ge\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{SW}(Y)\), with complexity polynomial in \(m\), \(|\Theta|\), encoding length, benchmark support, and demand queries, independent of the raw replication factor \(N\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity multi-unit CWE welfare problem: buyer types \(\theta\) carry mass \(\mu_\theta\), item types have per-capita capacities \(q_j\), packages are integral count vectors with linear prices, and the output is a stable mass allocation achieving at least \(\frac{1}{2}\) of benchmark welfare.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4.1's polynomial bound is in the raw buyer count \(N\), and the paper supplies no invariant proving that the evolving bundle, price, and \(\textsc{RaisePrices}\) histories collapse to polynomially many type-level states; the proponent identifies this gap but does not resolve it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the benchmarked welfare and CWE-construction results in Theorems 3.1, 4.1, and 4.2. It leaves the revenue approximation in Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 2, the item-pricing lower bounds, and the \(\frac{2}{3}+\varepsilon\) CWE efficiency lower bound unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper's agent-by-agent price increases and bundle merges be compressed to polynomially many type-level states, independent of \(N\)?
  • Can the endogenous package catalogue and its mass allocation be represented with polynomial support while preserving the \(\frac{1}{2}\) welfare guarantee?
  • Does the benchmarked continuous problem admit a Class A algorithm through demand-oracle or configuration-LP methods?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity, continuous-population version of the paper’s welfare-guaranteeing CWE algorithm. I would call it Mass-CWE Welfare.

The relevant anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It states that Algorithm \(\ref{cwe-alg-poly}\) returns a combinatorial Walrasian equilibrium whose social welfare is at least half that of an optimal allocation. Its polynomial-time content is supplied by Theorem 4.1, also proved here: the algorithm runs in polynomial time using demand queries. More precisely, the preceding Theorem 3.1 establishes the \(\frac12\)-guarantee relative to an arbitrary initial allocation \(Y\), and Theorem 4.1 shows that the refined implementation preserves this guarantee in polynomial time; taking \(Y\) to be optimal gives Theorem 4.2.

I would not claim a hardness anchor: the paper mentions hardness of welfare maximization and cites Briest for a revenue lower bound, but it does not prove a named NP-hardness theorem of its own.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover.

There is a finite catalogue \(M\) of item types, with per-capita supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\). There is a finite set of buyer types \(\Theta\), with \(|\Theta|=\tau\), and a rational population distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\). A type is its complete valuation specification, including its valuation \(v_t\) for every integral bundle of item copies. Demand is accessed through the same kind of demand oracle as in the paper.

The input also contains a finite-support benchmark allocation \(Y\). If \(y_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\), then \(\sum_B y_{t,B}=\mu_t\), and the total mass of every item type used by \(Y\) is at most its supply \(q_j\). Its welfare is \(SW(Y)=\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}v_t(B)\).

The output must consist of:

  • a measurable partition of item mass into finitely many integral package classes \(\mathcal B\), with package \(B\) available at mass \(z_B\);
  • nonnegative package prices \(p_B\);
  • an allocation \(x_{t,R}\), where mass \(x_{t,R}\) of type \(t\) receives the integral set \(R\subseteq\mathcal B\).

The package masses must respect item supplies. Every positive-mass assignment must be demand-maximizing: if \(x_{t,R}>0\), then \(R\) maximizes \(v_t(R)-\sum_{B\in R}p_B\) among all available package sets. Thus prices are linear over the seller’s bundles, exactly as in a CWE, unsold mass is permitted, and every individual infinitesimal buyer still receives an integral bundle. Only the aggregate assignment \(x\) is fractional.

The problem asks for such a mass-CWE with \(SW(x)\ge \frac12 SW(Y)\). With \(Y\) chosen as an optimal unconstrained allocation, this becomes the continuous analogue of Theorem 4.2: find a CWE achieving at least half of optimal welfare.

The natural regime is a large standardized procurement or spectrum market. Suppose \(N\) firms buy combinations of a fixed set of service or spectrum components, but only \(\tau\) valuation profiles recur: for example, thousands of firms may fall into a few dozen package-demand types. The seller has \(Nq_j\) copies of item type \(j\), and each firm receives an integral package. The market is large because \(N\gg\tau\), not because the goods are divisible.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity model. A finite replicated market with \(N\mu_t\) buyers of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) copies of item \(j\) normalizes to the mass instance above. Conversely, rational masses can be scaled by a common denominator to produce a finite replicated market. The continuous object is the population distribution; bundle integrality and the seller’s bundling operation remain intact.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The mirror preserves the distinctive ingredients of the paper: arbitrary combinatorial valuations, bundle formation by the seller, linear prices over the reduced market, demand-maximizing buyers, market non-clearance, and social-welfare optimization. It does not merely replace an integral allocation by a fractional one. A unit of population receives one integral package; \(x_{t,R}\) records how much indistinguishable population receives each package.

I would expect this benchmarked Mass-CWE Welfare problem to be Class A. The paper’s algorithm interacts with buyers only through demand queries. In a high-multiplicity market, all buyers of type \(t\) answer the same query, so the individual-agent loop should be compressible into operations on residual type masses. Price increases, bundle merges, and rejected mass can be processed at type level. The expected complexity target would be polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the encoding length, the support size of \(Y\), and the number of demand queries, with no dependence on the raw population size \(N\).

That conclusion is not automatic. The continuous algorithm would need to prove that the ascending-price and irreversible-merging argument survives when a positive mass of identical agents may be split among several tied demanded bundles. This is a real technical question, not a change of notation. Configuration-LP and column-generation methods also become natural here, since the population constraints are linear in mass and the exponentially many configurations are exactly what demand oracles can price.

The mirror should not be overstated. If one removes the benchmark \(Y\) and asks the algorithm to find a welfare-optimal starting allocation for arbitrary combinatorial valuations, the problem likely remains hard for reasons living in the item structure and valuation combinatorics, not in population multiplicity. The paper itself emphasizes that its initial allocation is essential for efficient implementation. Thus the continuous relaxation plausibly helps with the equilibrium-construction stage while leaving the underlying welfare-maximization stage hard.

The weakest point is therefore the supply scaling. If the paper’s single finite stock of items is held fixed while the number of buyers tends to infinity, almost all mass must receive nothing, and the mirror becomes economically uninteresting. My proposal scales item copies with population size. That is a co-modification of the market, but it is the natural high-multiplicity regime for repeated procurement or replicated combinatorial auctions, and it is explicitly the kind of population-linked modification that continuization requires. The second weakness is that Theorem 4.2 does not itself prove the mass-compressed algorithm; it supplies the exact finite analogue and a strong structural template. The continuous theorem would be new work—but it would be recognizably the authors’ question rather than a tractable problem obtained by stripping away their combinatorial market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorems 4.1–4.2; there is no missing-result objection, since the paper does give a named polynomial-time welfare guarantee.

The literal high-multiplicity version is degenerate. The paper has \(m\) indivisible objects but can have arbitrarily many buyers. With \(N\) repeated buyers and fixed \(m\), at most \(m\) buyers receive nonempty bundles. After normalizing welfare by \(N\), almost the entire population disappears, and the distribution \(\mu\) has no substantive role in the CWE computation. The theorem is then merely being padded with zero-valued agents, not continuized.

The proposed repair—scaling supplies to \(Nq_j\)—is much stronger, but it changes the problem. The paper’s valuation is \(v_i:2^M\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}\), where a bundle is a subset of a finite set of named objects. With \(N\) copies, one must either label the copies, making the item universe grow as \(\Theta(Nm)\), or identify copies and replace subsets by multi-unit count vectors. The latter is a new multi-unit combinatorial-auction model, not the paper’s CWE model. In particular, the endogenous partition and bundle-merging operation must be redefined.

The proposed mass notation also hides this difficulty. If a package class \(B\) has positive mass, a buyer’s demand depends on how many copies of \(B\) are available, not merely on the set of package classes \(\mathcal B\). A faithful model therefore needs multiplicities or count vectors. But bundle merging can generate many such vectors, and the endogenous partition may have support growing with the scaled supply. The paper’s configuration LP handles a fixed partition; it does not provide a finite-dimensional description of the partition itself.

Nor is the paper’s algorithm automatically compressible by buyer type. Its proof creates one initial bundle and one initial price for each agent, maintains an individual pool, and uses the agent-specific order produced by \(\textsc{RaisePrices}\). Identical buyers can subsequently occupy different bundles at different prices and follow different merge histories. The published polynomial bound is polynomial in the raw number \(n\), not in the number of types or in the encoding length of multiplicities. Applying it to an \(N\)-fold lift therefore takes \(\Theta(N)\) work. A genuinely continuous theorem would need a new invariant showing that this evolving distribution of bundles and prices admits a succinct aggregate representation; the paper supplies no such invariant.

That said, this is not an airtight negative case. If one accepts per-capita supplies, anonymous multi-unit valuations, and a carefully bounded package grammar, then Mass-CWE Welfare is a coherent and potentially worthwhile high-multiplicity problem. Welfare is identity-free, and positive mass can receive integral bundles. The honest conclusion is therefore that the proponent has not yet established a continuous algorithm, but the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is weak: the strongest proposed mirror survives once the market is modelled as a replicated multi-unit economy.
