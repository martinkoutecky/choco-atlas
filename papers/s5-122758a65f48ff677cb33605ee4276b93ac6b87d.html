Why no mirror

The proposed mass-electorate extension is a coherent question about the paper’s agenda-setting game. But Theorem 1 is a strategic characterization and Theorem 4 a geometric result; neither states a computational result of the required kind. The proponent also acknowledges that gap, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

A mass-electorate extension could cover the finite-alternative equilibrium outcome characterization in Theorem 1. It would leave Theorem 4’s spatial geometry and the paper’s other results without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a mass electorate version of their agenda control model. A type could be a voter’s full preference type, represented in the paper’s spatial setting by an ideal point. In a large assembly with many repeated preference blocs, \(\mu_t\) would be the share of voters of type \(t\). The agenda setter proposes policies over \(H\) rounds; a proposal passes when a majority by mass prefers its continuation outcome. A possible new computational question is to compute the subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome from the type distribution, initial default, and horizon.

But this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that question. The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which characterizes equilibrium outcomes in finite-alternative settings by iterating the agenda setter’s favorite-improvement map and shows that, with at least \(|X|-1\) rounds, the agenda setter gets her favorite policy from every default iff the problem is manipulable; and Theorem 4, also proved here, which establishes generic manipulability in the spatial model with at least three dimensions under its non-coplanarity condition. These are strategic and geometric results, not claims that an underlying problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

The proposed mass-electorate question is a plausible *new* population continuization: repeated blocs give many agents but relatively few types. The paper’s continuous policy space and transfer policies do not themselves continuize the population, which remains finite. Under your anchor requirement, though, there is no admissible positive case to build from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium outcomes, and Theorem 4 gives a geometric condition for generic manipulability. Theorems 2 and 3 use continuous policy spaces, but the population remains finite. None asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity. That leaves no paper-stated computational problem for ChoCo to chart.

The best version of the proposed mirror is coherent: let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of each complete preference type, and say a proposal is accepted when more than half the mass prefers its continuation outcome. For finite \(X\), define \(\phi_\mu(x)\) as the agenda setter’s favorite policy among those strictly preferred to \(x\) by a majority of mass (including \(x\)). The equilibrium outcome is then \(\phi_\mu^T(x_0)\). But with explicitly listed alternatives, one can compute the weighted majority comparisons and iterate this map; after at most \(|X|-1\) steps it reaches a fixed point. This is a valid high-multiplicity formulation, but the paper’s characterization makes the resulting computation look like straightforward aggregation and iteration, not a new algorithmic landscape. High multiplicity is a sensible regime here; it is not itself the objection.

Theorem 4 is an even thinner computational anchor. In a finite-support spatial model, its non-coplanarity condition can be checked with determinant tests. A richer question—computing \(\phi_\mu(x)\) when policies vary continuously—would involve maximizing the agenda setter’s utility subject to a weighted majority constraint. For Euclidean types, that constraint amounts to weighted halfspace queries over ideal points. This could be a worthwhile optimization problem, but the paper provides no finite encoding for its continuous policy space or utilities. Making it computationally precise would require selecting a new input model and would put the main difficulty in the policy geometry.

So I would not recommend this paper as a strong, paper-grounded continuization target: its named results are strategic and geometric, and the clearest finite-alternative mirror collapses to an explicit map iteration. The negative case is weakest against the spatial optimization variant. A carefully encoded version could be a good new computational question about a mass electorate, but that possibility is not established by the results this paper actually presents.
