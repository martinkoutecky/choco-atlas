The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is that the authors already study exactly the kind of population the programme wants to continuize: a continuum of agents of measure \(1\), with a state \(s\in M(V)\) giving the population shares of valuation types. So the high-multiplicity regime is plainly meaningful for public-good provision. But that is also the main obstacle to a *mirror*: the paper’s central model is already continuous-population.

The closest named structural result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For a regular social choice function, robust implementability and weak coalition-proofness hold if and only if payments are \(p_0\) when the good is not provided and \(p_1\) when it is provided, and provision is monotone in the shares of net beneficiaries and net opponents: if one state has at least as many beneficiaries and no more opponents than another, its provision decision must be at least as high. That is a characterization of admissible mechanisms, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 are likewise structural mechanism-design results, not complexity results.

One could formulate a new algorithmic problem from Section 6—for example, optimize expected surplus over provision rules on a finite list of rational population states, subject to the Theorem 2 constraints. But the paper does not give a named complexity result anchoring that problem, and the population in it is already continuous. Calling it a continuous mirror would therefore miss both requirements.

So I cannot supply a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem per anchor. The paper strongly supports the *plausibility* of a continuous-population model for this domain; it does not provide a discrete computational result whose continuous mirror this case can honestly argue for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate the positive case names, but it cannot anchor a continuous mirror. It characterizes robustly implementable, weakly coalition-proof social choice functions; it does not state the complexity of a computational problem. And its society is already a continuum: states are distributions \(s\in M(V)\), and the result is about how provision depends on population shares. So the proposed mirror would neither take a discrete computational result from this paper nor continuize its population.

A stronger attempt might turn Section 6’s second-best problem into an algorithmic one: give a finite list of rational states and a prior, then compute an optimal provision rule and payments subject to the paper’s constraints. That could be a sensible follow-on project. But the paper does not define that computational input or output, and the population in it would still be continuous. Choosing a finite state list and a mechanism encoding would create a new algorithmic problem; it would not supply the missing computational result for a mirror. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 have the same limitation: they are mechanism-design characterizations, not complexity claims.

The negative case is strong on the programme’s actual criterion: there is no discrete computational result here to continuize, and the central population model is already continuous. It cannot establish that no useful computational project could grow from the paper. The finite-state optimization is the clearest possible growth path, but it is a new question, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
