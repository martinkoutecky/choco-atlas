The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(t\ge3\), given \(m\) candidates, a target \(p\), and a society supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings \(r_i\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\), what is the minimum total mass-weighted number of demotion operations needed to make \(p\) a co-winner under \(t\)-approval, when each ranking type's mass may be split among campaign outcomes? Equivalently, choose masses \(y_{i,S}\ge0\) ending with each size-\(t\) approval set \(S\), minimize \(\sum_{i,S}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\), and require \(\sum_{i,S}y_{i,S}(\mathbf{1}[p\in S]-\mathbf{1}[c\in S])\ge0\) for every \(c\ne p\), where \(\sum_S y_{i,S}=\mu_i\) and \(\kappa_i(S)=\rho_i(w_i(S))-t\) for the least-preferred member \(w_i(S)\) of \(S\) in \(r_i\).

The model it lives in

Use complete rankings as types, rational masses \(\mu_i\), and decision variables \(y_{i,S}\) for the mass of type \(i\) ending with approval set \(S\). Minimize \(\sum_{i,S}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\), where \(\kappa_i(S)\) is the minimum number of demotion operations per unit mass needed to make \(S\) the top-\(t\) set; impose type-mass conservation and linear co-winner constraints. Although there are exponentially many sets \(S\), the pricing oracle can enumerate the least-preferred member and choose the other \(t-1\) members by reduced cost.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's constructive, unpriced \(t\)-Approval-TNC hardness for fixed \(t\ge3\); it leaves the approximation results, Borda results, candidate-specific prices, and destructive results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What additive rounding guarantee relates the continuous optimum to a finite election with \(N\) indivisible voters?
  • Which voter- or candidate-dependent price variants retain a polynomial pricing oracle?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s \(t\)-approval hardness result. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for every fixed \(t\ge 3\), finding a minimum-cost constructive \(t\)-approval-TNC strategy is NP-hard. The underlying problem takes an election and a preferred candidate \(p\), and seeks a sequence of demotion operations that makes \(p\) a co-winner; in the unpriced model, each operation costs one. The paper’s Lemma 1 gives the decision form used in the proof: for fixed \(t\ge 3\), deciding whether a strategy with at most \(tq\) demotions exists is NP-hard. The proof reduces from a restricted exact-cover-by-3-sets problem, showing the construction for \(t=3\) and extending it to each fixed \(t\ge4\). That is a substantive hardness anchor, not just an algorithmic lead.

My lead mirror is Continuous \(t\)-Approval-TNC. Fix \(t\ge3\). An instance consists of \(m\) candidates, a target \(p\), and a society supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings \(r_1,\ldots,r_\tau\), with binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\ge0\) summing to \(1\). A mass of agents with the same ranking can be split among different campaign sequences. Demoting a candidate by any valid number of positions on mass \(x\) costs \(x\), the mass-weighted version of one unpriced operation. The question is to find a finite aggregate plan of such demotions minimizing total cost, subject to \(p\) being a co-winner afterward. The final score of candidate \(c\) is the mass whose resulting ranking puts \(c\) in the top \(t\).

This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem: rankings, demotion operations, the scoring rule, the constructive goal, and the per-operation cost are all retained. The population is the continuous object; the campaign actions are applied to portions of it. A plausible regime is a large election with, say, hundreds of thousands or millions of voters but only hundreds or thousands of recurring complete ranking types. Each type is complete for this unpriced problem: its members have the same ranking and face the same operation costs. A campaign that can target a fraction of a large audience makes fractional mass a natural quantity.

There is a compact exact formulation. For each initial ranking \(r_i\) and each size-\(t\) set \(S\), let \(y_{i,S}\) be the mass ending with \(S\) as its approved set. Let \(w_i(S)\) be the least-preferred member of \(S\) in \(r_i\), and let \(\rho_i(c)\) be \(c\)’s rank there. The minimum number of operations per unit mass needed to make \(S\) the top \(t\) is \(\kappa_i(S)=\rho_i(w_i(S))-t\): demote the candidates above \(w_i(S)\) that are not in \(S\). Thus the mirror asks to minimize \(\sum_{i,S}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\), subject to \(\sum_S y_{i,S}=\mu_i\) for every \(i\), \(y_{i,S}\ge0\), and \(\sum_{i,S}y_{i,S}(\mathbf{1}[p\in S]-\mathbf{1}[c\in S])\ge0\) for every \(c\ne p\).

Although this LP has exponentially many set variables, its pricing problem is polynomial. For a given initial ranking and dual weights on candidates, enumerate which candidate \(w\) is the least-preferred member of \(S\). Its cost is then fixed; among candidates ranked above \(w\), choose the \(t-1\) with the best reduced-cost coefficients. Trying each \(w\) takes polynomial time in \(m\). The standard separation-to-optimization machinery therefore gives an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the masses. I expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable. This is a concrete case where the population relaxation changes the computational question: the discrete reduction relies on choosing whole set-voters, while the continuous plan can split each type’s mass.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 1’s constructive, unpriced \(t\)-approval result. It does not continuize outcomes, time, or probabilities, and it makes no claim about the paper’s Borda, candidate-specific price, or destructive results. It raises useful follow-ups: what integrality gap or additive rounding guarantee relates the continuous optimum to an election with \(N\) individual voters, and which pricing extensions preserve the polynomial pricing oracle?

The weakest point is that a fractional campaign can allocate, for example, \(0.3\) of a ranking type to one sequence and \(0.7\) to another. That is not an exact finite-election strategy when individual voters are indivisible. It is the intended high-multiplicity relaxation, but the bridge back to discrete campaigns needs to be established. The mirror remains plausible because it preserves the authors’ underlying intervention and asks what happens when the audience is measured in shares—the same kind of demographic targeting that motivates their model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a convincing case against the \(t\)-approval anchor. Theorem 1 is a named computational result, and its continuous mirror is a natural high-multiplicity version of the same intervention. The paper’s individual voters can be grouped by ranking here: in the unpriced model, voters with the same ranking are indistinguishable for the problem. Large electorates with recurring ballot types are a plausible regime, and a campaign that acts on shares of those groups is a coherent scenario.

The proposed LP also appears to model the intervention faithfully. For an initial ranking and a desired top-\(t\) set \(S\), let \(w\) be the least-preferred member of \(S\). Every nonmember ranked above \(w\) must be demoted at least once; demoting those candidates yields \(S\) in the top \(t\) using exactly \(\rho(w)-t\) operations. So the stated cost is not an arbitrary relaxation. Once mass can split among resulting approval sets, the winner constraints are linear. For pricing, fix \(w\) and choose the other \(t-1\) members from candidates ranked above it; additive reduced costs make that choice efficiently searchable. I see no fundamental obstacle to the claimed polynomial optimization.

The best negative argument is that the bridge back to finite campaigns remains unfinished. A fractional plan might send \(0.3\) of one ranking type to one action and \(0.7\) to another; that is not itself an exact strategy for indivisible voters. The paper’s hardness reduction relies on such whole-voter choices, and the mirror does not establish how much rounding error a finite election incurs. But this limits what the continuous result says about discrete campaigns; it does not make the continuous question ill-posed or uninteresting. The programme explicitly treats the high-multiplicity relaxation as an object worth studying.

Arguments from individual targeting, operation granularity, or the fact that the continuous problem may be easier do not rescue the negative case: the first is absent from this anchor’s model, the second has a faithful mass-weighted interpretation, and an easier answer is precisely what the programme seeks to discover. This anchor survives.
