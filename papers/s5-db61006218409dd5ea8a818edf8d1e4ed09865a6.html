What the mirror covers

The proposal would cover verification for a new anonymous, finite-horizon nonatomic mechanism logic; it does not carry over the finite-player model-checking result or the paper's existing auction properties.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead, and sole anchor, is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: assuming the functions in \(F\) are computable in polynomial space, model checking SLK\([F]\) with imperfect information and memoryless strategies is PSPACE-complete. In the paper’s terms, the input is a finite weighted concurrent game structure \(G\), a position \(v\), a sentence \(\varphi\), and a set \(P\) of values; the question is whether \(J\varphi K^G(v)\in P\). The paper gives the polynomial-space upper-bound argument. Its completeness claim includes the lower bound from the qualitative Boolean SLK fragment; that lower bound is not developed in the displayed proof.

A continuous version I would put to the authors is Continuous-Population SLK\([F]\) Model Checking. Its society is a unit mass of agents distributed over a finite set \(T\) of complete types. A type fixes everything the mechanism uses about an agent: valuation, available reports and actions, costs or budgets, and information. The input gives the positive masses \(\mu_t\) as binary rationals, with support size \(\tau\). The agents are infinitesimal, and mechanism supplies and outcomes are normalized per capita or scale with population mass.

The mechanism is an explicit finite-horizon anonymous protocol. At each stage, \(\nu_{t,q}\) records how much type \(t\) mass is in protocol and observation state \(q\). A strategy for a type assigns action probabilities at its observable histories; the corresponding action flow is \(x_{t,q,a}=\nu_{t,q}\sigma_{t,q}(a)\). Rational piecewise-affine transition rules update the mass configuration from these flows. Labels such as type-level utility, revenue, and allocation are rational piecewise-affine functions of the flows and configuration. Imperfect information is represented by rational polyhedral sets of possible aggregate worlds and each type’s observations of them. Strategies are uniform within a complete type and observation class, as the paper’s strategies are uniform across indistinguishable positions.

The decision problem is: given this instance, an SLK\([F]\) formula \(\varphi\), and a rational threshold \(r\), is the formula’s value at the initial mass distribution at least \(r\)? Strategy quantifiers range over the type policies just described; knowledge ranges over compatible aggregate worlds; temporal operators follow the protocol until its terminal stage. A yes or no answer is the solution. The same language can ask whether some equilibrium implements a target allocation and payment rule for a given \(\mu\), or whether the resulting outcomes meet individual-rationality or budget-balance constraints. Those are applications of the underlying verification problem, not changes to the question being computed.

A plausible regime is a large procurement or capacity market with many small buyers and sellers of standardized goods. There may be hundreds of thousands of participants but only dozens of valuation, budget, and information types. Agents of each type share the same preferences and available actions; the auction aggregates bids and determines per-capita allocations and payments. This is a meaningful high-multiplicity regime, and the mass flow is the natural decision variable when the mechanism acts on aggregate bids. It also keeps the paper’s central concern—checking strategic and epistemic properties of a mechanism—rather than merely making outcomes fractional.

I expect this mirror to be continuum-specifically hard, though that is a research conjecture, not a hardness result. Quantifying over fractional type policies and evaluating knowledge over sets of aggregate distributions introduces real optimization inside strategic quantifiers. The paper’s PSPACE result is a strong baseline, but does not settle the continuous version. A natural first question for a prover is whether the piecewise-affine finite-horizon case remains in PSPACE, or whether its nested strategy and knowledge quantifiers yield a stronger lower bound. Further questions include whether a useful LP or separation formulation exists, and whether finite populations with \(N_t/N\) close to \(\mu_t\) approximate the continuous verification value, with a bound in \(N\), \(\tau\), and the horizon.

The soft spot is that the paper’s equilibria are those of a finite strategic game, while a unilateral deviation by an infinitesimal agent does not change aggregate flows. A faithful continuum model therefore needs a nonatomic best-response or Wardrop-style equilibrium interpretation, and authors may regard that as a substantive change. I still think the mirror is worth asking: it retains the same mechanism-level verification questions and makes the population itself the continuous object. It covers the anonymous, large-market regime and the paper’s strategic-property verification results there; it does not claim to mirror every finite auction in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor: it proves PSPACE-completeness for model checking SLK\([F]\) on finite weighted game structures. The strongest case against a mirror is that its complexity result depends on having finitely many named agents, each with a strategy whose unilateral replacement can change the play. That feature is central to the paper’s equilibrium formulas, not just a detail of its auction examples.

Consider the proponent’s improved setting: a large procurement market with finitely many valuation and information types, divisible per-capita supply, and anonymous aggregate transitions. It is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But for an atomless agent, changing strategy moves zero mass. If the mechanism’s state and outcomes depend only on aggregate flows, that deviation cannot test the paper’s Nash condition. If we preserve the agent’s individual effect on their own payoff, we need a marked representative agent alongside the aggregate population. Then the state, strategies, and equilibrium semantics are no longer those of the proposed mass-flow model. Alternatively, allowing each type’s mass to split among policies makes the population policy a distribution over strategies; the paper’s strategy quantifier and epistemic operator must then be given new semantics.

A finite-horizon, piecewise-affine version of that model could still be a worthwhile computational question. But it would be model checking for a new kind of nonatomic or mean-field strategic logic, not a high-multiplicity relaxation of the model-checking problem Theorem 2 classifies. The paper’s PSPACE result provides no direct bridge to that question because the finite theorem is about an explicit game structure, while the continuous version needs both a new aggregate input representation and a new account of individual deviations.

That is the best negative case, and its limit is clear: the resulting computational theory could be valuable in its own right, and the authors might recognize it as a natural extension of their work. I can argue that this anchor is a poor fit for the programme’s continuous-mirror method; I cannot honestly establish that no scenario makes it worth pursuing.
