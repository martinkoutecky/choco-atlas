The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of the article’s weighted tally. But the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its equation and tables describe the voting system; they are not named complexity results. So there is no qualifying anchor to quote, and I cannot present this as a case built on one.

A plausible scale-up would be a large consultation among hospital organizations, patients, and other healthcare professionals. Let \(G=\{P,A,H\}\) denote those three groups and \(R=\{\mathrm{SA},\mathrm{A},\mathrm{D},\mathrm{SD},\bot\}\) the four ballot choices plus nonresponse. The input is a distribution of ballot mass \(\mu_{g,r}\), with group masses in the summit’s proportions \(31/50,12/50,7/50\). Each unit of mass in a group has that group’s vote weight: \(w_P=1\), \(w_A=31/24\), and \(w_H=31/14\). These weights reproduce the article’s totals of \(31,15.5,15.5\) effective votes.

Call the problem Continuous Summit Tally: given these rational masses, determine whether the quorum is met and calculate the weighted score and whether it reaches the article’s agreement thresholds. In the paper’s units, quorum means at least \(90\%\) of the population responds. With \(W=\sum_g w_g\sum_{r\ne\bot}\mu_{g,r}\), the score per unit of population mass is
\[ S=\sum_g w_g\bigl(2\mu_{g,\mathrm{SA}}+\mu_{g,\mathrm{A}}-\mu_{g,\mathrm{SD}}\bigr). \]
The reported strong-agreement threshold is \(S\ge0.85(2W)\); the agreement threshold is \(S\ge0.85W\) and below the strong-agreement threshold. Multiplying by the population size recovers the article’s point units. The solution is the quorum result, score, and threshold results. This is an exact linear-time calculation in the number of represented types.

The population story is plausible if many organizations or people participate but share one of three stakeholder roles and the corresponding weight; there are at most fifteen role-and-response types, even if the population is large. The mirror covers the tally, group weighting, quorum, and reported thresholds—not the Delphi process or the summit’s wording decisions. It would likely be tractable, but that conclusion is about this proposed extension, not a result established by the paper. It could also prompt a new robustness question: how much ballot mass must change for a statement to cross an agreement threshold?

The weak point is central: the paper reports a system used by fifty delegates; it does not pose a computational problem or study a high-multiplicity regime. The continuous tally is a recognizable extension of its rule, but it is not a continuous version of any named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper contains no computational result to mirror. It reports a voting procedure: group weights, a quorum, and thresholds for classifying the total score. It states no algorithmic problem or complexity claim. The tally formula and table define the procedure; they are not complexity results. The proponent acknowledges this, so their proposed continuous tally is an extension of the paper’s rule rather than a mirror of a result in it.

The extension is well formed. A large consultation can plausibly have many participants in a few stakeholder groups, and role plus response is a complete type for this tally. But the input then has at most fifteen role-and-response types, and the output comes from evaluating fixed weighted sums and comparisons. That makes it a sensible way to report a large consultation, but it does not supply the paper with a computational problem whose complexity the programme could chart.

The suggested robustness question does not repair the gap. The article specifies how to score votes, but says nothing about which vote changes are available, what they cost, or how changes across statements might be coupled. Add those choices and one can define a mass-transfer problem; under the article’s fixed categories, its constraints are linear. A richer problem might be worth studying, but its strategic model would come from those added assumptions, not from a computational result in this paper.

I would not claim that no scenario could make the weighted tally a useful continuous object. Large consultations make that scenario plausible, and the continuous formulation is natural. The honest negative case is narrower: the paper offers no worthwhile *complexity mirror* as written. Its tally can be continuized, but the proposed extension supplies the computational question that the paper itself never asked.
