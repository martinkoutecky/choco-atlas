The continuous mirror question

Given finite \(C\), rational \(\mu\) over \(T=\mathcal L(C)\), and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), compute \(a\in C\) and \(\pi\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_c\pi(t,c)=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi(t,c)=q_\mu(c)\), where \(q_\mu(c)=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}(t)=c}\mu_t\), and \(\pi(t,c)>0\) only if \(a\succeq_t c\). Prove the worst-case bound \(\sup_d \operatorname{SC}_\mu(a,d)/\min_b\operatorname{SC}_\mu(b,d)\le 2+\alpha\) over measurable populations and compatible \(\alpha\)-decisive metrics, and determine whether the bound is tight.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity ordinal metric-distortion model: \(C\) is finite, \(\mu\) is mass over complete ranking types, \(q_\mu\) is plurality mass, and the output is \(a\) with a feasible coupling \(\pi\); performance is the worst-case \(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(a,d)/\min_b\operatorname{SC}_\mu(b,d)\) over compatible metric realizations.

The objection that survived

The proponent does not fully resolve whether latent metric distances must be included in the agent type; otherwise \(\mu\) specifies rankings while the cardinal realization remains an adversarial measurable population rather than a fixed finite-type object.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 2 and Corollary 1, Theorem 1 and Proposition 5 for \plumatching, and the plurality-only frontier underlying Theorems 3 and 4. It leaves the unrestricted randomized frontier, Condorcet and matching-uncovered examples, and the fairness-ratio result as separate extensions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the full-ranking continuous minimax problem admit a rule better than \(2+\alpha\) for finite \(m\) and \(\alpha<1\)?
  • Can the coupling formulation be solved efficiently when \(\mu\) is given implicitly rather than by explicit ranking-type support?
  • Does the \(2+\alpha\) guarantee extend to a continuous fairness objective based on the worst \(\rho\)-fraction of population mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a genuine high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s ordinal metric-distortion problem. The paper contains no NP-hardness, \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness, or parameterized-hardness theorem, so the case must rest on its named polynomial-time matching result and its named distortion theorems. That is still a credible computational mirror, although a more modest one than a discrete-hardness result dissolving in the continuum.

Take a finite candidate set \(C\), let \(\mathcal T=\mathcal L(C)\) be the set of complete rankings, and let the society be a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal T)\). A type is a complete ranking, because that is the complete information the paper’s rule uses. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). The natural regime is a large electorate choosing among a small slate of candidates or facility locations: millions of residents may fall into a few stable ideological, demographic, or geographic preference classes, with \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\ll n\). This is precisely the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s election model. A finite profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) becomes \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and duplicating every voter leaves all normalized distortion ratios unchanged.

For the metric part, let a feasible realization be a measurable population of voter points with mass measure \(\lambda\), a type map whose pushforward is \(\mu\), and a common metric \(d\) on voters and candidates that respects the rankings and, when relevant, \(\alpha\)-decisiveness. Social cost is now \(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(c,d)=\int d(x,c)\,d\lambda(x)\). This is continuization of the population, not of the candidate or outcome space: \(C\) remains finite, and randomization remains an action available to the rule.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(m\ge3\) and every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), the rule \(\plumatching\) has distortion \(2+\alpha\) on \(\alpha\)-decisive metric spaces. In particular, for unrestricted metric spaces \((\alpha=1)\), it achieves distortion \(3\), matching the known lower bound for deterministic rules. The rule selects a candidate whose integral domination graph admits a perfect matching; the paper proves both the upper bound and tightness.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathrm{Continuous\ Plurality\text{-}Matching\ Distortion}_\infty^\alpha\). Given \(C\), a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, and \(\alpha\), define the plurality mass \(q_\mu(c)=\mu(\{t:\operatorname{top}(t)=c\})\). Find a candidate \(a\) and a mass-preserving coupling \(\pi\) between voter types and top-choice mass such that \(\pi(t,c)>0\) only when \(a\succeq_t c\). The required guarantee is that, for every feasible metric realization \(d\), \(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(a,d)\le(2+\alpha)\min_{b\in C}\operatorname{SC}_\mu(b,d)\).

This is not merely a softened version of the paper’s problem. It retains complete rankings, plurality-based matching, the hidden metric, triangle inequalities, and the worst-case ratio objective. The finite perfect matching becomes a coupling of voter mass to top-choice mass. The proof survives almost verbatim: the matching transfers mass from each voter \(x\) to a top-choice type \(y\) whose top candidate is weakly below \(a\) in \(x\)’s ranking; triangle inequality and \(\alpha\)-decisiveness then give the \(2+\alpha\) bound after integrating over the coupling. Proposition 5 further shows that tie-breaking cannot improve the worst-case bound.

The second anchor is Lemma 2, also proved here, the Ranking-Matching Lemma. It states that for every election, every voter-weight vector \(p\), and every candidate-weight vector \(q\), some candidate \(a\) has a \((p,q)\)-domination graph with a fractional perfect matching, and such a candidate can be computed in strongly polynomial time.

Its continuous counterpart is the following named problem:

\(\mathrm{Mass\ Ranking\text{-}Matching}_\infty\). Given \(C\), a type distribution \(\mu\), and \(q\in\Delta(C)\), find \(a\in C\) and nonnegative masses \(\pi(t,c)\) satisfying \(\sum_c\pi(t,c)=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi(t,c)=q(c)\), and \(\pi(t,c)=0\) unless \(a\succeq_t c\). Equivalently, for every measurable set \(S\) of types, the \(q\)-mass of candidates weakly defeated by \(a\) somewhere in \(S\) must be at least \(\mu(S)\). A solution is the candidate \(a\) together with the coupling \(\pi\).

This problem is tractable in the natural high-multiplicity encoding: test each candidate by a max-flow computation on the \(\tau+m\)-size type–candidate graph. Its complexity is polynomial in \(m\), the number of represented types, and the bit length of \(\mu\) and \(q\). The important point is that the continuous object is doing real work: repeated voters are aggregated into mass, while the matching certificate remains a substantive property of the underlying voting problem. This is a Class A mirror.

The third anchor is Theorem 4, proved here. It states that for every \(m\ge2\) and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), no randomized social choice rule that has access only to plurality scores can achieve distortion better than \(2+\alpha-2/m\) on \(\alpha\)-decisive metric spaces.

The continuous problem is:

\(\mathrm{Plurality\text{-}Only\ Metric\ Distortion}_\infty^\alpha\). The society has distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, but the rule observes only the plurality vector \(p(c)=\mu(\{t:\operatorname{top}(t)=c\})\). It must output a lottery \(\Phi(p)\in\Delta(C)\). Its objective is to minimize the worst-case expected ratio \(\sum_c\Phi(p)(c)\operatorname{SC}_\mu(c,d)/\min_b\operatorname{SC}_\mu(b,d)\), over all ranking distributions and all compatible \(\alpha\)-decisive metrics having the same plurality vector.

Theorem 4’s lower bound survives exactly in the continuum. Use mass \(1/m\) for each of \(m\) top-choice types. Some candidate \(c^\star\) receives probability at most \(1/m\) from any plurality-only rule, while an admissible metric makes \(c^\star\) optimal and every other candidate sufficiently expensive. The resulting ratio is \(2+\alpha-2/m\), independently of whether the mass is represented by \(m\) voters, \(10^6\) duplicated voters, or a continuum. Theorem 3 supplies the matching upper bound through \(\smartdict\), so this restricted continuous problem has an exact optimum of \(2+\alpha-2/m\). It is tractable, but with a sharp information-theoretic boundary rather than a complexity barrier.

The authors should recognise these mirrors as their problems. Their paper already emphasizes that the number of voters may be large while the candidate set is small; it repeatedly normalizes voter counts into plurality shares; and the central proof converts voter–candidate incidence into a matching argument. The continuous formulation simply makes the mass quantities primary and exposes the high-multiplicity regime that the finite notation already supports. It is not prior art collision: the paper still defines \(V\) as a finite set, reasons with finite sums and finite matchings, and does not formulate a computational landscape over population distributions.

The mirror covers the Ranking-Matching Lemma, the deterministic full-ranking rule \(\plumatching\), and the plurality-only randomized frontier. It does not claim to continuize every result in the paper. Natural follow-up questions include whether the exact full-ranking minimax rule over continuous societies can be computed, whether the randomized full-ranking frontier between \((3+\alpha)/2\) and \(2+\alpha\) changes under continuum populations, and whether the fairness-ratio result extends to a continuous upper-tail cost such as the worst \(\rho\)-fraction of voter mass.

The weakest point is that this paper’s contribution is primarily approximation and information theory, not discrete computational hardness. The continuous matching problem is likely to be polynomial for the same reason as the finite high-multiplicity aggregation, and the robust metric-distortion objective remains an infinite-dimensional adversarial optimization rather than an explicitly solved LP. Thus the case is not that continuization reveals a new hardness collapse. It is that the paper contains a particularly faithful population-level computational problem whose central algorithm, proof, tight bounds, and lower-bound constructions all pass naturally from voter counts to mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed continuum is not where this paper’s computational content lies. The paper’s input is an ordinal profile, while its objective is defined by a hidden global metric. A distribution over rankings alone therefore does not specify the agents’ cardinal locations or distances. If ranking is declared to be the whole type, the proponent has changed the metric model into a robust information problem. If metric data are included so that types are genuinely complete, arbitrary realizations produce a much richer, potentially infinite type space rather than the fixed finite \(m!\)-type model.

Theorem 1 is therefore the strongest anchor, but it is also the closest to a mere high-multiplicity quotient. For rational \(\mu\), choose \(n\) with \(n\mu_t\in\mathbb{N}\) and clone each ranking type \(n\mu_t\) times. The proposed mass coupling is exactly the weighted Hall condition for that finite election; with plurality weights, it expands to the paper’s ordinary perfect matching among cloned voters. The \(2+\alpha\) proof then replaces sums by normalized sums or integrals without changing the combinatorial problem. Irrational masses add no algorithmic content unless an input model for real numbers is supplied. Thus the continuous \(\plumatching\) problem is a compressed implementation of the existing finite rule, not a new population-level optimization landscape.

Lemma 2 is weaker still. It already permits arbitrary fractional voter weights \(p\) and candidate weights \(q\) on a finite graph. Its continuous analogue is precisely the same finite weighted-flow test after aggregating voters with identical rankings. If one starts from an arbitrary measurable population, the matching graph sees only the ranking, so the measure can immediately be pushed forward to ranking types. The alleged continuum disappears under that pushforward. This is a useful observation about implementation, but not a worthwhile new mirror of the paper’s central result.

Theorem 4 also does not supply a population-driven problem. Its lower-bound witness has \(n=m\) voters, one top-choice voter per candidate. Replacing each voter by a continuum of identical copies leaves the plurality vector and every normalized distortion ratio unchanged. The formula \(2+\alpha-2/m\) is already a symmetry and information lower bound over candidates, not a phenomenon caused by population multiplicity. The matching upper bound from \(\smartdict\) likewise already depends only on normalized plurality scores. A continuous plurality-only formulation is mathematically legitimate, but it is the same minimax game written with shares instead of counts.

A more ambitious proposal—optimizing the best full-ranking randomized rule over \(\mu\), or replacing the fairness statistic by the worst \(\rho\)-fraction of population mass—would be a reasonable research direction. But it is no longer a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s named computational results; it is a new robust social-choice problem. And if the metric realization is included among types, the finite high-multiplicity structure that makes the ChoCo programme computationally attractive largely disappears.

This negative case is ultimately not airtight. Voting is exactly the kind of setting where high multiplicity is natural: many voters, few candidates, and repeated or near-repeated rankings. The paper itself explicitly treats \(n\) as large and normalizes voter counts into shares. Under the programme’s rules, “the continuous theorem is just the normalized finite theorem” is not by itself an objection. A rational distribution over ranking types, together with the weighted matching algorithm and its \(2+\alpha\) guarantee, is a legitimate Class A mirror.

So I cannot honestly defeat the first anchor universally. The best defensible conclusion is not “no worthwhile mirror,” but “a faithful yet technically modest mirror, with little evidence of a new complexity boundary.”
