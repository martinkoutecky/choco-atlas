The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t make a positive case that meets your requirements. Theorem 1 proves an extremal lower bound for multi-color discrepancy; Theorem 2 transfers a lower bound to consensus division; and Theorems 3–5 give lower bounds on the relaxation needed for EF and PROP allocations. They establish that certain allocations do not exist, not that a computational problem is hard or tractable.

A continuous population model could use types \((g,v)\), where \(g\) is an agent’s group and \(v\in\{0,1\}^m\) is their valuation vector, with mass \(\mu_{g,v}\). The decision would still be a partition of indivisible items, perhaps minimizing the worst EF or PROP relaxation across positive-mass types. But these theorems’ bounds depend on the *number of agents*. In EF and PROP, repeating a type adds no new fairness constraint, while weighting constraints by mass would change the fairness notion. The paper gives no named result showing how its bounds behave under that change. Calling any of Theorems 1–5 an NP-hardness or tractability anchor would overstate what it proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no theorem anchors to defeat: the positive case raises none. That is the strongest point against a mirror. Theorems 1–5 bound worst-case discrepancy or the relaxation needed for fair allocations. They prove that certain instances have no allocation meeting a given bound; they do not classify the complexity of finding an allocation, or establish an algorithm for one. The quantities \(\mathrm{disc}(n,k)\), \(\mathrm{cd}(n,k)\), \(\mathrm{ef}(n_1,\ldots,n_k)\), and \(\mathrm{prop}(n_1,\ldots,n_k)\) are extremal existence parameters. The paper’s probabilistic arguments establish lower bounds on those parameters, not computational results about a named problem.

The best salvage is the proposed type model: represent society by masses over pairs of group and valuation type, then compute an allocation minimizing the EF or PROP relaxation. That is a coherent problem, but the paper’s fairness conditions apply to every agent. For a fixed allocation, two agents of the same group and valuation impose the same constraint, so their masses do not change feasibility. The operative input is the support of the distribution. Theorems 3–5 build their bounds from many independently sampled valuation constraints; Theorem 1 similarly uses \(n\) sets as constraints. Repeating a constraint adds nothing. A type-based version therefore reduces the population parameter to the number of distinct constraints, rather than turning population mass into a quantity the paper’s results analyze.

One could make mass affect the objective by asking what fraction of the population is envy-free or by minimizing mass-weighted violations. That would be a meaningful research question, but it changes the fairness requirement from “every agent” to an aggregate one. The paper gives no result about that objective. Allowing fractional item assignments to obtain a continuous optimization problem would change the outcome space as well; with indivisible items retained, the allocation remains a finite combinatorial choice, and the population weights still disappear under the paper’s universal fairness tests.

So the strongest defensible negative case is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize, and its natural high-multiplicity reading collapses to a support-based finite allocation problem. A new algorithmic study of allocation over valuation types might be worthwhile in its own right. The paper does not provide an anchor for it, and claiming that it is a continuous mirror of these lower-bound theorems would overstate the connection.
