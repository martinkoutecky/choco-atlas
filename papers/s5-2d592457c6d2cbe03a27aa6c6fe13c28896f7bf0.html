What the mirror covers

The proposals cover the STV distortion bound under doubling metrics, PluralityMatching’s ultra-metric distortion guarantee, and the deterministic lower bound; they do not cover the paper’s other results or establish a computational-complexity landscape.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s treatment of the population as a weighted electorate. One qualification up front: the paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named computational results are guarantees on how well voting mechanisms perform. The mirrors below are anchored in those algorithmic results; they do not turn them into complexity-class results.

For the regime, take a large election whose voters fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete types: each type is a location in a finite metric space, with its distances to the candidates and its induced ranking. Give each type a rational mass \(\mu_x\), with \(\sum_x\mu_x=1\). Think of many voters concentrated at a few repeated ideal points or precinct locations, so \(N\gg\tau\), while the number of candidates \(m\) is modest. A ranking alone is not a complete type here: voters with the same ranking but different distances can have different social costs. The mechanism still sees only the mass of each ranking; the metric realization is used to assess distortion.

Lead — Theorem 5.5, proved here. The paper proves that PluralityMatching returns a candidate with distortion at most \(2\) under any ultra-metric space. Its proof uses Proposition 5.4, the perfect-matching existence result cited from Gkatzelis, Halpern, and Shah; the paper says the matching-based candidate can be identified in strongly polynomial time.

The continuous problem is: given a candidate set, a rational mass profile over complete voter types, and the promise that the profile has an ultra-metric realization, find a candidate \(a\) and a certificate \(\pi_{xy}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_y\pi_{xy}=\mu_x\), \(\sum_x\pi_{xy}=\mu_y\), and \(\pi_{xy}>0\) only when type \(x\) ranks \(a\) at least as high as type \(y\)’s top candidate. The candidate is the mechanism’s decision; \(\pi\) is a fractional domination matching that certifies its choice. The required guarantee is \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(a)\le 2\min_{b\in C}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(b)\), for every promised ultra-metric realization. A solution is the candidate, the coupling, and a proof of that bound.

I expect this to be tractable: the certificate is a transportation flow on at most \(\tau^2\) type pairs, with rational capacities, and candidate feasibility can be checked by flow. Rational mass profiles can also be scaled to discrete electorates to establish existence, though actually expanding the electorate could be too costly in the mass encoding; the flow formulation is the natural continuous algorithm. This co-modification from integral matching to fractional mass matching is squarely within scope. It asks the same question about the mechanism’s choice and distortion, while making the electorate itself continuous.

Theorem 3.2, proved here. The paper proves that when the underlying metric has doubling dimension \(d\), the distortion of STV is \(O(d\log\log m)\). Its continuous mirror takes an input ranking-mass profile and a bound \(d\), and asks for a bound on the worst-case distortion of weighted STV over all metric realizations of that profile with doubling dimension at most \(d\). In each round, each type’s mass supports its highest-ranked active candidate; with parallel-universe tie handling, any candidate surviving some elimination sequence counts as a winner. A solution is a proof that every such winner has distortion \(O(d\log\log m)\).

I expect the guarantee to carry over: the argument is about fractions of voters and support propagation, and rational mass profiles can be scaled to finite electorates. Weighted STV itself can be run directly on the support. This mirror covers a central result of the paper, though its solution is a performance theorem rather than a new complexity classification. It also poses natural follow-ups: whether the bound can be improved to \(O(d)\), as Conjecture 1.4 proposes, and how the upper bound compares with lower bounds for low-dimensional metrics.

Tightness companion — Proposition 5.2, proved here. The paper proves that there is a voting profile on which every deterministic mechanism has distortion at least \(2\) under an ultra-metric. The continuous problem is: given any deterministic ordinal rule, produce a rational mass profile and an ultra-metric realization on which the rule’s selected candidate has distortion at least \(2\). A solution is that profile and realization. The paper’s equal split between two candidates becomes two opposite ballot types of mass \(1/2\), so the obstruction carries over directly. This rules out a deterministic guarantee below \(2\) by a lower bound that transfers from the discrete case; it is an approximation impossibility, not NP-hardness.

Together, the two ultra-metric questions say that mass-based PluralityMatching reaches the best possible deterministic distortion bound. They also generate questions about randomized rules, and about whether the same mass-flow approach gives useful guarantees beyond ultra-metrics.

The weakest point is that these mirrors are unusually easy to bridge from finite electorates: rational masses can be replicated, and the paper’s proofs already reason in terms of population fractions. That is evidence that the high-multiplicity regime is sensible, not a novelty claim. But a referee could reasonably view some of the continuous statements as repackaging finite results, and none of these anchors opens the programme’s central complexity question of whether discrete hardness dissolves. My positive case is therefore for a natural, computable continuous formulation of the paper’s mechanism guarantees, not for a new complexity landscape already latent in its theorems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a scope mismatch. Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 5.5 bound the distortion of fixed voting rules; Proposition 5.2 gives a lower bound on every deterministic rule. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem whose input is a continuous society. Their continuous versions may be valid, but they answer the programme’s target only if distortion guarantees themselves count as the computational question.

That distinction does not make the proposed mirrors ill-posed. For Theorem 5.5, rational masses over \(\tau\) types give a natural fractional domination matching, computable by a flow on those types. For Theorem 3.2, the support-propagation argument already uses population fractions, so its weighted version is natural. Proposition 5.2 carries over by putting mass \(1/2\) on each ballot. These are all coherent continuizations, and I cannot reject them on grounds of type choice, a degenerate limit, or an implausible population regime.

The sharper negative point is about what they contribute to ChoCo. In these mirrors, the population’s size is not the source of a computational bottleneck: weighted STV runs directly on the profile, and the matching certificate compresses to a flow over type masses. The paper’s results supply performance guarantees, not the sort of missing pricing problem, complexity boundary, or algorithmic landscape that motivates the programme. Thus the proposed mirrors are better viewed as mass versions of the paper’s analysis than as openings into that landscape.

A more ambitious computational question would be to calculate worst-case distortion from a rational mass profile. But the paper’s adversary ranges over joint metric realizations consistent with the rankings. Ranking masses alone do not specify that geometry; adding a full metric space and a measure makes a coherent problem, but moves beyond the paper’s stated results. That might be worth studying, but the paper does not make it a compelling next computational question.

This is not an airtight case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The three anchors survive as sound continuous-population questions under a broad reading of “mirror.” The negative case is strongest only under ChoCo’s narrower aim: the paper offers natural continuous performance analyses, but no clear computational-complexity question whose continuization exposes a new tractability boundary.
