The case FOR (proponent)

The paper’s strongest positive feature is that its population is already a sensible continuous object. It assumes a continuum of citizens of total mass \(1\), with regional masses \(1-\lambda\) and \(\lambda\). A voter type is a region and an ideal government point \(t\in[0,1]\); mass is described by the regional distributions \(F_a(t\mid R)\). Citizens vote over a government type \(x\in[0,1]\), valuing it by how close it is to their ideal point. A setting with large regions and stable distributions of political preferences makes that model plausible.

That does not provide a continuous *mirror*, though: the paper already makes the population continuous. Its named results are proved here, but none is a computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. For example, Lemma 1 establishes that a Condorcet winner exists for the first-stage policy vote: under its stated preference condition, \(\bar{x}\) is the unique winner; otherwise the winner set is either the secession set \(S\) or \(\{\bar{x}\}\), and sufficiently high cultural distinctiveness makes it \(S\). Proposition 1 says secession is avoided exactly when
\[ \pi(t_B-\Delta c_B)-\kappa_B \leq \pi(\Delta c_A-t_A)+2\pi t_m+\kappa_A. \]
When that condition holds, the unified country implements \(\bar{x}\); otherwise region \(B\) attempts secession, with failure leading to national policy \(t_m\) and success to regional policies \(t_R\). These are equilibrium and comparative-static results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

So there are no qualifying computational anchors here, and I cannot state one continuous computational problem per anchor under the requested rule. Turning equilibrium determination for encoded distributions into a complexity problem could be a new question, but it would not mirror a named computational result of this paper. The paper supports the plausibility of continuous-population modeling; as a proposed mirror, it is already on the continuous side of the line.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper already makes the population continuous. It models a mass-one continuum of citizens, divided into regions of masses \(1-\lambda\) and \(\lambda\), with ideal points distributed according to \(F_a(\cdot\mid R)\). There is no discrete population result here for continuization to transform.

Neither named anchor supplies the missing computational result. Lemma 1 characterizes the Condorcet winner of the paper’s already-continuous policy vote. Its closest computational question—compute that winner from an encoded distribution—is a new algorithmic layer on the same model, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper. Proposition 1 characterizes the equilibrium choice between accommodation and secession in that same continuous model. Asking for an algorithm to determine the equilibrium likewise adds a computational question the authors did not pose; it does not mirror a complexity theorem. Propositions 2–6 are comparative statics, not computational classifications either.

One could define finite-support rational distributions and ask about equilibrium computation, robustness, or policy manipulation. Those could be worthwhile standalone questions. But the finite-support encoding would be a new problem specification, and the paper offers no discrete computational result for it to mirror. Calling that a mirror would stretch the term beyond the programme’s stated anchor requirement.

This case does not rest on an objection to continuous mass or on claiming that the answers would be unchanged or easy. The paper’s population is already a sensible continuous object. The narrower point is that its results are equilibrium and comparative-static characterizations, not computational results. That defeats the proposed anchors as mirrors, though it cannot establish that no related computational project would be worthwhile.
