The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based positive case for this paper: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. The closest candidates are WH1, which is a hypothesis, and the Section 5/Table 1 finding of no statistically significant treatment effect. Neither is a named computational result, so there is no valid anchor here—and no per-anchor prover question to state.

There is a plausible population-level setting behind the experiment: a large online referendum with many residents and a modest number of preference and information classes. A type could record a resident’s preferred alternative, preference intensity, and information signal; its mass would be that class’s fraction of residents. Agents choose an alternative and bid, aggregate bids select the winner, and mean earnings are computed from the paper’s payoff rule. One might then ask how equilibrium earnings compare under the two information levels. But that requires an equilibrium model the paper does not supply; its stated contribution is an empirical comparison of human behavior, not a computational question. So this is a possible follow-up, not an honest mirror of a named result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no named computational anchors to defeat: the proponent correctly identifies none. The paper states a working hypothesis and reports an exploratory experimental finding—no detected treatment difference, with a Kruskal–Wallis \(p\)-value of \(0.5264\) from samples of 80 and 23 people. It proves no complexity result and gives no algorithmic problem. Turning that finding into a question about whether average earnings converge under a continuum of voters would make it a statistical or behavioral question, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The strongest replacement is a plausible one: model a large referendum population by preference intensity and information signal, then ask how equilibrium earnings differ between the two information levels. But the paper supplies no equilibrium concept, behavioral model, or learning dynamics; it explicitly treats the experiment as exploratory because no accepted theory predicts the behavior. Choosing a strategic model and asking to compute its equilibrium could be worthwhile research, but that would create a new computational question rather than continuize the paper’s result. With bids fixed, the mechanism’s aggregate outcome and earnings are just sums over the population types, which does not recover the paper’s empirical question about how people choose those bids.

So the negative case is strong on the point the programme can grade: this paper offers no computational result to mirror. The broader claim that no useful continuum study of compensation elections could ever be made would go too far; the defensible claim is that such a study would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
