The continuous mirror question

Given a nondegenerate, explicitly listed domain \(D\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\) and a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\), determine whether there are rational quotas \(q_j\in(0,1]\) such that, for every \(\nu\in\Delta(D)\), the judgment \(A_q(\nu)\) belongs to \(D\), where \(p_j(\nu)=\sum_{t\in D:t_j=1}\nu_t\) and \(A_q(\nu)_j=1\) exactly when \(p_j(\nu)\ge q_j\); if so, output such quotas and \(A_q(\mu)\).

The model it lives in

Types are admissible judgment vectors \(t\in D\), and \(\mu_t\) is their population mass. The decision variables are issue quotas \(q_j\); the rule thresholds each type’s population share of acceptance and must return a judgment in \(D\) for every distribution over types. The task is feasibility, with no separate objective.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4.5 neither singles out threshold quotas nor characterizes when they preserve the domain, so the case does not establish why this particular rule family is the right continuous counterpart.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns quota based anonymous aggregation over population shares and leaves the paper’s broader characterizations of arbitrary local possibility domains and its other aggregator classes aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether suitable quotas exist and finding them, in terms of \(n\), the support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Which local possibility domains admit quota rules, and how does that class relate to domains whose anonymous aggregators use \(\oplus\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a quota based continuous version of anonymous judgment aggregation. It keeps the paper’s central object—a collective judgment that must remain rational—and makes the electorate a distribution over complete individual judgments.

The anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved in this paper. Given an explicitly listed domain \(D\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\), it gives an \(O(|D|^2n^2)\)-time algorithm that either reports that \(D\) is not a local possibility domain or outputs a local possibility integrity constraint with \(O(|D|n)\) clauses whose models are exactly \(D\). In the paper’s terms, being a local possibility domain means admitting an anonymous, non-dictatorial issue-by-issue aggregator; equivalently, by Theorem 2.2, one with ternary components from \(\{\wedge^{(3)},\vee^{(3)},\mathrm{maj},\oplus\}\). This is the best anchor because it is a named algorithmic result tied directly to anonymous aggregation. The paper’s other results do not offer a stronger named hardness anchor.

A plausible large population is a national organization, union, or large institution whose members take yes/no positions on the same set of policy issues. A type is a person’s complete judgment vector. Many members share a type because they share a role, policy platform, or institutional template; thus the number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct types. The set \(D\) lists the individually admissible judgments, while the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\).

I would ask the prover this specific question: Quota-Continuous Judgment Aggregation. The input is \(n\), an explicitly listed admissible domain \(D\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\), and a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\). Find rational quotas \(q_1,\ldots,q_n\in(0,1]\) such that the following rule always returns an admissible collective judgment. For every population distribution \(\nu\) over \(D\), let
\[ p_j(\nu)=\sum_{t\in D:t_j=1}\nu_t, \qquad A_q(\nu)_j= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if }p_j(\nu)\ge q_j,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]
The requirement is \(A_q(\nu)\in D\) for every \(\nu\in\Delta(D)\). A yes solution consists of the quotas and the resulting judgment \(A_q(\mu)\); a no answer says no such quotas exist. This is an existence problem, with no separate cost objective. The rule is anonymous and issue-by-issue, and its inputs are shares of the population rather than named voters’ positions.

This is a genuine continuous analogue of one strand of the paper’s problem: design an aggregation rule that turns population judgments into a judgment still allowed by the rationality domain. It is quota-restricted, which makes its action and solution precise. The connection to Theorem 4.5 is substantive: if such quotas exist, applying the rule to empirical distributions of sufficiently large finite electorates gives anonymous non-dictatorial aggregators on \(D\). Therefore \(D\) must be a local possibility domain, and Theorem 4.5 gives a polynomial-time necessary-condition test and a compact integrity-constraint description. The mirror covers that anonymous-aggregation result, not the paper’s wider set of characterizations.

My provisional expectation is Class C, continuum-specifically hard. The finite anchor is in P, so it gives no hardness that transfers upward. But the quota question must choose cutoffs so that every point in the continuous polytope of population mixtures avoids every inadmissible rounded judgment. That robust threshold-design step is absent from Theorem 4.5. There may be an LP or separation-based route to tractability; for fixed quotas, checking whether a particular inadmissible output can occur is a linear feasibility question. The open challenge is to choose the quotas efficiently as \(n\) grows.

The weakest point is that the paper’s finite anonymous aggregators need not have a natural extension to arbitrary real masses: in particular, parity via \(\oplus\) has no evident quota analogue. My proposed problem therefore narrows Theorem 4.5 to a quota-based population rule, rather than claiming that all local possibility domains have continuous mirrors. The further questions are whether the domains admitting such quota rules have a syntactic characterization, how that class sits inside the local possibility domains, and whether the quota-design step is tractable in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.5 is the only strong computational anchor, and its algorithm is about the domain \(D\): given the complete list of admissible judgment vectors, decide whether \(D\) is a local possibility domain and, if so, construct a formula for it. Its running time depends on \(|D|\) and \(n\). The electorate, its size, and the proportions of its judgment types do not enter the input.

The voter count appears only indirectly: a local possibility domain admits an aggregator of some arity. The theorem does not take a particular electorate’s votes and aggregate them, or ask how aggregation behaves as the electorate grows. A faithful continuous version of the recognition task would still recognize a property of \(D\); adding a distribution \(\mu\) would leave the answer and computation unchanged. A population-aware question must add a new aggregation problem.

That is what the quota proposal does. It asks for one fixed vector of issue cutoffs that preserves admissibility at every population distribution. The supplied \(\mu\) does not affect whether those cutoffs exist; it is used only to report the rule’s output afterward. This is a coherent mechanism-design question, but Theorem 4.5 supplies only a necessary-condition test for it. It does not characterize quota rules or explain why cutoffs are the natural continuous counterpart of the paper’s aggregators.

The cutoff restriction also narrows the anchor substantially. The paper’s local possibility result includes affine domains and the coordinatewise operation \(\oplus\), alongside conjunction, disjunction, and majority. A single weighted threshold per issue has no evident way to represent parity over arbitrary population mixtures. That does not make a quota question uninteresting; it means the proposed mirror selects a new, smaller family of aggregation mechanisms from a result that classifies a broader family.

Broadening the mirror creates its own modeling choice. An arbitrary anonymous selector \(A:\Delta(D)\to D\) can simply choose a most frequent feasible judgment, with a fixed tie-breaking order. That gives a unanimous, population-based rule for every \(D\), but it drops the paper’s issue-by-issue independence and makes existence uninformative. Keeping issue-by-issue independence means specifying how each decision depends on its population share. Requiring ordinary continuity of a Boolean-valued decision function would make it constant on the connected interval \([0,1]\), conflicting with unanimity. Allowing discontinuities avoids that problem, but then a rule class or finite representation is needed. Quotas provide one choice; the paper gives no reason to privilege it over other choices.

A more objective-driven version—such as choosing the admissible collective judgment minimizing weighted disagreement with \(\mu\)—would make the population distribution central. It may be a sensible problem for large organizations. But it would be a winner-selection optimization problem over \(D\), not the domain-recognition and formula-construction problem of Theorem 4.5. Its relevance and difficulty would need their own case.

I cannot make a strong case that judgment aggregation has no high-multiplicity regime. Large organizations can plausibly have many members sharing a complete judgment vector, and population shares can be meaningful there. The negative case is therefore about the gap between that plausible regime and this paper’s result: the theorem’s computation is independent of population, while each population-sensitive version requires a substantive new modeling choice. That is a good reason to resist presenting Theorem 4.5 as an especially strong continuous mirror. It is not enough to rule out a worthwhile continuous aggregation project altogether.
