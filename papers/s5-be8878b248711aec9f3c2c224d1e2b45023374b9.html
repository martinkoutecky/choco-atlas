The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. It proves existence and impossibility theorems, but no named theorem says NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar. The query-complexity statement in the introduction is attributed to Plaut and Roughgarden, not proved here. So this cannot be presented as a strong ChoCo case about a complexity classification. Still, it has a credible population-continuation case, led by Theorem 1.

The natural model keeps goods indivisible for every individual and continuizes only the population. Let \(T\) be a finite set of valuation types, with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Let \(G\) be a finite set of recurring good classes, with per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\). An individual of type \(t\) receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), or, if repeated copies are meaningful, an integral count vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\). Write \(x_{t,B}\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). Complete allocation requires

\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=\rho_g \]

for every good class \(g\).

EFX must be imposed support-wise, not on type averages: whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{u,D}>0\),

\[ v_t(B)\ge v_t(D\setminus\{g\}) \]

for every \(g\in D\). Thus every infinitesimal agent receives an integral bundle and satisfies the paper’s original EFX condition. Only the distribution of bundles across a large homogeneous population is fractional.

My lead mirror is Two-Type Complete Continuous EFX, anchored in Theorem 1, proved in this paper:

There exists a complete EFX allocation when each agent has one of two general valuations.

Here “general” means normalized and monotone, not additive, and “complete” means that every item is allocated. The theorem is unusually well suited to continuization because its hypothesis already identifies precisely two valuation types. A plausible regime is a large course-allocation or recurring-resource population with millions of students but only two exact valuation profiles, \(\mu_a\) and \(\mu_b\), and course capacities scaling with population. The number of agents is large while \(\tau=2\); the two types include all information relevant to preferences.

The continuous problem is:

Instance: rational masses \(\mu_a,\mu_b\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\), and two normalized monotone valuation functions \(v_a,v_b\) over integral bundles.

Question: find a complete mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) satisfying support-wise EFX.

Solution: an explicit finite-support distribution of integral bundles meeting the supply equations and all support-wise EFX inequalities.

This is not average EFX, fractional ownership, or a lottery over outcomes for one finite electorate. The mass \(x_{t,B}\) records how many agents receive each integral bundle. That is exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation of two valuation classes.

There is also a genuine finite-to-continuous dictionary. If all masses and supplies are rational, clearing denominators produces a finite population with cloned agents and cloned good copies. Conversely, normalizing such a finite allocation gives \(x\). With symmetric copies, the finite instance still has exactly two general valuation functions. Thus Theorem 1 strongly suggests that the rational continuous feasibility problem is always a YES-instance in this regime. The computational question left open is whether one can find the allocation in time polynomial in the number of good classes, valuation-description length, and the number of types, rather than in the population denominator. I would expect a Class A theory for fixed \(\tau=2\), perhaps through a configuration formulation or a compressed leximin argument, but the paper itself does not establish that algorithmic claim.

The main further questions are whether a polynomial-size support always exists, whether the support-EFX constraints admit column generation, and whether arbitrary value-oracle valuations make even this two-type version query-hard. Extending from two types to a bounded number of types would be the natural next step.

A useful secondary anchor is Theorem 19, also proved here:

There exist an instance \(I\) with three agents, general valuations, seven items, and a partial EFX allocation \(X\), such that in every complete EFX allocation the valuation of agent \(1\) is strictly worse than in \(X\).

This is a limitation of the paper’s lexicographic-potential approach. The partial allocation is

\[ X=\bigl(\{g_1,g_2\},\{g_3,g_4\},\{g_5,g_6\}\bigr), \]

with \(g_7\) unallocated; \(X\) is EFX, but every complete EFX allocation makes agent \(1\) worse off.

Its continuous analogue is Protected-Mass Continuous EFX Completion. Use three valuation types with masses

\[ \mu_1=\mu_2=\mu_3=\frac13, \]

seven recurring good classes with per-capita supply \(1/3\), and the valuation functions satisfying the inequalities constructed in Theorem 19. The initial partial allocation assigns mass \(1/3\) of type \(1\) the bundle \(\{g_1,g_2\}\), mass \(1/3\) of type \(2\) the bundle \(\{g_3,g_4\}\), and mass \(1/3\) of type \(3\) the bundle \(\{g_5,g_6\}\), leaving the \(g_7\)-mass unallocated.

The question is to maximize

\[ \theta(x)=\inf\{v_1(B):x_{1,B}>0\} \]

over complete support-wise EFX allocations, and decide whether

\[ \theta(x)\ge v_1(\{g_1,g_2\}) \]

is achievable. This preserves the theorem’s pointwise meaning: every member of the protected type must remain at least as well off as the distinguished agent in the finite construction. It does not replace that guarantee by an average over type \(1\).

I expect the finite obstruction is likely to survive under this support-wise formulation, because a positive-mass type cannot conceal a bad bundle behind averaging. That would be a transfer of the combinatorial obstruction, though not a hardness result. However, this is precisely where continuization may produce a genuinely new phenomenon: fractional mixing among integral bundles could create a completion unavailable to three named agents. Determining whether the obstruction survives is therefore a worthwhile boundary problem.

The weakest point is clear. The paper’s results are existential, not computational, and its general valuations may be given only by value queries. Moreover, scaling supplies with population changes the one-off-item model into a recurring-course or recurring-resource model. The positive case survives only if that co-modification is accepted as the honest high-multiplicity regime. I believe it is: without scaled supplies, a continuum of agents would face only finitely many indivisible items and the model would become degenerate. With scaled supplies and support-wise EFX, the problem remains recognisably the paper’s problem while making population multiplicity computationally meaningful.

So the strongest honest verdict is: a good continuous-population mirror exists, led by Theorem 1, and it plausibly opens a Class A configuration problem. But under a strict requirement that the anchor itself be a named complexity theorem, this paper has no eligible anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case wins at the programme’s entrance requirement: this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Theorems 1–3 and 19 are existence or proof-technique results. They do not state polynomial-time solvability, NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, approximation, or even a search problem with an input model. The query-complexity result mentioned in the introduction is attributed to Plaut and Roughgarden, not proved here. Thus there is no computational result whose continuous-population analogue this paper asks us to classify.

Theorem 1 is nevertheless the strongest possible rescue. Its “two general valuations” hypothesis really does describe a legitimate high-multiplicity regime: many agents may share one of two complete valuation types. Recurring course classes with per-capita capacities are a defensible co-modification, and I would not reject the mirror merely because supplies must scale with population.

But the proposed problem is not a computational mirror of Theorem 1. It is a new search problem: given masses, capacities, and arbitrary valuation functions, find a support-wise EFX configuration. The paper gives no representation for those valuations and no complexity claim. With value-oracle valuations, “polynomial in the valuation description” is undefined; with explicit tables, the natural input already has size \(2^{|G|}\). Moreover, support-wise EFX is not simply the paper’s finite allocation problem with fractional variables: one must select a mutually compatible family of integral bundles, after which masses are assigned to them. The suggested configuration or column-generation formulation is a promising research direction, but it is invented by the proponent rather than inherited from a computational theorem in the paper.

Clearing denominators only reinforces this point. For rational masses and capacities, one can clone agents and item copies and recover a finite instance to which Theorem 1 applies, assuming a chosen semantics for repeated item classes. That establishes a finite-to-continuous correspondence, but not an algorithmic result. The continuous question would become worthwhile only after adding a substantial new contribution: an input model, a search or optimization objective, and a complexity classification. Those may be good ChoCo questions, but they are not mirrors of a computational result actually present here.

Theorem 19 fares worse as an anchor. It is a seven-item witness showing that one particular lexicographic potential cannot always be increased. It is neither a complexity theorem nor a computational problem. The proposed quantity

\[ \theta(x)=\inf\{v_1(B):x_{1,B}>0\} \]

also changes the claim. The paper protects one named agent in one finite allocation; \(\theta\) protects every positive-mass member of a valuation type. That is a reasonable new robust objective, but it is not stated or implied by the theorem.

There is a genuine modelling fork. If each type is forced to receive one common bundle, the construction is merely a population-scaled copy of the three-agent witness, with no meaningful continuum structure. If type mass may split among several integral bundles, as a proper high-multiplicity model permits, then the original seven-item pigeonhole argument no longer directly applies: there are many cloned copies and many possible supported bundles. One would need a new theorem showing that every such distribution still violates the type-level protection constraint. If that theorem were proved, it would be an interesting result about a new continuous fair-division model—not a computational consequence of this paper.

The same objection applies to Theorems 2 and 3 if one tries to promote them after the fact: they are also existence statements. Their finite cardinality bounds do not provide a complexity target, and scaling the population requires independently deciding how “\(n-2\)” or “\(n+3\)” should translate into mass.

So I would not claim that no defensible high-multiplicity fair-division model exists. The two-type model is genuinely sensible, and the proponent’s strongest mirror is not defeated on modelling grounds. The stronger conclusion is narrower but decisive for this programme: this paper contains no named computational result to mirror. Its proposed continuous questions would be new algorithmic projects built around existential fair-division theorems, not continuizations of the paper’s computational content.
