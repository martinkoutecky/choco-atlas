The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the level of what this paper gives the programme to mirror: it states no result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2 is a payoff characterization for unit-surplus markets. Proposition 3 gives a structural way to identify essential links, but does not establish a complexity bound or an algorithmic classification. A continuous algorithmic question could be built around either result, but it would be a new computational project attached to the paper, not a mirror of one of its computational results.

Theorem 2 is still the best candidate for a mirror, and a large market with repeated buyer and seller types is plausible. But its key operation is deleting the link between two particular agents. In an atomless population, deleting one buyer–seller pair changes no aggregate mass. Their credible outside options can therefore be their existing stable payoffs; since those payoffs sum to the link’s surplus, Nash bargaining reproduces the existing division. The equal-split restriction on essential links disappears. Deleting an entire buyer-type–seller-type channel could have an effect, but that models the breakdown of trade between two cohorts, not bargaining breakdown between a matched pair. It asks a different economic question.

The same problem limits the best version of the Proposition 3 idea. One could define an essential type-pair link as carrying positive mass in every optimal type-level matching and study how to find such links. That may be worth studying, but it is a new algorithmic extension, and the paper’s finite-graph characterization does not anchor a computational complexity question about it. So the negative case is not that no worthwhile continuous matching project could be devised. It is that none of the plausible projects I can see preserves this paper’s defining individual bargaining operation *and* supplies the computational result the programme is looking for.
