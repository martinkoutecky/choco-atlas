The continuous mirror question

Given finite type supports \(P\) and \(R\), rational masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, strict type-level rankings, utilities, benchmarks, and bottom-type exemptions, choose thresholds \(L_P,L_R\). Retain a pair \((i,j)\) when each non-exempt type’s utility meets its benchmark minus its side’s threshold; run mass DA and find thresholds minimizing \(\sum_{i\in P}p_i\lvert\{j\in R:(i,j)\text{ is retained}\}\rvert\), subject to its assignment \(x(E)\) equaling the full-graph assignment \(x(E_{\mathrm{all}})\). Return the thresholds and common assignment.

The model it lives in

A balanced bipartite market of applicant and program types, each carrying rational population mass and strict preferences over opposite-side types. Mass DA produces an aggregate assignment \(x_{ij}\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the matched mass between types \(i\) and \(j\); each person still receives one match, while the type-level assignment can be fractional.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3.2’s conditional outcome-preservation claim and the proposal-list reduction question. It leaves the paper’s high-probability loss bounds, equilibrium result, and experimental findings without a transferred guarantee.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can mass DA on rational type masses be formalized so its output and running time are polynomial in support size and input bit length?
  • Does minimizing the thresholds that preserve the full mass-DA assignment admit a polynomial-time algorithm?
  • Under what assumptions, if any, do the paper’s short-list guarantees extend to markets with repeated exact types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is about which proposal lists a large matching market can safely shorten while preserving the deferred-acceptance outcome.

A strict reading of your anchor rule exposes a limit: the paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. The closest anchor is an algorithmic result about preserving the output of DA.

That anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in the paper. It says that on event \(\mathcal E\)—where every edge in every stable matching is acceptable—woman-proposing DA on the acceptable-edge set produces the same stable matching as DA on the full edge set. The same holds for any superset of the acceptable edges formed by the paper’s loss-threshold rule. The paper’s quantitative analysis supplies high-probability conditions for \(\mathcal E\); Theorem 3.2 itself is the output-preservation claim.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large placement or hiring market with repeated applicant and program archetypes. An applicant type includes the applicant’s complete utility ranking over program types; a program type includes its complete ranking over applicant types and its capacity. Millions of agents may share a much smaller number \(\tau\) of exact types. This is plausible where credentials, specialties, and application preferences recur in large cohorts. Each type’s mass is its fraction of the market. The finite market’s one-person matches become a mass assignment \(x_{ij}\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the fraction of the population matching applicant type \(i\) to program type \(j\). Each person still gets one match; the flow is fractional because it aggregates many people.

My lead question is Minimum Threshold Lists for Population DA. The input is two finite type supports \(P,R\), rational masses \(p_i,q_j\) summing to one on each side, strict type-level preference rankings and cardinal utilities \(u_i(j),v_j(i)\), and for each type a benchmark utility and an indication of whether it belongs to the bottom \(\sigma\) mass. For thresholds \(L_P,L_R\), retain a pair \((i,j)\) when each non-exempt type values the pair at least its benchmark minus its threshold.

Run mass DA on a retained edge set by having unmatched proposer mass offer to its next available type; each receiver type holds its most-preferred offers up to its mass and rejects the remainder. Let \(x(E)\) be the resulting assignment, and \(x_{\rm all}\) the assignment from the full graph. The problem is to choose \(L_P,L_R\) minimizing the mass-weighted proposer list length
\[ \sum_{i\in P} p_i\,\bigl|\{j\in R:(i,j)\text{ is retained}\}\bigr| \]
subject to \(x(E)=x_{\rm all}\), and return the thresholds and common assignment. This states the underlying matching and list-selection problem; the assignment flow is the necessary aggregate representation.

I expect this version to be tractable in \(\tau\) and the input bit length: only finitely many utility-loss breakpoints can change the retained graph, and each candidate graph can be checked by finite-type mass DA. That expectation needs a formal bound on the number of DA updates and exact arithmetic; it is not established by Theorem 3.2. The mirror covers the paper’s proposal-list reduction and its DA outcome-preservation result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s equilibrium, or its full probability and loss analysis. The main follow-up is whether the paper’s high-probability acceptable-edge guarantees survive when the score structure is shared exactly within types, and how the discrete cohort market approximates the mass assignment.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s private scores are independently drawn for individual pairs, and its public ratings are continuously distributed. Exact duplicate preference types are therefore not the regime driving its theorems. A large market with repeated archetypes makes high multiplicity plausible, and Theorem 3.2 addresses the same DA list-reduction question, but the paper’s concentration argument does not automatically transfer. The mirror is credible as a new computational question about proposal truncation; it is not a continuous restatement of the paper’s existing complexity result, since the paper has no such complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3.2 is too thin to support the proposed computational mirror. The paper has named algorithmic results, so it would be inaccurate to say it has no computational theorem at all. But Theorem 3.2 is a conditional preservation claim: if the retained graph contains the viable edges, DA returns the same matching. It does not ask how hard it is to find such a graph, nor classify an algorithmic problem. The paper’s substantial results are probabilistic bounds on losses, acceptable lists, and proposal counts.

The proposed “Minimum Threshold Lists” problem adds a new optimization objective to that preservation claim. With rationally encoded utilities, a global threshold changes the graph only at finitely many utility-loss breakpoints—at most one per type pair on each side. Enumerating pairs of breakpoints and running mass DA on each candidate graph appears polynomial in the support size and input bit length. That may be a useful extension, but its computation comes from a small finite search plus ordinary DA, not from the paper’s analysis or the continuous-optimization machinery ChoCo is meant to bring into reach. Allowing a separate threshold for every type could make the selection problem richer, but that would be a substantially different question from the paper’s common loss threshold.

The deeper obstacle is that the paper’s quantitative result gets its force from independent, continuously distributed public ratings and pair-specific private scores. If copies of a type share those complete preferences and scores, increasing their mass does not create more independent samples: the concentration that drives the paper’s \(n^{-1/3}\) loss bounds and short lists does not follow. If instead each copy keeps independent pair-specific scores, then copies are no longer identical in the complete preference information the problem uses, and the support needed for a faithful high-multiplicity model can grow with the population. The proposed ranking-over-type-classes model avoids that problem by changing what agents rank and sharing utilities across copies; it is coherent, but it removes the stochastic mechanism behind the paper’s main result.

That makes this a strong objection to this particular mirror, but not a convincing universal case against every mirror. Large placement markets with repeated applicant and program archetypes are plausible, and a mass version of DA’s proposal-list preservation is well-posed. The paper’s authors might recognize that as their problem, even though it is not what their concentration bounds establish. So the negative case is honestly weak at the final step: it shows that the proponent’s anchor yields little computational work and that the paper’s main quantitative engine does not transfer automatically; it cannot show that no worthwhile type-level stable-matching mirror exists.
