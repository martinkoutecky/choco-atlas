The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible computational anchor. In the supplied text, there is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. The claims about deferred acceptance (DA) and top trading cycles are market-design guarantees, not numbered computational results; the cited Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem concerns implementing lotteries. So I cannot honestly give an anchor-based continuous problem or present its complexity as a result of this paper.

The strongest plausible follow-up mirror is Continuum Student-Proposing Stable Assignment. Take a finite set \(S\) of student types and schools \(H\). Each type \(s\) has a strict ranking \(\succ_s\) of schools; each school \(h\) has a strict priority order \(\succ_h\) over student types. The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_s\) with \(\sum_s\mu_s=1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\) with \(\sum_h\kappa_h\ge1\). A type must include every attribute relevant to its preferences and to school priorities. Find rational assignment masses \(x_{s,h}\ge0\) such that
\[ \sum_{h\in H}x_{s,h}=\mu_s,\qquad \sum_{s\in S}x_{s,h}\le\kappa_h. \]
Require stability: for every \(s,h\), let \(A_{s,h}=\sum_{g:\,h\succ_s g}x_{s,g}\) be the mass of type \(s\) assigned to a less-preferred school, and let \(B_{s,h}=\sum_{s':\,s\succ_h s'}x_{s',h}\) be the mass at \(h\) with lower priority than \(s\). There must be no pair with \(A_{s,h}>0\) and either unused capacity at \(h\) or \(B_{s,h}>0\). Each individual still receives one school; \(x\) records how the nonatomic mass of an identical type is split. The task is to compute a stable assignment, not maximize a separate welfare objective.

A plausible regime is a large centralized school-placement or residency match: many applicants, relatively few complete preference-and-priority types, and seat capacities represented as shares of the cohort. The type count \(\tau=|S|\) would need to remain much smaller than the number of applicants. This is recognizably the paper’s matching problem: it asks for the same kind of stable assignment DA is meant to produce, with capacities scaled to the population. I would expect this formulation to be tractable through a type-level DA or flow algorithm, but that is a proposed result, not one established in the paper. Natural follow-ups are its running time in \(\tau,|H|\), and input bit length; its exact correspondence with finite DA when masses and capacities are scaled to integer counts; and how CADA’s target-school signals change the type model.

The weakest point is decisive for the requested grading: this is a plausible new computational question, but it is not a mirror of any named computational result in this paper. The paper motivates the matching object and its guarantees; it does not supply the computational anchor the programme asks us to continuize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. It describes deferred acceptance (DA) and top trading cycles, and discusses their stability, fairness, efficiency, and incentive properties. It does not formulate a computational problem, analyze its complexity, or establish an algorithmic bound. The proposed mirror therefore has no result in the paper as its anchor.

The continuum stable-assignment model is nevertheless coherent. A large school-placement or residency match can have many applicants sharing complete preference and priority types, with capacities scaled to cohort size. Treating each type as divisible mass is a sensible high-multiplicity formulation; that is not a flaw in the mirror. The difficulty is that “compute a stable assignment” would be a new computational study built around DA’s role in the paper, not a continuization of a computational claim the paper makes. The authors would recognize the matching problem, but there is no computational result here whose continuous counterpart this would be.

A stronger version could include complete cardinal-utility types and ask for a mass assignment that is ex ante efficient, or incorporate CADA’s target-school signals. Those are plausible questions, but they turn the paper’s example and mechanism discussion into new optimization problems; they do not supply the missing anchor. The paper’s individual strategy-proofness claims also do not carry over unchanged: a single agent has zero mass, so preserving a meaningful incentive question requires a different deviation model, such as a positive-mass coalition. That may be worthwhile research, but it changes the question again.

So the negative case is strongest under the programme’s stated anchor requirement: none of these is a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. Its weakness is equally clear: a continuous stable-matching project could be worthwhile on its own, and this paper gives it a natural motivation. If the programme accepts extensions of named procedures as anchors, the proposed mirror may survive; under the stricter computational-result standard, it does not.
