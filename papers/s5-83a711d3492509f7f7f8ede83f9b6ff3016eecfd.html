What the mirror covers

Theorem 3 is the only proposed anchor; the paper’s other convergence results and its binary-test equivalence are also non-computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest opening is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. For the no-self-awareness algorithm, with \(\theta_{\mathrm{TX}}\ne\theta_0\) and Assumptions 1 and 2, it proves that every agent rejects the transmitted hypothesis if \(d_{\mathrm{ave}}(\theta_{\mathrm{TX}})>d_{\mathrm{ave}}(\bar\theta_{\mathrm{TX}})\): its belief in that hypothesis tends almost surely to \(0\), and each other hypothesis’s belief tends to \(1/(H-1)\). If the inequality is reversed, every agent instead puts almost all belief on the false transmitted hypothesis. The equality case is excluded. This gives a natural lead for an *analytic* population extension: give mass \(\mu\) to complete agent types, describe interactions with a mixing kernel, and ask whether the target belief converges to \(0\) or \(1\) for almost every agent, perhaps with a continuum analogue of the Perron-weighted KL threshold. It is the paper’s partial-information learning regime that such a model would mirror.

That is not, however, a qualifying ChoCo anchor. Theorem 3 classifies asymptotic learning; it makes no claim about computational complexity, running time, or algorithmic tractability. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Theorem 4 are also convergence results, while Proposition 1 gives an equivalent binary-test formulation. None is a named computational result of the kind the anchor requirement calls for. The paper’s stated future direction of continuous social learning concerns a continuous parameter space, rather than a continuous population.

So there is a plausible continuum extension of the paper’s subject, and the authors might recognize it as a social-learning model. But its central question would be analytic, not computational. Under the stated rules, I can’t honestly turn it into a positive ChoCo case: the paper supplies no named computational anchor, and therefore no continuous computational problem to hand to a prover per that requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest candidate, but it gives an almost sure convergence classification, not a computational result. It says that the sign of \(d_{\mathrm{ave}}(\theta_{\mathrm{TX}})-d_{\mathrm{ave}}(\bar\theta_{\mathrm{TX}})\) determines whether agents reject or adopt the transmitted hypothesis. It says nothing about the complexity of deciding that sign, finding a transmission policy, or computing an intervention. The other numbered results are likewise about convergence. Calling the update rules “algorithms” does not supply the missing complexity question.

The most convincing population extension would give mass to complete agent types and replace the finite network by a type based mixing kernel. But that leads to an integral social learning process and questions about its limiting beliefs and convergence—the analytic questions the programme distinguishes from its computational focus. One could pose a decision problem about learning versus mislearning for that model. Yet the paper does not pose it, and its computational meaning would depend on how the likelihood distributions and mixing kernel are encoded. With finitely represented types and kernels, evaluating the analogue of the threshold is ordinary numerical and spectral computation; any harder version would need additional structure or policy choices absent from the paper. Optimizing the network or communication policy might be worthwhile, but it would be a new design problem, not a mirror of one of this paper’s results.

There is also a modeling cost: an agent’s behavior depends on both its signal model and its position in the interaction network. Grouping agents by signal model alone does not preserve the process. A type based kernel or a graph limit can make aggregation plausible for some populations, but then the continuum dynamics and their convergence conditions need to be developed anew. That is a reasonable research direction, but it reinforces that the natural mirror here is analytical.

I cannot establish that no plausible population scenario could lead to a worthwhile computational question. Large cohorts of similar sensors or users with type based interactions could support a sensible continuous model. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no computational result to mirror, and turning its learning analysis into a computational ChoCo problem requires adding a distinct task. On the material given, that is not a strong case for prioritizing a continuous mirror of this paper.
