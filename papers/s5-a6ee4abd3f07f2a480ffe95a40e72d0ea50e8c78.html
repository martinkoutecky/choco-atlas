The continuous mirror question

Given a finite hierarchy and \(\tau\) complete student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each with a matroid-rank valuation over \(m\) course categories, and rational per-enrollee seat supplies \(q_g\), find rational masses \(x_{t,S}\) assigning each type among integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\). Require that each type’s mass is preserved and total use of each category \(g\) is at most \(q_g\). At every internal node \(i\), among reallocations within its subtree that preserve type masses and use no more than its incoming seat vector \(b_i(x)\), the allocation must maximize descendant aggregate utility \(V_i\) and make the children’s aggregate-utility vector Lorenz-dominant. Determine whether such an allocation can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), hierarchy size, and rational input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A large course-allocation system with repeated complete student types arranged under campuses, schools, and programs. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), a matroid-rank valuation, and a fixed hierarchy location; \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass receiving integral bundle \(S\), and course-seat supplies scale with enrollment.

The objection that survived

Lorenz comparisons of cohort total utility can favor larger cohorts even when their per-student outcomes are worse; a per-capita criterion might better fit some student-fairness goals, though it would be a different version of the paper’s node-level criterion.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers SMA’s polynomial-time result for matroid-rank leaves with Lorenz fairness and its associated local welfare and fairness guarantees. It leaves the MGYS heuristic, its experiments, and the chores extensions outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the nodewise welfare and Lorenz requirements be satisfied and computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), hierarchy size, and \(L\)?
  • Can pricing over integral bundles be handled efficiently from matroid-rank oracles in this hierarchical mass model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that the Sequential Multilevel Algorithm (SMA) can be implemented in polynomial time in the number of nodes \(n\) and items \(m\), whenever every internal node uses one of the paper’s four criteria in \(\mathcal F\): Lorenz dominance, weighted leximin, maximum weighted Nash social welfare, or maximum weighted \(p\)-means welfare. The proof uses the matroid-rank structure established in the paper and existing monolevel allocation algorithms. The result is about computing the allocation itself, not about solving an encoding. The paper also establishes that SMA’s output is both multilevel utilitarian-optimal and fair at every internal node under the chosen criterion.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is course allocation in a large university system. Its tree could run from the university through campuses and schools to programs and student cohorts. A student’s type would specify their complete course matroid-rank valuation, their place in that hierarchy, and their entitlement weight. For example, the matroid could capture acceptable courses, schedule conflicts, and course-category limits. The regime is one with many students but relatively few distinct complete types: repeated profiles within large cohorts, rather than an assumption that different students are interchangeable. Course seats scale with enrollment, while each individual still receives an integral set of courses.

I would ask the authors to consider Continuous Multilevel Matroid-Rank Lorenz Allocation. Its instance is a finite hierarchy with \(m\) course categories and \(\tau\) supported types. Each type \(t\) has rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a matroid-rank valuation \(r_t\) on the course categories. Each course \(g\) has rational seat supply \(q_g\) per unit of population. The type description includes its hierarchy location and all parameters used by the allocation problem, so two students share a type only if they are indistinguishable for that problem.

A solution is a collection of nonnegative rational masses \(x_{t,S}\), one for each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\). Here \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) students who receive exactly \(S\). Require
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,S:\,g\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_g \]
for every type \(t\) and course \(g\). Thus the assignment is fractional only in how a nonatomic population is divided among bundles; each unit of population still receives an integral bundle.

For each node \(i\), let \(T(i)\) be the types below it, and define its resource flow and aggregate utility by
\[ b_{i,g}(x)=\sum_{t\in T(i),\,S:\,g\in S}x_{t,S}, \qquad V_i(x)=\sum_{t\in T(i),\,S}x_{t,S}r_t(S). \]
The question is to find a feasible \(x\) such that, at every internal node \(i\), the allocation below \(i\) maximizes \(V_i\) among reallocations of the same type masses using at most the same incoming resource vector \(b_i(x)\), and the vector of its children’s utilities \((V_j(x))_{j\in C(i)}\) Lorenz-dominates the vectors from those alternatives. This is the continuous mass-allocation analogue of the paper’s local welfare and fairness requirements. The solution is the bundle-mass assignment \(x\) satisfying those conditions.

I expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable, though that is a research conjecture, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 1. The formulation has exponentially many bundle columns, but only \(\tau\) types; it invites column generation or convex optimization. The key pricing step appears compatible with matroid optimization: for a type, optimize rank value against dual prices on course categories. Lorenz comparisons over the finite hierarchy may then be handled by a sequence of optimization problems. A useful complexity target would be polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), the hierarchy size, the bit length \(L\) of the rational data, and the cost of evaluating the matroid-rank oracles.

The mirror covers SMA’s multilevel allocation problem for matroid-rank leaves and Lorenz fairness; it does not claim to continuize every result or the MGYS heuristic. Nor is this a hardness-dissolution result: the paper’s named complexity anchor is already a polynomial-time algorithm. The continuous question would instead ask whether that kind of guarantee survives when population size is replaced by a compact distribution over complete types.

My weakest point is the modeling bridge. The mirror scales course-seat supplies with population and treats group utility as the integral of individual utility. Those are natural high-multiplicity choices, and the programme explicitly allows such co-modifications, but the paper does not analyze this mass formulation. The theorem also gives polynomial time in \(n\) and \(m\), not in the mirror’s support size \(\tau\); establishing the latter is part of the open question. Finally, if real course preferences yield almost no repeated complete types, this particular regime offers little compression. The case rests on large cohorts with genuinely repeated matroid profiles, not on treating merely similar students as identical.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1’s \(n\) counts nodes in a fixed hierarchy, not the people represented by those nodes. In the paper’s hospital example, a hospital is one leaf with a matroid-rank utility; the number of staff or patients inside it does not enter the theorem’s population parameter. Replicating people inside that hospital therefore does not create a high-multiplicity instance of Theorem 1. A mirror for the paper’s organizational applications would need a separate argument that the allocation agents themselves occur in large numbers with repeated complete types.

The course-allocation version repairs that gap: make students the leaves, group identical student profiles into types, and scale seat supply with enrollment while keeping each student’s bundle integral. But the proposed mirror still needs to settle what fairness means for a cohort. The paper compares utilities of child nodes; \(V_j(x)\) is a cohort’s total utility, so its value changes with cohort size even when per-student outcomes do not. Per-capita or measure-based Lorenz fairness could address this, but that choice defines a substantive part of the new problem.

That leaves a credible mirror after repair. Its mass-over-bundles formulation appears close to a type-based configuration optimization, and matroid structure may make the pricing step tractable. The paper’s polynomial bound in \(n\) does not establish a bound in support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\), so the high-multiplicity question remains distinct. Existing algorithms for monolevel matroid-rank fairness provide useful machinery, but do not answer that compressed-input question ([General Framework for Fair Allocation under Matroid Rank Valuations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07311)).

So the negative case narrows the anchor: the theorem does not itself expose a population axis in the paper’s group-level applications, and the proposed course mirror needs a careful fairness definition. I cannot honestly turn those points into a case that no worthwhile scenario exists. Large cohorts of repeated student types, or many facilities with repeated capacity and approval profiles, are plausible regimes; a support-polynomial continuous mirror of SMA could be a worthwhile computational question. The case against this anchor is therefore weak at its hardest point.
