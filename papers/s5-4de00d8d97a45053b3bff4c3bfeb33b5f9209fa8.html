The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible continuous mirror here, but the paper supplies no qualifying numbered anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. It says that finding a Kemeny median is NP-hard, but cites Bartholdi, Tovey, and Trick (1989) and Hudry (2012); that is prior work, not a numbered result proved here. Its own decomposition bound and findings from the simulations are presented in prose.

The strongest mirror I can defend is the paper’s underlying Kemeny optimization problem. Call it Weighted Kemeny Median\(_\infty\). An instance is a finite alternative set \(A\), a support \(S\subseteq LO(A)\) of \(\tau\) strict rankings, and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) represents the fraction \(\mu_t\) of a large population that holds ranking \(t\). Find a linear order \(\pi\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\,d_H(\pi,t). \]
A solution is an optimal ranking \(\pi\). This preserves the paper’s objective: minimize total disagreement with individual rankings. The continuous object is the population distribution, not the ranking space.

A plausible regime is a large association or public consultation with hundreds of thousands of respondents ranking the same slate, where many respondents share complete rankings and the observed support is in the hundreds or thousands. That is a high-multiplicity instance of the paper’s group-ranking problem: the agents are numerous, while the distinct preference types are comparatively few. The authors should recognize the mirror because it replaces counts of experts with proportions while keeping their input, objective, and output intact.

I would expect this problem to be hardness-transferring (Class B). Given a discrete profile with \(n_t\) agents of each ranking \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), where \(N=\sum_t n_t\). This takes polynomial time, and multiplying the continuous objective by \(N\) recovers the discrete objective exactly, so the minimizing rankings are unchanged. The paper’s cited NP-hardness therefore motivates a continuous question, but it cannot serve as the required anchor. Further questions include whether SCC size or support size yields useful parameterized algorithms, and how ties in the weighted majority relation affect the decomposition.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s own contribution is primarily experimental—how SCC decomposition behaves under three synthetic models at \(n=35\) alternatives and small numbers of experts—not a numbered complexity result about Kemeny optimization. So this is a plausible mirror of the problem the paper studies, but it is not an anchor-compliant positive case under your stated rule.

That leaves the stronger Kemeny optimization mirror. It is a sensible high-multiplicity problem: repeated rankings have natural masses, and large consultations provide a plausible setting. The exact connection to finite profiles does not defeat it: setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) preserves the minimizing rankings. Nor can I use hardness from the alternatives as an objection; under the programme’s criteria, that could be a valid Class B result. Existing high-multiplicity work would support the regime, not count against it.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as supplying an anchor-compliant result, but weak against the broader claim that a continuous Kemeny question is worthwhile.
