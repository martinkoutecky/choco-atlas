The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no admissible named complexity anchor: the supplied text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithm 1 and Algorithms A1–A4 describe a procedure, but the paper does not give a numbered complexity theorem for them. Its main contribution is the DWCEA allocation rule and an empirical application. I can’t honestly restate a qualifying anchor or claim the paper proves that problem is in P.

The strongest continuous-population candidate is a type-distribution version of the DWCEA allocation problem in Section 3. Consider a permit scheme with many facilities in repeated standardized classes. A type \(t\) records a facility’s per-facility claim \(c_t\), economic weight \(w^1_t\), and renewable-energy weight \(w^2_t\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of facilities. Given rational masses with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and a per-facility estate \(e\), ask for parameters \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\ge 0\) and allocations \(x_t\) such that
\[ x_t=\min\{c_t,\lambda_1w^1_t,\lambda_2w^2_t\}, \qquad \sum_t\mu_t x_t=e. \]
The solution is the allocation by type. This is an allocation-rule problem, not an optimization problem: the paper specifies no scalar objective. A stricter mirror would also need to define how to select among feasible parameter pairs, since the authors introduce an algorithm for choosing a particular allocation without prioritizing either criterion.

The regime is plausible if many facilities share the same complete per-facility profile and the number of such profiles \(\tau\) is much smaller than the number of facilities. The paper itself treats bankruptcy as a model for sharing scarce resources among individuals, so this preserves its underlying claims problem. Its 27-country application, however, does not itself supply that high-multiplicity regime.

This candidate looks tractable from the finite-support, piecewise-linear structure, but that is a conjecture about a new problem, not a result of this paper. It covers the DWCEA rule only; the clustering and EU Council power comparisons are not part of the mirror. The weak point is that moving from heterogeneous countries to repeated facility types is a substantive change, and the exact “no priority” selection algorithm would need a well-defined extension to type masses. The divisible permits alone do not establish a continuous-population mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its central formal contribution is a double weighted allocation rule, followed by Algorithm 1 for selecting an allocation and an empirical comparison across EU countries. Those may be computationally relevant, but the paper makes no complexity claim for them. So the proponent has no qualifying complexity result to anchor a mirror on.

The strongest candidate is still coherent: model many standardized facilities by types \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), and compute allocations satisfying
\[ x_t=\min\{c_t,\lambda_1 w^1_t,\lambda_2 w^2_t\}, \qquad \sum_t\mu_t x_t=e. \]
That scenario is more plausible than treating the 27 EU countries as a high multiplicity population. But it lifts the paper’s allocation formula into a new regime; it does not mirror a computational result the paper established. The estate and each agent’s allocation are already divisible, and the paper already gives a procedure for computing the rule on listed agents. The continuous version replaces sums by mass weighted sums. A question about extending its particular selection procedure to masses could be asked, but it would be a new algorithm analysis problem, with no stated complexity result in this paper for it to extend. Choosing a canonical allocation among feasible parameter pairs would also introduce a new selection question.

The clustering results are empirical, and the power index comparison depends on named countries and coalitions under the EU voting rule; neither supplies another computational anchor for continuization. The negative case is weakest at the facility scenario: it is a sensible high multiplicity application, and someone could reasonably study its computation. But on the programme’s stated remit, that possibility is not enough to make this paper a worthwhile continuous mirror: the paper’s substantive results concern an allocation rule and its application, not the complexity landscape of a computational problem.
