The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(T_A,T_B,T_D\), rational type masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b,\delta_d\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict rankings of pairs of opposite-side types, choose masses \(x_{abd}\ge 0\) with marginals \(\mu,\nu,\delta\). For type \(a\), let its assigned pair have distribution \(q_a(b,d)=x_{abd}/\mu_a\), and define \(q_b(a,d)=x_{abd}/\nu_b\) and \(q_d(a,b)=x_{abd}/\delta_d\). Independently sample one agent from each side and draw each agent’s assigned pair from its type’s distribution; let \(I(x)\) be the probability all three prefer the sampled triple’s partners to their assigned pairs. Maximize \(1-I(x)\); for a fixed \(c<1\), ask whether the optimum is \(1\) or at most \(c\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents in each of three populations, described by rational distributions over complete preference types. A feasible allocation \(x_{abd}\) assigns mass to complete families; its marginals equal the population masses, and the objective is the probability a randomly sampled triple is stable.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the 3G-MSM gap-hardness assertion in Theorem 1.1. It leaves the 3G-MSS hardness claim, the 3PSA results, and the 3G-MSM approximation algorithm unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For some fixed \(c<1\), is it hard to distinguish \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty=1\) from \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty\le c\) on rational type-mass instances?
  • Can fractional allocations create stable type-mass marriages when every finite realization with the same type rankings has a positive blocking-triple density?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is the 3G-MSM part of Theorem 1.1, stated as a special case of Theorem 3.1 and proved in this paper. The full theorem says that for some absolute \(c<1\), it is NP-hard to approximate both 3G-MSM and 3G-MSS within factor \(c\). I rely on the 3G-MSM assertion: it is NP-hard to distinguish preferences admitting a marriage with all \(n^3\) triples stable from preferences where every marriage has at most \(c n^3\) stable triples. The paper proves this using a gap reduction from 3DM-3; the underlying gap hardness is established in its Appendix A. I do not claim a mirror here for the separate 3G-MSS assertion.

My lead mirror is Type-Mass 3G-MSM Gap\(_c^\infty\). Its instance has three finite sets of types \(T_A,T_B,T_D\), with rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b,\delta_d\) summing to \(1\) on each side. Each type has a strict ranking over pairs of types on the other two sides: \(a\) ranks \(T_B\times T_D\), \(b\) ranks \(T_A\times T_D\), and \(d\) ranks \(T_A\times T_B\). A feasible continuous marriage is a nonnegative array \(x_{abd}\) whose marginals are those masses. Thus \(x_{abd}\) is the mass of complete families of types \((a,b,d)\); every unit of mass still receives one whole family.

To define its objective, sample one agent uniformly from each side. Given their types, draw each agent’s current partner-type pair according to the allocation \(x\). The sampled triple is unstable if each agent prefers the proposed pair to their assigned pair. Let \(I(x)\) be the probability of that event, and set
\[ \operatorname{stab}_\infty(x)=1-I(x),\qquad \operatorname{OPT}_\infty=\max_x \operatorname{stab}_\infty(x). \]
The problem asks for an allocation maximizing this stable-triple density; its gap version distinguishes \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty=1\) from \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty\le c\). This is the same normalized objective as the paper’s count: stable triples are measured among all possible triples. The fractional entries of \(x\) describe how a continuum of agents is grouped into complete families, not fractional assignments for individuals.

A plausible regime is a large three-way placement market with applicants, employers, and regions or training sites. Many people on each side share a small number \(\tau\) of complete preference types, and their masses record population shares. A person’s ranking depends on the combination of the other two categories, just as the paper’s agents rank pairs. Here the number \(N\) of people is much larger than \(\tau\). That is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s matching question: the matching structure and mutual blocking remain central, while mass is the natural unit for describing cohorts.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be hard, perhaps continuum-specifically hard, because the objective couples three assignment distributions through blocking, giving a cubic optimization problem over a three-way transportation polytope. But Theorem 1.1 does not establish that conclusion. Its reduction uses individual-level preference lists, and a fractional mass allocation can split a type among several family types. The continuum gap problem could therefore admit allocations that have no discrete counterpart. The right research question is whether such fractionalization destroys the gap or whether hardness can be rebuilt directly for type masses; if the latter, whether the reduction’s combinatorics live in \(\tau\) or in population multiplicity.

The main weakness is that the mirror assumes agents care about counterpart types rather than the identities of people within those types. In a setting where identities materially affect rankings, those distinctions belong in the type description, and \(\tau\) may grow until the high-multiplicity regime is no longer persuasive. For the stated placement scenario, though, category-level preferences are a substantive, recognizable regime, not a relaxation invented just to make the computation easier. I expect the original authors to recognize this as their problem under homogeneous populations. The paper’s separate 3G-MSS hardness and its 3PSA results remain outside this proposed mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1.1’s hardness is built from preferences over named people. Its reduction encodes a 3DM instance in gadget-specific rankings, so the construction does not automatically survive compression into a small set of types. Cloning those agents preserves multiplicity but does not reduce the number of distinct preference types: the gadget roles still differ. The proposed mirror instead assumes that everyone of a type ranks pairs of *types* the same way. That is a real restriction on the source problem, and the theorem gives no hardness or approximation result for it.

That objection narrows the claim, but it does not defeat the mirror. Anonymous preferences over pair types make sense in a large placement market, and the type-mass model is a coherent limit of finite instances. With preferences defined at the type level, a rational family allocation can be realized by sufficiently many agents. The fraction of triples that are stable is exactly the probability that three independently sampled agents form a stable triple. So the proposed density objective is not an arbitrary substitute for the paper’s count; it is its normalized version in a homogeneous regime.

The mirror also presents a computational question, though it does not obviously unlock the programme’s LP techniques. Its blocking objective is generally cubic in the allocation masses and nonconvex. That makes a tractability payoff uncertain, but a hard or nonconvex continuous version could still be a useful boundary result. The paper’s reduction failing to transfer is likewise uncertainty about the answer, not a reason the question is unworthy.

I can make a case for caution: this is a restricted, anonymous version of 3G-MSM, and its connection to the paper’s hardness theorem remains to be established. I cannot make an honest case that it has no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario. The proponent’s Type-Mass 3G-MSM gap problem survives the strongest objections I can support.
