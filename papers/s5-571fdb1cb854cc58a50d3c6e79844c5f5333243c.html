The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a tie-breaking order, a target \(c^\star\), an integer \(k\), and a finite support of partial-ranking types \(P_t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), let \(A_t\) be the candidates that type \(t\) can rank first in a completion. A feasible completion assigns \(x_{t,a}\geq 0\) for \(a\in A_t\), with \(\sum_{a\in A_t}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\); candidate \(a\)'s plurality score is \(\sum_t x_{t,a}\). Decide whether some feasible completion puts \(c^\star\) in the top \(k\), and whether every feasible completion does.

The model it lives in

A continuous partial-plurality electorate with partial orders \(P_t\) as types, masses \(\mu_t\), and mass-allocation variables \(x_{t,a}\) over each type's permitted first choices. Each unit of mass receives a complete ranking; only aggregate mass splits across completions. There is no cost objective: the queries test existential or universal top-\(k\) rank membership under the given tie-breaker.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's plurality results for necessary and possible top-\(k\) winners. It does not cover the veto or other scoring-rule results, fixed-\(k\) results, or top-\(k\) set results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuous problem remain hard when the support size \(\tau\) is fixed or otherwise bounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of plurality’s necessary and possible top-\(k\) questions over partial rankings. Its anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: under plurality, deciding whether a candidate is a necessary top-\(k\) winner is coNP-complete, and deciding whether a candidate is a possible top-\(k\) winner is NP-complete. I would lead with the necessary-winner result: its Exact Cover reduction has a particularly clear reason that divisible mass does not remove the hardness.

A continuous instance has candidates \(C\), a tie-breaking order, a target \(c^\star\), and an input \(k\). The society is a finite support of partial-ranking types \(P_t\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Agents of one type share the same partial order and the same permitted completions. For each type, let \(A_t\) be the candidates that can be ranked first in some linear extension of \(P_t\). A completion chooses masses \(x_{t,a}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{a\in A_t}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\); \(x_{t,a}\) is the mass of type \(t\) whose completed ranking puts \(a\) first. The plurality score is \(s_x(a)=\sum_t x_{t,a}\), and scores are ordered using the given tie-breaker.

Call this Continuous Partial-Plurality Top-\(k\). Its possible query asks whether *some* feasible mass completion puts \(c^\star\) in the top \(k\); its necessary query asks whether *every* feasible mass completion does. A yes witness for possibility is a feasible \(x\); a counterexample to necessity is likewise one feasible \(x\). Each unit of mass still receives a complete, integral ranking. Only the aggregate can split across completions. This is a population question: the variables allocate voter mass among the completions allowed by each type, rather than assigning probabilities to election outcomes.

A natural regime is a large consultation using a limited library of partial-ranking templates. For example, many respondents may rank different blocks of candidates while leaving candidates within a block unordered. The type is the template, and its mass is the fraction of respondents using it. The population can have millions of respondents while the number \(\tau\) of templates is much smaller. Replicating each type any number of times preserves its mass, so rational mass assignments also connect to discrete profiles with many repeated voters.

The necessary query appears to retain the hardness in Theorem 3. In its X3C reduction, give each element \(u\) a type of mass \(\delta=1/(3q+3)\), whose possible first choices are precisely the sets containing \(u\). Give a fixed type of mass \(3\delta\) a complete ranking with \(c^\star\) first. Put every set candidate ahead of \(c^\star\) in the tie-breaker, and ask whether \(c^\star\) is necessarily in the top \(q\). Its score is \(3\delta\). For \(c^\star\) to fall outside the top \(q\), at least \(q\) set candidates must each score at least \(3\delta\). They require at least \(3q\delta\) mass in total, exactly all the element-type mass. Each set candidate can receive mass only from its three elements, so meeting all those thresholds forces \(q\) disjoint sets covering every element. Conversely, an exact cover supplies such a completion. Splitting a type’s mass cannot evade this capacity argument. I therefore expect the continuous necessary query to be coNP-complete.

For the possible query, the paper’s Dominating Set construction also has a direct mass version. Use one type of mass \(1/|U|\) per graph vertex \(u\), with possible first choices \(N[u]\); place \(c^\star\) last in these partial orders and first in the tie-breaker. Its score is zero. Let \(S\) be the candidates with positive score. Every type must send its mass to a vertex in its closed neighborhood, so \(S\) must dominate the graph. Since \(c^\star\) wins ties, its rank is \(1+|S|\). Thus it can be in the top \(k\) exactly when a dominating set of size at most \(k-1\) exists. This gives NP-hardness; guessing the candidates ahead of \(c^\star\) and checking rational linear constraints gives NP membership. The printed reduction phrases its budget as \(k\); for the rank definition in the paper, I would state the reduction with budget \(k-1\), equivalently map a budget-\(b\) instance to top-\((b+1)\). With that index shift, I expect the continuous possible query to be NP-complete as well. Both hardness arguments are Class B: their combinatorics survive in the candidate/type incidence structure, even when mass is divisible.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s plurality necessary and possible top-\(k\) results only. It does not claim to cover veto, other scoring rules, fixed-\(k\) results, or top-\(k\) sets. Its weakest point is the scope of the hardness evidence: the reductions use a number of types that grows with the underlying graph or exact-cover instance, so they establish hardness with variable \(\tau\), not with a small fixed support. The possible-winner sketch also needs the budget shift made explicit. Those limits leave a focused, author-recognizable question: whether plurality’s top-\(k\) complexity changes when a large partial-ranking electorate is represented by rational masses over repeated types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case stick. The paper has explicit computational results to mirror, and plurality’s top-\(k\) questions survive the strongest obvious objection: allowing a type’s mass to split across permitted completions does not dissolve either reduction.

For Theorem 3’s necessary-winner result, take \(\delta=1/(3q+3)\). Give each X3C element a type of mass \(\delta\), whose permitted first choices are exactly the sets containing it, and give a fixed type of mass \(3\delta\) a complete ranking with \(c^\star\) first. Then \(c^\star\) has score \(3\delta\). With set candidates ahead of \(c^\star\) in the tie-breaker, its failure to be in the top \(q\) requires \(q\) set candidates to receive at least \(3\delta\) each. Their total demand uses all \(3q\delta\) of the element mass. Each such candidate can receive mass only from its three elements, so meeting all the thresholds forces the chosen sets to be disjoint and cover every element. Dividing mass cannot evade this capacity argument; the reduction’s thresholds force an integral exact cover.

The possible-winner result also has a natural mass version. Give each graph vertex \(u\) a type of mass \(1/|U|\), whose permitted first choices are \(N[u]\), and put \(c^\star\) last in those partial orders but first in the tie-breaker. Every vertex candidate with positive score ranks ahead of \(c^\star\); candidates with zero score rank behind it. The candidates receiving positive mass must dominate every vertex, since each type must send its mass to a permitted choice. Thus \(c^\star\) is in the top \(k\) exactly when the positive-score candidates form a dominating set of size at most \(k-1\). The budget shift is needed for the paper’s rank definition, but it does not weaken the mirror.

The best opposing concern is semantic: repeated partial-ranking templates may describe shared uncertainty rather than voters with identical actual preferences, and fractional completion splits aggregate mass across rankings. But the computation uses only each template’s permitted completions, so a large consultation with repeated templates is a plausible instance regime. Treating fractional assignments as the high-multiplicity version is also precisely the intended bridge, not a mismatch. The reductions use growing support, so they do not establish hardness for fixed small \(\tau\); that is a limitation of the evidence, not a reason to reject the mirror.

Both anchors therefore survive. Their continuous answers may remain hard, but that is a valid outcome for the programme, not a case against asking the questions.
