What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers finding a profitable \(q\)-merge from a given mass-valued coalition structure; it leaves full stability and convergence, split and individual deviations, and the wireless-network simulation aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • When \(q\) is part of the input, what is the complexity of finding a profitable merge for specified utility representations?
  • Which mass-dependent utility models admit efficient computation of a stable coalition structure under repeated profitable merges?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s \(q\)-merge search. A plausible regime is a large, regular wireless deployment with many links drawn from a modest number \(\tau\) of payoff-relevant types. A type must include everything that affects utility, such as the link’s radio configuration and interference profile. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, and a coalition is a subpopulation represented by its mass vector over types. This fits the paper’s general partition-form game and its specific merge operation.

My lead anchor is Lemma 1 in [8], which the paper cites rather than proves. Its content, as used here, is that the worst-case number of \(q\)-merge candidates from a coalition structure with \(|N|\) players is
\[ D(|N|,q)=\sum_{j=2}^{\min(q,|N|)}\binom{|N|}{j}, \]
and for fixed \(q<|N|\), this grows polynomially in \(|N|\), with degree \(q\). This is a bound on the number of candidate deviations to inspect, not a classification of the full stable-outcome problem.

The corresponding continuous question is Continuous \(q\)-Merge Improvement. Its input is a finite type set \(T\); rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); a current structure of \(K\) coalitions, each given by a rational mass vector \(a^k\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau}\), with \(\sum_{k=1}^K a^k_t=\mu_t\); an integer \(q\); and an exact, polynomial-time utility evaluator \(u_t\) for each type. The evaluator returns the per-capita utility of type \(t\) under a given mass partition.

The question is whether there is a set \(J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,K\}\), with \(2\le |J|\le\min(q,K)\), whose coalitions can merge profitably. The new coalition has mass vector \(\sum_{j\in J}a^j\); all other coalitions stay as they are. A merge is profitable if every type with positive mass among the deviators weakly improves its utility and at least one such type strictly improves. A solution is either such a set \(J\), or a certificate that none exists. This is the underlying deviation question, not an encoding of it.

For fixed \(q\), enumerating the candidates takes \(\sum_{j=2}^{\min(q,K)}\binom{K}{j}\) checks, polynomial in \(K\); the utility evaluations add their own cost in \(\tau\), input bit length, and evaluator size. Repeatedly taking profitable merges also terminates: every merge reduces the number of coalitions. The expected direction is therefore tractable for fixed \(q\) in this finite-cluster regime. The same formulation raises the follow-up questions of what happens when \(q\) is part of the input, and how much the choice of improving merge affects the terminal stable structure.

This is a credible mirror of the paper’s abstract \(q\)-merge problem: the paper defines coalitions and utilities through a partition structure, and in the continuous version a cohort can be divided into fractional masses while each member of a type remains indistinguishable for the utilities the model uses. It covers the \(q\)-merge neighborhood-search result, not the paper’s whole treatment of coalition formation or its MISO simulation.

The weak point is that the cited bound depends on the number \(K\) of current coalitions, not on the number of types or the total mass. Continuization does not itself make this search easier; it gives the same fixed-\(q\) enumeration over mass-valued coalitions. Also, real links often have distinct channel profiles, so a small-\(\tau\) regime needs a genuinely regular deployment with repeated payoff-relevant profiles. The case survives as a focused mirror of the paper’s general deviation model, but it is not evidence that continuization unlocks a new optimization method here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed anchor does not quite belong to this paper: Lemma 1 is a result cited from [8], while this paper’s equation (6) reports the resulting count of possible \(q\)-merges. That count is not a complexity result for finding a profitable merge or a stable structure. It counts candidate moves, leaving their evaluation to the utility model.

A direct continuous translation would give each of the \(K\) current coalitions a mass vector and count subsets of those coalitions. The answer is still \(D(K,q)\), independent of population mass, type support, and bit length. That is a valid restatement of the enumeration, but it does not ask a new computational question about a continuous population. To ask whether some merge is profitable, as the proponent does, one must add an evaluable utility model. The paper’s count alone says nothing about the complexity of that added part.

The most faithful alternative would use the MISO utilities. But there, a type must preserve the channel interactions that determine beamforming and payoffs. In a general network those profiles are link-specific, so an exact model need not have small support. With a continuum of generic channel types, zero forcing a positive-mass coalition would impose infinitely many channel constraints on a finite-dimensional beamformer. A finite-type symmetric network may avoid that problem, but its mass-based utility evaluator is an additional model; the paper does not supply one. That would make the result about a new stylized coalition game, rather than a complexity question extracted from this paper.

This does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A finite-type, mass-based \(q\)-merge game is coherent, and the proposed question can be repaired to track gains for each type-and-coalition block when a type is split across coalitions. My negative case is therefore weak on the user’s universal standard: such a model could be worth studying. What I can defend is narrower: this paper offers little reason to prioritize it as a ChoCo mirror. Its own contribution here is a tutorial account of deviation models and candidate counts, not a computational result whose population-mass version opens a substantive complexity landscape.
