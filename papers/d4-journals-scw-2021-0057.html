The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s Theorem 6.1, proved here. For a finite-player interval simple game \(v\), it says that each player’s Shapley–Shubik power is the Shapley value of the average game
\[ \widehat v(S)=\int_{[0,1]^n}\!\bigl[v(1_S,x_{-S})-v(0_S,x_{-S})\bigr]\,dx, \]
so that
\[ \Psi_i(v)=\sum_{i\in S\subseteq N} \frac{(|S|-1)!(n-|S|)!}{n!} \bigl[\widehat v(S)-\widehat v(S\setminus\{i\})\bigr]. \]
The paper derives this formula; it does not establish a complexity classification for computing it. I would use it as a computation-formula anchor only under the prompt’s formulation exception. There is no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result here.

My lead mirror is the Type-Aggregate Interval Shapley–Shubik Limit. Consider a large membership organization whose members cast graded approvals on a proposal. A member’s type records their complete role in the decision rule—such as voting rights or weight—and members of the same type are interchangeable. There are \(\tau\) types with rational population shares \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and many members per type. An approval profile gives each member \(i\) a level \(x_i\in[0,1]\). The society’s continuous state is the vector of type approval masses \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), with \(z_t\) equal to the fraction of the whole society contributed by type \(t\)’s approvals. A monotone rule \(F\) maps those masses to an outcome in \([0,1]\), with \(F(0)=0\) and \(F(\mu)=1\).

For precision, take \(F\) to be an explicitly represented rational polynomial. Write \(\mu_t=a_t/A\). For each positive integer \(q\), form a finite society with \(q a_t\) members of type \(t\), and define its interval game by
\[ v_q(x)=F\!\left(\left(\frac{1}{qA}\sum_{i\in N_t^{(q)}}x_i\right)_{t\in T}\right). \]
The instance is \((\mu,F)\); the requested solution is the vector of total power assigned to each type,
\[ \Pi_t=\lim_{q\to\infty}\sum_{i\in N_t^{(q)}}\Psi_i(v_q). \]
The limit is the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s index: it aggregates the index of the indistinguishable members of each type. The society is continuous because each \(\mu_t\) is a share, while \(F\) still describes the interval decision rule.

For this smooth, explicitly encoded case I expect Class A. The limit reduces to the one-dimensional integral
\[ \Pi_t= \frac{\mu_t}{2}\int_0^1 \left[ \partial_tF\bigl((1-r/2)\mu\bigr) + \partial_tF\bigl((r/2)\mu\bigr) \right]dr. \]
Here \(r\) is the fraction of the roll call preceding a member. As the population grows, the preceding members’ approval mass concentrates around \(r\mu/2\); the two endpoint profiles in the interval-game formula approach \((r/2)\mu\) and \((1-r/2)\mu\). A single member changes their type’s approval mass by \(1/(qA)\); summing those marginal contributions over the \(q a_t\) members of type \(t\) gives the displayed limit. For an explicitly listed rational polynomial, the integral is exactly computable by polynomial arithmetic. This derivation and algorithm are part of the proposed mirror, not results claimed by the paper.

The regime could be a large cooperative or association with many members in a small number of voting-rights classes. The counts grow while the number of types stays fixed or moderate. This matches the paper’s roll-call and power-index question: the mirror asks how the same index distributes power across interchangeable blocks of members. It covers Theorem 6.1’s index computation, not the paper’s axiomatization in Theorem 6.2 or its finite \((j,k)\)-game results. The paper’s continuous approval levels do not themselves make it a population-continuum paper: it keeps \(n\) named players. The proposed mirror takes the additional step of replacing those individuals by type masses.

The weakest point is that the paper studies power for named players in a finite game and does not claim a computational bottleneck. Aggregating power by type and taking the replication limit are natural extensions of its object, but an author or referee could reasonably view this as a new asymptotic index problem rather than a continuization of a computational problem the paper itself posed. Further questions include which nonsmooth monotone rules have a discretization-independent limit, how quickly finite populations approach it, and what complexity remains when the rule is given in a compact representation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.1 is the only proposed anchor, and it does not give ChoCo a computational result to mirror. It identifies \(\Psi(v)\) with the Shapley value of the average game \(\widehat v\). The paper also presents formulas as better suited to computation, but it gives no complexity classification, running-time analysis, or finite encoding for the general interval game. The theorem is an identity about the index; it leaves the computational problem unspecified.

The proposed type model is coherent. Repeated members can be interchangeable, and aggregating their power avoids the vanishing individual values in the limit. The smooth polynomial case even yields a concrete calculation. But that calculation comes from added choices: a rule \(F\) that depends on type approval masses, a replication limit, and an explicit polynomial representation. For that representation the displayed one-dimensional integral is straightforward to evaluate. To make computation harder or richer, one must choose a different representation; with arbitrary monotone \(F\), there is no finite input on which to state a standard complexity result. Those choices define a new asymptotic index problem rather than continue a computational question posed by this paper.

A weighted-threshold or other structured subclass could still be a sensible project, and the paper’s treatment of interval decisions is relevant background. The negative case is therefore not that no plausible continuous population model exists. It is that the paper’s central contribution is axiomatic, while the one computationally suggestive theorem supplies a formula rather than a complexity problem. Studying the proposed limit may be worthwhile on its own, but this paper gives ChoCo no computational result for that mirror to extend.
