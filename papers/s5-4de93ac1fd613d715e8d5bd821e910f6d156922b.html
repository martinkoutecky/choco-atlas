What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is narrow: Theorem 5, proved in this paper, says that as \(N_0\to0\), the transmitter-cooperation game with a single-user-decoding receiver has a nonempty core under both sum-power and per-antenna power constraints. It is a stability result, not a runtime bound. The paper separately says that general core checking is co-NP-complete, citing [22] in §II-B; that statement is background rather than a numbered result proved here. I would use Theorem 5 as my sole anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large uplink to a shared receiver, with many interchangeable radios from a small number of engineered channel, antenna, and power profiles. A type records the complete radio profile used by the game. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of transmitters with that profile; the number of transmitters can be large while the support size \(\tau\) stays moderate. Joint codebooks and shared channel information are already part of the paper’s cooperation model. The scenario is most convincing for repeated fixed installations; arbitrary fading users would often have nearly as many distinct types as transmitters.

Here is a precise continuous single-user-decoding \(m\)-core problem, mirroring one of the paper’s named notions of core. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, a positive rational noise level \(N_0\), and a nonnegative received-amplitude parameter \(b_t\) for each type. The \(b_t\) combines its channel and per-antenna power profile under a population-normalized power convention. For a coalition containing mass vector \(x\), let its received signal power be \(Q(x)=(\sum_t b_t x_t)^2\). This is the scalar-receiver coherent-beamforming form: amplitudes add within a coalition, as in the paper’s single-antenna per-antenna-power model.

The question is whether there are per-transmitter rates \(\rho_t\ge0\) such that the grand coalition’s total rate is allocated and no subpopulation can profitably deviate when the outsiders are treated as one coalition. In formulas, for every \(x\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), require \(\sum_t\mu_t\rho_t=\log(1+Q(\mu)/N_0)\) and \(\sum_t x_t\rho_t\ge\log(1+Q(x)/(N_0+Q(\mu-x)))\). The instance asks whether such a vector \(\rho\) exists; a vector is a solution. The mass vector \(x\) allows a fraction of each type to deviate, which is the population-continuous part of the question. This is exactly the merged-expectations core defined in the paper, with integer coalitions replaced by submasses.

I expect this scalar, finite-support version to be Class A, in a standard additive-precision formulation. For a proposed rate vector, separation asks for a violating \(x\). Its payoff depends on \(x\) through the single aggregate \(z=\sum_t b_tx_t\). At a fixed \(z\), the best violating coalition is found by a bounded fractional-knapsack calculation; what remains is a one-dimensional optimization over \(z\). That gives a plausible route to a separation oracle and then continuous-optimization methods for finding rates. Theorem 5 supplies the high-SNR structural target, but it does not establish this algorithm or its bit complexity. Exact precision at boundary instances, and whether the argument survives general MIMO channels, remain open parts of the mirror.

This covers the paper’s grand-coalition stability question for the SUD receiver, specifically its \(m\)-core result. It does not mirror the paper’s equilibrium-uniqueness analysis or its fixed-order SIC and time-sharing results. A useful next question is whether the continuous core is nonempty for all sufficiently high SNR for every finite-support type distribution, and how that threshold relates to the discrete high-multiplicity instances with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\).

The weakest point is the regime. Exact channel and power profiles must repeat often enough that \(\tau\ll n\); that is plausible in an engineered deployment, but not in a population with individually varying fading. The population normalization in \(b_t\) also needs to be stated as part of the model so aggregate received power remains finite. If those assumptions do not fit the authors’ intended MAC, this mirror loses much of its force.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is weaker than it first appears: Theorem 5 is a stability theorem, not a computational result. It establishes SUD core nonemptiness in the high-SNR limit; it gives no algorithm or complexity bound. The paper’s co-NP-completeness statement is background cited from [22], not a result proved here. So asking for the complexity of continuous core feasibility would be a new problem, not a computational mirror of Theorem 5. The other numbered results have the same issue: they concern equilibrium utilities or core properties, not algorithms for a computational problem.

There is also a deeper mismatch in the proposed mass model. The paper’s coalition utility depends on the number of radios, through the concatenated channel and the members’ power constraints. In the single-antenna case, a coalition of \(q\) identical transmitters can have received signal power proportional to \(q^2\) under per-antenna constraints. Two discrete societies with the same proportions \(\mu\), but different total populations \(n\), therefore need not have the same coalition values. The normalized distribution alone is not a sufficient state for this game.

The proposed formula \(Q(x)=(\sum_t b_t x_t)^2\) removes that dependence by scaling each transmitter’s amplitude with population size. That may define a coherent new model, and the proposed one-dimensional separation route may be useful for it. But it changes the paper’s power regime: with \(n\) fixed-profile transmitters, it implicitly makes each transmitter’s power shrink on the order of \(1/n^2\). Keeping fixed per-transmitter power, or even fixed total transmit power, gives a different population scaling. A better mirror could retain total population as an extra parameter or choose another normalization, but then it must justify that scaling as the authors’ intended transmitter-cooperation problem; it cannot derive it from the paper’s model.

That leaves a plausible mirror only under a specially chosen power convention and a highly repeated channel profile. The engineered-network scenario makes that convention conceivable, so this is not a proof that no useful continuous model exists. It is the strongest case against this anchor: the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror, and the proposed continuum depends on a new scaling choice whose connection to the paper’s physical game remains unestablished.
