The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), type rankings \(\succ_t\), acceptable ordered type pairs \(A\), rational per-capita limits \(\kappa_{tu}\), and rational weights \(\ell_{tu}\) satisfying \(u\succ_t u' \Rightarrow \ell_{tu}\ge \ell_{tu'}\), compute a maximum-weight Pareto-optimal mass circulation. For \(x_{tu}\), the mass of service received by type \(t\) from type \(u\), require \(0\le x_{tu}\le\mu_t\kappa_{tu}\) and \(\sum_u x_{tu}=\sum_s x_{st}\) for every \(t\), and maximize \(\sum_{(t,u)\in A}\ell_{tu}x_{tu}\) subject to Pareto optimality under the lexicographic rankings of the per-capita receipt vectors \((x_{tu}/\mu_t)_{u:\,(t,u)\in A}\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational mass distribution over interchangeable participant types. The exchange graph has types as vertices and acceptable type pairs as arcs; arc capacities scale with recipient mass, and each type’s total incoming and outgoing mass balance. Cycle masses induce arc flows, and additive arc weights define total welfare. Pareto comparisons use each type’s per-capita receipts in its ranking of provider types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 12 for preference-concordant weights and Theorem 9 for indifference with additive weights. It leaves the TTC result, Pareto characterization and recognition results, indivisible exchanges, and arbitrary weights with preferences outside its stated scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove a precise finite-to-continuous correspondence establishing when a feasible type-level circulation can be implemented as a symmetric individual exchange, including within-type trades and per-capita capacities.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here, but it is specifically a high-volume timebank mirror. The paper already allows divisible goods; the distinct move is to make the population a distribution over types, then ask how the exchange problems behave when many people share a type.

Take a timebank with \(N\) participants and \(\tau\) observed profiles, where \(\tau \ll N\). A type records everything the exchange uses: the participant’s ranking of acceptable provider types, their per-capita exchange limits, and their welfare weights. The society is \(\mu\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of participants of type \(t\). This fits the paper’s own “1 hour = 1 hour” setting if people rank service-provider categories—such as skill and availability profiles—rather than named individuals.

For each acceptable pair of types \(t,u\), let \(x_{tu}\) be the total mass of service delivered by type \(u\) to type \(t\). Set the pair capacity to \(c_{tu}=\mu_t\kappa_{tu}\), where \(\kappa_{tu}\) is the common per-capita limit for type \(t\) receiving from type \(u\). Require \(x_{tu}\ge 0\), \(x_{tu}\le c_{tu}\), and, for every type \(t\),
\[ \sum_u x_{tu}=\sum_s x_{st}. \]
The equality balances what that type receives and gives. Divisible service lets the cohort share its schedule evenly, so each member of type \(t\) can receive \(x_{tu}/\mu_t\) from each provider type \(u\) and give an equal total amount. A feasible exchange can be reported as cycle masses \(y_C\ge 0\), with \(x_{tu}=\sum_{C\ni(t,u)}y_C\).

My lead is Theorem 12, proved in this paper. It says that in a preference-concordant instance \(PX(w)\), a maximum-weight Pareto-optimal balanced exchange can be computed in polynomial time. Here cycle weight is additive, \(w(C)=\sum_{e\in C}\ell(e)\), and a more-preferred provider has at least as high a per-unit weight for the receiving agent. The theorem’s algorithm first finds a maximum-weight exchange and then improves it to a Pareto-optimal one without losing weight, using the paper’s preceding results.

The corresponding question is: given the type masses \(\mu_t\), type rankings \(\succ_t\), rational per-capita limits \(\kappa_{tu}\), and rational weights \(\ell_{tu}\) satisfying \(u\succ_t u'\Rightarrow \ell_{tu}\ge\ell_{tu'}\), find a feasible cycle-mass exchange maximizing
\[ \sum_{t,u}\ell_{tu}x_{tu} \]
that is also Pareto optimal. Compare exchanges by the per-capita receipt vectors \((x_{tu}/\mu_t)_u\), ordered by each type’s ranking: an exchange is Pareto optimal if no feasible exchange makes every type weakly better in this lexicographic order and at least one type strictly better. The answer expected from the paper is tractable, in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of type-pair arcs, and the rational input bit length \(L\).

This retains the paper’s core question: which balanced cycles should an exchange market choose, given preferences, capacities, and a social welfare objective? It does not merely fractionalize the goods; it aggregates the society into cohorts. For symmetric type-level limits and preferences, the comparison also has a useful link to the individual market: averaging any individual-level Pareto improvement over permutations within each type preserves feasibility and remains a Pareto improvement under lexicographic preferences. Thus, requiring a common per-capita schedule does not hide an individual-level improvement in this regime.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 9, also proved here. Under indifference over exchange partners and additive cycle weights, it shows that the full dual system for maximum-weight fractional dicycle packing is equivalent to one with constraints only for 3-cycles and equalities for 2-cycles. That gives a polynomial-size LP and a polynomial-time algorithm. Its mirror asks for a maximum-weight mass circulation on the type graph, with the same mass-scaled capacities and additive edge weights, but without preference or Pareto constraints. Its expected class is also tractable. This is a useful companion: it covers arbitrary additive weights in the indifference case, while Theorem 12 handles ranked preferences under concordant weights.

The paper’s strongest numbered anchors are algorithmic. It mentions the NP-hardness of integral dicycle packing as prior work, citing [11], but that is not a numbered hardness result of this paper and does not establish hardness for preference-aware integral \(PX(w)\). I would treat it as context for the boundary, not as an anchor. The paper’s own Theorem 12 and Theorem 9 are the results this mirror can actually rest on.

The scope is deliberately limited to the divisible-exchange results: Theorem 12’s preference-concordant welfare optimum and Theorem 9’s indifference case. It does not claim a continuous mirror for every result in the paper, or for arbitrary weights with preferences. Further questions include whether maximum-weight Pareto-optimal exchange remains tractable without concordance, and how closely the mass optimum approximates a finite exchange with \(n_t\) participants of each type.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. If participants care about *which named person* supplies a service, or have individual-specific limits or weights, those differences belong in the type description. Then \(\tau\) might grow nearly as fast as \(N\), and the computational gain disappears. The positive case rests on the narrower but plausible timebank regime where cohorts are genuinely interchangeable. The authors should recognize this as their problem: it uses their same balanced cycles, preferences, capacities, and welfare objective, with mass-scaled cohorts replacing the finite list of agents.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper’s computational object is built from bilateral relationships. An arc represents a particular buyer–seller pair, its capacity and weight belong to that pair, and in Theorem 12 the recipient ranks particular agents. A type that faithfully preserves those data must include an agent’s transaction neighborhood, pair-specific limits and weights, and preferences over partners. In timebanks or exchange networks where these vary by person, the number of types can grow with the population. Then \(\tau\) approaches \(N\), and the population does not become a useful mass description.

That is a serious problem for both anchors. Theorem 9 removes preferences, but its capacities and additive weights can still distinguish every ordered pair. Theorem 12 adds partner rankings, making faithful aggregation harder. In either case, replacing pair-specific arcs with \(\kappa_{tu}\) and \(\ell_{tu}\) assumes a highly exchangeable network: any member of one cohort can transact with the relevant members of another under the same pooled terms. That assumption changes what the graph records. It may be a good model for some systems, but it is not automatically a faithful continuization of the paper’s general bilateral exchange problem.

The proponent’s better version is also the strongest reply. A timebank in which people rank service-provider categories, cohorts share per-capita limits and welfare weights, and divisible service can be distributed symmetrically is a coherent population model. Its type-level balance can give every member the same per-capita schedule, and symmetrizing a Pareto improvement is plausible under those exchangeability assumptions. The authors might well recognize that as their problem. Theorem 9 has an especially natural analogue when participants are indifferent among provider categories.

So this is a conditional case against the breadth of the mirrors, not a decisive objection to either anchor. It identifies the modelling price: to keep \(\tau\) small, the analyst must assume away much of the named-partner structure that defines the paper’s general setting. But a category-based timebank is plausible, and the paper’s own motivation supports it. I cannot honestly claim that this defeats every worthwhile scenario; the negative case is weak precisely at that boundary.
