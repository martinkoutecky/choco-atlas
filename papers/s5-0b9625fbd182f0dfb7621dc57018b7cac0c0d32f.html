The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: the paper’s worker–task model has a natural continuous-population version, but the paper has no qualifying computational result to anchor it.

The three numbered theorems are proved in the paper, and each characterizes a compensation rule: Theorem 1 says the Egalitarian rule is the unique efficient, symmetric rule satisfying Group Productivity Monotonicity; Theorem 2 says the Shapley Value rule is the unique efficient rule satisfying Balanced Impact; Theorem 3 says the Individual Contribution rule is the unique efficient rule satisfying Consistency. None states a complexity result. Lemma 1 gives bounds on marginal contribution, Proposition 1 establishes an incompatibility between axioms, and Lemma 8 shows that problems with multiple optimal assignments form a nowhere-dense set. These are not results that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. The maximum-output assignment \(y(I,T,p)\) is defined in the paper, but no named result analyzes its complexity. So there are no valid anchors under the requested standard, and no anchor-backed continuous question I can honestly claim mirrors a result of this paper.

A plausible *unanchored* candidate comes from the model’s assignment-and-output core. Imagine a large employer allocating workers among recurring task or shift categories. A worker type is a complete productivity profile over those categories; workers of the same type are interchangeable. For example, a firm might have \(N\) workers but only a few dozen productivity types, with task capacities scaling roughly in proportion to \(N\).

Call the resulting problem Continuum Task Assignment and Egalitarian Compensation. An instance consists of finite worker-type and task-category sets \(A,Q\); rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) summing to \(1\); per-worker productivities \(p_{iq}\geq 0\); and rational task capacities \(\kappa_q\geq 0\), with \(\sum_q\kappa_q\geq1\). Find a mass assignment \(x_{iq}\geq0\) maximizing
\[ y^\star=\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{q\in Q}p_{iq}x_{iq} \]
subject to \(\sum_qx_{iq}=\mu_i\) for every worker type and \(\sum_ix_{iq}\leq\kappa_q\) for every task category. Return an optimal assignment and \(y^\star\). Under the continuous Egalitarian rule, each worker receives \(y^\star\), since the population has unit mass. Each worker still performs one task; \(x_{iq}\) records how much mass of type \(i\) performs task \(q\).

I would expect this candidate to be tractable: it is a transportation LP, with running time polynomial in the numbers of types and task categories and the input bit length. When capacities and type masses come from finite counts divided by \(N\), the corresponding transportation polytope has integral scaled vertices, so the aggregate assignment can reproduce the finite assignment optimum exactly. That makes this a natural population model, though it does not show discrete hardness dissolving.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a continuous version of their production and compensation setting. The mirror’s reach is limited, though: it covers the assignment-output substrate and evaluation of the Egalitarian payment, not the paper’s central axiomatic characterizations. Extending the Shapley or Individual Contribution rules would raise extra questions, especially how to define averages over optimal assignments in a continuum.

That is the weakest point, and it is decisive for the requested case: the proposed LP is a reasonable continuous computational question, but it is not anchored by a named computational result in this paper. The paper offers no proved computational claim for a continuous mirror to preserve, and its main contributions are axiomatic questions outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–3 characterize compensation rules by fairness axioms; Proposition 1 gives an incompatibility, and Lemma 8 is a topological result. None analyzes an algorithm or computational complexity. Restating those theorems for a society of productivity types would still be an axiomatic project, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. For Theorems 2 and 3, a mass-based version could replace removing one worker with removing a cohort or taking a derivative, but that changes the axiom; it does not create a computational result.

The proposed type-and-task model is sensible, and its LP is a legitimate continuous population formulation. But it computes an optimum for the assignment problem already defined in the paper. The finite problem is a weighted assignment problem, and with replicated worker and task types its scaled transportation formulation has integral optima. So the LP gives the same aggregate optimum; it does not expose a hard computational problem that the population limit might simplify. Computing the Egalitarian payment then just divides that output by population mass. That is a plausible application of a standard algorithm, but it is an unanchored and modest computational extension, not a mirror of one of the paper’s results.

A stronger computational follow-up might ask how to calculate Shapley or Individual Contribution payments for large populations of repeated productivity types. That could be worth studying, but it would require defining how the discrete rules’ marginal contributions or averages over optimal assignments behave in the limit. The paper neither poses nor answers that question. So the negative case is strong against a result-anchored mirror; it is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile project could grow from the model. The continuous workplace scenario is credible, but by itself it does not supply the missing computational contribution.
