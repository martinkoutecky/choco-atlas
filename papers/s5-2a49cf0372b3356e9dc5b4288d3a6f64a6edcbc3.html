The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative fact is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Sections 5–8 report simulations and an ABC calibration procedure, but give no complexity theorem or guarantee for a stated computational problem. So there is no result-anchored mirror here; any candidate has to be a new problem inferred from the paper’s aims.

The proposed calibration question is the best candidate, and the paper’s millions of electors across dozens of districts make a high-multiplicity regime plausible. But the proposed feasibility test—find \(\psi\) whose *expected* summaries match observations—changes what the paper calibrates. Its ABC procedure compares *realized* simulated summaries with the observed election and accepts parameter samples accordingly. That distinction matters because seat counts come from district winners, and the paper studies variation across runs. Replacing voters by masses can erase individual sampling variation and can change seat outcomes: applying a winner rule to expected vote shares need not give the expected winner. The paper’s CRP and community models also create shared randomness across voters, so preserving the calibration target means retaining a law over partitions or random populations, rather than just a single type distribution.

One can repair the proposal by asking for posterior or predictive probabilities under a continuous-population limit. That is a coherent inference problem, but its main computational burden would be inference over the model’s random communities, district environments, and parameters. It would be a new stochastic-inference problem, not a complexity result about a problem the paper already formulates. Likewise, a mass-level robustness or manipulation question would need an intervention and a cost model that the paper does not supply; its counterfactuals vary generative parameters rather than specify actions on voters.

This is a strong case against a *result-anchored* mirror, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A limit that preserves the paper’s shared randomness could be a plausible research direction if the authors regard that inference problem as theirs. The universal negative is therefore weak at that point: the paper offers no established computational result to continue, but its calibration objective could support a worthwhile new one.
