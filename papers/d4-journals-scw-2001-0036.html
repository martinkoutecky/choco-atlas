Citizens’ population sizes enter the fairness analysis as constituency sizes; they are not the agents whose yes/no choices form the winning coalitions. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case offers no affirmative anchor to defeat. It argues the negative itself: the paper has no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its strongest results are structural—whether these particular rules can be represented by a single weighted rule—and its power-index calculations concern fixed councils of 15 or 27 members. There is no complexity problem, algorithm, or hardness result here for a continuous mirror to classify.

The best rescue would be to study councils with many jurisdictions sharing complete types, where a type includes its voting weight and population. One could ask how Penrose or Banzhaf power, majority deficit, or quota design behaves as those type counts grow. That could be a sensible problem for some federations. But it is not anchored in this paper: its coalition members are state representatives, while citizens’ population sizes enter as constituency data and a fairness proxy. Continuizing citizens leaves the council’s coalition game finite; replacing the council with a continuum of jurisdictions changes the institution. Individual pivotality then needs a limiting normalization or interpretation of its own. Those choices might support a new asymptotic voting-power project, but they do not turn this paper’s fixed-instance evaluation into a computational result about continuous societies.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as a ChoCo mirror: there is no named computational result to mirror, and the plausible extension asks a different question. The universal claim is weaker. I cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous study of large, repeated-type councils; I would classify that as a new project rather than a mirror of this paper.
