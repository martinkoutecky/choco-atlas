The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the complete or partial ballot types for three candidates, decide whether there is a split \(x\), with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), and a candidate \(q\) such that \(q\) wins under IRV in both sub-societies \(x\) and \(\mu-x\), but loses in the full society \(\mu\); return a witnessing split when one exists. Use unique winners or a fixed tie-breaking rule.

The model it lives in

The types are the six complete rankings or the nine ballot forms with partial rankings. The instance is their rational mass distribution \(\mu\), summing to \(1\); the decision variables are the sub-election masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), and the task is feasibility of the two IRV wins and the full-society loss.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the complete-ballot characterization in Proposition 1 and the partial-ballot characterization in Proposition 2, including the paper's limiting conditions and IAC probabilities. It does not cover the empirical estimates, geographically constrained partitions, or elections with more than three candidates.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is continuous three-candidate IRV reinforcement susceptibility. It asks the same question as the paper’s arbitrary-partition version of the paradox, with population fractions replacing ballot counts.

Take three candidates \(A,B,C\). A type is a ballot ranking; for complete ballots there are at most six types, and for the paper’s partial-ballot model there are at most nine. The instance is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with total mass \(1\). In a large-election scenario, this means millions of voters but only a small number of distinct ballot types. A decision variable \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) assigns type \(t\)’s mass to sub-election 1; the remaining mass \(\mu_t-x_t\) goes to sub-election 2.

Call the problem Continuous IRV Reinforcement Susceptibility. Given \(\mu\), determine whether there is such a split for which the same candidate wins both sub-elections under IRV but loses in the full society. If yes, return the split \(x\) and the candidate. Assume unique winners, or fix a tie-breaking rule. This is a feasibility problem: its solution is a mass partition, not a ballot alteration or a fractional election outcome.

My lead anchor is Proposition 1 (Complete Ballots), proved in the paper. It assumes \(A\) is the full election’s IRV winner and \(C\) its plurality loser, leaving \(B\) as the runner-up. For the paper’s integer ballot counts, the runner-up paradox occurs exactly when \(Y>X-Z_2+1\) and \(Y>-X_1+X_2+Z_1-Z_2+1\). If also \(Y>X+1\) and \(Y>-X_1+X_2+Z+1\), there is a partition where \(B\) wins by a first-round majority in one sub-election and in the second round in the other. The plurality-loser paradox occurs exactly when all four conditions hold: \(Z>X-Y_2+1\), \(Z>X_1-X_2+Y_1-Y_2+1\), \(Z>-X_2+Y+1\), and \(Z>\frac13(X+Y)+1\); in that case \(C\) wins in the second round in both sub-elections. Here \(X=X_1+X_2\), \(Y=Y_1+Y_2\), and \(Z=Z_1+Z_2\), with the subscripts denoting the six rankings in Table 3.

For the continuous problem, use the same quantities as proportions. As the paper itself observes for its limiting case, the \(+1\) terms disappear. Thus the runner-up test becomes \(Y>X-Z_2\) and \(Y>-X_1+X_2+Z_1-Z_2\); the additional mixed-round condition becomes \(Y>X\) and \(Y>-X_1+X_2+Z\). The plurality-loser test becomes \(Z>X-Y_2\), \(Z>X_1-X_2+Y_1-Y_2\), \(Z>-X_2+Y\), and \(Z>\frac13(X+Y)\). These inequalities give an exact decision procedure for the continuous problem. A witness split can also be recovered by solving the corresponding finite feasibility constraints.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Proposition 2 (Partial Ballots), proved in the paper. It gives the analogous exact characterization when voters may submit the partial ballots represented by \(X_3,Y_3,Z_3\), with \(X'=X_1+X_2+X_3\), \(Y'=Y_1+Y_2+Y_3\), and \(Z'=Z_1+Z_2+Z_3\). In the integer election, the runner-up paradox occurs exactly when \(Y'>X'-Z_2+1\), \(Y'>-X_1+X_2+Z_1-Z_2+1\), and \(Y'>\frac13(-X_1+2X_2+2X_3+Z_1-Z_2)+1\). If also \(Y'>X'+1\) and \(Y'>-X_1+X_2+Z'+1\), a partition has the runner-up winning by a first-round majority in one part and in round two in the other. The plurality-loser paradox occurs exactly when \(Z'>X'-Y_2+1\), \(Z'>X_1-X_2+Y_1-Y_2+1\), \(Z'>-X_2+Y'+1\), and \(Z'>\frac13(X'+Y')+1\), with the plurality loser winning in round two in both parts. In the continuous version, again, each \(+1\) is removed.

For both mirrors I expect a tractable result—Class A for these three-candidate versions. The characterizations are explicit inequalities in a constant number of rational masses, and the problem’s output can be a witness partition. The authors would recognize the question: they define susceptibility by existence of a partition, analyze complete and partial ballots, and explicitly pass to proportions in their limiting case. This case covers those characterization results and their arbitrary-partition question. It does not cover the paper’s empirical estimates, geographically constrained precinct or district partitions, or IRV with more than three candidates.

The weakest point is also the most important qualification: the paper already takes almost exactly this continuous limit, both in its limiting inequalities and in its continuous-simplex probability analysis. The mirror is highly plausible, but a claim that this three-candidate question is a new research gap would be hard to sustain; the paper substantially answers it already. Also, arbitrary mass splits preserve the paper’s intentionally broad definition of susceptibility, but need not correspond to a socially meaningful division. Natural next questions are whether the paradox persists under balanced sub-election sizes, whether partitions can be restricted to meaningful group types, and how the complexity changes for more candidates. Those would extend the mirror rather than alter what this paper’s result establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is that the paper has already done them. This is a novelty collision, not an objection to whether continuous populations are sensible: elections with many voters and few ballot types are a natural high-multiplicity setting.

For both Proposition 1’s complete ballots and Proposition 2’s partial ballots, the paper explicitly takes the limiting case where vote counts become proportions and the \(+1\) terms disappear. Those are the strict inequalities for deciding whether a continuous distribution over the six or nine ballot types admits a split that gives the same losing candidate both sub-election wins. The paper also computes exact limiting probabilities over the continuous ballot simplex for IAC, including partial ballots. So the proposed decision question—given a distribution of ballot mass, does a paradox-producing split exist?—is already answered for the three-candidate cases the propositions cover.

Returning an actual split does not seem to create a new computational result here. The paper’s proofs derive bounds on the split variables and explain how to use them to construct partitions. More generally, with only six or nine mass variables, one can check the finite set of possible elimination orders using rational linear feasibility. Requiring equal-sized sub-elections, or a bounded difference in their profiles, adds linear constraints to that small problem. These variants may be useful, but they do not appear to open a new complexity landscape.

A geographically meaningful split is a stronger possible mirror, but it changes the problem’s structure. Keeping each precinct or district intact requires choosing whole units, as in the paper’s binary ILP; allowing continuous mass to split a precinct can erase the geographic meaning of the partition. A continuous model of contiguous regions could be worthwhile, but it would need spatially resolved population data and would ask a new geometric partition question, not extend the two anchored characterizations directly.

I would therefore reject the proposed mirrors of Propositions 1 and 2 as new research targets: the paper already gives their continuous formulations and answers. I can’t make the universal negative airtight, though. A continuous complexity study for more than three candidates, or a spatially continuous version of natural geographic partitions, could still be worthwhile. Those are plausible next projects, but they are broader questions than the anchors raised here.
