The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s first-best fluid throughput bound. The paper contains no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or similar. Its best computational anchor is the appendix’s fluid upper-bound proposition, cited there as prop:fluid_UB; the supplied text does not print a proposition number, so I won’t invent one. The proposition says that the optimum of the displayed Fluid LP is at least the long-run throughput of every matching policy, for any compatibility graph and model parameters. The paper gives the LP and computes it as an upper bound and to derive a priority heuristic; its proof adapts a result of Aouad et al. (2022).

Call the mirror Continuous First-Best Throughput Bound. A platform operates at high volume with a fixed set of agent types \(I\) and job types \(J\). An agent type is a complete class for the model: its members have the same compatible jobs and the same patience and other relevant characteristics. Job types likewise share the relevant characteristics. A plausible regime is a large ride platform or housing intake with many agents and jobs but relatively few compatibility classes. Scale arrivals by a common factor \(N\), keeping the type shares fixed; the continuous instance records the per-scale arrival masses \(\lambda_i\) and \(\mu_j\).

Given a bipartite compatibility graph \(E\), rational rates \(\lambda_i,\mu_j\), and reneging rate \(\theta\), find a maximum-throughput feasible allocation of matching mass per unit time. A solution gives \(x_{ij}\), the matching rate between compatible types, and \(y_i\), the residual supply rate for agent type \(i\). The paper’s fluid model is:

\( \begin{aligned} \max\quad & \sum_{(i,j)\in E} x_{ij}\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & \sum_{i:(i,j)\in E} x_{ij}\le \mu_j && \forall j\in J,\\ & \sum_{j:(i,j)\in E} x_{ij}+y_i=\lambda_i && \forall i\in I,\\ & \theta x_{ij}\le \mu_j y_i && \forall (i,j)\in E,\\ & x_{ij},y_i\ge 0. \end{aligned} \)

This is tractable: it has polynomially many variables and constraints in the type and edge counts, and rational linear programming is solvable in polynomial time in those counts and the input bit length. That complexity claim is my inference from the LP, not a complexity theorem stated by the authors. The paper’s own use of the LP makes this a particularly plausible mirror: it is an aggregate matching-flow question the authors already formulate and compute.

This case covers the fluid upper-bound computation for first-best throughput. It does not mirror the paper’s main strategic comparisons between FR, RND, and FRfb, because the LP does not model agents’ queue choices or equilibrium behavior. Nor does it show that a discrete hardness result disappears: the paper gives no corresponding hardness anchor.

The soft spot is that this LP is an upper bound on the dynamic stochastic system, not a proved exact fluid limit for it. That leaves two natural follow-up questions: under what high-volume assumptions does the bound become asymptotically exact, and can a continuous model retain strategic queue choice and equilibrium while remaining computationally tractable? The authors would recognize the first question’s starting point—their own computed LP—but whether they would accept either as a mirror of the paper’s central incentive-design problem is less certain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s single anchor repeats the paper’s Fluid LP. The proposed “Continuous First-Best Throughput Bound” has the same variables \(x_{ij},y_i\), constraints, and objective as the appendix’s LP. The paper already formulates and solves that LP as a throughput upper bound, then uses its dual values to construct a priority heuristic. Calling the flow variables “mass” does not create a new population-continuization question: the LP models matching rates, while the paper’s stochastic system still has discrete queue states and strategic queue choices. Its polynomial solvability follows from standard linear programming; that is an inference about an optimization problem the paper has already written down, not a new mirror.

One could ask whether the Fluid LP becomes exact when arrival rates scale with a large population. That is a sensible follow-up, but it asks for a fluid-limit or approximation result about the queueing process, not a new computational problem over a continuous society. A more ambitious rescue would keep queue choice and equilibrium in a high-volume limit, then ask how to compute or optimize throughput at equilibrium. That could be worthwhile, but it is a different mirror: the paper’s LP does not model those strategic dynamics, and optimizing over general state-dependent policies would need a more precise finite computational formulation.

That is where the negative case is weak. The paper’s platform setting has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many drivers can share compatibility and patience types, and the objective is aggregate throughput rather than who gets matched. A mean-field strategic model could preserve those features and pose a real computational question. I can reject the proposed LP mirror as already formulated and used in the paper, but I cannot honestly rule out that stronger strategic mirror.
