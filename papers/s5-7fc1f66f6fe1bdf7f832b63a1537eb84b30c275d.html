What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 concern equilibrium convergence in the static and infinite-horizon games; the paper's other results likewise analyze equilibria and bargaining outcomes rather than computation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest positive case under the programme’s anchor requirement. The paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The nearest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. Theorem 1 says that, for a regular sequence of arbitration initiation probabilities, any sequence of pure-strategy Nash equilibria in the static random-settlement demand game converges to the Nash solution when \(q=\tfrac12\), and to the generalized Nash solution with weight \(q\) otherwise. Theorem 2 gives the corresponding convergence for stationary equilibria in the infinite-horizon game, when the transformed probabilities \(g_n=p_n/[1-(1-p_n)\delta]\) are regular. These are equilibrium-convergence results, not computational-complexity results.

A large population of similar disputes might motivate a separate aggregate model, with dispute environments as types and mass representing their shares. But that would turn the paper’s two-player equilibrium question into a question about a population of disputes; it would not mirror a named computational problem proved here. Its decisions and objective would need new choices by the modeller, so I don’t consider it a sound anchor for a continuous mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offers no affirmative anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are the nearest candidates, but neither asserts a computational result: they characterize limits of equilibria under regularity conditions on the arbitration probabilities. Theorem 2 reduces the dynamic case to the static one using \(g_n=p_n/[1-(1-p_n)\delta]\). Asking an algorithm to compute those limits would mostly mean computing the Nash or generalized Nash solution of \(S\). That is a question about a continuous utility-outcome set, not a continuous society. Asking instead about finite-\(n\) equilibria or checking the regularity conditions would introduce computational problems the paper does not formulate or analyze.

The population axis is hard to supply without changing what the model is about. There are exactly two strategic actors, each choosing a demand; their roles and the arbiter’s bias \(q\) distinguish them. A plausible upgrade might treat a union and an employer association as large cohorts, with member types shaping their aggregate bargaining set. But if each side still makes one demand, the type distribution is only an input used to construct \(S\); the paper’s convergence theorem then applies to the resulting two-player problem. If members make separate demands or bargain over individual payoffs, the strategic game itself has changed. Either way, the theorem has not become a population-level computational result.

A distribution over many disputes is another possible framing, but it makes disputes the types, not people within a society bargaining over a common outcome. With independent disputes, the aggregate calculation decomposes into the separate two-player problems. Coupling them through shared arbitration capacity or other cross-dispute constraints might create a computational question, but that would add a new mechanism rather than continuize this paper’s model.

So the negative case is strongest at the level of the paper’s contribution: it supplies no computational problem for ChoCo to mirror, and its two nearest theorems remain equilibrium-convergence results even under the most plausible cohort interpretation. This is not proof that no useful computational extension could be built around bargaining sets generated from population data. That possibility is the weak point in a universal “no scenario” claim; it would be a new research direction, though, rather than a continuous mirror of the results presented here.
