The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(c\), ranking types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational thresholds \(\alpha_j\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t X_j(t)=\alpha_j\) for every \(j\ne c\), where \(X_j(t)=1\) exactly when type \(t\) ranks \(c\) above \(j\), compute the probability \(\Pr[G_j>0\ \forall j\ne c]\) to additive error \(\varepsilon\) with success probability at least \(1-\delta\), where \(G\) is Gaussian with covariance \(\Sigma_{jk}=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t X_j(t)X_k(t)-\alpha_j\alpha_k\).

The model it lives in

A critical high-multiplicity electorate has ranking types carrying masses \(\mu\), with \(\mu\) exactly at the pairwise victory thresholds. Model infinitesimal sampling uncertainty by a zero-sum Gaussian perturbation of the type masses with covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(\mu)-\mu\mu^{\mathsf T}\); ask for the probability that the perturbed society makes \(c\) beat every opponent.

The objection that survived

The nontrivial probability comes from Gaussian sampling fluctuations inherited from finite independent voters; if a continuous society must determine the outcome without a sampling-noise layer, this becomes an out-of-scope noise question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s critical large-electorate probability for a given candidate, including the Impartial Culture limit in Theorem 4; it leaves the subcritical and mixed rates, supercritical Mallows analysis, higher-order corrections such as Theorem 10, and the analytic-combinatorics machinery unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact-computation complexity of the Gaussian orthant probability as \(m\) and \(\tau\) vary?
  • Can additive approximation guarantees exploit ranking structure beyond sampling Gaussian perturbations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror I can defend is a critical large-electorate one. The closest named result is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: when every saddle-point coordinate is critical, \(\zeta_j=1\), the probability that candidate \(m\) is an \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\)-winner converges to
\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{m-1}\det(\mathcal H_K(\boldsymbol{\tau}))}} \int_{(0,+\infty)^{m-1}} e^{-\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}\mathcal H_K(\boldsymbol{\tau})^{-1}\mathbf u/2}\,d\mathbf u. \]
Here \(\mathcal H_K(\boldsymbol{\tau})\) is the Hessian of the cumulant generating function at the log saddle point. In this critical case, that integral is the probability that a centered Gaussian with this covariance lies in the positive orthant. Theorem 3 is a strong anchor for a continuous *probability* question, but it is not a computational-complexity result. The paper has no numbered theorem asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a comparable complexity class, so it supplies no anchor that meets the assignment’s strict anchor requirement.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Call it Critical Gaussian Winner Probability. An instance gives candidates \(C\), a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, a target candidate \(c\), and rational thresholds \(\alpha_j\). For each opponent \(j\), let \(X_j(t)=1\) if type \(t\) ranks \(c\) above \(j\), and \(0\) otherwise. Restrict the main problem to critical instances satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_tX_j(t)=\alpha_j\) for every \(j\). The society is the type-mass vector \(\mu\). Its sampling fluctuation is a Gaussian perturbation of that vector on the zero-sum tangent space, with covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(\mu)-\mu\mu^{\mathsf T}\). The question is to compute the probability that this perturbation makes \(c\) beat every opponent. Equivalently, output
\[ \Pr[G_j>0\ \text{for every }j],\qquad G\sim N(0,\Sigma),\qquad \Sigma_{jk}=\sum_t\mu_tX_j(t)X_k(t)-\alpha_j\alpha_k. \]
A solution is the exact probability, or an estimate within a specified additive error. There is no intervention variable or optimization objective in the paper’s problem; the decision event is whether the perturbed mass vector satisfies all the pairwise thresholds.

The regime is a large survey or election drawn independently from a stable preference culture. A complete ranking is a type, and \(n\) respondents share one of the \(\tau\) types; with \(m\) fixed, \(\tau\le m!\) stays fixed while \(n\) grows. The masses \(\mu_t\) describe the limiting composition. At critical thresholds, the deterministic composition sits exactly on the winning boundary, so the infinitesimal Gaussian fluctuations determine the limiting probability. That is why this mirror preserves the paper’s question: the paper itself studies that large-\(n\) probability limit, and Theorem 3 gives its Gaussian form. It is a narrow mirror of the critical limit, not of the paper’s finite-\(n\) parity corrections or its subcritical and supercritical rate formulas.

I would expect additive approximation to be tractable when \(m\) is fixed. With variable \(m\), exact evaluation of the growing-dimensional orthant probability could be continuum-specifically hard; an approximation algorithm might still be tractable. Those are prover questions, not results established by this paper. Useful follow-ups include the dependence on \(m,\tau\), and input bit length; the complexity of exact versus additive approximation; and whether the finite-\(n\) correction terms can be incorporated into a continuous mass model.

The soft spot is that the paper’s \(p_r\) is a probability law for independently sampled voters, not a realized continuum society. The mirror makes the limiting type masses the society, but retains Gaussian sampling noise to preserve the nontrivial critical probability. A referee could reasonably view that as continuing the noise model rather than the population. My positive case is therefore plausible and closely aligned with Theorem 3, but it does not satisfy the requested named-complexity-anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s sole anchor is Theorem 3, but it gives an asymptotic probability formula, not a computational result: it asserts no complexity bound or algorithm for a computational social-choice problem. Theorem 4 and Theorem 10 likewise give limiting probabilities and correction terms. The paper’s runtimes describe ways to evaluate those formulas; they do not establish a complexity landscape to continue.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity regime here: many independent voters drawn from a finite set of ranking types. That supports the *possibility* of a continuous model. The difficulty is that the paper’s input is a culture—a probability distribution from which discrete voter profiles are sampled. In a deterministic continuous society with masses \(\mu_t\), pairwise support shares are fixed, so whether a candidate clears each threshold is deterministic. At a critical threshold, the strict winner event simply fails. The nontrivial Gaussian orthant probability returns only when we retain sampling fluctuations around \(\mu\), which makes the proposed mirror a noise model over sampled profiles, not a computation on the continuous society itself.

The strongest computational reformulation—given rational type masses, compute the critical Gaussian orthant probability—does not rescue the anchor. The covariance matrix follows directly from the masses in \(O(\tau m^2)\) operations. For additive error, sample Gaussian vectors and test their signs; this gives a randomized approximation in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the inverse error tolerance. Exact evaluation would need a finite-precision output specification, and asking for relative error on rare events changes the task into a rare-event probability problem. Neither supplies the missing complexity result in the paper.

One could instead ask how much mass must move to create or destroy a Condorcet winner. That is a plausible continuous robustness problem, but it replaces the paper’s probability question with an intervention objective. It would be a new project attached to the same winner condition, not a mirror of one of these theorems.

So the negative case is strongest against the proposed critical-probability mirror: its continuous population disappears when the sampling noise is removed, and its additive computational version is straightforward. The universal claim is less secure: high multiplicity is natural here, and the paper’s future-work discussion could motivate separate continuous intervention questions. Those possibilities do not make the proponent’s named anchor a computational result.
