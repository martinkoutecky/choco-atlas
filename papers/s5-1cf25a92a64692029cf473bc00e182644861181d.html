The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not green this paper on the basis of the proposed mirror. It states no theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Its linear programs and tables calculate outcomes for one roof-selection case with three stakeholders and five alternatives; they do not establish an algorithmic result for a defined input family. The cooperative-game results it cites are prior work, not results proved here.

The strongest proposed extension is a community-wide decision, with stakeholder types defined by role, preferences, and payoff data and mass representing each type’s share. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity model. But the paper’s decision makers are role-bearing participants in one project: their judgments, coalition membership, and individual payoffs determine the negotiation. Repeating a role changes who holds decision power and how payoffs are allocated. Treating residents as a population instead would require a new account of how their masses determine AHP judgments, coalitions, and payoff constraints. Applying the model across many projects would likewise need some shared constraint or interaction to make it one problem rather than many separate roof choices. These may be worthwhile research directions, but they are not continuous mirrors of a computational result established in this paper.

The negative case is strongest on that distinction and weak as a universal claim. The paper itself recommends studying unlimited decision makers and a whole community, so a carefully specified continuous cooperative-game model could be worth investigating.
