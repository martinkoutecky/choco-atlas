The continuous mirror question

Given \A=[m], \E=2^{\A}, an unknown symmetric threshold function \tau:\M_k\times 2^{\E}\to\{0,\dots,k+1\}, and oracle access to \X_\tau^\infty(\mu)=\{W\in\M_k:\mu(G)<\tau(W,G)/k\ \forall G\subseteq\E\} for societies \mu\in\Delta(\E), determine the minimum number of queries needed to output a hypothesis with error at most \varepsilon and confidence at least \1-\delta, and characterize that query complexity via \operatorname{Ndim}.

The model it lives in

Approval types \E=2^{\A} form cohorts with mass \mu\in\Delta(\E); committees \W\in\M_k remain indivisible; the unknown object is a symmetric \tau, and each query returns the full committee set \X_\tau^\infty(\mu). The objective is PAC query complexity.

The objection that survived

The query formulation leaves the representation of unrestricted \tau, oracle-evaluation cost, and efficient hypothesis construction unspecified, so it establishes query complexity rather than an algorithmic complexity landscape.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 10 and 13: symmetric-GST and approximate-GST learning over approval-type masses. It leaves the structural linearity results and the likelihood theorems without a continuous intervention mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a learner matching the \operatorname{Ndim} bounds be implemented in polynomial time when \tau is succinctly represented?
  • What is the complexity of evaluating \X_\tau^\infty(\mu) for standard axioms such as CORE or EJR?
  • How do margin assumptions around \mu(G)=\tau(W,G)/k affect finite-query guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is conditional: the paper has no named NP-hardness, P, FPT, or runtime-complexity theorem. Its strongest computational results are sample-complexity theorems. If those count as admissible anchors, the following is a credible positive mirror; under a stricter anchor rule, no compliant positive case exists.

My lead mirror is *Continuous Symmetric-GST Axiom Learning*, anchored in Theorem 10, proved in this paper.

Let \(A=[m]\) be the candidates, \(M_k\) the integral \(k\)-committees, and \(E=2^A\) the approval types. A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(E)\), where \(\mu_R\) is the fraction of the population with approval set \(R\). For \(G\subseteq E\), write \(\mu(G)=\sum_{R\in G}\mu_R\).

A group-satisfaction-threshold function is \(\tau:M_k\times 2^E\to\{0,\ldots,k+1\}\). Define the continuous GST axiom

\[ X_\tau^\infty(\mu) = \left\{ W\in M_k: \mu(G)<\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k} \text{ for every }G\subseteq E \right\}. \]

The threshold value \(k+1\) represents a group that is never a valid deviation. The axiom is symmetric when \(\tau(W,G)=\tau(\sigma(W),\sigma(G))\) for every candidate permutation \(\sigma\).

The problem is this: an unknown symmetric GST axiom \(X_\tau^\infty\) is queried on societies \(\mu\) drawn from an unknown distribution \(\Pi\). The oracle returns \(X_\tau^\infty(\mu)\). After \(q\) labelled population queries, output a hypothesis \(h\) such that

\[ \Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}\bigl[h(\mu)\ne X_\tau^\infty(\mu)\bigr]\le\epsilon \]

with confidence at least \(1-\delta\). The objective is to minimize \(q\), or equivalently to determine the Natarajan dimension and then design an efficient learner.

This is a genuine population mirror. The many agents are participants in a large consultation or platform election who fall into repeated approval cohorts: two agents with the same approval set are identical for every object in the paper. The regime is \(n\gg q\), where \(q=|E|=2^m\) is the number of possible approval types, and population mass—not the number of named participants—is the input quantity. Committees remain indivisible \(k\)-subsets; only the population is continuous.

Theorem 10 states, for the class \(F_{sGST}\) of symmetric GST axioms, that

\[ \frac{1}{m!} \binom{2^m}{\lfloor 2^{m-1}\rfloor} \le \operatorname{Ndim}(F_{sGST}) \le 2^{2^m}\log(k+2), \]

and hence the lower bound is of order

\[ \Omega\!\left( \frac{2^{2^m}}{m!\,2^{m/2}} \right). \]

Thus learning an unrestricted symmetric proportionality axiom requires doubly exponentially many population queries in \(m\), up to the displayed factors. The theorem is proved in the paper, not merely cited.

The result transfers cleanly. For every rational society \(\mu\), choose a denominator \(N\) and create \(N\mu_R\) voters of type \(R\). Then

\[ \mu(G)<\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{R\in G}N\mu_R < \frac{\tau(W,G)}{k}N, \]

which is exactly the paper’s histogram inequality. The lower-bound witnesses in Theorem 10 are already rational uniform distributions over subsets of \(E\), so the lower bound survives the continuous model. The upper bound also survives because the same finite collection of hyperplane sign patterns controls the real simplex.

I would expect this mirror to be a Class-B-like result: the information-theoretic difficulty does not disappear when the population becomes continuous. The combinatorics live in the space of approval types and threshold functions, not in the identities of individual voters. The further computational questions are whether consistent learning can be implemented efficiently, whether compact representations of \(\tau\) reduce the bound, and how a margin condition—distance of \(\mu\) from the hyperplanes \(\mu(G)=\tau(W,G)/k\)—changes finite-population sample requirements.

A second, narrower mirror is *Continuous Approximate-GST Learning*, anchored in Theorem 13, also proved in the paper. Fix a GST axiom \(\tau\), but allow an unknown approximation parameter \(\beta\ge0\). Define

\[ X_{\tau,\beta}^\infty(\mu) = \left\{ W\in M_k: \mu(G) < \beta\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k} \text{ for every }G \text{ with }\tau(W,G)\ne k+1 \right\}. \]

Here \(\beta<1\) makes the axiom stricter and \(\beta>1\) makes it weaker. Given population queries \(\mu\) and oracle-labelled committees, the task is to learn the unknown \(\beta\), or equivalently the mapping \(X_{\tau,\beta}^\infty\), with PAC error at most \(\epsilon\).

Theorem 13 gives

\[ \operatorname{Ndim}(F_{\approx GST_\tau}) \le 4\log\!\left(\sum_{W\in M_k}|PD_\tau(W)|\right) + 4\log(8e), \]

and, for symmetric GST axioms,

\[ \operatorname{Ndim}(F_{\approx GST_\tau}) \le 4\log\!\left(|PD_\tau(\{1,\ldots,k\})|\right) + 4\log(8e). \]

The paper’s Corollary 2 specializes this to \(X\in\{\mathrm{CORE},\mathrm{JR},\mathrm{PJR},\mathrm{EJR},\mathrm{PJR+},\mathrm{EJR+},\mathrm{FJR}\}\):

\[ \operatorname{Ndim}(F_{\approx X}) \le 4\bigl(m+k+\log(8e)\bigr). \]

This mirror is expected to be Class A in the explicit-type regime. The relevant conditions are linear inequalities in the masses \(\mu_R\), and for the concrete axioms the potential deviating groups can be enumerated in terms of \(m\), \(k\), and the explicit type space. The natural next theorem would be an actual polynomial-time learner or exact interval algorithm for \(\beta\), rather than only the sample bound.

The authors would recognize both mirrors as their problems: the paper already defines mappings through histograms, proves that GST axioms are linear, and repeatedly reasons over fractional profiles. The continuous version simply makes the normalized histogram \(\mu\) the society itself and measures learning effort in population queries rather than in expanded voter records.

The weakest point is also clear. Mathematically, this is close to a normalization of the paper’s existing histogram model. The paper does not prove an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or approximation theorem for a discrete problem whose difficulty is then altered by continuization. Moreover, Natarajan dimension measures sample complexity, not running time. A strict ChoCo referee could therefore say that the paper supplies a plausible continuous-population reinterpretation but no qualifying computational anchor. The positive case survives only under the broader view that sample-complexity bounds are computational results—and it should be presented with that qualification rather than as a conventional complexity-landscape contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s gate: this paper has no named theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorems 1–5 are structural characterizations, Theorems 10 and 13 are PAC sample-complexity bounds, and Theorems 14–19 are likelihood results. None gives a runtime, an exact or approximation algorithm, parameterized complexity, or hardness result for a problem whose input is a society. The proponent’s proposed “next theorem” would therefore be new work, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

Even granting that sample complexity counts as computational, Theorem 10 does not produce a population-complexity phenomenon. Let \(h=\operatorname{Hist}(P)\) and \(\mu=h/n\). Every GST constraint has the form

\[ \left(\mathbf{w}_G-\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k}\mathbf{1}\right)\cdot h<0, \]

and therefore exactly equivalently

\[ \left(\mathbf{w}_G-\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k}\mathbf{1}\right)\cdot \mu<0. \]

Thus \(X_\tau(P)=X_\tau^\infty(\mu)\) for every finite profile. The paper’s theorem already ranges over arbitrary profile sizes and its lower-bound witnesses are rational histograms. Normalizing the histogram does not relax an integer program, remove population granularity, or introduce a new feasible region; it merely quotients out the irrelevant common scale.

That is not an objection that the answer happens to be unchanged. The deeper issue is that the named theorem contains no discrete computational task whose difficulty is altered by continuization. Its lower bound is driven by the enormous freedom to choose an arbitrary threshold function \(\tau\), indexed by subsets of the approval-type space. The same lower bound would arise for any normalized feature-vector domain. The agents’ multiplicity is mathematically incidental to the theorem, not the source of its difficulty.

The more favorable version—learning an unknown continuous GST axiom from queries \(\mu\)—does not repair this. A query returns the answer to an entire axiom involving potentially \(2^{2^m}\) groups. The query model hides the cost of computing that answer and says nothing about the representation of \(\tau\), the encoding of \(\mu\), or the time needed to produce the committee set. If \(\tau\) is unrestricted, its description is already enormous and the lower bound is largely an encoding or concept-class-size phenomenon. If \(\tau\) is given succinctly, Theorem 10 no longer supplies the claimed lower bound. Either way, the result does not chart the computational landscape of continuous societies.

Theorem 13 is weaker still. Its approximate-GST family only varies a scalar \(\beta\), and the same normalization identity applies:

\[ \left(\mathbf{w}_G-\beta\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k}\mathbf{1}\right)\cdot h<0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \left(\mathbf{w}_G-\beta\frac{\tau(W,G)}{k}\mathbf{1}\right)\cdot \mu<0. \]

The theorem gives an information-theoretic dimension upper bound, not an efficient learner. For a fixed explicit \(\tau\), one could ask for an algorithm to infer feasible intervals for \(\beta\), but that is a new algorithmic problem. For a succinct standard axiom such as JR or EJR, the generic proof does not establish polynomial-time access to all potential deviating groups. The continuous notation contributes no mass-transfer, optimization, or approximation issue.

The approval-cohort story itself is perfectly legitimate. This paper does have a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation: voters with the same approval set are indistinguishable, and \(\mu\) is meaningful. I would not object that approval voters lack multiplicity, nor that fractional committee membership is being introduced. The problem is narrower: the paper’s results already operate on anonymous histograms, and its stated contributions do not exploit a discrete-versus-continuous population distinction.

A genuine continuous mirror could be invented by adding a new problem—for example, minimum-mass perturbation of \(\mu\) to make a committee satisfy EJR, or continuous campaigning that transfers approval mass. Such a problem might be worthwhile, but it would be a new intervention problem, not a mirror of Theorem 10 or Theorem 13. On the anchors actually offered, the negative case is therefore strong: they establish that the paper can be rewritten in population notation, not that it contains a computational question whose continuization is a meaningful ChoCo contribution.

The honest limitation is that this is not a proof that no researcher could ever formulate an interesting continuous problem from the paper. If ChoCo accepts PAC query complexity as sufficient and treats normalized histograms as a genuine population model, the positive case survives. Under the programme’s stated emphasis on computational complexity and algorithms for distributional societies, however, neither proposed anchor clears the gate.
