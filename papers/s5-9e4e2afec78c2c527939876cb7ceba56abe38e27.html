The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many valuation types \(t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\) for good types \(g\), and additive valuations \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{R}_+\), choose \(\lambda_{t,S}\ge 0\) for \(S\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}\lambda_{t,S}=q_g\), and compute an allocation maximizing \(W_p(\lambda)=\left(\sum_{t,S}\lambda_{t,S}v_t(S)^p\right)^{1/p}\) for fixed \(p\in(0,1)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fair-division model with finite good types \(G\), valuation types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), per-capita supplies \(q\), integral per-agent bundles \(S\subseteq G\), aggregate allocation variables \(\lambda_{t,S}\), and \(p\)-mean objective \(W_p(\lambda)\).

The objection that survived

The main concern is whether forbidding one household from receiving two copies of a good type is sufficiently canonical as a high-multiplicity interpretation, rather than an added feasibility restriction.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Partition-based hardness of TheoremMNlower and the XOS value-query lower bound of TheoremQuery; it leaves Theorem 1, Theorem 8, and TheoremMNupper unextended.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuous \(p\)-mean problem remain NP-hard for fixed \(p\in(0,1)\) under rational masses and supplies with the one-copy-per-type constraint?
  • If households may receive repeated copies of a good type, what is the complexity of the resulting multi-copy problem?
  • Can the XOS value-query lower bound be formalized under a type-oracle interface exposing valuation types and their masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s fair-division problem in which goods remain indivisible for every individual agent.

Let \(G=\{g_1,\ldots,g_m\}\) be good types, with per-capita supply \(q_g\). Let \(T\) be a finite set of agent types, with masses \(\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type \(t\) includes its complete valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb R_+\). An allocation is a finite-support collection \(\lambda_{t,S}\ge 0\), where \(\lambda_{t,S}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\), subject to

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t}\sum_{S\ni g}\lambda_{t,S}=q_g \]

for every good type \(g\). Thus \(\lambda\) may be fractional in aggregate, but every unit of population still receives an integral bundle. The continuous \(p\)-mean welfare is

\[ W_p(\lambda)= \left(\sum_{t,S}\lambda_{t,S}v_t(S)^p\right)^{1/p}, \]

with the usual geometric-mean interpretation at \(p=0\) and essential-minimum interpretation at \(p=-\infty\).

This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime. For example, with \(K\) copies of every agent type and \(Kq_g\) copies of every good type, the discrete instance has \(N=K\) times as many agents while the number of distinct agent types remains \(|T|\). The limit \(K\to\infty\) is precisely the mass model above. A plausible setting is a large population of households with a few repeated valuation profiles and a catalogue of product types supplied in fixed per-capita quantities.

My lead anchor is the paper’s Section 6 result printed as TheoremMNlower—the supplied rendering gives the restatable name rather than a visible numeral. It is proved in the paper, by a reduction from Partition. Its full content is: for additive valuations and any fixed \(p\in(0,1)\), computing an allocation whose \(p\)-mean welfare is at least \(1/(m-n+1)\) times optimum, for all \(m,n\), is NP-hard. In particular, when \(m=n\), the required approximation factor is \(1\), so exact optimization is NP-hard.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \mathsf{HM\text{-}pMean\text{-}FD}_{\mathrm{add}}: \]

given rational agent masses, rational per-capita supplies, and additive valuations, compute an optimal population allocation \(\lambda\) maximizing \(W_p(\lambda)\).

The paper’s Partition gadget survives this continuization in a particularly clean form. Given integers \(s_1,\ldots,s_m\) and \(z=\sum_i s_i\), use \(m\) good types with \(q_{g_i}=1/m\). Give mass \(2/m\) to one special valuation type

\[ v(S)=\sum_{g_i\in S}s_i \]

and mass \(1-2/m\) to a zero-valued dummy type. This is the continuum analogue of two special agents and \(m-2\) dummy agents, replicated arbitrarily many times.

For every allocation, the total value delivered to the special population is at most \(z/m\). Since \(x^p\) is strictly concave for \(p\in(0,1)\), Jensen’s inequality gives

\[ W_p(\lambda)\le \left(\frac{2}{m}\right)^{1/p}\frac z2. \]

Equality is possible exactly when every special agent receives an integral bundle of value \(z/2\), with the goods split between two equal-mass subpopulations. Such a bundle exists exactly when the \(s_i\) admit a Partition. Aggregate mass does not make the goods divisible for an individual, so mixing bundles cannot attain equality unless an integral equal-valued bundle already exists.

I therefore expect this continuous problem to be Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the good types and their additive weights, not in the number of named agents. The continuum removes population identity, but it does not remove the integral-bundle constraint that carries the Partition obstruction.

A second, independent anchor is TheoremQuery, also proved in the paper by adapting Dobzinski et al. (2010). Its content is that, for XOS valuations and any \(p\in(-\infty,1]\), finding an allocation with \(p\)-mean welfare at least \(1/n^{1-\varepsilon}\) times optimum requires exponentially many value queries, for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).

Its continuous mirror is the same \(\lambda\)-allocation problem with \(m=r^2\) good types, each supplied at density \(1/r\), and \(r\) agent-type slots of mass \(1/r\). In the first valuation family every slot has the common XOS valuation \(f\); in the second, slot \(i\) has the hidden XOS valuation \(v_i\) from the paper’s construction. Each type can be replicated \(K\) times, so the population has \(Kr\) agents while the mass description still has only \(r\) valuation types.

The two families remain separated by the same welfare gap: the common-\(f\) family has optimum \(O(r^{4\delta})\), whereas the hidden family assigns the partition block \(T_i\) to type \(i\), giving every type value \(r\). Choosing \(\delta<\varepsilon/4\) makes an \(r^{1-\varepsilon}\)-approximation distinguish the families. But the value-oracle responses remain exponentially hard to distinguish. This is again Class B, now in an unconditional oracle sense.

The mirror is therefore not merely “fractional fair division.” Its defining feature is that population mass is continuous while each agent’s allocation remains an integral bundle. That is exactly the paper’s underlying problem, expressed in the high-multiplicity regime. The mirror covers the paper’s two lower-bound results; it does not claim that Theorems 1 or 8 automatically extend to the continuum.

The weakest point is the need to treat recurring good categories with per-capita supplies rather than one-off named objects. If the authors reject that co-modification, the mirror fails. But in a large market—many households, repeated valuation profiles, and \(K\) copies of each product type—it is a natural high-multiplicity interpretation, and the lead hardness result even works with only two agent valuation types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed anchors quietly move the source of combinatorial difficulty from the population to the goods, and therefore do not establish the claimed continuous-population phenomenon.

For TheoremMNlower, the paper’s reduction depends on exactly one copy of each good. With \(m=n\), the two special agents must partition the single multiset \(\{s_1,\ldots,s_m\}\), which is precisely the Partition obstruction. The proposed model with \(q_{g_i}=1/m\), however, is not literally a high-multiplicity copy of that instance unless bundles are allowed to contain multiple copies of the same good type. A finite realization with \(K\) population units contains \(K/m\) copies of every good type.

If repeated copies are allowed, the claimed equivalence with Partition fails. For example, \(s=(1,3,4,7,9,12)\) has total \(36\) and no subset of value \(18\). Yet with two copies of every good type, the four bundles

\[ \{9,9\},\qquad \{4,7,7\},\qquad \{3,3,12\},\qquad \{1,1,4,12\} \]

all have value \(18\), and together use exactly two copies of every good. Thus the replicated instance attains the Jensen equality although the original Partition instance is a no-instance. The paper’s reduction has not survived continuization; it has become a different multi-copy bin-packing problem.

The proponent can preserve the reduction by imposing an additional rule that each individual may receive at most one copy of each good type. But that is no longer the ordinary high-multiplicity version of the paper’s fair-division problem. It is a new categorical-allocation model with per-household unit caps. It may be sensible, but its computational interest comes from that added inventory constraint, not from replacing named agents by population mass. The proposed anchor therefore does not yet show that the paper itself supplies a worthwhile continuous mirror; it shows that one can design a related market model in which the original Partition gadget is artificially protected.

The query lower bound is weaker still as a population argument. TheoremQuery is an information lower bound caused by the hidden representation of an individual XOS valuation. The construction remains hard with one agent, or with a fixed finite collection of valuation types. Replicating those types \(K\) times changes no oracle response and contributes no new continuous structure. If valuations are supplied explicitly, the value-query lower bound is simply inapplicable; if they are supplied by oracles, the lower bound is exactly the paper’s original oracle lower bound with population labels duplicated. Either way, it does not test a computational question created by a continuous society.

So the best negative verdict is that the proposed mirrors are under-specified at the crucial point: whether good types are one-per-household categories or repeated copies of the paper’s goods. Under the former interpretation, they add a new feasibility restriction; under the latter, the Partition and XOS arguments no longer carry over as claimed. The query anchor also fails to make population multiplicity do any computational work.

That said, this is not an airtight universal rejection. A carefully specified market with repeated valuation types, per-capita supplies, and an explicit one-copy-per-category constraint is a legitimate high-multiplicity problem, and the Partition argument can then be made valid. Under the programme’s rules, that may count as a worthwhile Class B mirror. Thus I can defeat the proponent’s particular formulation and its claimed transfer, but I cannot honestly claim that no plausible continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
