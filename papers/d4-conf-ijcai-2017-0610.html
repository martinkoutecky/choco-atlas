What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the large-market incentive results, Theorems 7 and 8; it leaves the impossibility results and Nash-welfare characterizations untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: this paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or approximation theorem. Theorems 1–4 are impossibility results, Theorems 5–6 are Nash-welfare characterizations, and Theorems 7–8 concern large-market incentives and mechanism existence. Thus it does not supply a clean ChoCo computational anchor. Still, Theorem 7 gives a credible population-continuation starting point, with Theorem 8 as a weaker constructive companion.

The natural regime is a large population of agents whose valuations are identical up to a small number of piecewise-constant types. A type \(t\) is a density function \(f_t:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), constant on each interval of a common refinement \(X_1,\ldots,X_m\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of agents of type \(t\). This is plausible for many households, clients, or institutions valuing a fixed collection of geographic or service regions in the same coarse way, with \(\tau=|T|\) much smaller than the number of agents. The cake remains the paper’s divisible cake; the continuization is of the population, not of the outcome space.

My lead anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. In the paper’s connected-piece large-market model, an original market with \(n\) agents is replicated \(k\) times, producing \(nk\) agents. For any envy-free mechanism \(M\), if all agents report truthfully, the utility gain available to any one agent from misreporting tends to zero as \(k\to\infty\). Equivalently, if \(A\) is the truthful allocation and \(A'\) is the allocation after a best report by one agent, then for each relevant type \(i\),
\[ \frac{v_i(A'_{i,1})}{v_i(A_{i,1})}\longrightarrow 1. \]
The theorem is proved here, not merely cited.

A precise continuous problem suggested by it is Vanishing-Mass Manipulation in Cake Cutting. An instance consists of the type set \(T\), the masses \(\mu\), the common segment partition, and a succinct deterministic envy-free rule \(M\) defined for every finite realization whose type counts are proportional to \(\mu\). For a realization with \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), let \(u_t^N(g)\) be the value, under the true density \(f_t\), obtained by one type-\(t\) agent who reports \(g\), and let \(u_t^N(f_t)\) be its truthful value. Define
\[ G_N(M,\mu)= \max_{t,g} \max\!\left\{0,\frac{u_t^N(g)}{u_t^N(f_t)}-1\right\}. \]
The task is to compute the smallest \(K(\varepsilon)\) such that
\[ G_N(M,\mu)\le \varepsilon \]
for every valid \(N\ge K(\varepsilon)\), or certify that no such \(K\) exists. Its continuous form asks whether
\[ \limsup_{N\to\infty}G_N(M,\mu)=0. \]
The action is a report by a vanishing-mass agent; the objective is the worst relative manipulation gain, or equivalently the smallest population threshold guaranteeing \(\varepsilon\)-Nash behavior. A single agent in a realization of size \(N\) corresponds to a mass perturbation of \(1/N\).

This is recognisably the authors’ question: it preserves piecewise-constant valuations, envy-freeness, strategic misreporting, and connected pieces, while replacing a named electorate by its type distribution. Theorem 7 predicts Class A behaviour for natural segment-respecting mechanisms, although computing an explicit rate \(K(\varepsilon)\) is not done in the paper. Further questions include whether the rate can be bounded polynomially in \(m\), \(\tau\), the encoding length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), and whether the conclusion remains uniform over all deterministic envy-free mechanisms.

The supporting anchor is Theorem 8, also proved here. In the unrestricted allocation model, for every \(k\ge 2\), the mechanism that allocates each segment \(X_t\) evenly among all \(nk\) agents, in any deterministic fashion, is both envy-free and truthful. The reason is that every agent receives the same share of every segment. A misreport may introduce additional breakpoints, but cannot remove the breakpoints supplied by the truthful replicas, so it cannot improve the agent’s allocation. The paper also observes that the same idea works when each segment is allocated evenly only among agents who value it positively.

Its continuous counterpart is Segment-Equalizing Continuum Cake Allocation. Given \((T,\mu)\) and the common refinement \(X_1,\ldots,X_m\), output a measurable type-mass allocation \(q_t(x)\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{t\in T}q_t(x)=1 \]
and, on every segment \(X_j\),
\[ q_t(x)=\mu_t. \]
Thus each unit of population mass receives the same share of every segment; a deterministic finite implementation can be obtained by applying the paper’s segment-by-segment equalization rule to any denominator-clearing realization of \(\mu\). The question is to construct such a rule and verify envy-freeness and dominant truthfulness for every piecewise-constant report profile. This is an immediate Class A candidate, with construction and verification linear in the number of types and segments. A more interesting extension asks for the non-wasteful version, where \(X_j\) is divided only among types with positive density on \(X_j\), or for the minimum-waste truthful envy-free rule under additional efficiency constraints.

The case is plausible because the paper itself establishes that high multiplicity is meaningful for cake cutting: its entire Section 4 studies replicated populations, and Theorem 7 is explicitly a convergence result in that regime. Existing large-market work therefore supports, rather than collides with, the proposed mirror. The continuous programme would add the missing computational layer: compressed type-distribution input, explicit manipulation rates, finite descriptions of mechanisms, and complexity as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and bit length.

The weakness is substantial. Theorem 7 is an asymptotic incentive theorem, not a computational-complexity theorem, and Theorem 8 is a simple mechanism construction rather than a stated polynomial-time classification. Moreover, exact truthfulness for an atomless individual can become vacuous unless the problem tracks vanishing but positive mass deviations, as above. The connected-piece limit is also technically delicate because individual pieces shrink to zero. Consequently, this is a defensible positive mirror of the paper’s large-market contribution, but not a strong ChoCo case under the strict anchor rule. I would not use Theorems 1–4 as anchors: their finite-agent impossibilities may disappear when unilateral influence vanishes, and the paper gives no computational result showing what happens in that continuum limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational anchor in the ChoCo sense. Theorems 1–4 are impossibility results about mechanism properties, Theorems 5–6 are Nash-welfare characterizations, and Theorems 7–8 concern asymptotic incentives and mechanism existence. None states the complexity of a computational problem. Thus the proposed mirrors must first invent a computational problem before they can continuize anything.

Theorem 7 is the strongest possible anchor, but its content is inherently about a sequence of finite, identity-bearing economies. At population size \(N\), a deviating agent has mass \(1/N\), a named allocation, and positive utility. In the proposed society \(\mu\), replacing one type-\(t\) report \(f_t\) by \(g\) leaves \(\mu\) exactly unchanged. A rule whose input is only \(\mu\) therefore cannot react to the deviation. If individual bundles are retained literally, every agent receives measure-zero cake and utility, so the ratio in the proposed \(G_N\) becomes \(0/0\). If one replaces individual utility by type-average or expected utility, truthfulness follows from the agent having no aggregate influence; it is no longer the strategic question in Theorem 7.

The proposed \(G_N\) and \(K(\varepsilon)\) avoid this only by retaining the finite scale \(N\) and quantifying over a whole sequence of finite mechanisms. That is a legitimate asymptotic mechanism-analysis problem, but not a continuous-society problem. Moreover, \(K(\varepsilon)\) is not determined by \(\mu\): it depends on the particular finite mechanism \(M\), including how \(M\) treats agent identities and reports. The paper allows arbitrary envy-free mechanisms, whereas a continuum rule requires anonymity and a representation as a map on type distributions—additional modelling assumptions absent from the theorem. Computing a threshold for an arbitrary succinct \(M\) is therefore a new verification problem, not the high-multiplicity relaxation of a named computational task.

Theorem 8 fares worse. The proposed \(q_t(x)=\mu_t\) records only the aggregate fraction of each cake segment assigned to type \(t\). It does not specify a deterministic allocation to the continuum of individual agents of that type. A literal partition gives each individual zero cake; an expected allocation or lottery changes the paper’s deterministic model; scaling the cake supply with population produces a different economy. Even accepting \(q\) as legitimate aggregate bookkeeping, an individual deviation has no effect on it, so the claimed truthfulness is vacuous. A positive-mass deviation would be nontrivial, but that is a coalition-manipulation problem not treated by Theorem 8.

The same obstruction defeats stronger attempts based on the rest of the paper. Envy-freeness and Nash social welfare are defined through named agents’ utilities: \(v_i(A_i)\ge v_i(A_j)\) and \(\prod_i v_i(A_i)\). In a continuum, the product is undefined and the individual comparisons disappear. One could replace the product by \(\int \log u_t\,d\mu_t\), but that is a new type-level welfare objective. Likewise, the impossibility proofs in Theorems 1–4 rely on one identified agent changing their report while the other identified agents remain fixed. In a continuum that agent is null; preserving the argument requires a positive-mass coalition and hence a different incentive notion.

This is not an objection that high multiplicity is implausible. Section 4 is genuine evidence that replicated cake-cutting markets are sensible, and I would not treat existing large-market work as a collision. The problem is that replication here supports an asymptotic theorem about finite agents; it does not leave behind a nontrivial computational problem over the limiting distribution.

A coalition-based continuum theory of cake-cutting manipulation could certainly be worthwhile independently. But it would require changing the strategic object, the allocation semantics, or the welfare objective. Under ChoCo’s stricter mirror criterion, that is precisely the failure: neither proposed anchor survives as a computational question about a continuous society.
