The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is a scope objection: this paper gives no named result about computational complexity. Proposition 4.1 is the closest anchor, but it proves that certain rules admit profiles where removing losing-candidate ballots changes the committee. Propositions 4.2–4.5 establish fairness properties, and Section 5 reports heuristic searches without a complexity classification. A continuous version of ILVB is therefore an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Optimizing the mass that must be removed to change a committee would be a new computational problem, not a mirror of Proposition 4.1.

That does not support the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The paper’s own profiles aggregate voters by ballot type, and large elections with many repeated truncated ballots are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. A natural computational question would be how much mass must be removed from specified ballot types to change a committee or its party composition. The paper’s empirical search gives that question some motivation, even though it does not establish it as a result to mirror.

So the case against is strong only under the assignment’s anchor requirement: this paper supplies no qualifying computational result. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror is possible; the best candidate is plausible, but would extend the paper’s research question.
