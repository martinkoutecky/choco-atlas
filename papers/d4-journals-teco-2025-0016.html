The continuous mirror question

Given rational signal mass \(q>1/2\), an error threshold \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), and precision \(\xi>0\), consider a homogeneous population with mass \(q\) holding private signal \(1\) and mass \(1-q\) holding signal \(0\). Starting from an infinitesimal signal-seeded announcement that selects opinion \(1\), agents update asynchronously at rate one, follow the majority of announced mass, and break ties using their private signal. Compute, within \(\xi\), the least scaled time \(s\) at which the mass announcing \(1\) reaches \(1-\varepsilon\) and stays above that level.

The model it lives in

A two-type atomless population indexed by private signal, with mass \(\mu_1=q\) and \(\mu_0=1-q\), homogeneous mixing, and public states \(\bot,0,1\). An infinitesimal signal-driven seed selects opinion \(1\); thereafter all unannounced mass adopts \(1\), giving announced-\(1\) mass \(1-e^{-s}\) and threshold time \(s_\varepsilon=\ln(1/\varepsilon)\).

The objection that survived

The deterministic uniform-mixing flow does not represent the sparse random neighborhoods or the exact coupon-collector completion in Theorem 1.1; the proponent acknowledges the completion gap but does not derive the flow from the graph model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 1.1’s high-degree sparse regime at the level of correct-announcement mass. It leaves aside Theorems 1.2 and 1.3, low-degree completion, dense-graph cascades, and the exact finite-population stopping time.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the signal-seeded mean-field trajectory be derived as a limit of \(G(N,p_N)\) when \(\log N\ll Np_N\ll N\)?
  • How does the approximation behave when \(\varepsilon\) shrinks with \(N\), especially near the exact coupon-collector completion time?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper. It says that for fixed \(\delta\in(0,1/10]\), any \(\omega'(n)\to\infty\) with \(\omega'=o(\log n)\), and \(p=o(1)\) with \(p\gg \log n/n\), asynchronous majority dynamics on \(G(n,p)\), with independent private signals correct with probability \(1/2+\delta\), reaches all-correct consensus a.a.s. by \(n(\log n+\omega')\) updates. More precisely, it reaches that state exactly when every vertex has been selected at least once. The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result; this is its strongest relevant algorithmic runtime and outcome theorem.

A narrow continuous mirror is the high-degree, homogeneous limit of that setting. Take a population with two complete types, \(b\in\{0,1\}\), distinguished by private signal: mass \(\mu_1=1/2+\delta\) has signal \(1\), and mass \(\mu_0=1/2-\delta\) has signal \(0\). All agents have the same contact role. Their public state is \(\bot,0,\) or \(1\), and each infinitesimal unit of mass has a rate-one asynchronous update clock. In the mean-field contact limit, an updating agent follows the majority of announced mass; a tie, including the initially empty announced population, is broken by that agent’s private signal.

This regime has a concrete finite-population interpretation: \(N\) agents, expected degree \(d_N=Np_N\), with \(\log N\ll d_N\ll N\). A first cohort of size on the order of \(N/d_N\) can be large enough for its signal bias to concentrate while still being a vanishing fraction of the population. That motivates defining the continuum trajectory by seeding mass \(\eta\) with its private signals and then taking \(\eta\downarrow0\). The seed selects the correct majority; after that, the mass flow moves toward correct announcements.

Call the resulting problem Sparse Majority \(\varepsilon\)-Time. Its instance is rational \(q\in(1/2,1)\), target error \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), and requested additive precision \(\xi>0\). The population has signal masses \(q\) and \(1-q\); its continuous state records the mass of each signal type in each public state. The task is to return, within \(\xi\), the least time \(s\) after which the mass publicly announcing \(1\) stays at least \(1-\varepsilon\). Under the vanishing-seed convention, once the positive signal bias selects opinion \(1\), this mass is \(1-e^{-s}\). Thus the answer is \(s_\varepsilon=\ln(1/\varepsilon)\), computable in polynomial time to the requested precision: Class A for this mirror.

I think the authors could recognize this as their one-block, sparse, high-degree social-learning question in a population limit: it retains private signals, public announcements, asynchronous majority updates, and the correct-consensus objective. It continuizes the population itself; the continuous variable is opinion mass. It does not continuize the outcome space or replace the task with an axiomatic question.

The limitation is substantial: this mirror captures the mass-scale correctness mechanism, not Theorem 1.1’s exact finite-population stopping time. An atomless population has no last unselected individual, so exact unanimity becomes \(\varepsilon\)-consensus. It also washes out the early finite fluctuations that matter in dense graphs. I would therefore leave Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 outside this mirror: rare low-degree vertices drive the former’s slower completion, while the latter’s wrong-consensus cascade can be triggered by a single early announcement. A natural next question is whether a finite-type interaction kernel admits an efficient algorithm to decide which consensus its continuous mass flow reaches, and where that limit stops predicting finite-network behavior.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.1 is a real runtime result, so I would not dismiss it as lacking a named theorem. The problem is that its runtime is a stopping-time statement about a finite random graph, not the complexity of an algorithm that solves a computational task from a population description.

The proposed two-type mirror replaces the graph with uniform mixing. That is a sensible fluid approximation in the sparse, diverging-degree regime, but it drops the graph parameter \(p\) and the random neighborhoods that Theorem 1.1 reasons about. Its \(\varepsilon\)-consensus question asks when an atomless population has announced opinion \(1\) on mass at least \(1-\varepsilon\). It does not represent the theorem’s exact claim that *every* vertex is correct precisely when the last vertex has first been selected. The distinction is not just a tie-breaking convention: fixed positive \(\varepsilon\) discards the vanishing set of late or incorrect vertices; letting \(\varepsilon\) shrink with \(n\) brings finite-population resolution and coupon-collector behavior back into the model.

A stronger mirror could use a type distribution and an interaction kernel, then ask an algorithm to predict the eventual consensus or its probability. But for the paper’s homogeneous \(G(n,p)\) setting, the natural kernel is uniform, and the question reduces to the same mean-field flow. A nonuniform kernel would add community or interaction structure that the paper does not analyze; it may define an interesting computational problem, but it is no longer a mirror of Theorem 1.1. To preserve the sparse-versus-dense distinction and the role of early announcements, one would also need a stochastic microscopic entrance phase: a growing cohort of initial signals in the sparse regime, versus a single influential first announcement in the dense regime. The continuous mass flow would come after that finite-size event, rather than replace it.

So my case against is that the proposed mirror is an analytic fluid-limit question, while making it computationally rich requires adding structure and finite-size randomness that change what is being studied. This is not airtight: a well-designed type-kernel problem could be worthwhile in its own right, and a two-scale limit could formalize the early-seed mechanism. The negative case is strongest on fit: neither construction is yet a computational mirror compelled by the paper’s named result.
