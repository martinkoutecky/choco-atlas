The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), item classes \(G\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), additive values \(v_t(g)\), and a uniform bundle cap \(k\), let \(\mathcal B=\{B\subseteq G:|B|\le k\}\) and define \(\mu_t^\infty=\max_{\rho}\min_{\rho_B>0}v_t(B)\), where \(\rho\) ranges over distributions on \(\mathcal B\) satisfying \(\sum_B\rho_B=1\) and \(\sum_{B\ni g}\rho_B=q_g\). Compute, in time polynomial in the compressed input size, an aggregate allocation \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B\ni g}x_{t,B}=q_g\), and \(x_{t,B}>0\Rightarrow v_t(B)\ge(2/3)\mu_t^\infty\) for every type \(t\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity uniform-matroid allocation model: types \(t\) carry population mass \(\mu_t\), item classes \(g\) carry per-capita supply \(q_g\), and variables \(x_{t,B}\) assign type mass to integral bundles \(B\) with \(|B|\le k\), conserving every type mass and item supply while meeting the \(2/3\)-MMS threshold.

The objection that survived

The finite proof is one-agent-at-a-time, and its type-dependent ordering and recovery do not obviously preserve aggregate item supplies; the compressed configuration formulation may have knapsack-hard pricing.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the goods-side algorithmic results in Theorems 5 and 7, while leaving the existential approximation theorem, MMS existence and non-existence results, structural lemmas, and all chore results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(2/3\)-approximate aggregate allocation be computed in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|G|\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(q\), without expanding the denominator-scaled population?
  • Does the configuration LP admit efficient separation or pricing, or is the compressed problem continuum-specifically hard?
  • How does the paper's \(k\le2\) exact MMS-existence result translate to rational per-capita supply vectors \(q\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity allocation mirror, with Theorem 5 as the lead anchor.

Take a finite set of item classes \(G\), partitioned into categories \(C_1,\ldots,C_\ell\), with per-capita supply \(q_g\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) for each \(g\in G\). A feasible individual bundle is an integral set \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(|B\cap C_h|\le k_h\). Thus \(q_g\) describes how many copies of item class \(g\) exist per unit population, while every individual still receives an indivisible bundle. The feasibility promise is \(\sum_{g\in C_h}q_g\le k_h\).

There is a finite type set \(T\). Type \(t\) means a complete additive valuation vector \(v_t=(v_t(g))_{g\in G}\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of that type. The regime is \(N\to\infty\) with \(|T|\), \(|G|\), and \(\ell\) fixed or small: there are approximately \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), and \(Nq_g\) copies of item class \(g\). This is plausible for a large benefits programme distributing standardized devices, supplies, or course places to many households with a small number of recurring valuation profiles.

Let \(\mathcal B\) be the finite set of feasible integral bundles. An aggregate allocation is a matrix \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{B\in\mathcal B}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni g}x_{t,B}=q_g \]

for every \(g\). The allocation is not a fractional bundle: each unit of mass receives one integral \(B\). Only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

For each type \(t\), define its continuous high-multiplicity MMS by

\[ \mu_t^\infty = \max_{\rho} \min_{\rho_B>0} v_t(B), \]

where \(\rho\) ranges over distributions on \(\mathcal B\) satisfying

\[ \sum_{B}\rho_B=1, \qquad \sum_{B\ni g}\rho_B=q_g. \]

This is exactly the continuum version of partitioning the replicated item supply into feasible bundles and receiving the least valuable bundle. A solution at factor \(\alpha\) must satisfy

\[ x_{t,B}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_t(B)\ge \alpha\mu_t^\infty. \]

Equivalently, the optimization problem is to maximize the worst type’s guaranteed MMS ratio; the anchor-specific tasks below ask for fixed target ratios.

The finite-to-continuous dictionary is direct. For rational \(\mu_t\) and \(q_g\), multiply by a common denominator: \(N\mu_t\) becomes the number of agents of type \(t\), and \(Nq_g\) the number of item copies. Conversely, a rational aggregate allocation scales back to a finite allocation. Thus this is genuinely a high-multiplicity relaxation, not merely a lottery or divisible-goods problem.

The paper has no numbered hardness theorem; its NP-hardness statement about computing an MMS is cited in prose. I therefore use its two strongest numbered algorithmic results.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in the paper: for every goods instance \(I=\langle N,M,V,C\rangle\) with additive valuations and category cardinality constraints, a \((|N|/(2|N|-1))\)-approximate MMS allocation always exists and can be found in polynomial time. The allocation is a partition of all goods into agent bundles, each bundle contains at most \(k_h\) goods from category \(C_h\), and every agent \(i\) receives value at least \((|N|/(2|N|-1))\mu_i\). The continuous mirror is:

Continuous General-Cardinality MMS\(_{1/2}\). Given \((T,\mu,G,q,C,k,v)\) as above, output an aggregate allocation \(x\) satisfying

\[ x_{t,B}>0\Rightarrow v_t(B)\ge \tfrac12\mu_t^\infty \]

for every type \(t\). The factor is honestly \(1/2\), the limit of \(|N|/(2|N|-1)\), rather than the finite-\(N\) expression. The expected classification is Class A: a mass-scaled version of the paper’s ordering, valid-reduction, and bag-filling machinery should batch identical agents instead of processing them one by one. The real algorithmic question is whether those batches can be represented and found in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|G|\), and the bit-lengths of \(\mu\) and \(q\), rather than in the expanded population size. Theorem 5 is stated and proved in the paper [here](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10458-026-09731-1).

A useful independent anchor is Theorem 7, also proved in the paper: for the single-category instance \(I=\langle N,M,V,\langle(C_1,k_1)\rangle\rangle\), a \(2/3\)-approximate MMS allocation always exists and can be found in polynomial time. Here every bundle contains at most \(k_1\) goods, and each agent receives value at least \(2\mu_i/3\).

Its mirror is:

Continuous Uniform-Matroid MMS\(_{2/3}\). Given a type distribution \(\mu\), per-capita item supplies \(q_g\), additive type valuations, and one bundle cap \(k\), output an aggregate allocation \(x\) such that every unit of type-\(t\) mass receives an integral bundle \(B\) with

\[ |B|\le k \quad\text{and}\quad v_t(B)\ge \tfrac23\mu_t^\infty. \]

I expect this also to be Class A, and this is arguably the cleaner mirror: the single-category structure removes cross-category bookkeeping, while the paper’s ordered-instance and bag-filling invariants have natural quantile versions. Further questions are whether the \(2/3\) factor is tight in the continuum, whether exact MMS becomes possible for some supply profiles, and whether the paper’s \(k\le2\) existence result survives as a continuous statement.

The weakest point is that the mirror scales the item supply as well as the population. A referee committed to one-off unique objects could say that repeated item classes define a new allocation problem. I think that objection is weak in the intended regime: standardized seats, devices, or supplies are precisely high-multiplicity goods, and the paper’s cardinality constraints apply unchanged to each individual bundle. More seriously, the paper does not itself prove that its sequential algorithms batch cleanly by type mass. That is a genuine open step. But the proposed problems preserve the paper’s agents, additive valuations, indivisible bundles, cardinality constraints, MMS benchmark, and approximation objective; only the population is continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has not actually carried either theorem into a polynomial-time high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s algorithms are polynomial in the expanded numbers of agents and items. If \(\mu\) and \(q\) are binary-encoded, clearing denominators can create exponentially many agents and item copies. “Batching identical agents” is therefore the central theorem still missing, not a consequence of Theorem 5.

The ordered-instance reduction and bag-filling algorithm do not batch transparently. They repeatedly remove one named agent and one integral bundle, update the remaining-agent count \(n\), and use agent-specific permutations of item values. In the proposed model, recovering an aggregate allocation after those permutations requires a coupled mass-transport construction that preserves both item supplies and bundle cardinalities across types. The paper proves no such construction.

The same problem remains for Theorem 7. Removing cross-category bookkeeping makes the finite algorithm cleaner, but the continuous task is still a configuration problem. For a proposed threshold \(\lambda_t\), feasibility has the form

\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B\ni g}x_{t,B}=q_g,\qquad x_{t,B}=0\ \text{if}\ v_t(B)<\lambda_t . \]

This is an LP over bundle configurations. A column-generation pricing problem may require choosing at most \(k\) goods satisfying a value threshold while optimizing dual prices—a cardinality-constrained knapsack problem. Thus neither the \(1/2\) nor the \(2/3\) result supplies the claimed compressed algorithm. If \(|G|\) is fixed, enumeration makes the formulation nearly generic; if \(|G|\) grows, the substantive complexity has not disappeared.

The fixed-inventory limit is genuinely a bad mirror: with finitely many goods and an unbounded population, almost everyone receives nothing and MMS collapses. But the proponent’s repair—scaling item supplies per capita—is the strongest possible response, and it is permitted by the programme. Standardized devices, seats, or exhibits provide plausible high-multiplicity scenarios. Rational masses also scale back to finite instances, so this is not merely a lottery over divisible bundles.

Consequently, the negative case defeats the proponent’s unsupported claim that these are straightforward Class A continuizations. It does not honestly defeat the mirrors themselves. Under ChoCo’s rules, an unresolved or even hard high-multiplicity configuration problem is still a worthwhile computational question, and existing high-multiplicity allocation work supports rather than collides with it. Theorem 5 therefore survives as a legitimate, though technically unproved, mirror; Theorem 7 survives even more cleanly. I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror.
