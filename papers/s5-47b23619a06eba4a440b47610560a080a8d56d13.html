What the mirror covers

The proposed problem covers the paper’s two-good, incentive-compatible welfare allocation objective; it leaves the \(N\)-good optimality characterization and the observable-group results aside. It adds a finite computational encoding to an already distributional population model.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a finite encoding of the paper’s regular two-good primitives, such as rational piecewise-polynomial \(g\) and \(\lambda\), can the optimal cutoff pair or a certificate of pure-option optimality be computed exactly in polynomial time?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow: the paper has no named result about polynomial time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. Its strongest nearby anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper (Appendix D.1). For two goods, write a type as \(\theta=(1-t,t)\), and define \(\varphi(z)=(z\,\Pr[\Theta_2\le z],(1-z)\,\Pr[\Theta_2\ge z])\) and \(w(z)=\mathbb E[\lambda(\Theta)\max\{z\Theta_1,(1-z)\Theta_2\}]\). For any \(0<a<\theta_2^0<b<1\), there are unique positive weights \(m_a,m_b\) clearing supply, \(m_a\varphi(a)+m_b\varphi(b)=s\). The theorem says the pure-option mechanism is optimal exactly when, for every such pair, \(m_aw(a)+m_bw(b)\le [s_1/\varphi_1(\theta_2^0)]w(\theta_2^0)\). Otherwise, an optimum exists with two pure options and one mixed bundle, with quantities chosen to clear supply. This is a structural optimization theorem, not a complexity result; it motivates the proposed computational question but does not answer its complexity.

A concrete mirror is Two-Good High-Multiplicity Welfare Allocation. Consider a large public-housing cohort choosing between two categories of units. An instance lists \(\tau\) complete applicant types: type \(j\) has relative values \(\theta^j=(1-t_j,t_j)\), welfare scale \(\lambda_j>0\), and population share \(\mu_j>0\), with \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\). It also gives per-capita supplies \(s_1,s_2>0\). Given an allocation \(x_j\in\mathbb R_+^2\) to each type, welfare is \(\sum_j\mu_j\lambda_j\,\theta^j\cdot x_j\); feasibility requires \(\sum_j\mu_j x_j\le s\), and truthful choice requires \(\theta^j\cdot x_j\ge\theta^j\cdot x_k\) for every \(j,k\). The question is to return a welfare-maximizing feasible allocation, or its exact welfare. Mass is the fraction of applicants in each type; supplies scale with cohort size, and allocations can be per-person expected bundles implemented by lotteries or rotations.

This is a plausible regime when millions of applicants fall into relatively few preference-and-need classes. It preserves the paper’s underlying question—welfare-maximizing incentive-compatible allocation without transfers—rather than restricting the designer to a CEEI or to pure options. With rational input, the finite-support version is a bounded LP with \(2\tau\) allocation variables and \(O(\tau^2)\) incentive constraints, so I expect Class A, polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. Theorem 2 offers a useful structural lead: in the paper’s smooth two-good setting, an optimum can be represented using at most two threshold rules. Its smooth-density assumptions do not establish that cutoff characterization for atomic inputs, however.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already models a unit-mass society with a distribution of types, so this is chiefly a computational question about an existing continuous-population model, not a new population continuization. The proposed finite-support version also needs to be distinguished from the theorem’s regular, full-support setting. Its positive case rests on the genuine gap between the paper’s analytic characterization and the unasked algorithmic problem, not on a complexity result the authors already proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central objection is that there is no population to continuize. The paper begins with a unit mass of agents distributed according to \(F\), imposes aggregate supply constraints by integration, and requires incentive compatibility across the type space. It then represents society by \(G\), a distribution over relative-value types, with \(\lambda\) recording conditional welfare weights. Theorem 2 and its proof already optimize over that population: the threshold rules are combined using a measure on cutoffs. In the housing application, the appendix even derives the unit-mass model from steady-state arrival cohorts. A distribution of applicant types is therefore not a new mirror of the paper’s society; it is already the society the paper studies.

Theorem 2 is a named structural result, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. It characterizes when the pure-option mechanism is optimal and when an optimum can use a bundle. The proponent’s finite-support LP is a reasonable algorithmic question, but its input is a new finite encoding of that already-aggregated model. In particular, its atomic type distribution does not satisfy the paper’s later full-support density assumption. It may be a useful finite-type variant, but it is not a discrete-population model being relaxed to a continuum.

The stronger version would keep the paper’s regular two-good setting and specify a finite encoding—for example, rational piecewise-polynomial \(g\) and \(\lambda\)—then ask for an exact algorithm to find the optimal cutoffs or certify the pure-option condition in Theorem 2. That could be worthwhile algorithmic mechanism-design work. But it computes the optimum of the paper’s existing continuous-population model; it does not continuize the population. The missing computational analysis does not create a missing continuous mirror.

The observable-groups extension does not supply another population axis either: it already assigns groups population shares \(\pi_r\), integrates within each group, and splits aggregate supply across them. One could study computation with many encoded groups, but that would likewise be an algorithmic extension of a model whose population is already measured continuously.

So the honest negative case is narrow but strong for ChoCo’s remit: the paper offers no named computational-complexity result to mirror, and its society is already continuous. A finite-type LP or an algorithm for the cutoff characterization may merit study on its own; neither is a worthwhile *continuization* of this paper.
