The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational result to anchor a mirror to. The article gives no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. It mentions impossibility theorems, but only as unnumbered axiomatic results, and those are not computational-complexity anchors.

The strongest plausible positive case is the article’s distance-based aggregation rule. In a large public consultation on a fixed agenda, let a type be a complete judgment set, and let \(\mu_J\) be the fraction of participants with type \(J\). Given an agenda \(A\), its consistent complete judgment sets \(\mathcal J(A)\), a specified distance \(d\), and a rational-mass distribution supported on \(S\subseteq\mathcal J(A)\), ask for a consistent collective judgment set

\[ J^* \in \arg\min_{J'\in\mathcal J(A)} \sum_{J\in S}\mu_J\,d(J,J'). \]

This keeps the collective output as a judgment set while making the population continuous. It is a natural high-multiplicity version of the distance-based rule the article describes: many participants judge the same agenda, and the number of distinct judgment types can be much smaller than the population.

But this is a proposed follow-on problem, not a mirror of a named result in the paper. The article provides no complexity claim to restate, no basis for predicting whether this problem is tractable or hard, and no computational result for which it could serve as an anchor. Its weakest point is also its clearest limit: the paper is a conceptual survey of judgment aggregation, not a computational study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result to mirror. Its impossibility theorems concern combinations of axioms, and its distance-based rule is a proposal, not a complexity result. The proposed optimization,

\[ J^* \in \arg\min_{J'\in\mathcal J(A)} \sum_{J\in S}\mu_J\,d(J,J'), \]

is a coherent computational question, but it is a follow-on question created by the analyst. The paper does not specify the agenda representation, distance, or computational task whose complexity would be studied. Those choices could define very different problems, so calling this a mirror risks turning “mirror the paper” into “choose a new judgment-aggregation problem and continuize its population.”

That objection defeats the distance-based anchor as a *mirror of a computational result*. It does not defeat its best version as worthwhile research. A large consultation with repeated judgment types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and minimizing average distance to a consistent collective judgment is a natural question. The paper itself describes that rule family, so the connection is real.

The negative case is therefore narrow. It can reject a result-anchored mirror of this article; it cannot honestly support the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror. I do not see a sounder modelling objection to the consultation example: the population has natural types, and mass is meaningful. The case against is weak precisely at that point—the best version survives as a plausible follow-on, even if it is not a computational result already in the paper.
