What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness, tractability, or parameterized complexity. Its results are empirical findings from 162 students answering 16 two-patient allocation questions. For example, the paper reports strong support for “no exclusion” and that equality of health gains received the most support among classical principles in 11 of 16 situations. Those are not named computational results, so I cannot honestly restate them as complexity anchors.

There is a plausible continuous *setting* behind the study. A national health system might allocate treatment across large cohorts of clinically similar patients. A type could be a patient profile \((S_t,e_t)\), with \(S_t\) the initial health level and \(e_t\) the treatment effectiveness; \(\mu_t\) could be that type’s fraction of the eligible population. An allocation variable \(x_t\) could specify treatment time per patient of type \(t\). Large cohorts with relatively few profiles make high multiplicity plausible, and the paper itself asks whether abstract patients can represent larger groups.

But that does not yet define a prover-ready computational problem. The paper does not prescribe a rule or objective for choosing \(x\); it finds varied, sometimes conditional judgments and no principle followed by everyone in every situation. Choosing equality of gains, utilitarianism, or no exclusion as the objective would add a normative commitment the study investigates rather than settles. A feasibility or optimization problem built around one of those principles could be a useful new research direction, but it would not mirror a named computational result of this paper.

So the positive case is limited to this: cohort allocation is a credible high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s subject matter, with a natural type and mass interpretation. The weakest point is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper studies people’s choices and explanations, not the computational complexity of an allocation problem. There is no paper-anchored continuous question here to classify as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no computational result from the paper, and that is decisive for a paper-anchored mirror. The reported support for equality of health gains, the “no exclusion” principle, monotonicity, and consistency are findings about answers from 162 students in 16 fixed scenarios. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem. Recasting one of those findings as an optimization objective would supply a new problem; it would not mirror a result in the paper.

The strongest proposed setting is still credible: allocate treatment across large cohorts of patients with profiles such as \((S,e)\), represented by a distribution over types. The paper itself raises the possibility that its abstract patients represent groups. But a cohort model needs a policy rule or objective, along with a definition of how principles such as equality of gains or no exclusion apply at scale. The study supplies no agreed rule: respondents showed varied and often conditional judgments. Choosing one would turn a measured preference into a modeling premise. The resulting computational analysis could be worthwhile, but its complexity would belong to that new rationing model.

Treating respondents as the population does not rescue the mirror. A distribution over moral or decision types could support a new study of aggregate preferences, but that asks how to measure or predict responses. It does not yield a computational allocation result from this paper.

So the negative case is strong within ChoCo’s scope: there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous mirror, and the plausible cohort extension requires substantial new modeling choices. The broader claim that no scenario could make related work worthwhile is weaker. Large-scale healthcare rationing may merit a computational model; its connection to this paper would be motivation, not a mirror of the paper’s results.
