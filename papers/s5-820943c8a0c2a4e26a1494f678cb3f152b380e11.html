The continuous mirror question

Given a finite outcome space \(\mathcal O\), costs \(c\), a subadditive merge operation, budget \(B\), explicit comparison set \(C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete preference types, compute an efficiently sampleable distribution over pairs \((o^*,\nu)\). Require \(c(o^*)\le B\) and, in every realization, \(\sum_{t:o\succ_t o^*}\nu_t\le \gamma c(o)/B\) for every \(o\in C\), where \(0\le \nu_t\le \mu_t\). Require also \(\mathbb E[\nu_t]\ge \lambda\mu_t\) for every type, with \(\lambda=1-e^{-\alpha(1-\theta)}\) and \(\gamma=(\alpha+1)/(2\theta)\), for fixed \(\alpha>0\) and \(\theta\in(0,1)\).

The model it lives in

Use a type-indexed version of the paper’s uniform-income LP, with allocation variables \(y_o\) and prices \(p_{t,o}\). The price constraint weights each type by its population mass, \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{t,o}\le (\alpha/2)c(o)/B^*\), while demand constraints are imposed once per type. Dependent rounding and the paper’s merging operation produce discrete outcomes; the represented mass can be the full mass of a type in each realization.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the comparison-set randomized-core guarantee of Theorem 5 and the deterministic comparison-core guarantee of Theorem 6. It does not by itself cover guarantees against every outcome when the full outcome space is too large for an explicit comparison set.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the type-indexed LP and dependent rounding be implemented with running time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|C|\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Can the comparison-set guarantee extend to all of \(\mathcal O\) when it is given implicitly, using a separation or pricing oracle?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large participatory-budgeting electorate with repeated ranking ballots. For example, many thousands of residents may rank the same few dozen projects in only hundreds of distinct ways. A type \(t\) is a complete preference order over outcomes; in the paper’s ranking-ballot setting, it can be represented by a ranking of projects, with preferences over project sets determined by the voter’s top-ranked selected project. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) observed types, where \(\mu_t\) is their share of the electorate. Project sets stay discrete, their costs and budget stay as in the paper, and merging is set union. This makes the population continuous while preserving the paper’s actual core question.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It states that, under the subadditive merging assumption and given a comparison set \(C\), for any \(\alpha>0\) and cutoff \(\theta\in(0,1)\), a polynomial-time algorithm in \(n\) and \(|C|\) computes a randomized core with parameters
\[ \lambda=1-e^{-\alpha(1-\theta)},\qquad \gamma=\frac{\alpha+1}{2\theta}. \]
That means it outputs a distribution over feasible outcome and represented-voter pairs such that, in every realization, for every \(o\in C\), at most \(\gamma\,c(o)n/B\) represented voters prefer \(o\) to the selected outcome; each voter is represented with probability at least \(\lambda\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Randomized Comparison-Core. Given a finite outcome space \(\mathcal O\), costs \(c\), a subadditive merge operation, budget \(B\), explicit comparison set \(C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete preference types, compute an efficiently sampleable distribution over pairs \((o^*,\nu)\). Here \(c(o^*)\le B\), and \(\nu_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) is the represented mass of type \(t\). Every realization must satisfy
\[ \sum_{t:\,o\succ_t o^*}\nu_t \le \gamma\,\frac{c(o)}{B} \quad\text{for every }o\in C, \]
and each type must have expected represented mass at least \(\lambda\mu_t\). Equivalently, agents within each type can be represented exchangeably, so each has representation probability at least \(\lambda\). The expected direction is Class A.

There is a concrete reason to expect the type-compressed algorithm to work. In the paper’s uniform-income LP, give each type one price \(p_{t,o}\) for each compared outcome. The price constraint becomes
\[ \sum_t \mu_t p_{t,o} \le \frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{c(o)}{B^*}, \]
while the demand constraints are imposed once per type, rather than once per voter. The other constraints are the paper’s allocation and price bounds. Identical voters can be given the same price; averaging their prices also preserves the paper’s linear constraints. Thus the LP has size polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(|C|\), with rational input bit length \(L\), rather than depending on the electorate size \(n\). The output lottery still ranges over discrete outcomes: the fractional allocation is a construction tool, not a claim that outcomes have been continuized.

A second anchor is Theorem 6, also proved in this paper. It gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(n\) and \(|C|\) that computes a feasible deterministic outcome \(o^*\) satisfying
\[ \bigl|\{i:o^*\prec_i o\}\bigr| \le 11.6\,\frac{c(o)}{B}\,n \quad\text{for every }o\in C. \]
The corresponding Continuous Deterministic Comparison-Core problem has the same inputs as above and asks for one outcome \(o^*\) with \(c(o^*)\le B\) such that
\[ \sum_{t:\,o^*\prec_t o}\mu_t \le 11.6\,\frac{c(o)}{B} \quad\text{for every }o\in C. \]
I also expect this to be Class A. The paper derives its deterministic guarantee by repeatedly removing a represented group and merging outcomes. In a type-based version, each realization can treat a type as a whole, since its members have identical preferences. Each step then removes a positive-mass subset of the remaining support, so the process has at most \(\tau\) such steps. The prover’s task is to make the weighted partial-core selection and its running-time guarantee precise.

These are plausible mirrors because they replace the number of voters preferring an outcome with the share of the society that prefers it. The coalition’s proportional claim is naturally about a fraction of the electorate, just as it is in the paper’s core definition. The model does not give up individualized distinctions used by the paper: if voters differ in preferences or another problem-relevant feature, those distinctions belong in their types. In the paper’s own participatory-budgeting example, a ranking ballot is precisely the relevant preference description.

The paper contains no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question; these anchors are both positive algorithmic results. The proposed mirrors cover only the comparison-set guarantees of Theorems 5 and 6. Taking \(C=O\) would ask for guarantees against every outcome, but may lose the stated efficiency when \(O\) is exponential. Further questions include whether the comparison set can be handled through a separation oracle, how the coverage and approximation tradeoff changes with \(\alpha\) and \(\theta\), and whether stronger guarantees are possible for particular type structures.

The weakest point is that these continuous versions may be fairly direct weighted extensions of the paper’s LP and iteration, rather than a new complexity boundary. The guarantees are also only as broad as \(C\). And the scenario matters: if nearly every voter has a distinct ranking, then \(\tau\) approaches \(n\), and the support-sensitive benefit disappears. Still, this is not a collision with the paper’s model: its society is a finite voter list, while these questions take the distribution over voter types as the input. A straightforward positive answer would establish the continuous mirror; it would not make the mirror vacuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The paper has two named computational results, Theorems 5 and 6, and both ask for exactly the kind of algorithmic questions the programme targets. The proposed participatory-budgeting regime—many residents, relatively few repeated project rankings—is also a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Neither identity dependence nor a degenerate continuum rules it out.

Theorem 5’s mirror is especially hard to defeat. Its uniform-income LP compresses directly to types: the price constraint becomes
\[ \sum_t \mu_t p_{t,o}\le \frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{c(o)}{B^*}, \]
and each type contributes one demand constraint per compared outcome. The rounding still selects discrete outcomes; grouping identical voters changes the population representation, not the outcome space. The represented mass bound is the natural fractional version of the paper’s coalition-size bound. This looks like a genuine Class A mirror, even if the proof is a weighted extension rather than a new technique.

Theorem 6 is the more vulnerable anchor, but only in its claimed efficient implementation. The paper’s expectation argument establishes a large represented set; by itself, it does not show how to find a suitable realization in time polynomial in \(\tau\). A type-compressed deterministic algorithm needs that step made explicit. Still, this is a gap in the proponent’s algorithmic justification, not a reason the continuous deterministic-core question is ill-posed or unworth asking. With common prices for identical types, each realization represents whole types, so the iterative process can remove types in at most \(\tau\) rounds.

The best negative point is therefore modest: the randomized mirror may be a fairly direct weighted restatement, and the deterministic mirror’s proposed runtime needs proof. Those points could lower the expected novelty or require more work. They do not defeat either anchor under the stated standard. Existing high-multiplicity work further supports the regime rather than counting against it.

So the honest negative case is weak: I can identify a proof obligation for Theorem 6 and a limited novelty claim, but I cannot defend the universal conclusion that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror.
