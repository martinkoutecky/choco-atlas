The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Its propositions establish axiomatic facts: which decision policy satisfies the stated conditions, when the two aggregation paths can conflict, and how merging postulates correspond to judgment aggregation properties. None analyzes the complexity of a computational problem.

Proposition 6 is the proponent’s closest candidate, but it says that, for a complete agenda, two defined aggregation paths yield equivalent collective judgments. That is a representational correspondence, not an algorithmic result. The stronger version would put rational masses over complete belief-base types and compare weighted merging with weighted judgment aggregation. Such a population could be sensible; repeated agents with the same beliefs are not a problem for high multiplicity. But extending Proposition 6 in that way remains an axiomatic question. To make it computational, one would have to choose particular operators and ask how quickly their outputs can be computed—an interesting possible follow-on, but a new problem the paper does not formulate or answer. The same gap applies to the other results: none supplies a computational anchor.

So no anchor raised here survives as a continuous computational mirror of this paper. The limit of this negative case is that it cannot show a continuous, high-multiplicity study of belief or judgment aggregation would be uninteresting. It can show that such a study would draw its computational question from beyond this paper, rather than continuize one of its results.
