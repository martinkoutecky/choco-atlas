The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a convex rational worth function \(F\) on \([0,\mu]\) with \(F(0)=0\), let \(\sigma_t=\int_0^1 \partial_t F(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda\). Find masses \(h_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) receiving payoff \(\ell_t+1\), where \(\ell_t=\lfloor\sigma_t\rfloor\), and assign the remaining mass of each type payoff \(\ell_t\). Require \(\sum_t(\ell_t\mu_t+h_t)=F(\mu)\) and, for every \(z\in[0,\mu]\), require \(\sum_t\bigl(\ell_t z_t+\max\{0,z_t-(\mu_t-h_t)\}\bigr)\ge F(z)\). Among feasible \(h\), minimize \(\sum_t\bigl((\mu_t-h_t)(\ell_t-\sigma_t)^2+h_t(\ell_t+1-\sigma_t)^2\bigr)\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic society with finitely many complete agent types and rational masses \(\mu_t\), governed by a convex worth function \(F\) on type-mass vectors. Each individual receives an integer payoff; the decision variables are the type masses \(h_t\) receiving the higher quota, and the objective minimizes population-scaled squared distance from the continuous Shapley shares.

The objection that survived

To preserve complete types, agencies may need to encode their exact object-owner incidences; aggregating those incidences can erase the Hall constraints, while retaining them may make the type support grow with the population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the core-feasible indivisible Shapley allocation question for positive games, expressed over type masses. It leaves aside the finite owner-list matching algorithm itself, the results for general games, and the empirical case studies.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which owner-list game families does a fixed-type mass worth function preserve the allocation constraints of Theorem 7.1?
  • Do finite indivisible Shapley allocations converge to the proposed mass allocation under a natural scaling of population and supply?
  • Can the coalition constraints be separated or optimized efficiently as the number of types grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named hardness result to anchor on. My lead is the paper-proved Theorem 7.1, because it gives a concrete algorithmic result for assigning indivisible objects while preserving the paper’s fairness criterion.

Theorem 7.1 starts with players \(N\) and a list of objects, each owned by a group of players. It defines a game by \(v_{\mathcal S}(A)=|\{S_j\in\mathcal S:S_j\subseteq A\}|\). The theorem says that Algorithm 7 finds an allocation assigning each object to one of its owners such that each player’s number of objects is exactly their indivisible Shapley value; its running time is \(O(|\mathcal S|(|\mathcal S|+|N|)^{3/2}+|\mathcal S||N|(|\mathcal S|+|N|))\). The result is proved in this paper, in Appendix C.1.

A plausible regime is a large standardized network of local agencies or hospital units sharing a fixed set of roles. A type records the unit’s full role in the game: its mandate, costs, eligibility, and other attributes the model uses. There may be many units but only a few distinct types. The number of indivisible service slots or opportunities grows with the network. This is a scenario-specific case for high multiplicity, not a claim that every hospital system fits it.

My proposed mirror is Continuum Core-Feasible ISV for Positive Type Games. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a convex rational worth function \(F\) on \([0,\mu]\), with \(F(0)=0\). \(F(z)\) is the per-capita worth of a coalition containing mass \(z_t\) of each type; total supply per capita is \(F(\mu)\). It describes a population of size \(q\) with \(q\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), and \(qF(\mu)\) indivisible objects. The continuous Shapley share per member of type \(t\) is the high-multiplicity marginal limit
\[ \sigma_t=\int_0^1 \partial_t F(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda. \]

A solution assigns every individual an integer payoff. Within type \(t\), members receive either \(\ell_t=\lfloor\sigma_t\rfloor\) or \(\ell_t+1\); let \(h_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the mass receiving the higher payoff. Require efficiency,
\[ \sum_t(\ell_t\mu_t+h_t)=F(\mu), \]
and core stability: every measurable coalition must receive at least its worth. Equivalently, for every mass vector \(z\in[0,\mu]\), require
\[ \sum_t\left(\ell_t z_t+\max\{0,z_t-(\mu_t-h_t)\}\right)\ge F(z). \]
The left side is the least payoff a coalition of those type masses could obtain if it selected the lower-paid members first. Among feasible \(h\), choose one minimizing the population-scaled squared distance to the Shapley shares,
\[ \sum_t\big[(\mu_t-h_t)(\ell_t-\sigma_t)^2+h_t(\ell_t+1-\sigma_t)^2\big]. \]
The answer is the masses \(h_t\), together with any measurable assignment of the higher payoff to that mass of each type. The individual awards remain indivisible; fractional values arise only as type-level population averages.

I expect a tractable result for fixed \(|T|\) and bounded-degree worth functions, with the main computation being a finite-dimensional optimization over the masses \(h_t\) and coalition constraints. The variable-support complexity is open; a matching or separation algorithm may extend the paper’s approach, while the coalition constraints could also expose continuum-specific hardness. The mirror covers Theorem 7.1’s positive-game allocation question, not the paper’s other case studies or its results for general games.

The soft spot is that the matching theorem relies on an explicit finite owner list. A type-level worth function can discard incidence details, and the line-integral Shapley share is a proposed high-multiplicity limit, not an identity proved by this paper. Still, this is a recognizable extension of the authors’ question: it keeps the continuous object as the population, preserves integer payoffs per individual, and asks for the same Shapley-based fair allocation subject to coalition stability. Further questions are whether finite ISV allocations converge to this solution, which owner-list games admit such a type-level description, and how complexity changes as the type support grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7.1 is a real computational result, so the case against cannot rest on there being nothing to mirror. It gives a polynomial-time algorithm for an explicit object-owner list. The key input is the incidence structure: \(v_{\mathcal S}(A)=|\{S_j\in\mathcal S:S_j\subseteq A\}|\), and the algorithm must find a matching that respects those owners. The paper’s examples have little population structure among these players: parties are few, image regions have distinct locations, and hospitals or coalition parties have different roles.

A larger agency network is the strongest alternative scenario. But an agency’s complete type for Theorem 7.1 must include its role in the object-owner incidences, because those incidences determine both coalition values and feasible assignments. If many agencies truly share that complete role, each object’s owner set contains whole classes of identical agencies. A coalition containing \(99\%\) of such a class still lacks the class’s objects. The resulting worth function has jumps at full type masses; the smooth \(F\) and line-integral Shapley share proposed in the case for do not describe that game. A stepwise, finite-type model could repair the formulation, though it would leave within-class assignments largely arbitrary.

The alternative is to let agencies have different local ownership patterns, then aggregate those patterns statistically. That can give a smooth mass-level worth function, yet type masses alone no longer determine whether an allocation exists. Theorem 7.1’s matching proof relies on Hall constraints for individual subsets of objects and owners; local incidence patterns can violate those constraints even when the aggregate worth looks feasible. Encoding the patterns as complete types makes the support grow with the network. Encoding them with a graph or hypergraph limit would retain more structure, but would require a new input model and a new asymptotic matching question. It would be a promising extension, rather than a direct high-multiplicity mirror of this theorem.

So the strongest negative case is that this theorem’s relevant multiplicity is hard to separate from its incidence structure: coarse types lose the matching constraints, while complete types often eliminate the compression. That is a substantial problem for the proposed mirror. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A deliberately repeated, finite-type ownership design with a meaningful mass-scale allocation could still support one, after a more careful formulation. I can argue that Theorem 7.1 is a weak anchor for the programme’s standard continuous-society model; I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no scenario would be worth studying.
