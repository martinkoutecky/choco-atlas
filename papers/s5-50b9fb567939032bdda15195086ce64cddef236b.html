Why no mirror

Under faithful atomless semantics, the uniform assignment \(\lambda_{t,k}=\mu_t/K\) equalizes coalition utilities and is immediately stable, so the hardness mechanism disappears. The nondegenerate alternative is a different bloc-level game, not a recognized continuous-population mirror.

What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a cohort-coherent population mirror of the paper’s \(\mathrm{sumCIS}\) problem.

The lead anchor is Theorem \(6.1\), proved in this paper: \(\mathrm{sumCIS}\) is \(\texttt{PLS}\)-complete.[Gairing and Savani](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.05322) The underlying problem is: given a symmetric additively separable hedonic game represented by an undirected edge-weighted graph, find a partition of the players such that no player has a strictly improving deviation that is simultaneously sum-in feasible and sum-out feasible. A deviation to coalition \(c\) must improve the deviator’s utility, have nonnegative total edge weight into \(c\), and have nonpositive total edge weight in the deviator’s current coalition. The theorem is proved here by reduction from \(\textsc{LocalMaxCut}\): two supernodes force exactly two effective coalitions, and every stable outcome induces a locally optimal party-affiliation solution.

I would mirror it as \(\mathrm{CohSumCIS}_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a symmetric rational interaction kernel \(a:T\times T\to\mathbb Q\). Type \(t\) means a large cohort of agents with the same interaction value toward every type. The regime is \(N\gg\tau=|T|\): for example, many members of a small number of departments, professional cohorts, or organizational blocs whose members are indistinguishable and whose coalition decision is made jointly by the cohort.

There are \(\tau\) possible coalition labels. The decision variable is a coherent mass assignment \(\lambda\), with \(\lambda_{t,k}\in\{0,\mu_t\}\) and \(\sum_k\lambda_{t,k}=\mu_t\): every type’s mass belongs to one coalition. The utility of type \(t\) in coalition \(k\) is \(u_t(k)=\sum_{s\in T}a_{ts}\lambda_{s,k}\). Moving type \(t\) from its current coalition \(k\) to \(\ell\) is feasible when its utility strictly increases, \(u_t(\ell)>u_t(k)\), while \(u_t(k)\le 0\) and \(u_t(\ell)\ge 0\). The question is to output a coherent assignment with no such move. This is exactly the paper’s sum-in/sum-out stability predicate, with population counts replaced by rational masses.

The model preserves the paper’s computational object: coalitions, additive pairwise utilities, unilateral-improvement logic at the cohort level, and the same potential-function intuition. It does not fractionalize bundles or outcomes; only the population is represented by rational proportions. A finite graph instance embeds by taking one type per player, \(\mu_i=1/n\), \(a_{ij}=nw_{ij}\), and \(a_{ii}=0\), so the utilities and stable assignments are preserved exactly.

I expect \(\mathrm{CohSumCIS}_\infty\) to be Class B: hardness transfers. The hard combinatorics in Theorem \(6.1\) live in the interaction graph and the coalition assignment, not in the names of individual agents. The embedding above means a polynomial algorithm for the continuous problem would solve the paper’s \(\mathrm{sumCIS}\) instances. In the genuinely high-multiplicity regime with fixed \(\tau\), however, exhaustive search is fixed-parameter rather than polynomial in \(\tau\); that is an important further question, not something the theorem settles.

The serious weakness is the coherence assumption. If identical agents are allowed to split freely between coalitions, the direct atomless unilateral-deviation model largely collapses: with two coalitions, assigning half of every type to each coalition makes every type see the same aggregate utility in both coalitions, so no strict unilateral move exists. Thus I would not claim that the paper has a straightforward Wardrop-style continuum analogue. The defensible claim is narrower: its \(\mathrm{sumCIS}\) problem has a natural high-multiplicity extension for populations organized into coherent, jointly moving types. Whether the authors accept that as their problem depends on whether cohort-level coalition formation is admissible in their intended application.

This case therefore covers Theorem \(6.1\) emphatically, not the whole table. The main follow-up is to determine whether a nontrivial split-mass version can be recovered using positive-mass deviations, or whether every independent-agent continuum version becomes trivial.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Theorem \(6.1\), and its proposed mirror depends on the one assumption that cannot be inherited from high multiplicity: that all agents of a type must move together.

In the paper, a deviation is made by one player. If many players have the same type, they remain individually movable; identical preferences do not force them to occupy the same coalition. The natural atomless version therefore permits a type to split across coalitions. That destroys the proposed hardness mechanism. With \(K\) available coalitions, assign every type uniformly:
\[ \lambda_{t,k}=\frac{\mu_t}{K} \qquad\text{for every }t,k. \]
Every type then has exactly the same utility in every coalition,
\[ u_t(k)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_s a_{ts}\mu_s. \]
No strict improving move exists, so the outcome is automatically sum-in/sum-out stable. If arbitrary new coalitions are allowed, as in the paper, the degeneration is even worse: partitioning the atomless population into null coalitions gives every agent utility \(0\), and a unilateral move cannot create a positive-mass coalition. Thus the genuine independent-agent continuum has a trivial stable outcome, not a meaningful \(\mathrm{PLS}\) search problem.

The proposed restriction \(\lambda_{t,k}\in\{0,\mu_t\}\) avoids this only by replacing individual deviations with synchronized bloc deviations. That is not a high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper. It is a different finite game whose players are the \(\tau\) blocs. The masses merely become coefficients in their utilities; no population mass can be transferred fractionally, and the continuous population has disappeared from the strategic object. In particular, the hardness embedding with one type per original player uses \(\tau=n\), exactly the regime in which there is no multiplicity at all. For fixed \(\tau\), the coherent problem is just finite enumeration over bloc assignments, independent of how large the underlying cohorts are.

One could impose positive-mass coordinated deviations to prevent the atomless collapse, but that changes the solution concept from individual stability to a new coalitional-stability problem. Its deviation size, coordination rule, and post-move utility convention would all have to be supplied externally; none is forced by \(\mathrm{sumCIS}\). Likewise, indivisible departmental blocs could be a worthwhile application, but then the departments—not the underlying population—are the agents. That may be a legitimate weighted coalition-formation model, yet it is not a continuous-population mirror of this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the stated anchor: the faithful continuum is degenerate, while the nondegenerate version preserves the paper’s hardness only by abandoning its individual-agent and high-multiplicity semantics. I cannot honestly rule out an interesting separate theory of coordinated cohort formation, but that is not enough to establish a worthwhile ChoCo mirror.
