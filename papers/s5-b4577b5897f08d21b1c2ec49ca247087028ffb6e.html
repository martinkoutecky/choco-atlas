The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Its numbered results concern incentives: for example, Proposition 1 constructs an envy-free mechanism with inefficient limit Nash equilibrium outcomes when the penalty set is unbounded; Theorem 2 says that with a bounded penalty set, the limit Nash equilibrium outcomes of every envy-free mechanism are exactly the allocations envy-free for the true preferences. Neither asserts a complexity result.

The paper’s computational claims are attributed to other work: Section 3.2 cites Arunachaleswaran et al. (2019) for a polynomial algorithm, and the introduction says the earlier complexity results appear in Velez’s companion paper. Treating Theorem 2 as an anchor for a computational population problem would change its subject from strategic outcomes to computing allocations. So, under your anchor requirement, there is no honest per-anchor question to propose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–2 characterize outcomes of reporting games; they do not give complexity bounds or algorithms. The paper’s computation discussion points to algorithms in other work, and it explicitly says its own complexity results appear in a companion paper. So neither Proposition 1 nor Theorem 2 supplies an honest computational anchor. Asking for the complexity of a continuous version of either would change the subject from incentives to allocation computation.

The strongest replacement would be a continuum rent-division problem: scale room supplies with the population, represent renters and rooms by types, and compute an envy-free allocation, perhaps one maximizing minimum utility. That is a sensible computational question, and the co-scaling is natural. But it follows the algorithmic work the paper cites, rather than mirroring a result this paper proves. The paper’s central object is a game in which a named roommate can change her report. In an atomless population, one renter’s deviation changes no mass and therefore cannot change an aggregate allocation. The strategic claim then loses its operative content. Making an entire positive-mass type report together would restore impact, but it turns the players into coordinated blocs; that is a different strategic model from the one analyzed here.

Room identities and the one-room-per-agent assignment sharpen the mismatch: a continuum model needs room supplies to scale and must replace individual assignments with allocations across room types. Those are reasonable modelling choices, but they produce a market-allocation problem, not a continuous version of this paper’s named incentive results. This is not an objection that the answer might change or that a continuous algorithm might be easy; it is that the computational result to mirror is absent, and the most promising substitute changes the paper’s central question.

I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous rent-division problem exists. That broader claim is hard to defend: the scaled allocation problem could be worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is strongest, and more limited, on this paper specifically: its results are about strategic interaction among individually acting roommates, while its computational results are elsewhere.
