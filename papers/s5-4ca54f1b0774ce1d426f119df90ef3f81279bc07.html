The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that any finite matching market with contracts whose firm choices satisfy substitutes can be embedded in a salary market whose firm demands satisfy gross substitutes, with a set of contracts stable exactly when its image is a stable matching. The paper also describes salary adjustment as finding a stable match. But Theorem 1 is a structural representation result; it does not classify a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise. The algorithm discussion is not a numbered complexity result.

So I cannot give an anchor-backed continuous problem under your stated rule: the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. The nearest positive direction would be to study a high-volume hiring market with many workers and employer units grouped by their complete preference and choice types, representing each type’s share as mass. A continuous version of Theorem 1 could ask whether the salary embedding and its stability correspondence extend to measurable mass allocations. That is a plausible population-continuity question, but it would be a new structural extension—not a computational mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on the programme’s stated gate: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is a structural representation result. It constructs salary levels from the ranking of contracts for each worker–firm pair, then proves that stable contracts correspond exactly to stable matchings and that the salary market satisfies gross substitutes. It makes no claim about complexity, approximation, or parameterized algorithms. Section D compares the steps of two existing adjustment algorithms, but gives no complexity result; its worked example is a two-worker, two-firm market.

The best continuous follow-up is easy to imagine: a high-volume hiring market with finitely many complete worker and firm types, each present in a rational mass, and a question about whether the embedding and stability correspondence extend to mass allocations. That is a plausible population regime, and I would not dismiss it on the ground that agents have identities or that individual agents have zero mass. But the question it directly poses is whether a structural theorem lifts to a continuum. To make it computational, one must add a new problem—such as computing a stable mass allocation from type data. The paper neither states a complexity result for computing stable matchings nor specifies a mass-market input or algorithm. That could be worthwhile research on its own, but it is not a computational mirror of a result this paper proves.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo mirror under the requested standard, not because a continuous matching market is incoherent, or because its answer is known, but because the paper offers no computational claim for the continuization programme to classify.
