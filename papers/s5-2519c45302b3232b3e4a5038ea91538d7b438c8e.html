The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror on. The numbered results concern existence, axioms, and topological continuity—not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified.

The nearest apparent anchors do not meet that requirement. Theorem 6, restating Diamond’s result from elsewhere, says there is no ethical social welfare preorder on \(l_{\infty}^{[0,1]}\) satisfying anonymity, strong monotonicity, and continuity in the supremum-metric topology. Theorems 11–13, proved here using the paper’s characterization, give existence results for ethical social welfare orders under topologies refined by the relevant order-generated topologies. Theorem 20, also proved here, gives an order satisfying anonymity, strong monotonicity, and strong distributive fairness semiconvexity that is continuous in a topology induced by a quasi-pseudo-metric. These are mathematical existence and compatibility claims, not computational results.

There is a nearby continuous-looking subject, but it does not yield a convincing mirror of those results. The paper studies orders over infinite welfare streams: the coordinates represent generations, and the topology concerns closeness between streams. Treating generations as a continuum would continuize the time or outcome space, not the population whose computational problems ChoCo studies. Treating the alternatives or ethical orders as types in a distributed society would change the object being ranked and the problem being solved. Neither supplies an honest type, mass-transfer decision, and objective that the authors would recognize as their problem.

So I cannot state one continuous computational problem per anchor: there are no eligible anchors, and assigning this paper a tractability, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness prediction would be invented. The paper is relevant neighboring work on axiomatic continuity, but it is neither a collision with ChoCo’s population-continuization programme nor evidence for a particular computational mirror. The weak point in any positive case is exactly this mismatch between continuity of a preference over streams and computation over a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper contains no computational result to mirror. Its numbered results characterize continuity, establish existence or impossibility of ethical social welfare orders, and construct quasi-pseudo-metrics. None classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity or gives an algorithm. A computational question could be added, but it would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

The cited theorems do not supply a better anchor on closer inspection. Theorem 6 is an axiomatic impossibility for a specified topology. Theorems 11–13 establish existence when the topology is chosen to fit the preorder; Theorem 20 constructs a compatible topology from a preorder. One could ask whether a finite input description of a preorder and topology makes continuity decidable, or whether a compatible order can be found efficiently. But the paper specifies no such input descriptions, and its objects range over arbitrary subsets of \(l_\infty\), topologies, and orders. Supplying finite encodings would define a separate computational framework. Replacing the domain by a continuous population would not provide those encodings or make that question a mirror.

Nor does the infinite horizon furnish the missing population. The coordinates of a stream are generations across time; the alternatives are welfare streams, and the paper ranks those alternatives. Turning generations into a continuum would continuize time or the alternative space, which is outside ChoCo’s scope. Turning people with different welfare streams into a distribution of “types” would instead make the object a population of preferences or outcomes. That changes the problem the paper studies. The paper’s anonymity and present-sensitivity axioms also concern how generations are treated, not how mass transfers between interchangeable agents should be computed.

The strongest possible reframing is to place a computational policy problem on top of the paper’s welfare orders—for example, optimize welfare over a feasible set of streams, or aggregate a distribution of citizens’ ethical preferences. Such a problem might be worthwhile, but its decision variables, input, and objective would need to be supplied independently. The quasi-pseudo-metrics in Theorems 20–25 do not change that: they encode an order and quantify comparisons; they do not define an algorithmic social-choice problem over a population.

So the negative case is strong for this paper, though not a proof that no adjacent project could be worthwhile. The cited results have no computational target, and the best routes to one either add a new optimization problem or continuize time, preferences, or outcomes instead of the population.
