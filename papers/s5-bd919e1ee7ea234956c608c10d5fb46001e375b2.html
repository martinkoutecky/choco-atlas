The continuous mirror question

Given rational class masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2>0\) with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), and a finite rational antichain \(\mathcal{B}\) of shift-minimal winning profiles in \([0,\mu_1]\times[0,\mu_2]\), declare \(x\) winning when \(x_1\ge b_1\) and \(x_1+x_2\ge b_1+b_2\) for some \(b\in\mathcal{B}\). Is this rule representable by weights \(w_1>w_2\ge0\) and quota \(q>0\) exactly when there are no \(b^1,b^2\in\mathcal{B}\) and losing profiles \(y^1,y^2\) with \(b^1+b^2=y^1+y^2\)?

The model it lives in

A two-type society carries rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\); winning profiles follow the finite dominance boundary \(\mathcal{B}\). The decision variables are two class weights and a quota, while the certificate question compares two winning boundary profiles with two losing profiles having equal total mass in each class.

The objection that survived

The discrete theorem does not establish that two balanced mass trades suffice once fractional coalition profiles are allowed; that certificate bound remains an open geometric question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two-type weightedness test in Theorem 5.9; it leaves the \(r=1\) case, larger numbers of voter types, broader trade-size results, and enumeration results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every non-threshold rule with such a rational boundary admit a balanced trade using two winning boundary profiles and two losing profiles?
  • Can threshold representability for this finite rational boundary input be recognized in time polynomial in its encoding length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5.9, proved in this paper. It says that a complete simple game with two types of voters is weighted if and only if it is \(2\)-invariant trade robust: there is no trade involving at most two shift-minimal winning coalition types whose aggregate type counts equal those of losing coalition types. The theorem resolves Conjecture 6.1 of Freixas and Molinero (2009). It gives a concrete decision characterization, though it is not a P, NP-hardness, or other complexity-class result; I would not present it as one.

Here is a continuous version of that decision problem. Imagine a large organization with two membership classes. Every member in a class has the same role in the voting rule, and each member votes yes or no on a proposal. Let \(\mu_1,\mu_2>0\), with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), be the population shares of the two classes. A coalition is now described by \(x=(x_1,x_2)\), where \(0\le x_i\le\mu_i\) is the mass of class \(i\) voting yes.

An instance consists of those class masses and a finite rational list \(\mathcal B\) of shift-minimal winning mass vectors. Define \(x\succeq b\) to mean \(x_1\ge b_1\) and \(x_1+x_2\ge b_1+b_2\), and declare \(x\) winning exactly when \(x\succeq b\) for some \(b\in\mathcal B\). The question is whether there are weights \(w_1>w_2\ge0\) and a quota \(q>0\) such that, for every possible mass coalition \(x\), it is winning exactly when \(w_1x_1+w_2x_2\ge q\). A yes solution is such weights and quota. A proposed no certificate is a pair of winning boundary profiles and a pair of losing profiles with equal aggregate mass: \(x^1+x^2=y^1+y^2\). Such a trade contradicts any weighted representation.

I would expect this fixed two-type problem to be Class A when the rule is given by a finite rational boundary. The winning region has a finite polyhedral description in two dimensions, so exact threshold recognition looks like a finite separation-feasibility problem. The sharper question is whether the discrete theorem’s certificate bound survives: does every nonweighted continuous rule of this form have a \(2\)-invariant mass trade? The two-mass formulation makes that a specific question for a prover, rather than a general claim that continuous voting is easier.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem. The paper already represents complete games by coalition vectors indexed by equivalence classes of voters. The continuous model replaces their integer class counts with mass coordinates, while retaining the same shift order, the same weightedness question, and the same kind of trade obstruction. The regime is a large electorate with two genuinely interchangeable roles; the number of voters grows while the number of types remains two. This covers Theorem 5.9’s weightedness characterization, not the paper’s enumeration results or its broader trade-robustness questions.

The weak point is that a finite discrete game does not determine a unique continuous winning region: normalizing its boundary vectors is a modeling choice, and boundary behavior can depend on the sequence of games used to approach the limit. Also, the two classes are defined by their roles in a voting rule, not by preference rankings. The mirror survives those limits because the continuous instance specifies its winning region explicitly and preserves the paper’s exact underlying question—whether the rule is additively representable. The main follow-up is whether discrete weightedness and \(2\)-trade certificates converge to the corresponding mass properties as the electorate grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is that Theorem 5.9 is a structural characterization of finite games: a complete simple game with two voter types is weighted exactly when it is \(2\)-invariant trade robust. The paper gives no input encoding, running-time bound, algorithm, or complexity classification for recognizing weightedness. The continuous threshold-recognition problem in the case is therefore a new computational problem, rather than a mirror of a computational result the paper establishes.

That objection survives the better version of the proposed mirror. A two-class atomless electorate with rational masses and a finite list of winning boundary vectors is a coherent continuous voting model. But a finite game \(G\) does not determine its winning rule at off-grid mass vectors. Specifying a rational boundary list makes the continuous problem well-posed; it also supplies information that Theorem 5.9 never contains. This limits the claim that the theorem itself has been continuized.

There is a further gap in preserving the theorem’s distinctive content. Allowing arbitrary mass mixtures in a trade condition leads toward ordinary convex separation. Restricting the condition to two winning and two losing mass profiles preserves the theorem’s form, but whether that bound remains sufficient is a new geometric question. It could be worthwhile, but the paper’s result does not answer or motivate it in the same direct way.

These points make the mirror less securely grounded in the paper’s computational contributions. They do not defeat it: the two-type population regime is sensible, and the continuous recognition problem could be worth studying on its own. I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile mirror survives this anchor.
