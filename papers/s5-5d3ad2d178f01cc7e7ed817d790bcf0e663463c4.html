The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) alternatives, \(C\) constituencies, positive weights \(\lambda_k\), and for each constituency \(k\) a finite support \(\mathcal T_k \subseteq S_n\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_k(\sigma)\) summing to \(1\), return a permutation \(\pi\) satisfying \(\max_{k\in[C]} \lambda_k \sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal T_k}\mu_k(\sigma)d_\tau(\pi,\sigma) \le 2\min_{\rho\in S_n}\max_{k\in[C]} \lambda_k \sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal T_k}\mu_k(\sigma)d_\tau(\rho,\sigma)\).

The model it lives in

Each type is a constituency and a complete ranking \((k,\sigma)\); each constituency has a rational-mass distribution over its ranking types. The output is one permutation, and its objective is the maximum across constituencies of weighted conditional expected Kendall-\(\tau\) distance.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem III.2’s permutation, median Kendall-\(\tau\) branch with arbitrary rational type masses; it makes no claim about the paper’s other distances, min-distance variants, partial rankings, or empirical results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the factor \(2\) be improved for arbitrary rational masses, or is it tight?
  • How does the best approximation guarantee depend on \(n\), \(C\), and total support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is narrow: the paper’s multiclass median Kendall-\(\tau\) problem, with each class interpreted as a large constituency whose rankings have a distribution over types.

My anchor is Theorem III.2, proved in this paper. For permutation rankings and median Kendall-\(\tau\) distance, the authors form an LP relaxation over pairwise-order variables and apply their recursive deterministic rounding algorithm, mmKT-Conv. The theorem says the resulting permutation has cost at most twice the optimum of that LP. Since the LP is a relaxation of the permutation problem, this gives a 2-approximation to the original objective. It applies across classes with their stated positive class weights. The paper’s NP-hardness claims appear in prose and refer to [3]; they are not numbered hardness theorems, so I would not use them as the anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Let the alternatives be \([n]\), and let there be \(C\) constituencies. Constituency \(k\) has a finite support \(\mathcal T_k\subseteq S_n\) and rational masses \(\mu_k(\sigma)>0\), with \(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal T_k}\mu_k(\sigma)=1\). It also has weight \(\lambda_k>0\). A complete type is the pair \((k,\sigma)\): an agent’s constituency and ranking. Define
\[ F_\mu(\pi)=\max_{k\in[C]}\lambda_k \sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal T_k}\mu_k(\sigma)d_\tau(\pi,\sigma). \]
Call the approximation task Mass-MinMax-Kendall-2: given \(n,C\), the supports, rational masses and weights, return a permutation \(\pi\) satisfying
\[ F_\mu(\pi)\le 2\min_{\rho\in S_n}F_\mu(\rho). \]
The support size is \(\tau=\sum_k|\mathcal T_k|\); the masses and weights are encoded as binary rationals. An equivalent single society distribution can include constituency labels, with each \(\mu_k\) the conditional distribution within constituency \(k\). This conditional form matches the paper’s per-class averages.

This is a population interpretation, not a continuous outcome: mass is the share of a constituency with a given complete ranking, while the published aggregate remains an ordinary permutation. The weighted version follows the paper’s LP proof by replacing each uniform class average with its \(\mu_k\)-weighted average. Its pairwise coefficients become \(\lambda_k\sum_\sigma\mu_k(\sigma)\mathbf 1[\sigma(x)<\sigma(y)]\); the LP and rounding still have size polynomial in \(n,C,\tau\), and the rational input length \(L\). Thus Theorem III.2 gives a tractable, Class A answer for the stated 2-approximation task.

A plausible regime is a large civic consultation or ranking platform with millions of participants, a fixed set of alternatives, and a handful of stakeholder constituencies. Within each constituency, many people share one of relatively few recurring complete rankings, so \(N\) can be far larger than \(\tau\). The objective asks for a ranking that controls the cost-weighted *average* Kendall disagreement in the worst constituency. That is close to the authors’ own motivation: they define classes of rankings and use a maximum over class-level distances. Treating classes as constituencies and their rankings as population distributions preserves their central question.

I would not claim that continuization makes the exact problem easy. Every unweighted finite instance is a special case, using uniform mass on each class’s ranking set. The paper states that the multiclass MinMax problems are NP-hard by appeal to [3], so exact optimization in this mirror should be expected to inherit hardness from the discrete problem. The 2-approximation is Class A; the exact companion is plausibly Class B. Further questions include whether any algorithm beats factor two for arbitrary rational masses, and how exact complexity varies with \(n\), \(C\), and \(\tau\).

The scope here is just Theorem III.2’s permutation, median-\(\tau\) branch. I would not use it to claim a mirror for the paper’s min-distance, footrule, partial-ranking, or genome experiments.

The weakest point is that this is a close high-multiplicity extension: the paper already averages rankings within each finite class, and its LP proof carries over with little change. It does not establish new exact tractability or show that population multiplicity dissolves the paper’s hardness. I still find it a credible mirror because the large-population, small-support regime is sensible for this problem, and the theorem directly answers a computational question over that continuous population model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the sole anchor, Theorem III.2, is that its proposed mirror looks like a weighted restatement of the paper’s existing LP result. The theorem already optimizes class-level average Kendall-\(\tau\) disagreement. Replacing each uniform class average with rational masses changes the pairwise coefficients to weighted sums; the proof’s rounding argument still applies to those averages. So the continuous version seems to require little new structure or algorithmic work.

That is a concern about the mirror’s research yield, not a reason the question fails. A ranking platform with many participants, a fixed set of alternatives, and a few constituencies with repeated rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The objective depends on class averages, not individual identities, and the continuous limit does not make Kendall-\(\tau\) disagreement vanish. The class label can be part of a complete type, with each constituency’s conditional distribution retained.

I can’t honestly defeat this anchor. The weighted extension may be a modest corollary, but the mirror is well-posed and fits the programme’s population model. Under the stated standard, the negative case is weak here.
