What the mirror covers

The attempt repackages Algorithms 1–2 as type-mass elicitation, while Propositions 1, 3, and 4, the SVR/IP discussion, and the experiments receive no valid continuous theorem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under an explicit common-report cohort model, does linear-kernel mass elicitation admit a polynomial-time column-generation algorithm in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input length?
  • Can cohort-level PVM obtain a non-vacuous incentive-alignment theorem without replacing unilateral deviations by coalition deviations?
  • For quadratic kernels, what is the complexity of the mass winner-determination or pricing problem, and does any hardness depend on population multiplicity?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is qualified: the paper has a natural continuous-population analogue, but it does not contain a qualifying computational-complexity anchor under ChoCo’s strict rule.

Its numbered results are Proposition 1 (an efficiency-loss bound), Proposition 2 (incentive alignment), Proposition 3 (an ex-post regret bound), and Proposition 4 (individual rationality). All are proved in this paper, but none asserts membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Section 5 discusses integer-program formulations, but no numbered theorem establishes their complexity. Thus Proposition 2 is the best anchor-adjacent result, rather than a formally compliant complexity anchor.

The mirror I would nevertheless propose is Continuous PVM Mass Allocation, based on Proposition 2.

Consider many bidders in repeated spectrum markets, with \(N\) bidders but only \(\tau\ll N\) complete bidder types. A type \(\theta\) specifies the bidder’s valuation \(v_\theta:\{0,1\}^m\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), query costs, ML features, and all other relevant parameters. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be the fraction of bidders of type \(\theta\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). To avoid the trivial limit in which a fixed number of spectrum licenses is allocated to a vanishing fraction of the population, let item \(j\) have per-capita supply \(\rho_j\); in an \(N\)-bidder realization, there are \(N\rho_j\) copies of that item. Each individual still receives an integral bundle \(x\in\{0,1\}^m\); only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

Write \(y_{\theta,x}\) for the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving bundle \(x\). Feasibility is

\[ \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^m}y_{\theta,x}=\mu_\theta \]

for every type \(\theta\), and

\[ \sum_{\theta,x}x_jy_{\theta,x}\le \rho_j \]

for every item \(j\). Social welfare is

\[ W(y)=\sum_{\theta,x}y_{\theta,x}v_\theta(x). \]

A query to type \(\theta\) for bundle \(x\) reveals \(v_\theta(x)\) for the entire mass of that type. The continuous version of Algorithm 1 repeatedly applies the paper’s ML estimator to the currently known type-bundle values, chooses a feasible mass allocation maximizing inferred welfare, and queries every positive-mass type-bundle in that allocation whose value is not yet known.

For the PVM analogue, run this elicitation procedure once on the full type distribution and once after removing each positive-mass type cohort. Let \(y^{(-\theta)}\) be the allocation from the run excluding type \(\theta\), and let \(y^\star\) be the reported-welfare-maximizing allocation among the resulting candidates. The total payment charged to type \(\theta\) is

\[ P_\theta = \sum_{\eta\ne\theta,x}y^{(-\theta)}_{\eta,x}\hat v_\eta(x) - \sum_{\eta\ne\theta,x}y^\star_{\eta,x}\hat v_\eta(x), \]

with per-bidder payment \(P_\theta/\mu_\theta\).

The precise computational question is:

Given rational \(\mu\), \(\rho\), type valuations, initial reports, and a specified ML estimator \(A\), compute the final feasible mass allocation \(y^\star\), the \(\tau+1\) elicitation transcripts, and the type-cohort payments \(P_\theta\); determine whether the resulting allocation has welfare at least \(K\), or whether it can be produced using at most \(B\) distinct type-bundle queries.

This is recognisably the paper’s problem: the bundle space remains exponential, ML still selects which values to elicit, the final allocation is based on reported values, and the leave-one-out runs still support externality-style payments. The difference is that repeated indistinguishable bidders are represented by mass, so the number of elicitation runs is \(\tau+1\), rather than \(N+1\).

The relevant anchor is Proposition 2, proved here. It states that under PVM, for every bidder \(i\), and for every fixed strategy profile of the other bidders, every utility-maximizing strategy of \(i\) also maximizes

\[ v_i(a^{\mathrm{PVM}}_\sigma) + \sum_{j\ne i}\hat v_j(a^{\mathrm{PVM}}_\sigma). \]

In words, a bidder’s incentives are aligned with allocative efficiency, although PVM is not strategyproof. In the continuous mirror, the same statement is required for each positive-mass type cohort: fixing the other reports, every report maximizing that cohort’s total utility must maximize its true welfare contribution plus the reported welfare of all other cohorts.

I would expect the linear-kernel version to be Class A. The mass allocation is a configuration LP with exponentially many bundle columns. Its pricing problem has the form

\[ \max_{x\in\{0,1\}^m} \left( \widetilde v_\theta(x)-\sum_j\pi_jx_j \right), \]

where \(\pi_j\) are item shadow prices. For an additive or linear inferred valuation, this pricing problem decomposes item by item, giving a plausible polynomial-time column-generation algorithm in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length. The continuous model removes dependence on the number of repeated bidders while preserving integral bundles for each individual.

The full quadratic-kernel version is more likely Class B: its pricing problem can encode quadratic \(0\)-\(1\) optimization, and hardness would live in the bundle/item structure rather than in population multiplicity. Rational finite auctions embed into the mass model by setting \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\), so arbitrary-bidder hardness can survive when the number of distinct types is allowed to grow. A separate open question is whether optimizing the adaptive query policy itself creates a genuinely continuum-specific Class C problem.

The paper’s other results could support secondary questions. Proposition 1 gives the continuous efficiency bound

\[ W(y_v^\star)-W(y_{\hat\vartheta}^\star) \le \widetilde W^t(y^t)-W(y^t) + W(y_v^\star)-\widetilde W^t(y_v^\star), \]

under truthful type reports. Propositions 3 and 4 suggest continuous regret and type-cohort individual-rationality questions. I would not use them as separate anchors, however: they are mechanism guarantees, not complexity results.

The weakest point is substantial. Proposition 2 concerns individual bidders, whereas a literal continuum gives each individual zero mass. My construction therefore treats a repeated type cohort as the strategic unit. An individual-level version would require an infinitesimal leave-one-bidder-out limit and a derivative notion of payment, which the paper does not provide. Also, scaling item supply per capita changes the fixed-license spectrum setting, though it is necessary for a nondegenerate high-multiplicity regime.

So the positive case is: this paper contains a genuinely plausible high-multiplicity mirror centered on type-level elicitation, configuration-LP winner determination, and type-cohort PVM payments. But under the programme’s formal anchor rule, I would label it “natural mirror, no qualifying named computational anchor,” rather than claim a fully compliant positive result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative argument is formal and decisive: this paper has no qualifying computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–4 establish efficiency, incentive-alignment, regret, and individual-rationality identities. None gives a complexity classification, an exact or approximation algorithm, a parameterized result, or a hardness theorem. Section 5 only reports that certain integer programs are practically solvable. Thus a continuous PVM would be a new mechanism-design project, not a continuous mirror of a computational result proved by this paper.

Even relaxing that requirement, Proposition 2 does not survive the proposed aggregation without changing the strategic object. In the paper, bidder \(i\) has an individual report history \(\hat\vartheta_i\), can strategically alter it, and thereby affects the adaptive queries, inferred welfare, final allocation, and payment. The proof works because the leave-one-out run \(a^{(-i)}\) is computed in a separate instance that excludes precisely that bidder.

A valuation type \(\theta\) does not determine this state. Two bidders with the same valuation can have different initial queries, report histories, random seeds, or strategic reports. Since the machine-learning estimator operates on those reports, the continuous state would have to be a distribution over valuation–history–strategy tuples, not merely a mass \(\mu_\theta\) over valuation types. The assertion that one query to type \(\theta\) reveals \(v_\theta(x)\) for its entire mass is therefore an additional common-information rule, not a consequence of high multiplicity. It removes the paper’s individual elicitation problem.

There are only two ways to repair this. If all members of a type cohort are forced to submit one common report, the cohort becomes a single strategic meta-bidder. Excluding the whole cohort and charging \(P_\theta/\mu_\theta\) then defines a new coalition mechanism; it is not Proposition 2 applied to repeated bidders. Proposition 2 concerns unilateral deviations while all other bidders’ strategies are fixed. If, instead, individual deviations are retained, an atomless bidder has zero effect on the aggregate allocation and transcript, so every report is effectively a best response and incentive alignment becomes vacuous. A nontrivial marginal-payment or derivative mechanism would be a new construction, especially because adaptive queries and allocation argmaxes are discontinuous.

The proposed configuration LP is mathematically legitimate, but it has discarded precisely the part of the paper that Proposition 2 concerns. Whether its pricing problem is easy or hard is not the objection; either answer could be interesting. The problem is that the LP is now a replicated-market allocation problem with a separately invented information-sharing and payment model. With fixed spectrum licenses, increasing the bidder population makes normalized supply vanish. Keeping the model nondegenerate by imposing per-capita supply \(\rho_j\) creates \(N\rho_j\) copies of every license and hence a different auction. That may be a sensible high-multiplicity auction, but it is not the paper’s setting merely written with masses.

The same problem affects the other propositions. Proposition 1 can be rewritten as an aggregate welfare inequality only after imposing a new common estimator and common type reports. Proposition 3 then becomes a cohort-regret statement, while Proposition 4’s individual rationality no longer follows from aggregate payments unless a rule specifies how heterogeneous bundle assignments and payments are distributed within each cohort. These are valid questions for a new model, not preserved results of this one.

So the positive case has identified a plausible adjacent research direction: a replicated combinatorial auction with cohort agents, configuration-LP allocation, and type-level externality payments. I cannot honestly claim that no such model could ever be worthwhile. But under ChoCo’s stated test, it is not a qualifying mirror: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its one plausible anchor loses its individual strategic meaning under population aggregation.
