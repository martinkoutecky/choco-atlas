What the mirror covers

The proposed computation concerns the sharp joint-distribution bounds in Theorem 1 and treatment-effect bounds in Proposition 5; it does not create a discrete-to-continuous bridge for these results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is to turn the paper’s sharp causal bounds into an explicit optimization problem over population mass. For a finite school set \(J\), finite outcome set \(\mathcal Y\), cutoff \(c_j\), and comparable pair \((j,k)\), give the solver the observed limiting distributions of outcomes and reported local-preference sets on either side of the cutoff, plus the admissible local-preference supports. The variables are masses on latent cells recording the true local preference and potential outcomes. The question is to find the sharp interval for
\[ \mathbb E[Y(j)-Y(k)\mid Q_j=(j,k),\,S_j=c_j] \]
over all cell distributions consistent with the observations and the paper’s assumptions. A solution would give both endpoints and feasible distributions attaining them.

This is a plausible computational question adjacent to the authors’ work. Theorem 1 in Supplemental Appendix A.3, proved in this paper, characterizes sharp restrictions on the joint distributions of potential outcomes and true local preferences. Proposition 5, also proved here, gives bounds on treatment effects when the share of the target local-preference group is bounded away from zero. With finite outcomes and an explicit list of constraints, computing the bounds becomes a finite optimization problem; its complexity under more succinct inputs could be a further question. The proposed problem covers computation of sharp bounds under strategic reporting, not the paper’s empirical estimates or its behavioral model.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the required kind: no numbered result classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 1 and Proposition 5 are identification results, not complexity classifications. They therefore cannot serve as the required anchors.

There is a deeper obstacle to calling this a continuous mirror: the paper already models a continuum population. Its Section 2 assigns a probability measure \(P\) over student types and defines school capacities as population shares. In the Chilean example, the population is large—519,409 students across the study period, with more than 1,000 programs—but a complete type includes preferences, scores, and a potential-outcome function. Scores have continuous distributions, so the paper does not exhibit ChoCo’s characteristic regime of moderate support size \(\tau\) over finitely many complete types. Coarsening scores or outcomes into a manageable type set could change the local RD question the authors study.

So this is a credible computational follow-on: ask how efficiently sharp causal bounds can be computed from the paper’s continuum model. It is not an honest continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The strongest positive case is limited to that follow-on, and the paper’s existing continuum model is the central weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject the proposed anchors as continuous mirrors. The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result: Theorem 1 characterizes an identified set, and Proposition 5 derives bounds on treatment effects. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no discrete computational result here whose population can be continuized.

The strongest proposed version—compute the sharp bounds by optimizing over latent masses—could be a useful computational follow-on. With finite outcomes and explicitly supplied probability tables, it becomes a finite optimization problem. But those masses describe preferences and potential outcomes at the cutoff inside a model that already has a continuum population. They do not turn a discrete society into a continuous one. The input tables, or a succinct encoding designed for them, add a computational problem to the paper; they do not supply the missing discrete-to-continuous mirror. Proposition 5 is the same issue in a simpler form: its finite-outcome bounds reduce to arithmetic on probabilities, while more elaborate complexity questions would concern the encoding and latent-set constraints.

The model also resists a faithful finite-type reconstruction. Student types include scores, and the paper assumes those scores have absolutely continuous distributions; its identification results rely on one-sided limits at admission cutoffs. Keep exact scores and the types are not a moderate finite support. Merge nearby scores into types and the local RD comparison changes. Restrict attention to students at the cutoff and one gets a conditional distribution already used by the paper, not a new continuization of its population.

This does not show that computing sharp bounds could never merit a separate algorithmic paper. It does make the negative case strong on the question asked: that project would study computation within an existing continuum identification model, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
