The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type-interaction graph \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a threshold \(B\), and a fixed positive integer score \(\alpha\), decide whether the mass can be partitioned into finitely many coalition vectors \(x^j\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T}\) such that \(\sum_j x^j_t=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), each coalition’s positive support is a clique of \(H\), and \(\alpha\sum_j(\sum_t x^j_t)^2\geq B\).

The model it lives in

Each type is an atomless mass of network twins: members of a type form a clique and have identical all-or-none links to every other type, as specified by \(H\). A society is the mass distribution \(\mu\); an outcome partitions it into clique-supported coalition masses, and the normalized limiting welfare is \(\alpha\sum_j q_j^2\), where \(q_j=\sum_t x^j_t\).

The objection that survived

For the one-entry score, convexity gives an optimum in which each type’s mass goes wholly to one clique coalition, so the relaxation may add little beyond weighted clique partitioning; this limits its continuum-specific payoff but does not make the mirror ill-posed.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s \(\DGWF\) hardness for a fixed one-entry score; it leaves that theorem’s \(\DGIR\) and \(\DGNS\) hardness results and the paper’s algorithmic results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine whether the mirror is fixed-parameter tractable in the treewidth of \(H\), and how its complexity depends on the support size.
  • Map the complexity boundary for longer-range fixed scoring vectors.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Cohort-Score-SDG-Welfare\(_\infty\), a high-multiplicity version of welfare maximization. Theorem 9, proved in this paper, says that for every fixed scoring vector \(s=(\maxval)\) with \(\maxval>0\), each of \(\DGWF\), \(\DGIR\), and \(\DGNS\) is NP-hard. I rely on its \(\DGWF\) result: given a social network \(G\) and a bound \(b\), decide whether some partition of the agents into coalitions has social welfare at least \(b\). The paper proves this using 3-Coloring Triangle Covered Graph and a reduction to the complement graph. My mirror covers this welfare result, not the theorem’s stability results or the paper’s later algorithms.

In the mirror, a type is a cohort of graph twins: members of one type are mutually adjacent and have the same links to every other type. The input is a type-interaction graph \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a threshold \(B\); the score vector \(s=(\alpha)\), for fixed \(\alpha>0\), is fixed. A coalition is a mass vector \(x^j\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{T}\) whose positive support is a clique of \(H\), and the coalition vectors must satisfy \(\sum_j x^j_t=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\). Thus a type’s mass may be split between coalitions. Writing \(q_j=\sum_t x^j_t\) for a coalition’s total mass, the question is whether an allocation has welfare at least \(B\), with welfare \(\alpha\sum_j q_j^2\); an optimization solution is a finite family of coalition mass vectors attaining the maximum. With this one-entry score vector, any coalition containing nonadjacent types would put some members at distance greater than \(1\), where the paper’s scoring convention assigns \(-\infty\). So requiring clique support preserves the paper’s feasibility condition.

This is the natural aggregate scaling of the original objective: for a finite coalition of size \(r\), welfare is \(\alpha r(r-1)\); dividing total welfare by population size squared gives the limit \(\alpha q^2\). That positive scaling does not change which finite partition maximizes welfare. A credible regime is a large organization or platform where links are determined by role: many members share each role, and the relation between two roles determines all links between their cohorts. There can be \(M\) members per type and \(\tau\) types, with \(M\) much larger than \(\tau\). Type membership records the agents’ complete relevant network position, while mass records their population fraction.

The reduction behind Theorem 9 survives the permission to split mass. Take a 3CTCG instance \(G\) with \(3n\) vertices and \(n\) disjoint triangles, let \(H=\overline{G}\), and give every type mass \(1/(3n)\). A clique in \(H\) contains at most one vertex from each specified triangle, so every coalition has mass at most \(1/3\). Since total mass is \(1\), welfare is at most \(\alpha\sum_j q_j/3=\alpha/3\). A 3-coloring attains \(\alpha/3\): each color class gives a clique of mass \(1/3\).

Conversely, attaining that threshold forces equality in the bound. There must be exactly three nonempty coalitions, each of mass \(1/3\). Each must take a full type from each of the \(n\) triangles to reach that mass. Thus the types in each triangle are assigned one to each coalition; splitting a type cannot fill a triangle’s full-mass slot. The three coalitions therefore yield a 3-coloring of \(G\). This gives NP-hardness for the continuous decision problem by the same reduction. I expect hardness to transfer: the hard combinatorics lives in the type-interaction graph, while the mass variables do not help at the tight welfare threshold. This is a Class B mirror, not a claim of continuum-specific hardness.

I think the authors would recognize this as their welfare problem with a population of exchangeable agents. The distance-based utility, score vector, coalition formation, and welfare objective all remain; only the partition of individuals becomes an allocation of type mass. The precise further questions are whether the mirror is fixed-parameter tractable in structural parameters of \(H\), such as its treewidth, and how the result changes for longer-range score vectors.

The weak point is the regime’s symmetry: arbitrary social networks need not have large cohorts with identical links, and the reduction’s type count grows with its input. So this supports a hard mirror for high multiplicity across cohorts, not a claim that few-type instances are hard or that the model fits every social network.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the mirror covers a narrow network regime. For many social networks, agents sharing a role do not have identical links to every person in every other role. Preserving the paper’s exact distances then requires finer types, potentially one per agent. The proposed model instead makes the network a deterministic blow-up of a type graph: each type is a clique, and links between types are complete or absent. The reduction also has a number of types that grows with the hard instance. That limits what it says about societies with many people but few types.

The one-entry score makes this restriction especially consequential. Any coalition containing a nonadjacent pair has welfare \(-\infty\), so the continuous problem is weighted clique partitioning of the type graph. Its welfare formula is sound: after normalizing by population size squared, it is \(\alpha\sum_j q_j^2\), where \(q_j\) is coalition mass. But fractional splitting adds little freedom here. Represent allocations by assigning each type’s mass among the clique coalitions it can join; the feasible choices form a product of simplices, and the objective is convex. A maximum therefore exists at an extreme point, where each type’s mass goes wholly to one coalition. This caps the mirror’s continuum-specific payoff, though it does not invalidate the question.

Those objections do not defeat the best version of the mirror. A deterministic role-based network—say, an organization where every member of a role has the same direct contacts—makes true-twin cohorts a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The type graph then preserves the relevant adjacency and distance information exactly. The proposed reduction also survives mass splitting: in the 3CTCG construction, each type has mass \(1/(3n)\), each clique coalition has mass at most \(1/3\), and welfare reaches \(\alpha/3\) exactly when the types form three full-mass clique classes. Those classes give a 3-coloring.

So the negative case can argue that this is a specialized Class B mirror with limited continuum-specific payoff. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: Theorem 9 is a named computational result, and the role-based blow-up is a coherent population regime the authors could recognize. Under the stated standard, the anchor survives.
