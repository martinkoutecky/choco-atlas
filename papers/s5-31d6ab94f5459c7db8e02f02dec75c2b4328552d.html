The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and ordinal rankings of types promised to be induced by at least one metric \(d\) on \(T\), compute a mass-pairing plan \(x\) from \((T,\mu,\succ)\). Require \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and require \(V_d(x)\ge \frac{5}{8}\mathrm{OPT}_d(\mu)\) for every compatible \(d\), where \(V_d(x)=\sum_{t<u}x_{tu}d(t,u)\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_d(\mu)\) is the maximum value over feasible mass-pairing plans.

The model it lives in

A society consists of rational mass on a small set of repeated metric profiles. A plan pairs mass between types, with \(x_{tt}\) representing same-type pairs; each unit of mass still receives one partner. The plan uses only the type rankings and masses, while its value is evaluated against any compatible hidden metric. The target is the paper's \(8/5\) approximation guarantee against the best fractional mass pairing.

What the mirror covers

Covers only Theorem 3.1's metric Maximum Weighted Matching result; leaves the Max \(k\)-Matching, Max \(k\)-Sum, Densest \(k\)-Subgraph, and Max TSP results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an ordinal mass-pairing plan achieve the \(8/5\) guarantee against the fractional optimum for every compatible metric?
  • Can such a plan be computed in time polynomial in the number of types and the mass encoding length?
  • What finite-to-continuous rounding or convergence result relates these plans to the paper's integral matchings?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the paper’s maximum-weight matching result. It keeps the paper’s central objective—maximize the total hidden metric weight of matched pairs—and its information restriction—algorithms see rankings, not weights.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that for every input ranking induced by metric weights, the authors’ randomized algorithm gives an expected \(8/5\)-approximation to Maximum Weighted Matching. The algorithm first greedily matches two-thirds of the agents, then flips a fair coin: either it randomly matches the remaining agents among themselves, or it keeps a random half of the greedy matching and randomly matches the released agents across to the unmatched agents. The guarantee is against the maximum-weight matching for every hidden metric consistent with the rankings. The paper has no named NP-hardness result for this problem; its relevant anchor is therefore algorithmic.

The continuous version I would ask is Ordinal Type-Mass Matching\(_{8/5}\). A large student population, for example, could be divided into recurring skill or opinion profiles. Let \(T\) be the \(\tau\) profiles, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each profile \(t\) has an ordinal ranking \(\succ_t\) of the other profiles. The rankings are promised to be consistent with at least one hidden nonnegative metric \(d\) on \(T\): if \(u\succ_t v\), then \(d(t,u)\ge d(t,v)\). The metric is not given to the algorithm. Here a type is a complete profile for this problem: agents of type \(t\) have the same metric weight to every other type.

The solution is a rational mass-pairing plan \(x\), based only on \((T,\mu,\succ)\). For each pair of types, \(x_{tu}\) is the mass of pairs formed between them; \(x_{tt}\) is the mass of same-type pairs. Feasibility requires, for every \(t\),
\[ 2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}=\mu_t. \]
For a compatible metric \(d\), its value is \(V_d(x)=\sum_{t<u}x_{tu}d(t,u)\), and let \(\mathrm{OPT}_d(\mu)\) be the maximum value over all feasible mass-pairing plans. The task is to find one \(x\) such that
\[ V_d(x)\ge \frac{5}{8}\mathrm{OPT}_d(\mu) \]
for every compatible \(d\). The fractional entries describe what share of each cohort gets each partner type; each individual still receives one integral partner.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a district may have hundreds of thousands of students but only a few dozen recurring profiles, and diversity of skills or opinions is still the reason to pair people. The paper itself uses student team formation as an application. Grouping interchangeable students into types preserves the hidden-metric matching objective and the ordinal input. It does change the output from a particular matching to a type-level mass plan, which is the natural population-scale version.

I expect this \(8/5\)-feasibility problem to be Class A. The paper’s greedy, within-group random, and cross-group random steps appear compressible to type masses: greedy steps can consume batches of a type pair, and the random completions have finite type-level coupling formulas. Averaging the discrete algorithm’s random matchings also gives a deterministic mass plan, since value is linear in the plan. The natural complexity target is polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass encoding length \(L\), rather than in the number of represented individuals. This argument predicts tractability; it is not a theorem established in the paper.

The scope is deliberately just Theorem 3.1’s Maximum Weighted Matching result. I would not claim that this also continuizes the paper’s \(k\)-matching, clustering, densest-subgraph, or TSP results.

The weakest point is the benchmark: Theorem 3.1 compares an integral matching with an integral optimum, while this mirror compares a mass plan with a fractional coupling optimum. The discrete guarantee does not by itself prove the continuous one. A proof must extend the metric charging bounds to mass flows, or establish that the high-multiplicity discrete optima converge to the fractional benchmark; the type-level algorithm also has to avoid expanding the population. That is a real proof obligation, but it follows directly from the paper’s model and its matching algorithm. A useful next question is whether fractional mass plans improve the best possible ordinal guarantee, and how to state the exact finite-to-continuous rounding relationship.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is a modeling one. Theorem 3.1 is a real algorithmic result, so there is no basis for dismissing it as merely axiomatic. But its agents are points in a metric space, and each agent’s ranking is over named potential partners. A type for this problem must preserve the relevant pairwise weights, not just a broad label such as “similar skills.” Grouping students by broad profile can therefore erase exactly the variation that determines the matching value. Keeping the full interaction pattern may instead leave almost every student as a separate type.

The proponent’s proposed type model also narrows the paper’s setting: a metric on types gives two members of the same type distance zero. That is a coherent model of repeated, identical points, and might fit cohorts of exact clones. It is less natural when students with the same recorded profile still have positive or varying compatibility with each other. A stronger mirror could use block-constant pair weights, including positive within-type values, but that requires a special exchangeable geometry rather than following directly from the paper’s metric-on-agents model. These concerns limit the range of persuasive scenarios; they do not rule out the clone or cohort scenario.

There is also a gap in the claimed transfer of the \(8/5\) guarantee. Theorem 3.1 compares its randomized algorithm with an integral optimal matching. Averaging its outputs gives a mass plan guaranteed against that integral benchmark. The proposed mirror compares against a fractional coupling optimum. The displayed mass-balance constraints allow fractional matchings that need not correspond to any finite integral matching, so the theorem alone does not establish the proposed guarantee. The authors would need to prove the limit carefully, or define the continuous feasible set as limits of realizable finite matchings.

That is the best negative case: the anchor needs a deliberately exchangeable population model, and its advertised guarantee does not follow just by averaging the paper’s algorithm. But a finite set of repeated metric profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a corrected mass model is available. Replication and a limit argument may well establish its guarantee. So I cannot honestly defeat every version of this anchor. The negative case is weak under ChoCo’s standard: the paper’s model does not itself supply a compelling type structure, but the better cohort mirror remains worth asking about.
