Why no mirror

Theorem 1 qualifies as computational because its LP characterization is used to calculate examples. The proposed mirror makes each type a bloc that sets one price for a positive mass of voters, while the paper’s strategic actors are individual players. In a nonatomic population, an individual’s price change has a different effect, so the proposed LP is a coherent extension but does not preserve the paper’s bargaining question.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is for a continuous version of Young’s canonical price problem in weighted voting. The natural regime is a large membership body—say, a federation or cooperative—with many members but only a modest number \(\tau\) of voting-weight and reservation-price classes. Members in a class have the same vote weight and minimum acceptable payment. The relevant quantities are then percentages of each class: a lobbyist buys a winning share of support, rather than a named list of individuals.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that every finite voting game without veto players, given positive floor prices, has a canonical equilibrium price vector, and that this vector is unique in general. The proof characterizes canonical prices through the linear program in equation (16) and its dual in equation (17), and the paper uses that formulation to compute examples. This is an existence and formulation result, not a claim that the problem is in P or NP-hard. It is the strongest anchor here because the paper offers no comparable named complexity result.

I would hand a prover this question: Continuous Canonical Prices for Weighted Voting. An instance has types \(t\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational voting weights \(w_t>0\), a quota \(q\), and positive rational floor prices \(\ell_t\). A coalition is a mass vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\); it wins if \(\sum_t w_tx_t\ge q\), and at type prices \(p\) costs \(\sum_t p_tx_t\). Let \(\mathcal W\) be the set of winning mass vectors and let \(\mathcal C_0\) be those minimizing cost at the floor prices. Define the guaranteed critical mass of type \(t\) by \(\kappa_t=\min_{x\in\mathcal C_0}x_t\), and let \(K=\{t:\kappa_t>0\}\).

The question is to find canonical type prices \(p\) and a least-cost winning mass vector at those prices. Define the canonical price vectors as those satisfying \(p_t\ge\ell_t\), \(p_t=\ell_t\) for \(t\notin K\), and \(p\cdot x\le p\cdot y\) for every \(x\in\mathcal C_0\) and \(y\in\mathcal W\). Among them, maximize \(\sum_{t\in K}\kappa_t(p_t-\ell_t)\), the price premium on mass guaranteed to be recruited in every floor-price-minimum coalition. Return a maximizing \(p\) and any \(p\)-minimum-cost winner; if the objective is unbounded, report that with a certificate. This keeps the paper’s buyer, winning condition, floor prices, and canonical price objective while making coalition size fractional.

I expect the bounded instances to be Class A. The price constraints have a separation procedure: for a proposed \(p\), maximize \(p\cdot x\) over \(\mathcal C_0\) and minimize \(p\cdot y\) over \(\mathcal W\). Both are linear programs in \(\tau\) variables, so an ellipsoid approach can optimize the outer linear objective. That is a proposed tractability route, not a result of Theorem 1. A main follow-up is to prove whether this LP is actually the limit of the paper’s finite canonical-price LPs under repeated type proportions, and at what rate. Other natural questions are whether comparable separation works for compactly represented non-threshold games, and how continuous equilibrium prices determine aggregate type incomes when several winning mass vectors tie.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it is still the lobbyist buying the cheapest winning coalition and members bargaining over the price of their voting power. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s canonical-price existence and computation, restricted to a finite-support weighted voting society; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s full treatment of arbitrary simple games, its income comparisons, or Theorem 2.

The soft spot is the meaning of “critical.” In the finite paper it concerns named players; here it concerns positive mass that must appear in every floor-price-minimum coalition. That is a natural aggregate definition, but Theorem 1 does not establish that it captures the limit of individual bargaining in a large electorate. The finite-to-continuous comparison is therefore the key proof obligation. If it fails, the price problem remains a coherent continuous market model, but the claim that it is the high-multiplicity mirror of Young’s result would be weaker.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The one anchor is Theorem 1, but it is an existence theorem with an LP characterization, not a computational-complexity result. The paper gives no running-time bound, hardness result, or input representation for computing prices in an arbitrary simple game. Equation (16) is indexed by winning coalitions; without a representation of the game and a way to optimize over those coalitions, it does not itself establish an algorithm. Theorem 2 adds a structural bound, not a complexity result. So the proposed mirror starts by supplying a computational problem the paper did not formulate.

The weighted-voting version is a sensible question in its own right, but its type-level price changes the unit of bargaining. The paper’s players quote prices individually; the proponent assigns one price to an entire type. A simple majority example shows the gap. Take \(n\) identical voters, each with floor price \(\ell>0\), and quota \(k<n\). Every minimum-cost winning coalition at floor prices is a \(k\)-subset, so no named voter belongs to every such coalition. In the paper’s canonical-price characterization, no voter can carry a premium above \(\ell\). In the proposed continuum, there is one type of mass \(1\), quota \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and the floor-price-minimum winner has mass \(\alpha\). Thus \(\kappa=\alpha>0\). Every price \(p\ge\ell\) satisfies the proposed canonical constraints, and the objective \(\alpha(p-\ell)\) is unbounded.

That mismatch is not a complaint that the continuous answer differs from the discrete one. It shows that the proposed \(\kappa_t\) replaces “this named player is unavoidable” with “some positive amount of this class is needed.” The former is the paper’s source of bargaining power; the latter makes the whole interchangeable class act as a supplier. The resulting LP may be useful, but it describes class-level market power rather than a high-multiplicity version of individual price-setting.

There are two natural repairs, and neither preserves the anchor cleanly. Keep individual price-setting and let each member’s mass tend to zero, and a single member’s quote cannot affect the winning coalition’s aggregate cost; the paper’s individual pivotality question loses its bite. Keep positive guaranteed mass as the basis for premiums, and the class must bargain as an aggregate, changing who the strategic actors are. One could study a limit of finite canonical-price correspondences or a market model for bloc bargaining, but either is a new question, not the computation of Young’s stated result over a continuous population.

The negative case has a real limit: a large federation with many interchangeable members could make a continuous bloc-market model plausible, and the proposed LP could be worth investigating. But that scenario does not repair the missing computational result in the paper or the shift from individual players to priced classes. As a computational mirror of this paper, the case against is strong; as an independent continuous model of lobbying, it is less conclusive.
