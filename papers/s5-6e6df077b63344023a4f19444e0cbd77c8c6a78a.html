What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover the ultrafilter characterization in Theorem 4.9 and the finite-society dictatorship consequence in Corollary 4.11; the paper’s other results are also axiomatic rather than computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: no theorem or proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. So I cannot give a continuous computational problem per anchor without inventing one the paper does not establish.

The closest result is Theorem 4.9, proved here. It says that for any society \(N\), an aggregation function on complete \(T\)-transitive fuzzy preferences that satisfies weak Pareto and IIA induces a unique ultrafilter of decisive coalitions. If it also satisfies strong Pareto, the social preference degree obeys the stated lower bound over that ultrafilter. Corollary 4.11, also proved here, specializes to finite societies: such a function is dictatorial, and under strong Pareto it is strong-dictatorial. These are axiomatic possibility and impossibility results, not computational-complexity results.

There is a limited foothold for a population-continuum reading: the paper allows non-finite societies, and a plausible regime could group many agents by their complete fuzzy preference, with each type’s mass representing its population share. But its results reason about coalitions and ultrafilters over agents, not algorithms on a finite-support distribution of types. Replacing those coalitions with masses would change the question, and the resulting aggregation theorem would remain axiomatic rather than computational. That is outside ChoCo’s stated scope.

So the strongest honest positive case is only that the paper studies aggregation over arbitrary-sized societies. It does not supply a named computational anchor for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no computational result to anchor one. Theorem 4.9 characterizes aggregation functions satisfying axioms; Corollary 4.11 gives the finite society’s dictatorship consequence. Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s non-finite societies do not change that: they are arbitrary sets of agents, not distributions over voter types.

The best population-based reading would group agents by their complete fuzzy preference and record each group’s mass. But Theorem 4.9’s central object is an ultrafilter of decisive *coalitions of named agents*. A type distribution does not encode that object. For any finite partition into types, an ultrafilter selects one cell, but the masses do not tell us which cell it selects. If we instead require coalitions to be measurable and decisions to depend only on mass, we have added a different aggregation model and must ask new axiomatic questions; the paper’s theorem does not supply a computational problem for it. Corollary 4.11 sharpens the mismatch: its dictator is an individual, and a mass distribution cannot identify that agent.

A charitable follow-on could introduce a finite representation of aggregation rules and ask how to compute their outputs, or impose anonymity and study which axioms remain compatible. But those would be newly specified computational or axiomatic projects, not continuous mirrors of the results proved here. The paper’s fuzzy degrees are continuous-valued preferences, while its society is not modeled as a continuous population. That distinction matters.

This is a strong negative case for *this paper as a ChoCo anchor*, not a proof that no related project could be worthwhile. The universal claim remains hard to establish: a carefully chosen explicit representation might yield a useful computational question. The paper itself, however, gives no reason to expect such a question to be its continuous mirror.
