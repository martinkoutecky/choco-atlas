The continuous mirror question

Given finite \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_s\) for single-resident types and \(\nu_\kappa\) for couple types, rational hospital capacity rates \(q_h\), complete type-level rankings, and preference lists, decide whether nonnegative variables \(x_{s,h}\) and \(y_{\kappa,(h,h')}\), plus unassigned mass, satisfy the type row sums and hospital loads \(\ell_h\le q_h\), while no positive-mass portion has an MM-blocking deviation under the measure versions of Definition 1. Study this problem also under the paper's no-single, list-length-two restriction.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a population-continuized HRC problem under the paper’s MM-stability notion. My lead anchor is Theorem 1.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says:

Given an instance of \((2,2)\)-hrc, deciding whether there exists a stable matching is NP-complete. This remains true when there are no single residents and every hospital has capacity \(1\).

The natural mirror is a high-multiplicity Hospitals/Residents problem with Couples.

Let \(H\) be a finite set of hospitals. There are finitely many single-resident types \(S\) and couple types \(K\). A single type \(s\in S\) specifies its acceptable hospitals and preference order. A couple type \(\kappa\in K\) specifies its two member profiles and a joint preference order over acceptable hospital pairs \((h,h')\), including possibly \(h=h'\). Each type has rational mass \(\mu_s\) or \(\nu_\kappa\). Hospitals have rational capacity rates \(q_h\), so a population of scale \(N\) gives hospital \(h\) capacity \(Nq_h\). Hospital preferences are over resident types, including the two member types of each couple type.

An aggregate matching consists of variables

\[ x_{s,h}\ge 0 \]

for mass of single type \(s\) assigned to \(h\), and

\[ y_{\kappa,(h,h')}\ge 0 \]

for mass of couple type \(\kappa\) assigned jointly to \((h,h')\). There are also unassigned variables. The row sums equal the available type masses, and hospital loads satisfy

\[ \ell_h = \sum_s x_{s,h} + \sum_{\kappa,(a,b)} y_{\kappa,(a,b)} \bigl(\mathbf 1[a=h]+\mathbf 1[b=h]\bigr) \le q_h. \]

The crucial point is that \(y_{\kappa,(h,h')}\) moves both members together. This is not fractionalizing the couple’s outcome: each infinitesimal couple receives an integral hospital pair. Only the population is continuous.

The matching is continuously MM-stable if no positive-mass portion of a resident or couple type has an MM-blocking deviation. The paper’s Definition 1 is used with the following measure substitutions:

  • “hospital \(h\) is under-subscribed” means \(\ell_h<q_h\);
  • “\(h\) prefers \(r\) to some assigned resident” means that \(h\) has positive assigned mass of resident types ranked below \(r\);
  • in the same-hospital cases, a positive capacity gap can accommodate two infinitesimal members;
  • in the full-hospital case, the required two witnesses are two distinct infinitesimal assigned residents satisfying the two preference comparisons.

Thus the continuous problem is:

\[ \mathrm{MM\text{-}HRC}_{\infty}\text{-Existence} \]

Given rational type masses, hospital capacities, preference lists, and MM-stability data, decide whether there exists a feasible aggregate matching with no blocking deviation.

For the direct analogue of Theorem 1, restrict to no single-resident types, couple lists of length at most \(2\), hospital preference lists of length at most \(2\), and capacities obtained by scaling the unit capacities of the finite model. A solution is a rational mass assignment \(x,y\) satisfying the capacity, couple-consistency, and zero-blocking conditions.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Consider a large medical allocation system with \(10^5\) or more applicants, including many dual-career couples, but a finite catalogue of hospital programmes, specialties, geographic zones, score classes, and preference templates. A couple type records everything relevant: both specialties, joint geographic priorities, acceptable hospital pairs, and any hospital-ranking attributes. The number of agents is much larger than the number of distinct types. Scaling hospital capacities with the cohort is natural rather than cosmetic.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. It retains hospitals, capacities, couples, joint hospital-pair preferences, and precisely the stability obstruction that makes HRC difficult. It does not replace the problem by a generic fractional assignment or by a linear relaxation of the paper’s IP. The IP variables in the paper are binary and indexed by named residents; here the variables are population masses, while the underlying bundle assigned to each resident or couple remains discrete.

My expectation is Class B: hardness should plausibly transfer. The reduction behind Theorem 1 encodes variables and clauses in the preference graph and in couples’ joint choices, not in large numerical population counts. The natural proof obligation is an endpoint lemma showing that the continuous gadgets cannot evade the Boolean choice by splitting one couple type across both options. If that lemma holds, the finite reduction can be scaled to a high-multiplicity population. If it fails, that failure would itself be an informative Class A result: it would show that the NP-hardness was caused by indivisible named residents rather than by the structure of couples and blocking.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in this paper. It states:

\(\mathrm{min\text{-}bp\text{-}hrc}\) is not approximable within \(n_1^{1-\varepsilon}\), for any \(\varepsilon>0\), unless \(P=NP\), even when there are no single residents and the resident-couple and hospital preference lists have length at most \(2\).

Here \(n_1\) is the number of residents. The proof uses many disjoint copies of the Theorem 1 construction together with the small instance in Figure 2, producing a gap between one blocking pair and at least \(k+1\) blocking pairs.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \mathrm{Min\text{-}BP\text{-}HRC}_{\infty}. \]

Given the same type-distribution instance, find a feasible aggregate matching minimizing normalized blocking mass. If \(x_{s,h_0}\) is mass of single type \(s\) currently assigned to \(h_0\), let \(\mathcal B_s(h_0;x)\) be the set of hospital deviations that MM-block for that assignment under the aggregate matching \(x\). Define \(\mathcal B_\kappa(p_0;x)\) analogously for a couple type currently assigned to pair \(p_0\). The objective is

\[ \operatorname{BP}_{\infty}(x) = \sum_{s,h_0} x_{s,h_0}\, |\mathcal B_s(h_0;x)| + \sum_{\kappa,p_0} y_{\kappa,p_0}\, |\mathcal B_\kappa(p_0;x)|. \]

Unassigned mass is treated as having the worst possible assignment. Equivalently, if the population scale is \(N\), then \(N\operatorname{BP}_{\infty}(x)\) is the high-multiplicity analogue of the number of blocking pairs. The question is to compute the minimum, or to approximate it within a factor such as \(N^{1-\varepsilon}\).

I would again initially expect hardness to transfer, because the gap construction is made from independent preference gadgets and not from accidental differences between named individuals. But this is less secure than the existence mirror: divisible mass may average across copies and weaken the one-blocking-pair versus many-blocking-pairs gap. Establishing whether the gap survives is itself a worthwhile result.

The main weakness is that exact repeated types naturally create ties among otherwise named residents, whereas Theorem 1 is stated for strict HRC preferences. I would handle this by using strict rankings over resident types and the paper’s HRCT/MM convention for within-type ties. That is a natural high-multiplicity co-modification, and the paper explicitly defines the ties extension, but it means the mirror is not literally the same finite strict-instance class. The second weakness is more substantive: fractional splitting may destroy the Boolean forcing in the reduction. I would acknowledge that openly rather than claiming Theorem 1 automatically transfers.

That weakness does not undermine the mirror itself. Whether \(\mathrm{MM\text{-}HRC}_{\infty}\)-Exist is hard, polynomial, or continuum-specifically hard is precisely the computational question created by continuization. Further questions include whether MM-stability has a finite LP or complementarity formulation, whether BIS-stability behaves differently under mass aggregation, whether the master-list and dual-market restrictions remain hard, and whether a discrete stable matching can be recovered from a continuous solution with only controlled additive error.

This case deliberately covers Theorems 1 and 2, not the paper’s IP correctness theorems, experiments, or cloning result. The paper’s named hardness results are the right anchors: they leave a real discrete-to-continuous question rather than merely inviting us to rewrite an existing encoding.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case establishes that a high-multiplicity HRC model can be written down. It does not establish that either theorem survives as a worthwhile mirror. Both hardness results depend on atomic, named couples and capacity-one posts; the natural continuum removes precisely that structure.

For Theorem 1, the reduction forces each couple to make one Boolean choice. In the continuous model, a type \(\kappa\) can assign mass \(x_{\kappa,p}\) to several hospital pairs. Thus the same nominal couple type can be partly “true” and partly “false”. Scaling capacities from \(1\) to \(N\) does not repair this: it merely gives \(N\) copies of each role, which may divide their mass between the two choices.

The paper’s own Figure 2 exhibits the problem. In the finite instance, no stable matching exists. In the natural mass version, assign half the couple mass to \((z_1,z_2)\) and leave half unassigned; assign half of \(c\)'s mass to \(z_1\) and half to \(z_2\). Both hospitals are full. The unassigned couple mass cannot block through \((z_1,z_2)\), because \(z_2\) prefers \(c\) to the couple’s second member. The mass of \(c\) assigned to \(z_2\) cannot move to \(z_1\), because \(z_1\) prefers the couple’s first member to \(c\). The continuous instance is therefore stable although the finite instance is not.

This is not merely an inconvenient change in the answer. It destroys the object encoded by the reduction: an atomic couple’s joint choice. Enforcing \(x_{\kappa,p}\in\{0,\mu_\kappa\}\) would preserve that choice, but it would also reintroduce the integer problem. Treating each cohort as indivisible makes mass a passive weight, not a population continuum. Adding a distinct priority coordinate to every clone has the same problem in another form: once the coordinate identifies each copy, the type space has recovered the named-agent structure.

The tie repair does not solve this. If identical clones are genuine copies, hospital priorities necessarily tie between them, and the HRCT/MM convention permits the fractional splitting above. If exact strict priorities are required, the copies must be distinguished by additional type information. The proponent therefore faces a real dichotomy: allow genuine high multiplicity and lose the theorem’s atomic Boolean gadgets, or preserve the gadgets by abandoning high multiplicity.

The same objection is even sharper for Theorem 2. “Number of blocking pairs” has no canonical continuum analogue. Counting individual blockers gives an uncountable quantity; counting blocking type relations ignores population multiplicity; and weighting them by mass produces a new objective, such as blocking mass. Under that latter interpretation, the Figure 2 construction has zero blocking mass in the fractional allocation above, whereas its finite optimum is one. The one-versus-\(k+1\) gap used by Theorem 2 consequently disappears before the disjoint-copy amplification begins.

One could define a sensible new problem minimizing the mass of agents involved in blocking relations. But that is not the paper’s minimum-blocking-pairs problem with counts replaced by masses. It is a new fluid stability objective whose value depends on whether arbitrarily small portions of a type may be sacrificed. Preventing such vanishing portions again requires lower bounds or all-or-nothing type assignments, which return to the discrete model.

The master-list and dual-market restrictions do not help: they simplify the preference descriptions but retain the same capacity-one gadgets and the same dependence on indivisible couple choices. A medical story with many repeated preference templates is plausible, so I would not object to the population regime on sociological grounds. The objection is semantic and computational: the natural atomless version is a fractional equilibrium problem, not the coupled stable-matching problem whose hardness the paper proves.

This negative case is not airtight. A fractional HRC theory could itself be valuable, perhaps as a Class A or Class C problem. But then its value comes from inventing and analysing a new notion of fractional stability, not from continuizing Theorems 1 and 2 faithfully. The strongest honest conclusion is therefore that this paper is a poor source for a direct ChoCo mirror, even though a separate continuum matching project may be worthwhile.
