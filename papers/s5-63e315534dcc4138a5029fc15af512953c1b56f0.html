The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Table 2, “Acceptance Probability under QMV.” The paper calculates acceptance probabilities for concrete Council rules and uses the resulting odds to argue that enlargement can make agreement much harder. Those calculations are a substantive computational result even though the paper does not state a complexity theorem. The square-root rule is less suitable as an anchor because the paper attributes it to Penrose and earlier work; the case for a mirror does not need it.

A faithful high-multiplicity version keeps the agents as voting jurisdictions. Consider a large federation with many jurisdictions drawn from a finite set of types \(T\), where type \(t\) specifies a jurisdiction’s voting weight \(a_t\) and other rule-relevant attributes. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of jurisdictions of each type. Under the paper’s impartial coalition culture, each jurisdiction independently votes yes or no with probability \(1/2\). For \(N\) jurisdictions, let \(A_N\) be the probability that the yes votes meet a quota \(q>1/2\). The raw probability tends to zero as the federation grows, but the rate of that decay retains the comparison the paper cares about:

\[ \bar a=\sum_t\mu_ta_t,\qquad I_q(\mu,a)=\sup_{\lambda\ge0}\left\{\lambda q\bar a- \sum_t\mu_t\log\frac{1+e^{\lambda a_t}}{2}\right\}. \]

In this setting, \(N^{-1}\log((1-A_N)/A_N)\) converges to \(I_q(\mu,a)\). The continuous question is how to compute that inertia rate from the type distribution, and how it changes when the composition, weights, or quota change. The Nice system’s additional population and member-count thresholds can be included as further type attributes and constraints. That gives a computational problem in support size and input precision, with a convex optimization formulation.

This also answers the proposed objection that the paper’s probabilities concern coalitions rather than populations: the two objects remain distinct. The continuous society describes the proportions of jurisdiction types; the probability law over coalitions is still the paper’s ICC assumption. A large federation of repeated, rule-equivalent jurisdictions is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the same weighted-voting problem. The article’s aggregate concern about institutional capacity and inertia survives there. Individual pivotality may vanish in the limit, so I would not make it the main mirror; the normalized aggregate odds preserve the paper’s central question.

The limitation is that this extends the paper’s computed result rather than mirroring a complexity theorem it proves. Still, Table 2 gives a clear computational anchor, and the paper’s own interpretation of its odds as a measure of the difficulty of reaching agreement makes the rate a natural continuous counterpart.
