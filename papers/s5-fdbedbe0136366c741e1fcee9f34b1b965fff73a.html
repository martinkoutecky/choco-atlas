The continuous mirror question

Given finite \(B\), term sets \(T_b\), complete type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), ordered priority blocks containing otherwise identical agents, and per-capita capacities \(\bar q_b\), compute an allocation \(x_{\theta,b,t}\) and unmatched masses such that no positive mass can block under the paper’s layered chosen-set rule, while every type’s assignment distribution weakly dominates its assignment under any other stable mass allocation.

The model it lives in

Use finite types \(\theta\in\Theta\) carrying mass \(\mu_\theta\), complete preferences over \(B\times T_b\), and ordered priority blocks. Variables \(x_{\theta,b,t}\ge0\) distribute each type’s mass among integral contracts \((b,t)\) or \(\varnothing\), subject to per-capita capacities; the objective is the agent-optimal stable mass allocation, not welfare maximization.

The objection that survived

The paper’s priority is a strict individual OML, so the proposed finite blocks need an explicit cloning or tie-breaking rule; if distinct preference types share a block, arbitrary tie-breaking can change \(x\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a batched cumulative-offer algorithm compute an agent-optimal stable \(x\) in time polynomial in \(|\Theta|\), \(|B|\), \(\sum_b|T_b|\), and the encoding length of the rational data?
  • What exact high-multiplicity dictionary relates rational mass allocations to finite markets formed by cloning types into contiguous strict-priority blocks?
  • Can any meaningful mass-strategy version of Proposition 5 preserve proxy-preference equivalence without replacing individual cadets by strategic cohorts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s cadet-optimal stable mechanism. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem, so this cannot honestly be presented as a test of whether discrete computational hardness dissolves. Its best anchor is instead an explicitly algorithmic structural result.

My lead anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper using Lemma 2 and Theorem 5 of Hatfield and Kojima (2010): “the cumulative offer algorithm produces a stable allocation under the USMA branch priorities. Moreover, this allocation is weakly preferred by any cadet to any stable allocation.” Lemma 2, proved in the paper, establishes that the USMA priorities satisfy unilateral substitutes; that is the condition enabling the cumulative-offer argument despite ordinary substitutability failing.

A natural regime is centralized university admissions of the kind proposed in Section 5.1. There are many applicants, perhaps \(10^5\) or more, but relatively few departments and tuition levels. A type \(\theta\) consists of a complete preference order \(P_\theta\) over department–tuition contracts, together with a priority block \(\rho_\theta\) in the admissions ranking. Applicants in the same type have the same preferences, eligibility, priority class, and contract options. A finite catalogue might contain \(\tau=10^2\) or \(10^3\) types while the population is orders of magnitude larger. Ties within a priority block can be resolved arbitrarily because agents in the block are otherwise identical.

The continuous instance has a rational mass vector \(\mu=(\mu_\theta)_{\theta\in\Theta}\), where \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(\theta\), and per-capita department capacities \(\bar q_b\). For the basic model, department \(b\) has terms \(t^0\) and \(t^+\), with capacities \((1-\lambda_b)\bar q_b\) and \(\lambda_b\bar q_b\). A decision variable \(x_{\theta,b,t}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) assigned department \(b\) under term \(t\), with \(x_{\theta,\varnothing}\) denoting unmatched mass. It must satisfy \(x_{\theta,\varnothing}+\sum_{b,t}x_{\theta,b,t}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,t}x_{\theta,b,t}\le\bar q_b\).

The contracts remain indivisible for each unit of mass: an applicant receives one department–tuition pair. The vector \(x\) records how a homogeneous population is distributed across such assignments; it does not give an individual a fractional department or a fractional tuition contract.

Continuous Cadet-Optimal Stable Allocation. Given \((B,\Theta,\mu,P,\rho,\bar q,\lambda)\), output a feasible mass allocation \(x\) such that no positive mass of any type can move to a more-preferred contract that the corresponding department would accept under the paper’s two-layer priority rule. Among all stable mass allocations, the output should be agent-optimal: for every type \(\theta\), its assignment distribution should first-order stochastically dominate that type’s distribution under any other stable allocation, using \(P_\theta\).

The continuous department choice rule is exactly the paper’s chosen-set rule applied to mass: first select the prescribed base-cost mass by priority, then prioritize applicants willing to accept the increased term for the remaining slots, and finally fill unused capacity with lower-term contracts when necessary. Stability is therefore a genuine mass version of the paper’s stability notion, not a replacement by welfare maximization.

I expect this problem to be Class A. The cumulative-offer algorithm can process a whole type’s mass at once. Each type–contract pair is proposed at most once, and branch decisions occur when a rational mass threshold is reached. Thus the computation should be polynomial in \(|B|\), \(\tau\), the number of terms, and the encoding length of the rational masses and capacities, rather than in the number of individual applicants. Lemmas 1 and 2 are precisely the structural facts needed for this compression: the law of aggregate demand survives, and the unilateral-substitutes argument continues to rule out problematic rejection reversals.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem. It preserves their branches, capacities, priority-based allocation, voluntary high-cost contracts, stability concept, and cadet-optimality criterion. It merely replaces repeated individual applicants by their mass and scales capacities per capita. It is especially plausible in the paper’s own centralized-admissions application, where many students compete for a finite set of departments and some seats may carry higher tuition. It is not an outcome-space relaxation: every unit of population still receives an integral contract.

The mirror deliberately covers Proposition 1 and its supporting Lemmas 1 and 2. I would not claim that it automatically mirrors Proposition 5 or the Nash-equilibrium pathologies of the USMA mechanism. A continuum game in which positive masses choose branch rankings and branch-of-choice contracts would be a separate question, and aggregation may change equilibrium incentives substantially. One natural follow-up is whether the equilibrium/proxy-preference equivalence in Proposition 5 survives for mass strategy profiles.

The weakest point is the population regime. Literal USMA cadets may have nearly unique order-of-merit positions and highly individualized preferences, in which case the number of complete types grows with the population and the high-multiplicity gain disappears. The case therefore depends on the paper’s centralized-admissions or cohort-based interpretation, with repeated preference and priority categories. If that regime is accepted, the proposed problem is a faithful continuous-population version of the paper’s central mechanism result; if not, this remains a mathematically clean but weakly motivated mirror rather than a convincing ChoCo instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is weaker than it appears. Proposition 1 is not a computational-complexity result: it gives existence, stability, and agent-optimality for a finite matching mechanism. The paper states no running-time bound, hardness result, approximation problem, or optimization formulation. Calling the cumulative-offer algorithm “computational” in the broad sense does not make it a ChoCo complexity anchor.

There is also a three-way incompatibility between the paper’s mechanism, high multiplicity, and a nontrivial continuous problem.

If the order-of-merit list \(\pi\) is preserved exactly, then priority is part of an agent’s complete type. Two cadets with different ranks are not indistinguishable: a capacity cutoff can accept one and reject the other. Since \(\pi\) is a strict ranking, the number of relevant priority types grows with the number of cadets. Replacing \(\pi\) by a continuum of priority percentiles merely gives a distribution over ranks; it does not produce the finite-type high-multiplicity object central to the proposed mirror. The resulting problem is a quantile version of deferred acceptance, not the paper’s finite-type computational relaxation.

The proponent’s alternative—finite priority blocks—is a legitimate new model, but it changes the paper’s strict priority structure. Stability and fairness must now be defined with weak priorities or a tie-breaking rule. If the tie-breaking is hidden, the mass vector does not determine the outcome; if it is discarded because agents in a block are “otherwise identical,” then one has changed the mechanism. This is not fatal as a modelling exercise, but it means Proposition 1 has not simply been continuized.

More importantly, once the block model is accepted, the proposed computation is only weighted deferred acceptance. Each type–contract pair is proposed at most once, so the batch algorithm has roughly \(O(\tau m k)\) proposal events, with rational mass arithmetic, where \(m\) is the number of branches and \(k\) the number of terms. There is no exponential type space, implicit optimization problem, separation oracle, or population-driven combinatorial obstacle. Lemmas 1 and 2 establish correctness of the choice rule; they do not create a continuous computational phenomenon. The continuum is functioning as a compressed input encoding for an already elementary finite algorithm.

The same problem affects the stronger results one might try to mirror. Proposition 3’s strategy-proofness becomes vacuous for an atomless population: one cadet’s report changes no positive mass and hence cannot change the aggregate allocation. If a positive mass of a type jointly changes its report, that is a coalition or a strategic cohort, not the individual incentive problem in the paper. Proposition 4 has the same issue: improving one cadet’s priority has measure zero, while improving a positive mass of priorities is a different comparative-statics statement. Proposition 5 could support a genuinely new continuum game only by making type masses strategic players, which abandons the paper’s individual-cadet interpretation.

Thus every route loses something essential. Retaining exact priorities eliminates finite multiplicity; collapsing priorities into blocks changes the mechanism; retaining individual strategic properties makes them measure-zero and vacuous; and retaining only the aggregate allocation yields a routine weighted implementation with no named computational result to mirror.

The honest weakness in this negative case is that the centralized-admissions interpretation is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. I am not treating existing high-multiplicity work as a novelty collision; it would support the modelling choice. A paper developing weighted matching-with-contracts algorithms or policy sensitivity could therefore be worthwhile. But that would be a new high-multiplicity implementation or comparative-statics paper, not a strong ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results.
