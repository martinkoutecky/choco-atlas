The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, a distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types satisfying the paper's Mallows assumption with dispersion \(\theta\) and ground-truth ranking, and a finite respondent scale \(n\), sample respondents from \(\mu\) and run LDP-KwikSort:RR with \(K\) queries and per-query privacy budget \(\epsilon/K\). Can the type-mass version of the protocol output estimated pairwise comparisons and a KwikSort ranking while retaining Theorem 2's bound of fewer than \(6\lambda_{\mathrm{RR}}\) pairwise-comparison errors with probability at least \(1-2^{-6\lambda_{\mathrm{RR}}}\), where \(\lambda_{\mathrm{RR}}=2\binom{m}{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\epsilon^2K}{(\epsilon+2K)^2}\frac{(\theta^*)^2n}{m(m-1)}\right)\) and \(\theta^*=\frac{\theta}{2-\theta}\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. A finite cohort of \(n\) respondents is sampled from it; each respondent independently answers pairwise queries using RR, and the curator aggregates reports by type to estimate pairwise comparisons and produce a ranking.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2 bounds statistical error for a finite sampled protocol; it does not analyze an input-size algorithm over μ or improve on processing \(nK\) reports.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Mallows-conditioned RR accuracy guarantee in Theorem 2; Theorem 3 has a parallel, relaxed-LiDP analogue. It leaves the paper's runtime accounting, empirical comparisons, and exact Kemeny optimization without a new mass-based result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the report counts be generated from type masses in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,\log n\), while preserving the finite-sample privacy and error guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is for the paper’s local-private approximate rank aggregation, not for rank aggregation in general. Its type is a complete ranking of the alternatives; \(\rho_t\) is the fraction of a large survey or crowdsourcing population with ranking \(t\). This is plausible when many respondents choose among the same small set of alternatives, so \(n\) is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings. Each person still answers locally randomized pairwise queries, and the curator uses the aggregate reports to estimate pairwise margins and return a ranking.

The paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. The strongest available anchors are its named algorithmic utility guarantees, Theorems 2 and 3, both proved here. The runtime discussion in §5.3 supports the case, but is unnumbered.

Lead — Continuous-Mass LDP-KwikSort:RR. An instance consists of \(m\) alternatives, a finite support \(S\) of ranking types with rational masses \(\rho_t\) summing to \(1\), a population scale \(n\) with \(n\rho_t\) integral, a total privacy budget \(\epsilon\), and \(K\) pairwise queries per person. The population satisfies the paper’s Mallows-model promise, with dispersion \(\theta\) and a ground-truth ranking. Each person answers through randomized response, spending \(\epsilon/K\) per query. The protocol must output estimated pairwise margins and an aggregate ranking, with utility measured by average Kendall distance to the population’s rankings.

Theorem 2 says that under the stated Mallows assumption, LDP-KwikSort:RR estimates \(cmp(L)\) with error less than \(6\lambda_{\mathrm{RR}}\) with probability at least \(1-2^{-6\lambda_{\mathrm{RR}}}\), where
\[ \lambda_{\mathrm{RR}} =2\binom{m}{2}\exp\!\left( -\frac{\epsilon^2K}{(\epsilon+2K)^2} \frac{(\theta^*)^2n}{m(m-1)} \right), \qquad \theta^*=\frac{\theta}{2-\theta}. \]
The continuous question is whether a protocol over type masses can give this margin-recovery guarantee while returning a useful aggregate ranking. I expect it to be tractable: the paper’s query processing and post-processing are polynomial in \(n,K,m\), and repeated types offer a natural route to compressed processing in \(\tau\).

A second, parallel question — Continuous-Mass LDP-KwikSort:Lap. Use the same instance and target, but have each person answer queries with the paper’s Laplace-based local randomizer. Theorem 3, also proved here, gives error less than \(6\lambda_{\mathrm{Lap}}\) with probability at least \(1-2^{-6\lambda_{\mathrm{Lap}}}\), where
\[ \lambda_{\mathrm{Lap}} =2\binom{m}{2}\exp\!\left( -(1-e^{-\epsilon/(2K)})^2K \frac{(\theta^*)^2n}{m(m-1)} \right). \]
The corresponding mirror asks for the same guarantee under the paper’s \(\epsilon\)-LiDP privacy notion. I also expect tractability, subject to specifying how the type-mass representation generates the randomized reports.

These questions mirror only the paper’s LDP-KwikSort privacy and utility analysis. They do not claim a continuous result for exact Kemeny optimization. A natural follow-up is whether the reports can be aggregated in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,\log n\), rather than by processing \(nK\) individual answers, while preserving the stated guarantees.

The weak point is substantial: the theorems concern finitely many individually randomized respondents. In an atomless population, averaging independent random responses can remove the very noise their guarantees analyze, and individual privacy also needs a finite person-level interpretation. Keeping a finite report scale \(n\) is therefore a necessary co-modification. It makes this a credible high-multiplicity, mass-represented version of LDP rank aggregation, but a less direct mirror of a strict atomless limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s computational questions, not whether a mass model can be defined. I cannot honestly claim that no scenario works: a large survey or crowdsourcing panel with repeated rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the proponent’s type choice fits it. Nothing about the paper’s local privacy requirement makes those respondents inherently individuated.

Theorem 2 is a utility guarantee for a finite randomized experiment: \(n\) people answer queries, each contributes independent randomized reports, and the error bound depends on \(n\), \(\epsilon\), \(K\), \(m\), and the Mallows assumption. If the population is treated as an atomless measure and reports are averaged at the mass level, the report noise that the theorem bounds disappears; there is also no positive-mass individual to protect. The proponent’s repair—retain a finite scale \(n\), with \(n\rho_t\) people of each type and independent randomizers—is sensible. But it makes the theorem a guarantee about a finite, exchangeable LDP protocol. It does not turn the theorem into a complexity result over a continuous society.

Theorem 3 has the same limitation. Its LiDP guarantee is defined through the effect of changing one person’s answer, and its error analysis again relies on finitely many randomized reports. A finite-scale type model can preserve that interpretation, so the theorem has a legitimate high-multiplicity analogue. But the paper supplies a privacy and statistical-utility analysis, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem over type masses. The Mallows assumption is a distribution for generating rankings; it does not by itself make the society the continuous object of the computation.

The proposed runtime mirror is the closest fit, but §5.3 analyzes the cost of this particular protocol: \(O(nK+m^2+m\log m)\). Its main population-dependent work is soliciting and processing individual reports. A question about aggregating reports by type, or about simulating their count distribution in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,\log n\), is coherent. Yet the paper does not identify a difficult optimization or decision problem over masses; it identifies the cost of handling a distributed stream of privately randomized responses. If the task is to preserve the individual LDP interaction, the protocol still has \(n\) participants. If the task is only to return a ranking from aggregate counts, that is a narrower computational question than the guarantees the paper proves.

So I would argue that this paper is a weak ChoCo anchor: its named results concern privacy and statistical utility, and its explicit complexity analysis is a running-time accounting for one protocol. The better finite-scale mirrors are well-posed, and the survey scenario is convincing; those facts prevent a defensible universal claim that no continuous mirror is worthwhile. My negative case is that the paper offers little of the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.
