Why no mirror

Both theorems are genuine polynomial-time results, but their strategic content depends on one voter changing the tally. The proposed \(1/n\) quantum preserves \(n\) discrete players and compresses their finite game into a mass profile; in the atomless limit unilateral deviations leave the tally unchanged. That changes the equilibrium question, so neither anchor yields a continuous computational mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorems 4.4 and 4.2, supported by the deviation results in Theorems 4.3 and 4.1; none survives as a population-continuous mirror, and the paper's other results are not covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s equilibrium-verification result for fixed-budget multiple-issue QV. It has a plausible large-population setting: a civic platform runs one ballot over several public projects, millions of participants share a common credit budget, and their utility vectors come from a relatively small set of rating templates. A type is a complete utility vector over the outcomes, together with the common mechanism parameters. Its mass is the fraction of participants with that vector. Thus \(n\) can be large while the number \(\tau\) of types stays modest.

There is an important detail: checking a *unilateral* deviation needs to preserve the influence of one person’s ballot. I would encode the society by rational type masses \(\mu_t\), with \(n\mu_t\) integral, and use a one-person mass quantum \(1/n\). A profile is a sparse table \(x_{t,a}\) of the fraction of society of type \(t\) using ballot \(a\), where \(n x_{t,a}\) is integral and \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). This is a continuous mass representation with finite-person granularity, rather than an atomless mean-field game.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper: for fixed-budget multiple-issue QV, deciding whether a supplied profile is a pure Nash equilibrium is in P. Its underlying problem is \(QVNEB\), with budget \(B\), agents \(N\), outcomes \(\Omega\), utilities \(u\), and strategy profile \(v\). The theorem uses Theorem 4.3, also proved here, which says that deciding whether one agent has a profitable deviation, \(DEVIATEB\), is in P.

The corresponding continuous question is Mass-Compressed Fixed-Budget QV Equilibrium Verification. The input is \(\Omega\), \(B\), \(n\), utility vectors \(u_t\in\mathbb Z^\Omega\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a mass profile \(x_{t,a}\), with \(a\in\mathbb Z^\Omega\) satisfying \(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}a_\omega^2\le B\). Define the tally by
\[ S_\omega=n\sum_{t,a}x_{t,a}a_\omega. \]
The winners are the outcomes maximizing \(S_\omega\), with ties resolved uniformly as in the paper. Ask whether \(x\) is a pure Nash equilibrium under a one-person deviation: for every occupied type–ballot pair \((t,a)\) and every feasible ballot \(b\), compare type \(t\)’s expected utility at the current winners with its expected utility after replacing one person’s ballot \(a\) by \(b\), which changes the tally to \(S+b-a\). The answer is yes exactly when none of those deviations is profitable. I expect this compressed problem to be tractable: the paper’s best-response dynamic program can be run once per occupied type–ballot pair rather than once per named voter. The natural bound is polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of occupied pairs, \(|\Omega|\), and the numeric budget \(B\); obtaining a bound polynomial in the *bit length* of \(B\) is a further question.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved here: \(QVNE\), the problem of deciding whether a supplied strategy profile is a pure Nash equilibrium in no-budget multiple-issue QV, is in P. The paper’s Theorem 4.1, also proved here, establishes the supporting result \(DEVIATE\in P\). This anchor deserves its own mirror because no-budget voters pay the quadratic cost of their ballots, so their incentives differ from those in the fixed-budget model.

The corresponding question is Mass-Compressed No-Budget QV Equilibrium Verification. Use the same type masses, population quantum, and sparse mass profile, but allow ballots \(a\in\mathbb Z^\Omega\) without a budget constraint. Given the common price parameter \(\alpha>0\), use the paper’s payoff: expected utility for the uniformly selected winner, minus \(\alpha\sum_\omega a_\omega^2\). The refund can be omitted from the deviation comparison because it does not depend on the deviating voter’s ballot. Ask whether any person represented by an occupied pair \((t,a)\) can improve by changing to any \(b\in\mathbb Z^\Omega\), updating the tally by \(b-a\) and paying the corresponding quadratic cost. I expect this problem to be tractable in the compressed parameters: Theorem 4.1’s polynomial best-response search is the relevant starting point, with one check per occupied type–ballot pair.

These mirrors cover the paper’s Section 4 equilibrium-verification results. They do not continuize the winner criteria, the paper’s collusion analysis, or its outcome space; those are separate parts of the paper, and Theorem 3.1 is not a computational-complexity anchor. The high-multiplicity setting is plausible for the authors’ problem: the paper motivates QV for practical public decisions, discusses population growth, and defines payoffs through aggregate vote totals. Grouping participants who have the same utilities and mechanism parameters preserves exactly the distinctions the game uses.

The weakest point is the one-person mass quantum. In a literally atomless population, a single voter has no effect on aggregate tallies: in fixed-budget QV every ballot then gives that voter the same outcome utility, while in no-budget QV the quadratic payment pushes toward zero votes. That is not the strategic question answered by the paper’s theorems. The proposed mirror therefore treats the population as a high-multiplicity society represented by type masses, while retaining a one-person deviation size. It is an exact compressed formulation of a large electorate, not a claim that the paper’s NE result becomes a substantive atomless equilibrium theorem. The further question of whether a useful equilibrium notion survives as the person-mass quantum tends to zero remains open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both computational anchors are atomistic. Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 ask whether any one named voter can profit by changing an integer ballot; Theorems 4.1 and 4.3 supply the corresponding single-voter deviation tests. In a genuinely atomless society, one voter has zero mass and cannot change any tally. For fixed-budget QV, every ballot then gives that voter the same expected outcome utility, so every ballot is a best response. For no-budget QV, the tally is still unaffected, while the quadratic payment makes the zero ballot the best response. The resulting equilibrium conditions lose the strategic content the paper studies.

The proposed one-person quantum preserves that content by giving each deviation size \(1/n\). It also makes \(n\) and the integral type counts part of the input. The equilibrium test is then exactly a finite repeated-player game written as a histogram: a deviation changes the integer tally by \(b-a\), and the algorithm checks one representative for each occupied type–ballot pair. That is a sensible high-multiplicity formulation, and grouping voters with identical utility vectors and ballots is justified by the paper’s payoff structure. The paper’s own fixed-budget and no-budget algorithms provide the starting point directly. But its strategic unit remains one finite voter throughout; the mass profile compresses the input without making the equilibrium question continuous.

Taking the limit does not preserve the missing strategic unit. Adding deviations by a positive mass of a type could make a continuum model strategic, but it changes the question to coordinated or coalition deviations. The paper discusses collusion, yet its named computational results concern unilateral Nash deviations. Treating a type’s entire mass as one player would also give the game a different set of players and incentives. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they do not continue Theorems 4.2 or 4.4.

So the negative case is strongest against an atomless equilibrium mirror and weaker against the proponent’s exact compressed finite-population version. I would not count high-multiplicity work as a collision or objection; it supports the plausibility of that version. The remaining case is a scope argument: these theorems derive their meaning from individual influence, which disappears in the atomless model and returns only with a finite-person quantum. Under the programme’s broad high-multiplicity remit, that may still be enough to make the proposed mirror worthwhile, so a universal “no scenario” verdict is hard to defend.
