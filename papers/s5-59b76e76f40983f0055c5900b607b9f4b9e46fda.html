The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest continuous mirror I can make is for the tutor–module assignment problem in a large online university system with repeated module offerings. There could be tens of thousands of tutors but relatively few complete tutor types: for example, groups sharing the same eligible modules, module preferences, qualification tier, and institutional priority tier. The mass of a type is its fraction of the tutor pool. Module places also scale with the pool, so each module has a per-capita demand. The HOU instance makes this scenario plausible in scale—3,982 candidates and 1,906 positions—but the paper does not report how many interchangeable types those candidates form.

There is a strict limit on the result anchors: this paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Section 4.2 gives a rough \(O(MTP)\) complexity estimate for PAMA, but it is not a numbered result and treats deadlock handling as constant time. The NP-completeness statement about stable teacher–school matching is attributed to reference [11], so it is prior work, not an anchor from this paper. I cannot honestly restate an eligible anchor or attach the continuous question below to a proved result of this article.

As a prospective mirror of the paper’s underlying assignment problem, I would ask: Continuous Rank-and-Preference Tutor Assignment. An instance has a finite module set \(M\), rational demand \(d_i\) for each module \(i\), and a finite support \(T\) of tutor types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each type specifies its eligible modules, its preference rank \(p_t(i)\) for each eligible module, and its priority rank \(r_i(t)\) at each such module. The output is an allocation \(x_{ti}\ge 0\) and unassigned mass \(u_t\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{i\in M}x_{ti}+u_t=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{ti}\le d_i,\qquad x_{ti}=0\ \text{if }t\text{ is ineligible for }i. \]
Choose among feasible allocations by lexicographically maximizing total assigned mass, then minimizing the module-rank cost \(\sum_{t,i}r_i(t)x_{ti}\), then minimizing the tutor-preference cost \(\sum_{t,i}p_t(i)x_{ti}\). This specifies a module-priority policy; reversing the last two criteria gives a tutor-priority variant. A solution is the rational allocation, with each unit of mass assigned to at most one module.

I expect this stated problem to be Class A: its variables and constraints give a linear optimization problem, or a min-cost flow formulation, with size polynomial in \(|M|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. It retains the paper’s core ingredients—capacity, eligibility, tutor preferences, and module rankings—and its authors would likely recognize it as a continuous version of tutor assignment. It also raises questions about full-demand feasibility, how assignments change when type masses or policy priorities shift, and when a fractional optimum can be realized by a finite assignment.

My weakest point is that this is a policy-defined rank optimization, not PAMA’s round-by-round procedure. PAMA’s pending slots, random module order, and deadlock resolution can affect its output; the paper does not characterize that output as an optimum of this objective. Also, its strict tie-breaking of tutor rankings may make many tutors distinct under a literal type definition. A convincing high-multiplicity regime therefore depends on repeated tutors sharing behaviorally relevant priority tiers, with individual tie-breaking having no substantive effect on their aggregate assignment. The article does not establish that condition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to mirror. It has no theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing PAMA’s complexity or a matching property. Section 4.2 offers a rough \(O(MTP)\) estimate, but it treats deadlock handling as constant time and does not analyze the procedure rigorously. The NP-completeness result is attributed to earlier work. So the proposed LP is not a continuous counterpart to a result of this article; it is a new problem built from some of the same ingredients.

That LP is also not a formulation of PAMA. It selects assignments by a lexicographic rank-cost objective, while PAMA proceeds through ranked lists, pending slots, and policy-dependent deadlock resolution. Those choices can make its outcome depend on module order and the selected policy. A stronger mirror would try to fluidize PAMA itself, but the paper does not specify one policy or characterize one outcome to fluidize. Choosing an objective or a deadlock rule would produce an analyst-designed variant, not a limit of a defined computational result.

There is also a substantial multiplicity problem. A tutor’s complete type would need to preserve their preferences, eligibility, and position in each module’s ranking. The paper deliberately breaks score ties to make those rankings strict, so grouping tutors by preference and qualification alone discards distinctions central to its assignment rules; preserving the rankings can make nearly every tutor a different type. A continuous score distribution could represent such distinctions, but that requires a new input model and may lead to a different matching problem, rather than a high-multiplicity mirror of PAMA.

The negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper: it offers no computational result to continue, and the proposed tractable LP changes the mechanism being studied. I cannot establish that no plausible large-scale tutor-assignment model would be worth studying. A system with repeated tutor profiles and coarse priority tiers could support a sensible continuous matching problem—but that would be a research direction inspired by the paper, not a mirror of one of its results.
