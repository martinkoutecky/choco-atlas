The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses of students by preference and priority-class type, partitioned into round-one matched students, leftover students \(L\), and arrivals \(N\), a stable round-one assignment, and initial school capacities, choose round-two assignments \(x_{b,h}\) of each type’s mass to schools or \(\emptyset\), and expanded capacities \(c'_h \ge c_h\). Preserve every round-one matched student’s school and require stability: no positive mass that prefers a school to its assignment may face either spare capacity there or assigned mass from a strictly lower priority class. Among feasible outcomes, maximize the mass from \(L\) assigned to schools, then minimize the mass from \(N\) assigned to schools.

The model it lives in

Use a continuum of students represented by rational masses of types specifying school preferences and priority classes; preserve the round-one assignment of matched mass, allow rational capacity increases, and choose a mass allocation to schools or \(\emptyset\). Optimize lexicographically by maximizing assigned \(L\)-mass and then minimizing assigned \(N\)-mass, subject to stability under strict priorities between classes and ties within a class.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s NP-hardness result for Problem 1 only; it leaves Problems 2–5, the polynomial-time mechanisms, and the other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the set-cover hardness persist under more restrictive shared-priority structures or other natural school-priority regimes?
  • What approximation or parameterized algorithms are possible in the number of supported types and schools?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the hardness result in Theorem 9, proved in this paper by reductions from Cardinal Set-Cover; the paper cites Karp for the source problem’s NP-completeness. I would anchor on Problem 1: in Setting A2, new students \(N\) arrive alongside the previously unmatched students \(L\). The task is to extend the original matching without breaking stability, first maximizing how many students from \(L\) are matched and, among such extensions, minimizing how many from \(N\) are matched. Theorem 9 says this problem is NP-hard.

A direct continuous version is Mass-Max-\(L\)-Min-\(N\) Stable Extension. An instance has a finite set of schools \(H\), rational masses for student cohorts, and a finite set of behavioral types. A behavioral type specifies a student’s ranking of schools and their priority at each school; schools rank types strictly, while treating agents of the same type alike. Cohort tags distinguish students already matched in round one, the leftover group \(L\), and the new arrivals \(N\); tags do not change preferences or priorities. The input includes a stable round-one assignment \(x^1\) and capacities \(c_h\). In round two, choose an assignment \(x_{g,h}\) of each cohort’s mass \(g\) to schools or \(\emptyset\), and capacities \(c'_h\ge c_h\), preserving every round-one matched student’s school and maintaining stability. Among these outcomes, maximize \(\sum_{g\in L,h\in H}x_{g,h}\), then minimize \(\sum_{g\in N,h\in H}x_{g,h}\). Mass measures how many agents receive each assignment: every agent still gets one whole school or remains unmatched.

For precision, let \(D_{b,h}\) be the mass of behavioral type \(b\) assigned somewhere it likes less than \(h\), \(V_h\) the unused capacity at \(h\), and \(P_{b,h}\) the mass at \(h\) of types the school ranks below \(b\). Stability requires that for each mutually acceptable type-school pair, \(D_{b,h}=0\), or both \(V_h=0\) and \(P_{b,h}=0\). Thus there is no positive mass wanting to move to a school that has room or could replace a lower-priority assignment. All masses and capacities use one common rational scale, so arrivals do not change how the two objectives are compared.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for large district admissions with late placements and arrivals: many students share preference lists and school-priority categories, while the number of distinct cohort-types \(\tau\) is much smaller than the headcount. Complexity should be stated in the number of schools, \(\tau\), and input bit length. The question retains the paper’s central features—two rounds, protected initial assignments, stability, leftovers, arrivals, capacity increases, and the same lexicographic objective. The population is the continuous object; assignments remain whole at the agent level.

I expect hardness to transfer. The paper’s set-cover reduction has a robust mass analogue. For each set, create a school \(h_i\), a round-one incumbent cohort that fills its capacity, and a new “gate” cohort that wants only \(h_i\). For each element, create a leftover cohort that accepts exactly the schools for sets containing that element. School \(h_i\) ranks its incumbent first, its gate cohort next, and its element cohorts below them. Give these cohorts equal rational mass \(\delta\). To match any positive mass of an element cohort at \(h_i\), stability forces the *entire* gate cohort there: any unmatched gate mass would block with the lower-priority element mass. Consequently, the schools whose gates are admitted must cover all elements. Minimizing admitted new mass is exactly minimizing the number of sets in a cover, scaled by \(\delta\). Conversely, a cover gives a stable extension: admit its gates and assign each element to its highest-ranked selected school containing it, expanding those schools’ capacities as needed. Replicating every cohort \(K\) times leaves the type support fixed while making the population arbitrarily large relative to \(\tau\). The reduction’s combinatorics therefore live in the schools and types, not in the number of individual agents.

This mirror covers Theorem 9, Problem 1 only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s other hardness results or its four polynomial-time mechanisms. It raises a useful comparison with Theorem 4: that theorem solves the reverse priority, minimizing \(N\) before maximizing \(L\), while Problem 1’s ordering is hard. Does that asymmetry survive in the mass model? Further questions include approximation and parameterized algorithms in \(\tau\), and whether the gate argument survives when priority within a cohort is refined by individual tie-breaks.

My weakest point is the priority model. The paper uses strict rankings of named students; grouping many students into one type makes schools indifferent among members of that cohort. Priority categories shared by many applicants make this a credible school-choice regime, but a referee might regard individual tie-breaks as essential. Refining types to preserve every such tie-break could make \(\tau\) grow with the population. The case survives when shared priority categories are accepted as part of the high-multiplicity instance regime; it is less persuasive for markets where every student has a distinct priority.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9, Problem 1 is a real computational anchor: it states an NP-hardness result for the lexicographic stable-extension problem. So the negative case cannot rest on the paper having no computational result.

The strongest objection is that the paper’s model uses strict school rankings of named students. If a type includes a student’s priority at each school, as a high-multiplicity type should, then a school’s strict ranking gives every student a distinct priority position. Exact types cannot repeat; their number grows with the student population. In that regime, replacing students by masses does not compress the instance. A large city alone does not fix this if its tie-break rules distinguish every applicant.

A better mirror can use shared priority classes, with schools tied among students in the same class and strict between classes. That is a substantive change to the paper’s strict-preference model, and its blocking notion must be stated for tied priorities. But it is a plausible school-choice regime, and the proposed set-cover construction appears to survive it: if any element mass is assigned to a school, an unmatched positive mass of that school’s higher-priority gate cohort would block, forcing the whole gate cohort to be admitted. The gates therefore remain discrete set-selection choices, even though the population is continuous.

That last point weakens the negative case rather than strengthening it: the mirror preserves the paper’s stability constraint and lexicographic objective, and its mass version can retain the reduction. The objection limits the mirror’s relevance to settings with shared priority classes; it does not defeat that mirror or support the universal claim that no scenario is worth studying. My honest negative case is therefore weak at its central burden.
