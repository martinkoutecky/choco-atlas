The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result. Its named results concern equilibrium and incentive properties, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. Under its assumptions, it gives equilibrium bidding functions for both the Nearest-VCG and D-NVCG rules. For NVCG, a local broker’s bid is \(\alpha_i-\sigma_i\omega_i(q-1)\) when \(\alpha_i>0\), and \(0\) otherwise. For D-NVCG, the same formula applies to brokers with \(\phi_i^1>c_i^V\); when \(\phi_i^1\le c_i^V\), the deduction is instead \(\sigma_i\omega_i[\ell/\sum_{i\in Q_d}\omega_i+(q-1)]\). Here \(\sigma_i=h_i/H_i\), and \(\ell\) is the number of locals whose first-round bids exceed their VCG prices. This is an equilibrium characterization, not a complexity classification or an algorithmic result about an encoded input. Proposition 2 likewise gives a first-order condition, and the other named results establish payment or strategic properties.

A high-multiplicity scenario is imaginable: repeated portfolio trades might attract many brokers grouped by role, package, and shared valuation or execution characteristics, with \(n\) brokers but relatively few types. One could ask how to allocate execution mass and determine fees across those types. But the paper’s mechanism qualifies one local broker per package and one global broker; a mass allocation across broker types would change that mechanism and its core constraints. The paper’s results do not establish the computational properties of that altered problem.

The conclusion says the design “eliminates any computational complexity” for winner determination, but that is not a numbered result; in the stated second round, winner determination is just a comparison of the weighted sum of local bids with the global bid. So there is no named computational anchor here on which to base a continuous computational question. The divisible portfolio is continuity of the outcome space, and the continuous signal distributions model uncertainty; neither makes the broker population continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered results concern equilibrium and incentive properties: Lemma 2 places the D-NVCG fees on the bidder-optimal frontier, Proposition 1 rules out certain bids, and Theorem 1 gives equilibrium bidding functions. None classifies the complexity of an encoded problem or presents an algorithm whose complexity depends on the input. The conclusion’s claim that the design “eliminates any computational complexity” is not such a result: with preset packages, the second round compares the global bid with a weighted sum of local bids.

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s best anchor, but it is an equilibrium characterization, not a computational task. Its formulas use the bidders’ valuations, weights, and a reverse hazard rate derived from the signal distribution. The paper does not specify a finite input representation for that distribution or ask how to compute an equilibrium from one. Turning the theorem into a complexity question would require adding an encoding and a computational objective that the paper does not study.

Repeated brokers in a few shared categories are a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But preserving the paper’s mechanism still selects one local broker per package and one global broker. The payment rules depend on the selected bidders’ bids and their pivotal role. Aggregating brokers into masses does not preserve which broker offers the lowest bid or which bid changes the outcome; under an atomless population, an individual winner has zero mass. The paper also assumes continuously distributed private signals, so brokers who differ in realized information are distinct problem types. One could posit cohorts with shared information, but that changes the information structure and still leaves the mechanism selecting individual winners.

A stronger alternative would let broker types supply portions of packages, with type-level capacities and mass allocations. That could yield a computational question, but it would replace the paper’s winner-take-package, local-versus-global contest with a different allocation and core-pricing problem. Given the paper’s finite package structure, that alternative appears to lead to a direct optimization model rather than uncovering a hard computational problem from the paper’s results. The fact that the portfolio itself is divisible, or that signals are continuous, does not supply the missing population continuization.

I cannot rule out that a new computational study of broker populations would be worthwhile. But it would be a new model and question, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. The proponent’s proposed anchors therefore do not establish a worthwhile mirror within the programme.
