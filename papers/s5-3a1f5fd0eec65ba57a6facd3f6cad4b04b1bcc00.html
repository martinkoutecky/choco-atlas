The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s trade-off between advice accuracy and worst-case welfare, in a large market where many buyers fall into a small number of repeated cohorts. Picture a provider allocating a service through one of two mutually exclusive programs, each able to serve any subset of its eligible customers. For example, a utility might auction access to one of two demand-response programs across many standardized customer classes. Each customer still receives either the service or nothing; mass records how much of each class is present.

Let \(G=\{A,B\}\) denote the programs and \(V\) a finite set of possible positive rational values. A full type is \(t=(g,v)\), and the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(G\times V\), with total mass \(1\). A feasible served allocation \(x\) has \(0\le x_{g,v}\le\mu_{g,v}\) and serves mass from at most one program. Its welfare is
\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_{g\in G,\,v\in V}v x_{g,v}. \]
The optimal allocation serves the entire higher-welfare program. Advice names a predicted program \(\hat g\). The true value distribution remains private: a deterministic fluid clock policy sees the public feasibility structure, \(\hat g\), and the mass exiting at each price. To make this a high-multiplicity model, all customers in a public cohort receive the same clock price; the policy cannot distinguish otherwise identical customers by name.

Lead: Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that any deterministic clock auction with a prediction that is \((1+\epsilon)\)-consistent—achieving that approximation when the prediction is optimal—has robustness \(\Omega((1/\epsilon)\log n)\), for constant \(\epsilon>0\). The continuous question I would hand to a prover is the Fluid Best-of-Both-Worlds Clock Frontier: given a finite value alphabet \(V\), a predicted program \(\hat g\), and a support bound \(\tau\), determine the minimum worst-case ratio
\[ \sup_{\mu:\,|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\le\tau} \frac{W_\mu(\mathrm{OPT})}{W_\mu(x^\Pi)} \]
over deterministic cohort-clock policies \(\Pi\) whose ratio is at most \(1+\epsilon\) whenever \(\hat g\) is optimal. The solution is a clock policy and its guarantee. The central question is whether the best robustness is polynomially bounded in \(\tau\), \(1/\epsilon\), and the input bit length, or whether a logarithmic support-size penalty remains.

I would expect this question to be tractable for fixed \(\tau\), with the relevant frontier depending on the number of value levels rather than the total population. As \(\tau\) grows, I expect a lower bound reminiscent of the paper’s \(\log n\) bound. The paper does not prove either claim for the fluid model.

A second useful anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here. It says that any deterministic clock auction with \(\alpha\)-consistency\(_\infty\)—always obtaining at least \(1/\alpha\) of the predicted set’s welfare—has robustness \(\Omega((1/\alpha)n^{1/(\alpha-1)}\log n)\), for \(\alpha\in[1+\epsilon,H_n]\). Its mirror is the Fluid Binding-Benchmark Frontier: in the same model, find the minimum worst-case ratio among policies that guarantee \(W_\mu(x^\Pi)\ge W_\mu(\hat g)/\alpha\) for every value distribution, even when \(\hat g\) is wrong. I expect this frontier to be tractable for fixed support and to worsen with \(\tau\); whether the paper’s steep dependence on population size survives as a dependence on support size is open.

These mirrors preserve the paper’s central objects: private buyer values, downward-closed feasibility, a public prediction of the welfare-maximizing set, clock prices, and welfare guarantees. They ask about the underlying auction problem, not a particular encoding. The many-customer regime is plausible because standardized cohorts can recur at scale, and mass is the natural way to state what fraction of such a cohort exits at a price.

The scope is deliberately narrow: these questions mirror Theorems 2 and 5, not every result in the paper. My main reservation is that the theorems cover personalized prices to named bidders, while the proposed high-multiplicity model gives identical customers identical clocks. The lower-bound constructions use growing ladders of bidder values, so they do not establish the same bounds when \(\tau\) is fixed. A continuous fluid model that retains arbitrary within-cohort personalization would be closer to the paper, but could erase the very type compression that makes this a high-multiplicity question. The mirror is strongest as a focused study of advice-augmented clock auctions in an anonymous, repeated-cohort market—not as a claim that the paper’s bounds already transfer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the kind of question the results ask. The paper explicitly says clock-auction analyses often set computational constraints aside to study information-theoretic limits. Theorem 2 and Theorem 5 bound the welfare guarantees of classes of mechanisms; they do not give the complexity of computing an intervention from a supplied society. Here \(n\) counts bidders whose private values the auction can probe through prices. In ChoCo’s usual setup, the population distribution is the computational input. Replacing bidders with masses changes what the mechanism can observe, as well as the size parameter.

For Theorem 2, the proposed fluid frontier is a coherent mechanism-design question, but “tractable for fixed \(\tau\)” does not follow from a frontier of best robustness guarantees. If the distribution over values is supplied to the solver, the two-program example reduces to comparing the programs’ total welfares; the prediction and hidden-value trade-off that drives the theorem has gone. If values remain hidden and the policy sees only aggregate exits, the task is to design an anonymous information-revelation protocol. To make that a computational problem, one would need to specify a finite representation of policies, allowed prices and histories, and what computation the policy performs. That could be worthwhile work, but it is a new question rather than a continuous version of the theorem’s computational content.

The same boundary applies to Theorem 5. Its \(\alpha\)-consistency\(_\infty\) guarantee is a natural fluid performance target, but the theorem establishes no computational frontier for achieving it. Its lower-bound proof adapts prices to adversarial assignments of values among named bidders. Uniform cohort prices remove that ability; allowing individualized prices restores it by making treatment history part of what distinguishes agents, potentially taking \(\tau\) back up to the population size. Either way, a fluid result would need a new analysis of a different mechanism class.

The population scenario itself is plausible: repeated customers, fractional mass, and per-person indivisible service fit the programme’s understanding of high multiplicity. I would not use prior high-multiplicity work or fractional allocation as objections. The negative case is narrower: neither anchor currently identifies a computational problem of the kind ChoCo is charting. If ChoCo includes computational synthesis of anonymous mechanisms under private aggregate information, these could be strong anchors for that expanded programme. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous auction project in every scenario; I can argue that these particular mirrors, as stated, are mechanism-performance frontiers rather than continuized computational results.
