What the mirror covers

The proposed population version covers only computation of a pooled swaps ranking and its inconsistency value; it leaves aside individual preference recovery and the paper's axiomatic characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the computation of the paper’s swaps preference from a large population’s pooled choice data. A natural setting is a large choice experiment in which millions of respondents face menus drawn from a fixed set. Treat one respondent’s menu and choice as a type \(t=(A,a)\). Its mass \(\mu_{A,a}\) is the fraction of respondents with that observation. When the number of respondents \(N\) is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct supported menu-choice pairs, repeated observations can be represented by their rational proportions. The resulting ranking is a population-level welfare summary; it does not claim to recover each respondent’s private ranking.

Call the lead question Mass-Weighted Swaps-Preference Recovery. An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(X\) and a support of at most \(\tau\) types \((A,a)\), each with a binary rational mass \(\mu_{A,a}\geq 0\), where the masses sum to one. The task is to return a strict linear order \(P\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{(A,a)} \mu_{A,a}\,|\{x\in A:xPa\}|. \]
A solution is any minimizing order, together with the minimum value if the inconsistency index is also wanted. This is the paper’s swaps-preference objective applied to population proportions.

My anchor is Proposition 8, proved in Appendix C. It establishes both directions of an exact computational equivalence: from any collection \(f\), assign each ordered pair \(x,y\) in a linear ordering problem the weight \(c_{xy}=\sum_{(A,y):x\in A} f(A,y)\); an optimal order for that problem gives the swaps preference. Conversely, from linear-ordering weights \(c_{xy}\), set \(f(\{x,y\},y)=c_{xy}/c\), where \(c\) is the sum of the weights, and set the other observation masses to zero; the swaps preference then solves that linear ordering problem. The paper uses this connection to motivate computation of the swaps preference and applies the index to experimental data in Section 7.

For precision, Proposition 8 is a named computational result, but it does not itself assert that the problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The paper contains no named result making such a complexity classification. The positive case rests on Proposition 8 as an exact optimization formulation—the kind of formulation the prompt allows as an anchor when the paper computes with it—not on a complexity theorem the paper does not provide.

I expect hardness to transfer. Integer-weight linear-ordering instances map to rational population masses by normalization, and the normalization does not change the minimizing order. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the ordering of the alternatives, so making observations into divisible mass does not appear to remove them. Proposition 8 supplies the exact bridge; the paper itself does not prove the hardness classification. Further questions include the complexity of the decision version, approximation guarantees, and whether useful algorithms are possible when the number of alternatives or the support size is restricted.

This mirror covers the computation of the swaps preference and its associated inconsistency value, not the paper’s full theory of individual welfare or its axiomatic characterizations. The authors could plausibly recognize the objective: the same menu-choice evidence and weighted swaps criterion produce the same ranking. The extension is to pooled population data, a natural fit with the paper’s use of relative frequencies and its cohort-level empirical discussion.

The weak point is that the paper’s \(f\) represents the relative frequency of observations for an individual, while this mirror interprets those frequencies as mass across individuals. Pooling changes the estimand from an individual’s welfare ranking to a representative population ranking. That is a real co-modification, and the paper does not establish that the authors would accept it. The case survives only if that population-level swaps ranking is accepted as a sensible extension of their underlying problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 8 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it does not establish the complexity of a computational problem. It proves an exact equivalence between computing the swaps preference and a linear ordering problem; Appendix C then points to existing algorithms for that problem. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary classifying the complexity of computing the swaps preference. So this anchor is a computational formulation, not the kind of complexity result the continuization programme is meant to mirror.

The best population version would give each person a full choice profile \(f_i\), let \(\mu\) describe the population, and compute
\[ \arg\min_P \int \sum_{(A,a)} f_i(A,a)\,|\{x\in A:xPa\}|\,d\mu(i). \]
That is a coherent *population consensus* problem, but it changes the paper’s target. The paper uses \(f\) to describe one person’s choices across menus, then seeks a welfare ranking for that person. Pooling people’s choices can make perfectly rational individuals look inconsistent: if half prefer \(x\) to \(y\) and half prefer \(y\) to \(x\), the pooled data assign positive mass to both choices from \(\{x,y\}\). The swaps preference is then a compromise ranking, not either group’s welfare ranking. The paper’s recovery result does not justify that interpretation for a heterogeneous population.

There are two ways to repair this, and neither yields the proposed mirror. If people share the same preference and choice process, pooling simply estimates the same \(f\) the paper already takes as input; population size supplies more observations, but no new computational problem. If instead the type records each person’s full choice profile, the natural output is a ranking for each type, which batches the original individual problem rather than creating a population-level one. A single ranking for the whole society requires an additional social-welfare interpretation absent from the paper.

This is a strong case against the proposed anchor, but the universal claim has a real weak point: the consensus problem could be worthwhile on its own, and the authors might accept it as an extension. The paper does not give enough reason to call that extension *their* problem.
