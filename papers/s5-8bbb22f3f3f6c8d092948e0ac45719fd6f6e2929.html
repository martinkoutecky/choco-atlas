The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(B\), a supported set \(T\) of strict preference types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and item prices \(c_b\), choose supplies \(z_b\ge 0\) and a complete assignment \(\pi_{tb}\) minimizing \(\sum_{b\in B}c_bz_b\). Require \(\sum_b\pi_{tb}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi_{tb}\le z_b\), and \(\pi_{tb}=0\) unless type \(t\) accepts item \(b\). The assignment must be popular: there is no feasible reassignment of the same atomless population, represented by a typewise coupling \(\eta_{tbb'}\) from current item \(b\) to new item \(b'\), whose mass preferring the new item exceeds its mass preferring the current item.

The model it lives in

Types carry population mass and each person receives one indivisible acceptable item; type mass may be split across assignments. The decision variables are per-capita supplies \(z_b\), assignment masses \(\pi_{tb}\), and reassignment couplings \(\eta_{tbb'}\) used to test popularity. Minimize \(\sum_b c_bz_b\) subject to a complete popular assignment.

The objection that survived

The reduction's combinatorics lie in the item universe and clause-specific preference types, so its hardness may persist without much tractability gain; the proponent acknowledges that possibility but does not address where the reduction's complexity lives.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's min-cost popular instance hardness result for strict lists of length at most two. It leaves the augmentation algorithms and hardness results, the perfect augmentation result, and the fixed-capacity min-cost popular matching algorithm aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the maximum vote margin over feasible reassignment couplings be dualized to give an exact polynomial-size LP for the minimum-cost popular inventory problem?
  • Does the discrete-to-continuous dictionary preserve or change the optimum under homogeneous replication, and which parts of the discrete hardness reduction survive fractional supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest positive anchor is Theorem 2 of Kavitha, Nasre, and Nimbhorkar: the min-cost popular instance problem is NP-hard even when every preference list is strict and has length at most two, and the hardness still holds when all lists come from a common master list. The authors prove this result in the paper, by reduction from monotone 1-in-3 SAT. [Theorem 2 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.2591)

A continuous mirror is Continuous Min-Cost Popular Inventory. Its input is a finite set \(B\) of item types and a supported set \(T\) of preference types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), where the masses are binary rationals summing to \(1\), and a strict list of at most two acceptable items. Lists follow one common master ordering. Each item \(b\) has a nonnegative price \(c_b\). Choose a purchased supply \(z_b\ge 0\) for every item and a complete assignment of the population to acceptable items, minimizing \(\sum_{b\in B}c_bz_b\).

Here is the popularity condition precisely. Represent the population as an atomless measurable space \(\Omega=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}\Omega_t\), with \(\lambda(\Omega_t)=\mu_t\). An assignment \(M\) gives each individual one whole item, and at most \(z_b\) mass receives item \(b\). It is popular if, under the same supplies, no feasible reassignment \(N\) has more mass preferring \(N\) to \(M\) than mass preferring \(M\) to \(N\); an unmatched person ranks below every acceptable item. A solution is the supply vector \(z\) and a complete popular assignment attaining minimum cost, or a report that no such assignment exists. For a cohort of \(n\) people, \(z_b\) represents \(nz_b\) seats and the objective is total cost per person.

A plausible regime is a large annual cohort seeking publicly funded training placements. Applicants list a first choice and backup; many share the same list, while the number of supported list types \(\tau\) is far smaller than the cohort. A ministry can fund additional seats at program-specific prices. The assignment remains individual and integral: the continuous quantities are population mass and seats per capita. That makes the society itself the continuous object, with added capacity as a necessary co-modification. The authors should recognize this as their question: buy item capacity at minimum cost so that no feasible reassignment wins a majority.

My tentative expectation is Class A for this mirror, with an algorithm polynomial in \(|B|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s SAT gadget relies on integer copy counts and thresholds such as zero copies versus a positive number; divisible supplies and splittable type mass may erase those constraints. I would look for a continuous popularity characterization that supports flow or LP-based optimization, but that step is the open work, not something the discrete theorem already supplies. If the hardness survives, it needs to survive fractional supplies; the stated reduction alone does not establish that.

The weakest point is the popularity condition itself. It compares two assignments person by person, so type-by-item totals alone do not determine the vote: the model must preserve which members of a type receive which items, as the measurable assignment does. That may make joint supply optimization harder than an ordinary transportation LP. It is also possible that the reduction’s clause-and-item combinatorics survive divisibility by a new argument. Those are reasons to treat tractability as a prediction rather than a verdict; they do not make the mirror ill-posed.

This case covers Theorem 2’s min-cost popular instance result only. The next questions are whether its continuous version admits an exact algorithm in \((|B|,\tau,L)\), whether optimal supplies have polynomial-size rational descriptions, and how its optimum compares with the discrete optimum under homogeneous replication.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper proves that min-cost popular instance construction is NP-hard, even with strict lists of length at most two and a master list. The proposed mirror preserves its central choices: population mass by preference type, per-item prices, added supply, complete assignments, and majority comparisons against feasible reassignments. A large cohort with repeated application lists and program-specific seat costs is a credible regime. Treating a type as a complete preference list is consistent with high multiplicity, and splitting a type’s mass across assignments is a natural atomless version of assigning its members different items.

The strongest case against is that this mirror may yield less leverage than its “inventory” framing suggests. The paper’s hardness gadgets rely on coordinating purchases across clause-specific people and shared items; their complexity is in the structure of the preference types and item universe, not in distinguishing many otherwise identical people. The reduction therefore gives little reason to expect that population continuization will simplify the hard cases. The popularity test also needs care: for each type, the vote against a reassignment depends on which individuals receive which pair of old and new items. Type-level assignment totals alone do not specify those pairings. A faithful formulation must preserve them, for example with type-level flows between current and proposed items, and optimizing supplies while ruling out every majority-improving reassignment may be substantially harder than an ordinary matching LP.

But neither point defeats the mirror. Hardness that survives continuization would itself be a useful boundary result, and the pairing issue can be made precise in an atomless population. The paper’s cited popular mixed-matching work also does not already answer the supply-cost problem: it concerns lotteries over matchings in a fixed finite instance. Nor does it make this a mere outcome-space continuation; the population is explicitly represented by masses.

So the negative case is weak. The proponent’s proposed mirror is well-posed and recognizable as the paper’s problem with a continuous population and corresponding per-capita supply. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists; the strongest defensible objection is that its computational payoff is uncertain, not that the question is out of scope.
