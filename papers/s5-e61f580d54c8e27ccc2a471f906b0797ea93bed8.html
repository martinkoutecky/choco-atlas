The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over location types \(t\in[0,1]\), let \(P_{\alpha,p}\) put probabilities \(p,1-2p,p\) on \(1/2-\alpha,1/2,1/2+\alpha\), respectively. With \(u(y,t)=1-|y-t|\) and \(\mathrm{ER}(P,\mu)=\max_{s,t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[u(y,s)]}{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[u(y,t)]}\), minimize over admissible \((\alpha,p)\) the worst-case ratio \(\sup_{\mu}\frac{\mathrm{ER}(P_{\alpha,p},\mu)}{\min_{z\in[0,1]}\mathrm{ER}(\delta_z,\mu)}\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution \(\mu\) over locations, with each location type carrying mass \(\mu_t\). The mechanism uses the same three-location lottery as in Theorem 4.3, independent of \(\mu\); utility is \(1-|y-t|\), and envy ratio is the maximum ratio of expected utilities across supported types. The mass values are part of the society description but do not affect this mechanism's objective or guarantee.

The objection that survived

The lottery and max-envy objective depend only on which location types have positive mass, so the distribution's mass values add no analytical leverage and the result transfers unchanged.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.3's approximation guarantee and optimality within the \((\alpha,p)\)-LRM family. It does not cover Theorem 4.4's lower bound for all randomized strategyproof mechanisms or the learning-augmented results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but the paper has no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under ChoCo’s criteria. Its theorems concern strategyproofness and approximation guarantees for mechanisms; they do not classify running time, hardness, or algorithmic complexity. So I would not present any of them as a ChoCo complexity result.

The nearest positive case is a large-city facility-siting regime: millions of residents, perhaps hundreds of neighborhood groups with shared ideal locations. A type is a location \(t\in[0,1]\), and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of residents. The mechanism chooses a facility location, or a lottery over locations, and the objective remains the paper’s envy ratio across residents. This makes the population the continuous object; it is not an outcome-space mirror.

The closest named result is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. It says that the \((\frac{\sqrt5}{2}-1,\frac25)\)-LRM constant mechanism—which chooses \(1/2-(\frac{\sqrt5}{2}-1)\) and \(1/2+(\frac{\sqrt5}{2}-1)\) each with probability \(2/5\), and \(1/2\) with probability \(1/5\)—is anonymous and strategyproof, achieves approximation ratio \(1+\frac{2}{\sqrt5}\), and is optimal within the family of \((\alpha,p)\)-LRM constant mechanisms.

A natural continuous extension would ask: over rational, finite-support location distributions \(\mu\), what parameters \((\alpha,p)\) minimize the worst-case envy-ratio approximation of this three-location lottery? The lottery is a direct, recognizable lift of Theorem 4.3. The paper’s reductions to extremal two-location profiles suggest that this restricted design problem may have the same tractable solution. But it would be a mechanism-performance question, not a complexity question of the kind the programme is set up to chart.

The paper’s Theorem 4.4, also proved here, says that every randomized strategyproof mechanism without predictions has approximation ratio at least \(1.12579\). That lower bound is not a complexity lower bound, and it does not automatically extend to a nonatomic population: an individual in that model has zero mass, so changing their report cannot affect the population distribution. The finite-agent incentive constraint that supports the theorem would need a new interpretation.

That is the weakest point in the positive case and, in my view, the decisive one for ChoCo: there is a credible continuous-population *analogue* of the paper’s mechanism-design questions, but no eligible computational anchor. The mirror could be useful as a new mechanism-design extension; it cannot honestly be sold as a computational-complexity mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the main reason these results do not make a ChoCo mirror: none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper proves strategyproofness, approximation guarantees, and optimality within mechanism classes. Its mechanisms are explicit formulas, not algorithms whose running time or hardness is at issue.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 4.3 has a plausible setting: residents in a large city can share location types. But the \((\alpha,p)\)-LRM mechanism ignores the reported locations, and the envy ratio gives no weight to type masses. For a distribution \(\mu\), its performance depends on which locations occur in the support, not on their proportions. Optimizing \((\alpha,p)\) over finite-support distributions therefore adds mass data the question never uses; the paper’s two-agent worst cases already capture the relevant support. One could instead study average envy, quantiles, or mechanisms whose outputs depend on population shares. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they change the objective or mechanism design problem rather than provide a computational mirror of this result.

Theorem 4.4 has a sharper obstacle. Its lower-bound argument uses an individual deviation between the profiles \((0.29,0.71)\) and \((0,0.71)\). In a nonatomic society, one individual has zero mass, so changing that person’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. The deviation constraint that powers the proof disappears. Giving a whole positive-mass type class the ability to move its report would restore an effect on \(\mu\), but that is a coalition or bloc incentive notion, not the individual strategyproofness studied here. Keeping finitely many agents and encoding their counts as proportions preserves individual deviations, but then the question remains a finite high-multiplicity mechanism problem—and still does not become a complexity result merely by expressing the counts as masses.

This does not make facility location an implausible high-multiplicity setting. It makes these particular theorems poor anchors for the programme: the envy objective is insensitive to population proportions, while the incentive constraints rely on agents whose individual influence vanishes in the continuum. The better alternatives either leave the mass representation computationally inert or replace the paper’s question with a new one. That is the strongest case against a worthwhile continuous mirror here; it is a modelling judgment, not a proof that no related continuum mechanism-design project could be valuable.
