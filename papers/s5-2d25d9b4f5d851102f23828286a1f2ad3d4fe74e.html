The continuous mirror question

Given a finite project set \(P\), common partition \(\mathcal Z\), rational costs and budget \(L\), and finite voter-type support \(H\) with rational masses \(\mu_h>0\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies an approval set and nondecreasing concave interaction tables, compute the IES outcome \(W\) and payment sequence using initial purse \(\mu_h L\) and type payment \(\min\{b_h,\mu_h q\,u_h(p\mid B)\}\). Does this mass-based rule satisfy EJRI-1 for every measurable coalition of mass at least \(\operatorname{cost}(T)/L\) that meets the paper’s marginal cohesiveness condition, with a positive-mass member witnessing the guarantee?

The model it lives in

A population measure with finite support \(H\) over complete voter types, each carrying mass \(\mu_h\); projects remain indivisible. Type \(h\) has utility \(u_h(W)=\sum_{Z\in\mathcal Z} f_{hZ}(|W\cap A_h\cap Z|)\). IES starts type \(h\) with purse \(\mu_h L\), selects an affordable project with minimum \(q\), and returns \(W\) and the payment sequence; the guarantee is mass-EJRI-1.

The objection that survived

The finite proof establishes its guarantee for voter groups; the mass version also needs a weighted proof for coalitions that select fractional slices of types, which need not correspond to a subset of voters in a chosen finite replication.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 1’s IES guarantee under substitute interactions and the IES procedure over type support. It leaves PIES for arbitrary interactions, the welfare comparisons, and the simulations outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove the weighted IES guarantee for arbitrary measurable fractional coalitions and give a bit-complexity bound polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input length.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s proportional-aggregation problem under substitute interactions. The population is the continuous object; the projects remain indivisible, and the outcome is still a funded subset.

The strongest anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper’s §4.3. It says that, with substitute interactions, Interaction Equal Shares (IES) returns an outcome satisfying EJRI-1: for every interaction-cohesive group \(S\) and project set \(T\), some member \(i\in S\) either gets utility at least \(\alpha(T)\), or gets utility greater than \(\alpha(T)\) after one additional project \(p^\ast\in T\) is added. Here cohesiveness requires \(|S|/n\ge \operatorname{cost}(T)/L\), and requires \(\alpha\)’s marginal utility for each \(p\in T\), in every context \(B\), to equal the least marginal utility among members of \(S\). The paper separately states in §3 that IES runs in polynomial time. Proposition 1 itself is a proportionality guarantee, not a numbered complexity classification; the paper offers no comparable named hardness result to anchor on.

The mirror’s instance is a project set \(P\), a common partition \(\mathcal Z\), rational project costs and total budget \(L\), and a finite support \(H\) of voter types with rational masses \(\mu_h>0\), where \(\sum_{h\in H}\mu_h=1\). A type \(h\) completely specifies an approval set \(A_h\subseteq P\) and, for every \(Z\in\mathcal Z\), a nondecreasing, concave interaction function \(f_{hZ}\). Its utility for funded set \(W\) is \(u_h(W)=\sum_{Z\in\mathcal Z} f_{hZ}(|W\cap A_h\cap Z|)\). Concavity is the substitute condition from the paper. The population might be a city with tens or hundreds of thousands of residents, where a standardized project list and a small catalogue of interaction profiles produce hundreds or thousands of distinct types. The important regime is \(n\gg\tau\), with \(\tau=|H|\); it is plausible for repeated ballots and shared views about substitutable projects such as alternative parking sites, though it will not describe every PB election.

The precise problem is Continuous EJRI-1 Aggregation under Substitutes: given that instance, compute a feasible funded set \(W\) by the continuous IES process, with payments as a certificate, and guarantee EJRI-1 for every interaction-cohesive coalition. Initially type \(h\) has aggregate purse \(\mu_h L\), corresponding to each unit of population mass receiving the same per-capita budget. If \(b_h\) remains when the current funded set is \(B\), type \(h\)’s aggregate payment for project \(p\) at affordability level \(q\) is \(\min\{b_h,\mu_h q\,u_h(p\mid B)\}\). The project’s \(q\)-value is the least \(q\) for which these payments cover its cost; IES funds an affordable project with minimum \(q\), updates the purses, and stops when none is affordable. The solution is the resulting \(W\) and payment sequence. The anchor has no global welfare-maximization objective: its criterion is proportional representation, and IES’s operative choice at each step is minimum affordability \(q\).

For the continuous guarantee, a coalition is a measurable group of population mass at least \(\operatorname{cost}(T)/L\). Its represented types determine the minimum marginal utility used in the cohesiveness condition. EJRI-1 then requires positive mass of that coalition to include members whose utility for \(W\) is at least \(\alpha(T)\), or whose utility after adding some \(p^\ast\in T\) is greater than \(\alpha(T)\). That is the anchor’s guarantee stated for mass rather than headcount.

I expect this problem to be tractable: continuous IES can compute each project’s affordability by aggregating over the \(\tau\) supported types, then repeat for at most \(m\) projects. With binary-encoded rational masses, costs, and utility tables, the natural target is time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. The underlying aggregation question is exactly the paper’s: how to produce a proportional budget outcome while accounting for project interactions. The mass formulation does not suppress those interactions or change the fairness test. It also gives a direct dictionary back to finite elections: voter counts \(n_h\) yield masses \(\mu_h=n_h/n\), and aggregate type purses reproduce the original equal per-voter budgets.

This case covers Proposition 1 and the paper’s IES result for substitute interactions; it does not try to mirror PIES or the welfare experiments. Further questions include whether a polynomial-time rule can guarantee EJRI-1 under arbitrary interactions, whether such a guarantee survives voter-specific project partitions, and how welfare optimization behaves subject to EJRI-1.

The weak point is that this is a modest complexity story. For rational masses, the continuous instance can be represented as a finite election by choosing a suitable denominator; the rule’s dependence on type counts can then make the continuous computation look like a compression of the existing one. The result does not uncover discrete hardness that disappears in the continuum. I would still defend the mirror as a sensible population-level formulation of the authors’ own aggregation question, and as a useful type-based regime—but not as evidence of a new tractability break.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is about its limited computational scope. Proposition 1 proves a fairness guarantee for IES; it does not classify the complexity of finding a proportional outcome. The paper’s separate claim that IES runs in polynomial time comes from iterating over projects and computing affordability. In the proposed mirror, each affordability calculation is a weighted sum over the \(\tau\) types. The continuous execution therefore appears to be a type-compressed implementation of the paper’s existing rule, rather than a new optimization problem.

For rational masses, that correspondence is exact. Choose a common denominator \(D\) and replace each type \(h\) by \(D\mu_h\) identical voters. Their aggregate initial budget is \(\mu_h L\). If the continuous affordability parameter is \(D\) times the discrete one, the aggregate payment is
\[ D\mu_h\min\{L/D,\;u_h(p\mid B)q_{\mathrm{discrete}}\} =\min\{\mu_h L,\;\mu_h u_h(p\mid B)q_{\mathrm{continuous}}\}. \]
Thus the project affordability ordering, payments, and funded set coincide. A stronger mirror could formalize EJRI-1 for positive-mass coalitions and prove the guarantee for every rational type distribution, but the paper’s proof seems to carry over by replacing voter sums with finite weighted sums. The finite support and shared type utilities leave little new structure for the continuum to expose.

That makes this a plausible low-priority mirror: its main deliverable may be a precise dictionary and a support-size implementation. But that is a weak basis for rejecting it. The programme explicitly values the discrete–continuous dictionary, and large participatory budgets offer a credible high-multiplicity regime. Proposition 1 is an algorithmic fairness guarantee even without being a complexity theorem. I cannot honestly make the negative case defeat this anchor; the argument mainly limits its likely novelty.
