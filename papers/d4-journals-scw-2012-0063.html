The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a direct, support-sensitive computation of the CLC rating. Let the types be complete weak rankings of \(m\) candidates, and let the input be the \(\tau\) supported types with binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. The task, CLC-Compute, is to output the exact rank-like rate \(R_x\) for every candidate, using the paper’s formulas on the Llull matrix \(v_{xy}=\sum_t\mu_t v^t_{xy}\). A solution is the resulting rate vector and its induced ranking. A plausible regime is a large election whose voters fall into relatively few ballot types, so \(\tau\) is modest compared with the population.

This is a sensible object for the authors: their rating is defined from relative vote frequencies, and their paper explicitly connects those frequencies to a distribution over ballot types. The expected class is tractable. Section 2.7 gives an \(O(m^3)\) procedure once the Llull matrix is formed; forming it from the support takes \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations. With binary rational masses, the exact computations also have polynomial bit complexity. This question covers computing CLC ratings, not the paper’s separate claims about clone consistency or majority principles. Further questions include a sharp bit-complexity bound and how the support-sensitive algorithm compares with computing from the aggregate Llull matrix alone.

But this is only an unanchored candidate mirror under the required rules. Theorem 8.1 proves continuity of the projected scores and rates; Corollary 8.2 states continuity with respect to vote frequencies; Theorem 4.3 proves a structural property of the indirect comparison relation. None asserts a computational complexity result. The paper’s \(O(m^3)\) runtime appears in the unnumbered procedure in Section 2.7. The paper contains no eligible named computational result, so there is no anchor for which I can properly state a per-anchor continuous problem. My weakest point is that the candidate computation is already close to the paper’s own frequency-based model and is already given an algorithm there. That makes the population interpretation plausible, while leaving little case for a new complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor problem is decisive: Theorem 4.3, Theorem 8.1, and Corollary 8.2 establish structural or continuity properties, not the complexity of a computational problem. Section 2.7 gives an algorithm, but the paper has no named computational result of the kind this mirror exercise asks us to carry forward.

The proposed CLC-Compute question is also already answered by the paper’s own model. Its equation (19) defines pairwise scores as averages over vote types, and Section 8 treats the rates as functions of relative vote frequencies. Given a supported distribution \(\mu\), form \(v_{xy}=\sum_t\mu_t v^t_{xy}\), then apply the paper’s procedure: \(O(m^3)\) arithmetic operations after aggregation, which takes \(O(\tau m^2)\). The paper even extends its domain to real-valued scores. So the continuous society is already the natural input to the rating method; adding \(\mu\) as an explicit input mainly changes the encoding. Corollary 8.2 states the frequency continuity directly, and the indirect comparison relation in Theorem 4.3 is computed by the Floyd–Warshall step in Section 2.7. None supplies a distinct complexity question that the paper leaves open.

A stronger replacement might ask for the minimum mass of voters that must change ballot type, under specified costs, to alter the CLC ranking or winner. That is a recognizable computational social choice problem, but it adds an intervention objective and cost model absent from the paper’s results. It could be worthwhile research on the CLC rule; it is not a computational mirror of one of the cited theorems. The strongest negative case, then, is that the paper already formulates and computes its rating over vote-frequency data, while offering no named computational result to mirror.

That case has a real limit: if “mirror” includes attaching a new bribery or robustness problem to any rule in a paper, then a continuous CLC intervention problem may be worth studying. I can’t honestly rule out its value. But that possibility is a new research direction, not evidence that any of the proponent’s anchors survives.
