The continuous mirror question

Given two disjoint stars \(G=(V,E)\), finitely many complete additive valuation types \(\Theta\) with \(u_\theta(v)\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(s_v\), and threshold \(t\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(x_{\theta,B}\) over nonempty connected bundles \(B\subseteq V\) such that \(\sum_B x_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) for every \(\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,B:v\in B}x_{\theta,B}=s_v\) for every \(v\), and \(x_{\theta,B}>0\) only when \(\sum_{v\in B}u_\theta(v)\ge t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete additive valuation types; each unit of mass receives an integral connected bundle in the two-star graph. Rational per-capita vertex supplies scale the available resource mass, and the question is whether every assigned unit can meet the egalitarian threshold.

The objection that survived

The Partition reduction uses \(\tau=q+2\) distinct valuation types, so it does not show that the source hardness persists when population size is large relative to support size; this bounds the high-multiplicity lesson but not the two-star mass problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The selected mirror covers the egalitarian threshold problem on two stars; Theorem 9 also has a separate utilitarian mirror on star collections. The paper's other graph-class and parameterized results remain unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of this two-star feasibility problem as a function of support size \(\tau\), number of leaves, and input bit length, especially when \(\tau\) is bounded?
  • When does the mass-and-supply model coincide with allocations on finite repeated copies of the resource graph while preserving connectedness and the threshold?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s utilitarian problem on stars. In a large land or service allocation, many households can share one of a finite set of additive valuation profiles, while each household still receives a connected set of indivisible parcels. If there are \(\tau\) profiles and \(K\) households of each profile, then \(n=K\tau\): the population can be large relative to its type support. The parcels need per-capita supplies that scale with the population. That is a co-modification of the allocation model, while the social choice remains over the population. The paper’s own related-work discussion of algorithms parameterized by the number of agent types also supports treating typed populations as a natural regime.

My lead anchor is Theorem 9, proved in the paper’s appendix from Parameterized Set Cover. It states that CUGW is W[2]-hard parameterized by the number \(k\) of disjoint stars, even when the graph is a disjoint union of stars. The continuous question I would hand to a prover is:

Continuous Utilitarian Connected Allocation on Stars. An instance consists of a graph \(G=(V,E)\) that is a disjoint union of stars; a finite set of complete additive valuation types \(\Theta\), with \(u_\theta(v)\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\); rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) summing to \(1\); and rational per-capita supplies \(s_v\) for the vertices. A solution is a nonnegative distribution \(x_{\theta,B}\) over nonempty connected bundles \(B\subseteq V\), satisfying \(\sum_B x_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) for every type and \(\sum_{\theta,B:v\in B}x_{\theta,B}=s_v\) for every vertex. Each unit of mass assigned to \(B\) receives that integral connected bundle; the type’s average bundle may be fractional. Maximize \(\sum_{\theta,B}x_{\theta,B}\sum_{v\in B}u_\theta(v)\).

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Its configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables, but its pricing problem is simple on stars. A connected bundle is either a singleton leaf or a center together with a subset of that star’s leaves. For a type and dual vertex prices, the best center bundle is found by including precisely the leaves with positive reduced value; singleton leaves can be checked directly. Thus separation is polynomial in \(|V|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), giving an exact polynomial-time LP algorithm. Theorem 9’s discrete Set Cover reduction depends on assigning named agents indivisibly to centers; the mass model can split a type across bundle patterns, which is exactly where that combinatorics can disappear. Further questions include which graph classes still have tractable pricing and how the continuous optimum relates to finite-population approximations.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 7, proved here by reduction from Partition. It states that CEGW is NP-hard when \(G\) consists of two disjoint stars. Its reduction uses positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_q\) summing to \(2b\), two star owners \(I_1,I_2\), and \(q\) item agents \(J_i\). Owner \(I_1\) values its center at \(b+1\) and its \(i\)-th leaf at \(a_i\); \(I_2\) has the symmetric values on the other star. Each \(J_i\) values the two corresponding leaves at the threshold \(2b+1\). The threshold is achievable exactly when some subset of the \(a_i\) sums to \(b\).

Here is the corresponding continuous question:

Continuous Egalitarian Connected Allocation on Two Stars. Use the same type masses, per-capita vertex supplies, and connected-bundle variables as above, but restrict \(G\) to two stars. Given a threshold \(t\), decide whether there is a feasible allocation measure \(x_{\theta,B}\) supported only on bundles with \(u_\theta(B)\ge t\). This asks that every unit of positive type mass receive an integral connected bundle meeting the threshold.

I expect this mirror to remain NP-hard: the Partition reduction survives even when each type’s mass can split among bundles. Set the mass of each type to one for the argument, equivalently scaling the normalized instance uniformly. Let \(\alpha_i\) be the mass of \(J_i\) assigned the first star’s \(i\)-th leaf. Both owner types must receive their centers: without a center, their connected bundle cannot reach \(2b+1\). So \(J_i\) must split between its two valued leaves, leaving capacities \(1-\alpha_i\) and \(\alpha_i\) for the respective owners. Each owner needs leaf value at least \(b\). The two owners’ total available leaf value is at most \(\sum_i(1-\alpha_i)a_i+\sum_i\alpha_i a_i=2b\). Their combined threshold requirement is also \(2b\), so both attain exactly \(b\) on average. Since every bundle in either owner’s support must have leaf value at least \(b\), the first owner’s supported bundles must include one with leaf values summing to exactly \(b\). That is a Partition solution. This is a Class B-type transfer for this anchor: the hard combinatorics live in the item weights and the threshold, not merely in population multiplicity. A follow-up is to locate where threshold-constrained bundle pricing becomes hard even on simple graph classes.

The finite-population bridge is concrete: for \(n\) agents, set \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/n\) and \(s_v=1/n\) for each original item. A discrete allocation induces \(x\) by counting the fraction of each type assigned each bundle. Repeating each type and the corresponding resource components \(K\) times leaves the type distribution and per-capita supplies unchanged while making \(n=K\tau\). The intended regime is therefore a population with many repeated households of each complete valuation type, not arbitrary named-agent idiosyncrasies.

These mirrors cover only the paper’s utilitarian and egalitarian welfare problems under connected allocation, on stars and two stars respectively. They do not continuize outcome lotteries or welfare axioms. The weakest point is the resource interpretation: the paper’s base instances have a finite list of indivisible goods, while the mirrors use per-capita supplies over repeated resource components. That is a real modeling choice, but it is the natural co-modification needed for a large population; every unit of mass still receives an ordinary integral connected bundle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can make a real objection to how closely these mirrors track the paper’s named results, but I cannot honestly make the universal negative stick.

The strongest objection is that scaling the population also scales the resources and can change the graph parameter. Theorem 9 is parameterized by the number \(k\) of star components. Repeating each star to serve a larger population changes the actual number of components to \(Kk\); keeping \(k\) fixed instead requires treating each star as a repeatable template with per-capita supply. That is a coherent model, but it is an extended repeated-resource setting, not a limit of instances with the same \(k\). Theorem 7’s Partition reduction likewise has a growing collection of distinct valuation profiles. Neither reduction itself supplies a fixed-support population sequence.

That objection narrows the bridge; it does not defeat the stronger mirror. For Theorem 9, repeated households sharing valuation profiles and repeated copies of a small collection of star templates are plausible. In that model the utilitarian configuration LP has a simple pricing problem: on each star, choose the best center bundle by including leaves with positive reduced value, and check singleton leaves. This is a concrete Class A question, even if the answer looks accessible. The related connected-allocation literature’s use of small numbers of agent types supports the regime rather than colliding with it ([Bouveret et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10239)). I found no continuous treatment that already settles this computational question.

Theorem 7 is harder to argue against. Its egalitarian mirror uses the essential minimum: almost every person must receive an integral connected bundle meeting the threshold. Fractional mass can split across bundles, but in the Partition construction the two owners together have exactly enough leaf value to meet their thresholds. Every bundle in an owner’s support must therefore attain its threshold exactly, forcing a supported subset of item weights summing to \(b\). That is a meaningful continuous question and a substantive boundary result, even though the reduction’s support grows with the instance.

So the best negative case is a request for care in describing the dictionary: the star-template model needs its resource replication made explicit, and the Partition mirror is not evidence that multiplicity itself caused the source hardness. But the programme permits the required supply co-modification, and these objections do not make either question ill-posed or unworthwhile. I cannot fairly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this paper.
