I can’t make an anchor-based positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Section 4 gives named approximation methods—OSLA, VPI, MAP, and myopic Bayesian RL—and Section 5 reports experimental comparisons. Those are contributions, but they are not the kind of named result the requested anchors require. The formal value equations describe the model; they do not establish a complexity classification.

The strongest intuition for a mirror would be a repeated contracting market with many providers grouped by capability type. But the paper’s learning is tied to persistent partners and their individual histories, so a population model would need to preserve those evolving beliefs as well. That could motivate a research question, but it would not mirror a qualifying result proved here. There are therefore no anchors to restate and no per-anchor continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no proved computational result to mirror. Its value equations define a Bayesian learning problem; OSLA, VPI, MAP, and myopic Bayesian RL are approximation methods; the evidence for VPI is experimental performance in particular finite settings. None is a theorem about complexity or an algorithm with a proved complexity guarantee. So the case for supplies no anchor from which to state a continuous counterpart.

The strongest alternative is the subcontracting scenario: many providers share capability, cost, prior, and payoff types, and coalitions assemble for repeated projects. A mass model could assign fractions of each type to teams and track learning. But the paper’s central feature is knowledge about *particular* partners carrying across tasks. Agent \(i\)’s belief \(B_i\) is a joint distribution over the types of its peers; what it learns depends on which partners joined its coalition and what outcome they observed. A population distribution \(\mu\) over capability types does not retain those identity-linked beliefs or their correlation with future coalitions. Preserving them would require putting each agent’s history, posterior over named partners, and bargaining state into its type. Those states can proliferate as interactions unfold, turning the proposal into a new mean-field partially observed coalition model rather than a direct continuous counterpart of the paper’s algorithms. Anonymous rematching could simplify the model, but would remove the persistent-partner knowledge transfer that motivates the paper’s second experiment.

That is a strong reason to reject this paper as a ChoCo mirror target, but it does not establish the universal claim. A carefully specified mass model of repeated contracting could still be worthwhile; it would need new assumptions and a computational result of its own. The negative case is strongest on the absence of an anchor and the loss of persistent identities, and weakest where the task permits substantial re-modelling. I would not spend ChoCo’s mirror effort on this paper, while stopping short of claiming that no scenario could yield valuable research.
