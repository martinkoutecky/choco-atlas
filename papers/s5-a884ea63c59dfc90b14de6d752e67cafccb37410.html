The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many house categories \(H\), a finite support \(\mathcal A\subseteq 2^H\) of dichotomous preference types with rational masses \(\mu_A>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_h\geq 0\) with \(r=\sum_{h\in H}\rho_h\leq 1\), assume each category is acceptable to a positive-mass type and, for every nonempty \(H'\subseteq H\), \(\sum_{A:A\cap H'\neq\varnothing}\mu_A>\sum_{h\in H'}\rho_h\). Compute an exact type-level allocation \(x_{A,h}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_A\mu_Ax_{A,h}=\rho_h\) and \(\sum_hx_{A,h}=r\) for each type, whose per-person acceptable allocations realize the successive egalitarian bottleneck levels. At each stage, with remaining types \(\mathcal A'\), categories \(H'\), masses \(\mu_A\), and supplies \(\rho_h^{\rm rem}\), select a nonempty \(\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A'\) minimizing \(\frac{\sum_{h\in H'\cap\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal B}A}\rho_h^{\rm rem}}{\sum_{A\in\mathcal B}\mu_A}\), breaking ties by largest total mass; give each type in \(\mathcal B\) that amount of acceptable supply per person, then recurse on the remaining types and categories. Measure running time in \(|H|\), support size \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\), and rational input length \(L\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete acceptability sets \(A\subseteq H\), with mass \(\mu_A\); categories have per-capita supplies \(\rho_h\). The decision variables \(x_{A,h}\) give each type's per-person allocation. The objective is the successive egalitarian maximization of acceptable allocation levels, represented by weighted bottleneck ratios of remaining acceptable supply to remaining population mass.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is narrow: the paper’s dichotomous house-allocation result. The anchor is Proposition 4, proved here: in the shortage part of a dichotomous house-allocation problem, every BTM satisfying stepwise EEET finds the Bogomolnaia–Moulin egalitarian solution. That solution works through successive bottleneck groups: at each stage, find a group with the smallest ratio of acceptable remaining houses to agents, give its members equal amounts of acceptable houses, and recurse on the remaining agents and houses. The paper’s setup assumes each object is acceptable to someone and, for every nonempty set of objects, more agents find something in that set acceptable than there are objects in the set; it handles the broader problem by decomposition.

This is a constructive computational anchor, not a complexity theorem: Proposition 4 does not establish polynomial time, and the paper has no named result asserting NP-hardness or membership in P. I would not claim otherwise. But it names an underlying allocation problem and proves that a specified algorithmic family computes its solution. Since the paper offers no comparable hardness result, it is the best anchor available.

I would hand the following to a prover as the lead mirror: Dichotomous Egalitarian House Allocation\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite set \(H\) of interchangeable house categories, a finite support \(\mathcal A\subseteq 2^H\) of dichotomous preference types, rational masses \(\mu_A>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_h\geq 0\), with \(r=\sum_{h\in H}\rho_h\leq 1\). A type \(A\) accepts precisely the categories in \(A\). Require each category to be acceptable to some positive-mass type and, for every nonempty \(H'\subseteq H\),
\[ \sum_{A:A\cap H'\neq\varnothing}\mu_A>\sum_{h\in H'}\rho_h. \]
The requested output is an exact type-level allocation \(x_{A,h}\geq 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\mu_Ax_{A,h}=\rho_h \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{h\in H}x_{A,h}=r\ \text{for each }A, \]
whose acceptable-house amounts realize the egalitarian bottleneck levels.

Concretely, at a stage with remaining types \(\mathcal A'\) and categories \(H'\), choose a nonempty set \(\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A'\) minimizing
\[ \frac{\sum_{h\in H'\cap\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal B}A}\rho_h^{\rm rem}} {\sum_{A\in\mathcal B}\mu_A}. \]
Choose a largest-mass minimizer, give each type in \(\mathcal B\) that ratio’s amount of acceptable supply from its neighborhood, then remove \(\mathcal B\) and those categories and continue. Any feasible \(x\) realizing these successive levels is an acceptable output. The computational question is to return such an allocation, with running time measured in \(|H|\), support size \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\), and rational input length \(L\).

The regime is plausible: many applicants in a city or housing program can share a small number of acceptability profiles over a small number of interchangeable unit categories, while housing supply scales with the population. The type is the complete acceptability set; mass is the fraction of applicants with that set; the decision variable is the per-person allocation to each category. This is population continuization, not merely fractionalizing the outcome. It also has a clean high-multiplicity bridge: when the rational inputs have common denominator \(N\), expand them into \(N\mu_A\) applicants of each type and \(N\rho_h\) houses of each category. The finite bottleneck ratios then equal the continuous ones. This is a co-modification of per-capita supply, but a natural one for the model.

I expect Class A, likely via repeated bottleneck computation using flow or LP machinery. The paper itself notes that Katta and Sethuraman use network-flow tools to compute the finite egalitarian solution. That prior result supports tractability; it does not make the continuous-population question a novelty collision. The continuous result still needs its own exact bit-complexity analysis and a precise finite-to-continuous dictionary. Further questions include whether the BTM equations give a polynomial type-compressed algorithm and whether analogous mass-based results extend from dichotomous preferences to the full weak-preference domain. My scope here is only Proposition 4, not the paper’s broader extension of PS or its fairness results for fractional endowment exchange.

The weakest point is the object-side scaling: the paper presents a finite set of houses, whereas the mirror groups repeated, interchangeable houses into categories whose supplies scale with population. That is credible for housing inventories with repeated unit categories, but less so when each house is uniquely described and matters individually. Also, Proposition 4 proves correctness of the mechanism’s output, not its runtime. Those limits make this a focused algorithmic mirror rather than a claimed continuous complexity theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 4 is not a complexity result. It says a family of finite-agent mechanisms selects the Bogomolnaia–Moulin egalitarian solution; it gives no running time, hardness, or type-compression bound. The paper’s main contributions concern efficiency and fairness of mechanisms for named agents. So Proposition 4 alone does not establish a computational-complexity gap for ChoCo to fill.

That objection does not defeat the proposed mirror. In a housing program with repeated unit categories and cohorts sharing acceptability profiles, the type is a complete description relevant to the allocation. Per-capita supplies are a natural co-modification. For rational masses and supplies, scaling by a common denominator gives a finite instance; identical applicants can be grouped without changing the bottleneck ratios. Thus the proposed type-level egalitarian-allocation problem is well-posed and genuinely continuizes the population. The finite-to-continuous correspondence would be a result to establish, not a novelty collision.

A stronger version would ask for an exact algorithm with running time polynomial in the number of categories, support size, and rational input length, then extend the question to the paper’s full weak-preference mechanism. The dichotomous case may reduce to weighted bottleneck or flow computation, so it may prove routine rather than reveal a surprising complexity boundary. But a likely tractable answer does not make the question unworthy, and existing finite flow methods do not by themselves rule out the continuous mirror.

So the negative case is weak at its central point. I can argue that the paper provides no complexity theorem to mirror and that Proposition 4 may yield a modest algorithmic contribution. I cannot honestly show that its dichotomous, high-multiplicity mirror is not worthwhile; the proponent’s housing-cohort scenario survives.
