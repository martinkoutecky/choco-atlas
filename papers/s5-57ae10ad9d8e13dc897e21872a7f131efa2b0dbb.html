What the mirror covers

Theorem 1.1 and Theorems 1.2–1.3 analyze expected counts under random preference models, including solvability rates for stronger notions of stability. None supplies a computational result to mirror; the integral formulas are analytical tools in those proofs.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern asymptotic counts in random instances: Theorem 1.1 bounds the expected number of weakly stable cyclic matchings; Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 bound expected numbers of weakly, strongly, and super-stable matchings under random partially ordered preferences. Those are results about probability and enumeration, not about whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. The integral formulas in Lemmas 2.1 and 3.1 support those analyses; they do not give a computational formulation the paper solves.

The closest possible continuous object would be a high-multiplicity stable-matching market: preference rankings are types, mass is the fraction of each side with each ranking, and a mass flow assigns the two sides while satisfying stability. A large labor or placement market with repeated preference classes could make that a sensible problem. But it would mirror stable-matching existence or allocation, not this paper’s results about the expected number of stable matchings in random finite markets. Treating its asymptotic count theorems as complexity anchors would overstate the case.

So I can’t give a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor* without inventing an anchor the paper does not contain. The honest positive case is limited to saying that stable matching has a plausible high-multiplicity analogue; this paper offers no named computational result for that analogue to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the level of the paper’s actual results. Theorems 1.1–1.3 analyze expected numbers of stable matchings under particular random laws; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The random input is built from independent preferences over *named individuals*. In the two-sided model, a complete type is therefore a ranking or partial order over the other \(n\) agents, and that type space changes with \(n\). Under the paper’s sampling law, repeated types become negligible as \(n\) grows. There is no fixed, moderately supported population distribution here for a continuous algorithm to operate on.

The best version of the proposed high-multiplicity mirror would use repeated preference classes in a large placement market, perhaps with preferences over classes of counterpart rather than named people. That can be a sensible continuous stable-matching problem. But it changes the paper’s random model and its central quantity. Its theorems count discrete bijections drawn from a random market; a continuous mass assignment can have an entire continuum of feasible stable flows. Turning that into a “number” would require choosing a new quantity—such as volume or entropy—and a measure for it. That could motivate a separate project, but it is not a continuous version of the results proved here.

The same obstacle applies to the cyclic result and to the partial-order results. One could define finite-type continuous variants of their stability notions, and then ask computational questions about finding stable flows. Yet those questions would concern new optimization problems, not the expected counts or random-instance solvability rates in this paper. The strict orders over named partners, and the paper’s random construction of partial orders from random coordinates assigned to those partners, are doing essential work in the probability calculations; aggregation removes that structure.

So the proponent’s high-multiplicity market is plausible as a research direction, but it does not supply an anchor for a worthwhile continuous *mirror of this paper*. The honest limit of this case is that no argument rules out every independently interesting model someone might build from stable matching. What the paper itself offers is unusually little support for ChoCo’s computational programme: its population is not naturally repeated over a fixed type space, and its named results are probabilistic enumeration rather than computational results.
