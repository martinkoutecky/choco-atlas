The case FOR (proponent)

There is no admissible anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Definition 1 defines the Shapley value; the discussion of exponential exact computation and polynomial-time approximation is unnumbered, with the approximation method attributed to prior work. I can’t honestly supply a continuous problem *per anchor* under your criteria.

The strongest positive seed, though not an anchor-grounded case, is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s majority aggregator. Imagine a large crowdsourcing service repeatedly administering the same questionnaire, with many workers sharing a small number of response patterns. A type is a complete response vector \(r\in K^m\); its mass \(\mu_r\) is the fraction of workers with that pattern. Given rational masses and the answer key, compute the lexicographically tie-broken majority response for each item and the resulting raw score and IQ. This is a recognizable continuous version of one of the paper’s studied aggregators, and is tractable by summing masses across the \(\tau\) supported types.

Its weakness is substantial: that question does not mirror a named computational result in the paper. It also captures the large-crowd limit of majority voting, not the paper’s empirical sample-size curves, its machine-learning aggregator, or its individual Shapley contributions. So it is a plausible continuous analogue of part of the study, but it cannot support the required anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the absence of an anchor. This is an empirical study, not a paper that states and proves a computational result: Definition 1 gives the Shapley value, while the discussion of exponential exact computation and polynomial approximation cites prior work. Under the requested standard, there is no named result here for a continuous mirror to classify.

The majority-vote seed is well-defined, but too slight to repair that gap. Given masses on response-vector types, compute each item’s plurality answer by summing masses, then score the resulting questionnaire. That takes \(O(m\tau k)\) arithmetic operations. It does not expose the kind of algorithmic landscape the programme seeks. A stronger version aimed at the paper’s crowd-size curves needs the sample size \(n\): the same distribution \(\mu\) can generate different finite-sample results at different \(n\). In the large-\(n\) limit, the curves collapse to the population plurality outcome, so the saturation phenomenon the paper measures is gone.

The more substantial candidate is Contextual IQ. But its object is each participant’s marginal contribution over random predecessor coalitions. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, and a plurality-based score is locally unchanged by an infinitesimal addition except at tie boundaries. A type-level attribution would need a chosen limit or normalization of the finite Shapley game; the paper supplies neither. Such a construction might be worth studying, but it would be a new cooperative-game model, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

One could also invent a minimum-mass-change question about altering the crowd’s answers or score. That may be a plausible continuous robustness problem, but it adds an intervention the paper never studies. The honest negative case is therefore strong against the proposed seed and against an anchor-based reading of the paper. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile problem could ever be inspired by it: the paper’s empirical findings could motivate new computational questions, but that would be a new research direction rather than a continuization of one of its computational results.
