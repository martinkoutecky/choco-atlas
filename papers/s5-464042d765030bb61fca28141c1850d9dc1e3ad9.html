The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational society distribution \(\mu\), rational target type-power vector \(b\), and threshold \(\alpha\), decide whether there exist nonnegative type weights \(w_t\) and per-capita quota \(q\) such that, for every replication level \(r\) with \(r\mu_t\in\mathbb{Z}\), the weighted rule on \(r\mu_t\) indistinguishable voters of type \(t\) has normalized type-total Banzhaf power \(P_{r,t}\) satisfying \(\limsup_{r\to\infty}\sum_t\lvert P_{r,t}-b_t\rvert\le\alpha\); equivalently, minimize this limsup.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity weighted simple-game model with type mass \(\mu_t\), \(r\mu_t\) interchangeable voters, per-capita quota \(q\), type weight \(w_t\), and objective \(D(q,w)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\sum_t\lvert P_{r,t}-b_t\rvert\).

The objection that survived

Type-total Banzhaf power is not canonical: under regular weighted replication the ordinary limit may collapse to \(P_t\propto\mu_t w_t\), while retaining finite lattice effects requires choosing a replication process and scaling that the proponent does not fully specify.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers inverse Penrose-Banzhaf design for weighted rules \(\mathcal{W}\) and the paper's major/minor reduction, but leaves the broader classes \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{C}\), finite enumeration and ILP comparisons, and finite-player residue effects untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under which quota and regularity conditions does \(P_{r,t}\) converge to \(\frac{\mu_t w_t}{\sum_u\mu_u w_u}\), and does this make the unscaled inverse problem trivially Class A?
  • Which replication, coalition, and first-order scaling convention yields a canonical discrete-to-continuous dictionary while retaining nontrivial finite effects?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible positive case, but I would not base it on the paper’s EU examples: six to twenty-seven named council members are not a convincing high-multiplicity regime. The strongest mirror is the paper’s own “oceanic game” direction: a very large population of repeated minor types, possibly alongside a fixed number of exceptional major members.

The type is a constituency or shareholder class whose members have the same ballot role, voting-rule parameters, and target influence. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the large body in class \(t\). A plausible regime is a federation with millions of small, interchangeable chapters and only \(k\) exceptional institutional members, where \(k\) is fixed and \(\tau\) is small compared with the population.

My lead problem is Continuous Type-Level Inverse Banzhaf Design. Its input is a rational type distribution \(\mu\), a rational desired power distribution \(b\) over types, an error threshold \(\alpha\), and the admissible class of voting rules, here weighted rules. A solution is a quota \(q\) and nonnegative type weights \(w_t\). For every replication level \(r\) for which \(r\mu_t\) is integral, construct \(r\mu_t\) indistinguishable voters of type \(t\), with winning condition

\[ \chi_r(S)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_t w_t |S\cap V_t^{(r)}|\ge rq. \]

For one voter \(i\) of type \(t\), let \(s_{r,t}\) be its number of swings,

\[ s_{r,t}=\sum_{S\subseteq V^{(r)}\setminus\{i\}} \bigl(\chi_r(S\cup\{i\})-\chi_r(S)\bigr). \]

The induced total Banzhaf power of type \(t\) is

\[ P_{r,t}=\frac{r\mu_t s_{r,t}}{\sum_u r\mu_u s_{r,u}}. \]

The continuous objective is

\[ D(q,w)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\sum_t |P_{r,t}-b_t|. \]

The decision question is whether some weighted rule has \(D(q,w)\le\alpha\); the optimization question is to minimize \(D(q,w)\). Thus the objective compares total influence assigned to a population type, not the influence of an individually named voter. If one wants the paper’s oceanic specialization, the \(k\) exceptional major voters are retained as atomic coordinates in addition to the replicated minor population.

The anchor is Theorem 1 (Alon–Edelman). It is cited by Kurz and Napel, not proved in this paper. In full, it says: for a simple game \(\chi\) on \(n\) players, with players \(1,\ldots,k\) designated as major and \(0<\varepsilon<1/(k+1)\), if the total normalized Penrose–Banzhaf power of the remaining players satisfies \(\sum_{i=k+1}^n B_i(\chi)\le\varepsilon\), then there exists a \(k\)-player simple game \(\chi'\) such that

\[ \sum_{i=1}^k |B_i(\chi)-B_i(\chi')| +\sum_{i=k+1}^n B_i(\chi) \le \frac{(2k+1)\varepsilon}{1-(k+1)\varepsilon}+\varepsilon. \]

The paper also notes that the construction preserves completeness and weightedness when the original game has those properties.

This is exactly the kind of finite-to-continuous bridge the mirror needs. In the high-multiplicity instance, the large minor population is represented by a mass type, while Theorem 1 says that whenever its total influence is small, the relevant inverse-design problem can be approximated by a finite \(k\)-player core with an explicit additive error. The paper’s own discussion of oceanic games makes the interpretation especially plausible: it explicitly says that a few atomic players in an ocean of infinitesimal voters reduce to the same kind of inverse problem studied in the paper.

I would expect this lead problem to be Class A when \(k\) and the number of repeated types are fixed: the ocean can be aggregated, the remaining rule has finitely many parameters, and Theorem 1 supplies an approximation guarantee. If \(k\) is part of the input and arbitrary simple rules are allowed, the natural expectation is instead Class B: the combinatorics live in the finite major-player core, so hardness should transfer rather than disappear. I would not claim a proved P result here; this paper contains no named NP-hardness, P, FPT, or similar complexity theorem. Its computational evidence is Appendix A’s exact ILP feasibility formulation and bisection method, not a complexity classification.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The mirror preserves the same simple-game object, the same Penrose–Banzhaf influence measure, the same inverse objective, and the same weighted/complete restrictions. Only the population representation changes: coalitions of indistinguishable voters are represented by type masses. This is not continuity of outcomes or an axiomatic profile simplex.

The weakest point is the definition of continuous Banzhaf power. In a literal atomless population, an individual voter is pivotal with probability zero. The type-total limit \(P_{r,t}\) is therefore a necessary co-modification, not a cosmetic rewrite. Moreover, for regular weighted rules the ordinary limit may collapse to a simple Penrose-style proportionality formula, losing the lattice effects studied in the paper. The honest response is to retain the \(\limsup\), or study a first-order scaled objective when needed. That creates further questions about Gaussian limits, discrete recovery, and the residue-class effects visible in Lemma 2. It is a real soft spot, but the paper’s own oceanic-game discussion and Theorem 1 provide unusually strong evidence that this co-modified continuous question is still recognizably theirs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a basic mismatch: the paper’s computational object is a finite Boolean function on named players, not a population distribution. Its only named theorem anchor is not a computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 is an approximation theorem for a fixed finite game; Lemmas 1–3 are counting and asymptotic calculations; and the ILP formulation has exponentially many coalition variables without a polynomial-time or hardness classification. Thus the proposed continuous inverse problem would be a new asymptotic voting-power problem, not a continuization of one of the paper’s computational results.

Theorem 1 also does not support the proposed oceanic mirror in the way claimed. Its premise is

\[ \sum_{i=k+1}^{n} B_i(\chi)\le \varepsilon, \]

that is, the *aggregate* Banzhaf power of all minor players is small. In a replicated population, each minor voter may have vanishing individual power while the replicated minor population has total power of order one. Then Theorem 1 does not apply. If its premise does apply, the entire continuous population contributes at most \(\varepsilon\) to the objective, and the theorem replaces it by a \(k\)-player finite game. The supposed continuum has then disappeared from the substantive problem.

The paper’s oceanic-game remark does not repair this. It says that an ocean of infinitesimal voters can be absorbed into a quota adjustment, leaving a modified game on the atomic players. That is evidence that such limits can be mathematically studied, but it is evidence against this being a population-continuization of the paper’s inverse problem: the non-atomic population contributes only a scalar shift, while all combinatorial design remains in the finite atomic core. With fixed \(k\), the mirror is essentially a finite inverse problem with a parameter; with growing \(k\), the finite combinatorics return.

More fundamentally, Banzhaf power has no intrinsic atomless analogue. The index is defined by whether changing one player changes the outcome under the uniform measure on finite coalitions. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass, so changing that individual never changes a mass-based outcome. If one retains individual power, every individual’s power collapses to zero. If one instead aggregates the power of all members of a type, one must specify a replication sequence, a random-coalition process, and a fluctuation scale. These are additional modelling choices, not consequences of the society distribution \(\mu\).

The proposed \(\limsup\) does not solve that problem. It makes the answer depend on the chosen finite approximations and preserves precisely the lattice artefacts that the paper studies through quota intervals and residue classes. A first-order rescaling could retain those effects, but then one has defined a new local-limit problem, with no canonical choice between \(r^{1/2}\)-, \(r\)-, or finer scalings. It is not a well-defined continuous society problem determined by \(\mu\).

Indeed, in the regular weighted case the natural limit degenerates. Replicate type \(t\) \(r\mu_t\) times, give each copy weight \(w_t\), and place the quota in the central-limit window. The total pivotality of type \(t\) is asymptotically proportional to \(\mu_t w_t\), so

\[ P_t \longrightarrow \frac{\mu_t w_t}{\sum_u \mu_u w_u}. \]

Consequently, for any desired type-power vector \(b\), choosing

\[ w_t\propto \frac{b_t}{\mu_t} \]

implements \(b\) exactly in the limit. The inverse problem therefore has zero error for essentially every target, and the finite impossibility phenomena, heuristic gaps, and distinctions between \(\mathcal S\), \(\mathcal C\), and \(\mathcal W\) vanish. That is a genuine continuum degeneration, not merely the observation that continuity “fails to help.”

There is no satisfactory middle ground. If one keeps weighted rules and takes the ordinary limit, the inverse problem becomes trivial. If one keeps the paper’s richer simple-game structure and its finite pivotality effects, one must retain an entire sequence of finite games and a coalition probability model; \(\mu\) alone is insufficient. If one keeps a fixed atomic core, the problem is finite again. And if one aggregates by type, the target and objective have been materially redesigned.

A large federation of repeated chapters or a shareholder population is a plausible high-multiplicity story, so the negative case should not pretend that no such scenario can be imagined. The stronger conclusion is narrower: none of those scenarios yields a nondegenerate, intrinsically defined continuous mirror of the paper’s computational contribution. The proponent’s proposal could become worthwhile as a new asymptotic theory of type-level pivotality, but that would be a new problem requiring an explicit coalition process and scaling theory. Theorem 1 and the paper’s oceanic remark do not establish that it is the paper’s continuous mirror.
