The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a conditional yes, with an important limitation: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorems 1–9 and Lemmas 1–3 concern equilibrium existence, equilibrium payoffs, comparative statics, and institutional design. None asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized tractability, or an algorithm for a computational problem. Thus, under the programme’s strict anchor rule, there are no qualifying anchors, and I cannot honestly present this as a paper whose computational result has a direct continuous mirror.

There is nevertheless a credible continuous-population research problem here.

Take a finite type set \(T\), with a society described by \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). A type records everything relevant to the bargaining problem: discount factor \(\delta_t\), utility function \(u_t\), voting weight or veto status, and recognition role. The intended regime is a very large assembly, federation, or constituent body with many interchangeable delegates in each of finitely many roles: \(\tau=|T|\) is fixed or small while \(n\gg\tau\). Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of delegates of type \(t\), not uncertainty about an individual.

Use a non-unanimous quota \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). A public recognition state specifies the distribution over the next proposer’s type and which types have been ruled out. Let \(\ell_s\) be the mass of agents who, in state \(s\), have zero probability of proposing next.

The action of a tagged current proposer is a continuum coalition and allocation:
\[ (x_p,(\eta_t,y_t)_{t\in T}), \]
where \(x_p\) is the proposer’s share, \(\eta_t\leq\mu_t\) is the mass of type-\(t\) voters recruited, and \(y_t\) is the payment to each recruited voter. The constraints are
\[ x_p+\sum_{t\in T}\eta_t y_t=1, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\eta_t\geq\alpha. \]
A type-\(t\) voter accepts when \(u_t(y_t)\) covers her discounted continuation value. Rejection moves the game to the next recognition state. Markov strategies depend on the public recognition state and the current proposer type, not on irrelevant past votes.

The proposer-power question is then:

Given \((T,\mu,\alpha)\), the recognition kernel, discount and utility data, an initial state, and a threshold \(z\), compute or decide whether the initial proposer’s payoff is at least \(z\) in every Markov perfect equilibrium. A solution consists of the value, an equilibrium proposal and coalition-mass profile, or an approximation to these.

This is plausibly the authors’ problem rather than an arbitrary tractable simplification. It retains their fixed surplus, non-unanimous coalition formation, predictable recognition, dynamic continuation values, and Markov equilibrium concept. What changes is precisely the population regime: individual legislators are replaced by masses of interchangeable legislators.

The closest named support is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states:

If the voting rule requires \(q<n\) votes and the recognition process has one-period predictability of degree \(q\)—at every structural state, at least \(q\) players are ruled out as the next proposer—then the proposer at \(t=0\) captures the entire surplus in every MPE.

Its continuous analogue is the problem just defined restricted to instances satisfying
\[ \ell_s\geq\alpha \]
at every reachable state. The initial proposer can recruit a winning coalition entirely from next-period losers, so the continuous answer is \(x_p=1\). This is a Class A result: checking the mass condition and constructing the zero-payment coalition is immediate. It is not a computational theorem of the paper, but it gives a clean continuous decision problem whose equilibrium logic survives exactly.

The strongest named support is Theorem 6, also proved here, because it explicitly studies a high-multiplicity limit. For games \(G_n\) with
\[ \frac{q_n}{n}\to\alpha_v, \qquad \frac{d_n}{n}\to\alpha_p, \]
Theorem 6 says that, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), all sufficiently large games have first-proposer share within \(\varepsilon\) of
\[ V(\alpha_v,\alpha_p,\delta)= \begin{cases} 1-\dfrac{\delta(\alpha_v-\alpha_p)} {1-\alpha_p(1+\delta(1-\alpha_v))}, & \alpha_p\leq\alpha_v,\[1.2ex] 1,& \alpha_p>\alpha_v. \end{cases} \]

The corresponding continuous problem, *Continuum Proposer Share*, takes \(\alpha\), predictable-loser mass \(\beta\), and common discount factor \(\delta\), and asks for the limiting expected payoff of the initial proposer. Its answer in the symmetric recognition regime is
\[ V(\alpha,\beta,\delta)= \begin{cases} 1-\dfrac{\delta(\alpha-\beta)} {1-\beta(1+\delta(1-\alpha))}, & \beta\leq\alpha,\[1.2ex] 1,& \beta>\alpha. \end{cases} \]
This is again Class A in the stated regime: the answer is closed form. Theorem 6 should be treated as supporting evidence and a known special case, not as a novelty collision. The paper proves a discrete-to-continuous limit; it does not classify algorithms for arbitrary typed distributions, heterogeneous utilities, or recognition kernels.

Theorem 2 supplies a useful robust variant. It states that if at least \(q\) players have probability at most \(\varepsilon\) of being recognized next, then in every MPE the first proposer offers no other player more than
\[ \frac{\hat\delta\varepsilon} {1-\hat\delta(1-\varepsilon)}. \]
The continuous version replaces “at least \(q\) players” by a mass at least \(\alpha\). It asks for the maximal per-agent payment to an almost-loser type under \(\varepsilon\)-predictability. This should also be tractable in the finite-type transferable-utility model, via fractional coalition selection.

The principal weakness is that the paper’s proposer is a named individual, whereas a literal nonatomic population has no positive-mass individual. The mirror must therefore retain a tagged proposer whose payoff is recorded separately from the population allocation. Without that tag, “the first proposer captures the entire surplus” becomes meaningless or collapses into a statement about zero mass. This is a genuine modelling issue, though it is a forced co-modification of the population model rather than a change of subject.

A second weakness is empirical rather than conceptual: ordinary legislatures are small. The regime is more convincing for a large federation, mass delegate assembly, or replicated institutional body than for a seven-member committee or a conventional parliament.

So the positive conclusion is modest but real. The paper does support a sensible continuous-population analogue, especially through Theorem 6’s explicit \(n\to\infty\) analysis. Its natural ChoCo questions concern computing equilibrium proposer power from a finite distribution of delegate types and a recognition kernel. But because the paper has no named computational result, this is a promising new computational mirror inspired by the paper—not a mirror of an existing complexity theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at the programme’s entry point: this paper contains no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1, Theorems 2–9, and Lemmas 1–3 concern equilibrium payoffs, equilibrium structure, comparative statics, and institutional design. None defines a finite computational input, an output problem, an algorithm, or a complexity classification. A proposed task such as “compute the initial proposer’s payoff in every MPE” would be a new computational-economics project inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.

Theorem 1 does not repair this. Its most natural continuum translation would replace \(q/n\) by a quota \(\alpha\), and require a mass \(\ell_s\ge\alpha\) of next-period losers. But the discrete proof depends on the integer slack

\[ |H|\ge n-q+1,\qquad |L|\ge q, \]

which guarantees \(|H\cap L|\ge1\). After normalization, this becomes only

\[ \mu(H)\ge1-\alpha,\qquad \mu(L)\ge\alpha, \]

which guarantees no positive-mass intersection. The single decisive witness in the proof has disappeared in the nonatomic limit. The same issue affects the robust version in Theorem 2.

One can repair this by imposing a positive slack condition such as \(\ell_s\ge\alpha+\eta\), or by retaining a distinguished atomic proposer and finite-size correction. But then the result is either a new robust theorem or a reintroduction of the identity-level feature that drove the original proof. Under the slack condition the answer is simply that the proposer gets \(1\); checking the condition is not a computational problem. With a finite type space, selecting the cheapest winning mass is just fractional coalition selection by continuation values. The unresolved object is the recognition kernel and the MPE fixed point, for which the paper supplies neither a finite representation nor an algorithmic result.

Theorem 2 is no stronger. Its proposed “maximum per-agent payment” is not defined by the continuous society alone: agents of the same preference or utility type may still have different recognition histories, while a continuum has no canonical individual whose payment is being optimized. If one makes payment type-level and assumes finitely many exchangeable types, the coalition problem becomes a fractional knapsack calculation. If one retains arbitrary identity- and history-dependent recognition, then the type space must encode those histories and generally ceases to have high multiplicity. Either way, the computational content is newly added rather than inherited from the theorem.

Theorem 6 is the strongest point for the other side, but it supports only admissibility, not a worthwhile mirror. It shows that a large, role-replicated legislature is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; that must be conceded. It also already gives the proposed symmetric continuum limit \(V(\alpha_v,\alpha_p,\delta)\) in closed form. Thus the exact “Continuum Proposer Share” problem offered by the proponent is already answered by the paper as an asymptotic comparative-static theorem, and it is not computational. Generalizing it to heterogeneous utilities, types, or recognition kernels could certainly produce a new paper, but that generalization is no longer a mirror of Theorem 6’s result.

The tagging manoeuvre does not create a computational anchor either. It is a reasonable way to preserve the phrase “the first proposer,” but it means that a measure-zero distinguished office receives the entire fixed surplus while the continuum describes only the surrounding legislature. That may be a legitimate model, but the hard object is then the dynamic allocation of an institutional role, not computation over a continuous society in the ChoCo sense.

The honest concession is that the paper does suggest a plausible adjacent research programme on finite-type, role-replicated bargaining. Theorem 6 makes the population regime defensible, and this is not a case where high-multiplicity work should count against the paper. But the continuous-population component contributes no computational theorem, no pricing problem, and no complexity landscape. Every genuine algorithmic question has to be supplied by a new model of the recognition process and equilibrium computation. On the programme’s stated standard, that is not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
