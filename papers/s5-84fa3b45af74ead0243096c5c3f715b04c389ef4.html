The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved here, but they compare winning probabilities rather than classify the complexity of a problem:

  • Theorem 1: With one voter holding more than half the initial voting weight, voters independently favoring \(A\) with probability \(p>0.5\), and any \(m\ge1\) delegators whose votes are assigned uniformly at random, delegation weakly increases the probability that \(A\) wins, for any delegator weight distribution.
  • Theorem 2: With equal initial voter weights under the same preference and delegation assumptions, delegation weakly decreases the probability that \(A\) wins, for any \(m\ge1\) and any delegator weight distribution.

Propositions 1–3 give limiting probability claims. None of these results says that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Equations (1) and (2) express the winning probabilities, but the paper does not give a complexity result for evaluating them. So there are no anchors meeting your stated criterion.

The nearest possible mirror would take voter types to be weight classes, with mass recording each class’s share of voters, and delegator types recording their per-capita weights. Uniform delegation in the continuum would give every voter the same added weight per capita; with fixed preference shares by type, one could calculate the resulting weighted support and determine the winner. But that model makes the outcome deterministic and loses the finite-election randomness central to Theorems 1 and 2. Preserving that randomness would require additional modeling choices, and the resulting question would not be a direct continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s results are about probabilities, not computational problems. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 compare the probability that \(A\) wins before and after delegation; Propositions 1–3 establish limits. None classifies an algorithmic problem. Equation (1) suggests a possible new task—computing the exact win probability—but the paper neither poses nor solves it. A continuous mirror would therefore need to add a computational question before there is anything computational to mirror.

The weight-class proposal does not preserve the theorems’ central object. With a fixed continuous distribution of voter types, preference shares and weight shares are fixed, so weighted majority has a deterministic outcome. In the equal-weight case with \(A\)-preference share \(p>1/2\), \(A\) wins. The finite-election probability loss in Theorem 2 comes from random votes and delegation assignments; with a growing population and fixed \(p>1/2\), those fluctuations disappear. Scaling \(p\) toward \(1/2\) to retain them would make this a new asymptotic question.

The Dominant-Weight case is harder to carry over. If the dominant voter becomes a positive-mass type, many voters’ independent preferences average out; the type no longer acts like one randomly decisive voter. If it remains one voter with a random preference and macroscopic weight, the model preserves an individual pivotal agent alongside the continuum. That hybrid can be studied, but the theorem’s key uncertainty then comes from the retained individual and finite delegation events, rather than from a continuous population.

There is a way to preserve the delegation lottery: let finitely many delegators place macroscopic weight on randomly sampled individuals from an atomless electorate, creating random point masses. That is a coherent candidate model, but its nontrivial outcomes depend on keeping those finite random placements. If delegators are also made a fluid population, the placements average out. And a complexity result about evaluating the remaining finite lottery would concern that finite stochastic structure; it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

One could also introduce a policy problem, such as choosing type-level delegation caps to maximize the chance that the preferred alternative wins. That might be useful research, but this paper fixes delegation as uniform and random. Choosing caps, costs, or targeting rules adds a control problem that the paper does not formulate. Its numerical correlations and Sybil discussion do not supply a computational anchor either: they report associations and consequences of identity splitting, not algorithmic results.

So I would not prioritize a ChoCo mirror of this paper. The case is strongest for the two main theorems: their effects depend on finite-sample randomness that a deterministic continuum erases, while restoring that randomness requires retaining the discrete events doing the work. The universal claim remains hard to prove: a hybrid continuum with random atoms could be worth studying. But that would be a new stochastic delegation model, not a continuous computational mirror of the paper’s results.
