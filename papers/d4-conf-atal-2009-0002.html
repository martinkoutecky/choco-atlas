The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational complexity result of the required kind, so I can’t honestly supply an anchor-based positive case.

It does have numbered results, but they are mathematical guarantees about the negotiation procedure: Theorem 1 says an interior outcome is Pareto efficient exactly when the two utility gradients point in opposite directions; it is attributed to earlier work [12]. Theorem 2 proves that at any interior point there is a jointly improving direction with equal directional derivatives. Theorem 3 proves that, along the proposed direction, the ratio of the agents’ utility gains tends to \(1\) as the step size tends to zero. Theorem 4 says a \(\tau\)-satisfying Pareto-efficient outcome becomes fully Pareto efficient as \(\tau\) tends to zero. None asserts a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class, or gives a complexity result for an underlying computational problem. The experiments are numerical comparisons, not numbered complexity results.

A population mirror is imaginable—for example, repeated buyer–seller negotiations grouped by shared utility types—but independent negotiations would simply run the paper’s pairwise procedure separately. Making the population interact through one shared agreement would instead change the problem to negotiation between heterogeneous blocs, with a new aggregate-fairness question. Since the paper already makes the outcome space continuous while keeping exactly two negotiators, that extension would not provide a convincing mirror of a named computational result about continuizing the population.

So there are zero qualifying anchors, and therefore zero continuous problem statements to give under the requested anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “case for” offers no positive anchor: it explicitly says there are none. That is the strongest objection here. Theorem 1 is attributed to earlier work; Theorems 2 and 3 concern the geometry of improving directions and infinitesimal utility gains; Theorem 4 concerns the limit of the stopping tolerance. The experiments compare numerical outcomes. None states a complexity result for a computational problem. The continuous issue space does not supply the missing population axis.

The strongest candidate for a better mirror is a distribution of bilateral negotiations, with a type recording both agents’ utility functions, the issue domain, and the current agreement. That is a coherent high multiplicity regime: many identical dyads could arise in repeated commerce. But each dyad has its own agreement, and the paper’s fairness criterion compares the two agents within that dyad. With independent negotiations, the population contributes weights over repeated runs of the same procedure; a joint population objective or constraint would need to be added. A shared resource constraint or a population-wide fairness target could make the computation interesting, but neither appears in the paper’s results.

A two-bloc model is a stronger alternative. Aggregate each bloc’s members into a utility function, then apply E-DD to those two utilities. If members within each bloc have identical utilities, multiplicity adds no structure to the negotiation. If they differ, equal gains for the two aggregate utilities do not ensure that each member benefits or receives a fair gain; specifying those guarantees creates a new multilateral fairness problem. In either version, the paper supplies no complexity claim for a population distribution.

So I would argue against a worthwhile *mirror of this paper*: its results give no computational-complexity anchor, and the natural population extensions either preserve the bilateral problem or require a new problem definition. This is not proof that no useful research question can be built from the setting. The negative case is weakest at that universal claim: a carefully specified model of heterogeneous blocs could be worthwhile, but its value would come from a new fairness and computation question, not from continuizing a named result here.
