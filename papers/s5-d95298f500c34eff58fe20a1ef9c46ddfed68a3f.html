What the mirror covers

No computational theorem is mirrored. The candidate touches only Theorem 3’s complete-graph Shapley identity and leaves the network-specific tree results and axiomatic characterizations aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow: this paper has no named computational result of the kind you require as an anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes the average tree solution axiomatically; Theorem 2 characterizes when it is a Harsanyi solution; Theorems 3 and 4 identify average tree solutions with the Shapley value and equal surplus division in special cases. None classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. So there is no compliant anchor, and no anchor-indexed mirror question to claim for this paper.

The closest foothold is Theorem 3, stated in this paper and assembled from its Proposition 4 and results cited from Herings et al. It says that on a tree communication graph, averaging the DFS contribution vectors gives the earlier average tree solution; on a complete graph, averaging the \(n!\) DFS contribution vectors gives the Shapley value; and the average tree solution is Harsanyi exactly when its tree sets are contained in the DFS trees. This is a substantive bridge between communication hierarchies and allocations, but it is not a complexity result.

A plausible *unanchored* research question would take a large cooperative platform whose members fall into \(q\) interchangeable roles, with complete communication among members. Let role \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let a coalition’s worth be \(V(x)\), where \(x_t\) is its mass of role \(t\) and \(V\) is a rational polynomial with \(V(0)=0\). In a finite approximation with \(N\mu_t\) members of each role, set \(v_N(S)=V((|S\cap N_t|/N)_t)\). The question is to compute, for each role, the limiting aggregate Shapley allocation as \(N\) grows. The proposed output is
\[ A_t=\mu_t\int_0^1 \partial_t V(s\mu)\,ds. \]
For a sparse polynomial \(V\), this has a direct exact computation. Types encode identical roles and identical effects on coalition worth; mass is the fraction of members in each role. This mirrors only Theorem 3’s complete-graph/Shapley branch, and its expected direction is tractability.

The weak point is substantial: that question is not a continuous version of a computational problem this paper studied, because the paper gives no such problem. It also restricts the characteristic function to a compressed, type-mass form and omits the paper’s network-specific tree structure. So the candidate may be recognizable to the authors as a limit of their Shapley case, but it does not establish a strong ChoCo mirror for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no compliant anchor here. Theorem 3 is the proponent’s closest result, but it identifies average tree allocations with the Shapley value or equal surplus division in special cases. Theorems 1 and 2 are also axiomatic or structural. None states a computational problem or classifies its complexity. The role-mass Shapley question therefore has to be invented alongside the paper; it is not a continuous version of a computational result the paper makes.

The proposed question also lands on established continuous game theory. For a coalition-worth function \(V\) of type masses, the aggregate allocation
\[ A_t=\mu_t\int_0^1 \partial_t V(s\mu)\,ds \]
is the finite-type Aumann–Shapley diagonal value. Aumann and Shapley developed values for nonatomic games, and Calvo and Santos explicitly study continuum finite-type games and their approximation by discrete multichoice games ([Aumann and Shapley](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781400867080_A26058720/preview-9781400867080_A26058720.pdf); [Calvo and Santos, 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165-4896(99)00054-2)). Prior work alone does not invalidate a well-posed mirror. Here, though, the remaining computational question depends on a newly chosen encoding of \(V\); the paper supplies no algorithmic structure for it. The DFS/BFS distinction, rooted trees, and Harsanyi dividends have disappeared from the proposed calculation.

A network-sensitive version could restore those structures, but a distribution over types alone does not encode an arbitrary communication graph or an arbitrary coalition-worth function. Preserving those details may require types that distinguish many individual vertices. A symmetric block-network limit could avoid that and might be worth studying, but it would add a graph limit, a limiting tree model, and a restricted class of games. That is a new computational problem, rather than a mirror anchored in this paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong for treating this paper as a ChoCo computational-mirror candidate: it offers no computational result to mirror, and its cleanest population-continuum extension is already a standard value. The literal claim that no scenario could ever yield worthwhile research is weaker; a carefully chosen network regime could still support a new project.
