The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that loss sharing is naturally expressible in population shares. Imagine a large group of residents facing a common financial loss, with many people but few distinct economic and bargaining types. A type could be \((e_t,a_t)\), where \(e_t\) is initial endowment and \(a_t\) is the greatest contribution that type will accept. The society is an explicit distribution \(\mu\) over those types; \(\mu_t\) is their share of the population.

A possible follow-on problem, inspired by the paper but not anchored in one of its results, is: given rational \(\mu_t\), endowments \(e_t\), acceptance limits \(a_t\), a per-capita loss \(\ell\), and a proposer type \(p\), find contributions \(b_t\) such that
\[ 0\le b_t\le \min(e_t,a_t),\qquad \sum_t\mu_t b_t=\ell, \]
while minimizing \(b_p\), the proposer’s contribution. The output is the type-level contribution vector, or a report that no unanimous allocation exists. For an explicit finite support, this is a linear program and should be tractable. The many-agent, few-type regime is plausible for a broad public loss-sharing arrangement with standardized endowment and acceptance categories. The paper’s authors would likely recognize the underlying loss-division question, though the acceptance limits and proposer objective require a behavioral model the paper does not supply.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no result of this paper to mirror; they explicitly label their LP a follow-on problem. That matters here: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its CEA, Reverse Talmud, and Gaertner–Xu sections describe allocation rules, while its numbered hypotheses and results report experimental behavior. So there is no computational result in the paper for the proposed continuous question to continue.

The LP is a valid loss-allocation problem, but its inputs and objective come from outside the study. The paper does not provide acceptance limits \(a_t\), or ask how to minimize a proposer’s contribution. It studies sequential proposals, rejection, unanimity, and two different ways to resolve deadlock. A type-level contribution vector with acceptance caps drops those features. It also has a direct solution: set the proposer’s contribution to zero if the other types’ weighted capacities can cover \(\ell\); otherwise charge the proposer the shortfall, subject to their capacity. That calculation takes \(O(\tau)\) time. It is a sensible allocation exercise, but it does not recover the paper’s findings or present the kind of computational question whose complexity landscape this programme is meant to chart.

A closer population version would retain bargaining and unanimity. That makes the model’s main difficulty different: proposal histories and responses affect later behavior, while a single dissent can block agreement. A type containing only endowment and an acceptance cap cannot represent those observed dependencies. Adding histories and behavioral states could preserve more of the experiment, but then the researcher would need to supply a new strategic model and decide how unanimity works in an atomless society. The paper supplies no computational problem or result that settles those choices.

This is a strong paper-specific negative case, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A large public loss shared among households in a few standardized endowment classes is plausible, and a richer loss-allocation model could be worth studying. The honest conclusion is narrower: the paper offers no computational anchor, and the proposed LP is a separate, very simple follow-on. A compelling continuous computational question may still be built around loss sharing, but the case for it would need to come from that new model rather than from this paper’s results.
