The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: continuize the population of records on which a preference formula is interpreted, and mirror the paper’s preference-property checking. I would not claim this captures the paper’s whole contribution or makes its central winnow query more tractable.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It gives the complexity of checking properties of a preference relation defined by an intrinsic preference formula \(C\) whose atoms use one domain. If \(C\) has width at most \(m\), span at most \(n\), and satisfiability of a conjunctive formula with \(n\) conjuncts takes \(O(T(n))\), then irreﬂexivity can be checked in \(O(mT(n))\), asymmetry in \(O(m^2T(n))\), transitivity in \(O(m^2n^mT(\max(m,n)))\), negative transitivity in \(O(mn^{2m}T(\max(m,n)))\), and connectivity on a schema of arity \(k\) in \(O(kn^{2m}T(m))\). It also gives \(O(mn^mT(\max(m,n)))\) for testing whether one such preference formula implies another. These are bounds in the formula’s structure; they do not establish a blanket polynomial-time result in the full input size.

The corresponding continuous problem would be Continuous Type-Support Preference-Axiom Testing. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of \(k\)-attribute rational tuple types, a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) with positive mass on each listed type and total mass \(1\), and a DNF rational-order preference formula \(C\). Treat each type as a complete profile: people with that profile are indistinguishable for the preference query, and \(\mu_t\) is their population share. The task is to return which of the five properties in Theorem 3.1—irreﬂexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, negative transitivity, and connectivity—hold for the relation induced by \(C\) on \(T\). In particular, report whether it is a strict partial order. A solution is the exact five-bit answer, with a strict-partial-order answer derived from irreﬂexivity and transitivity.

A plausible regime is a large service or benefits population whose records use a standardized, shared set of profile attributes: many people, but relatively few distinct profiles. The paper’s preference formulas can express the planner’s comparisons between such profiles, and the property checks matter because the paper uses order assumptions to justify winnow and ranking results and algorithms. This is a population interpretation of its tuple types, not an appeal to continuity of the outcome space.

I expect this support-restricted problem to be Class A: enumerate the relevant type pairs or triples and evaluate \(C\), giving a direct polynomial-time check in \(\tau=|T|\) and \(|C|\). Theorem 3.1 is the anchor for the *kind* of computational question—checking preference axioms—and supplies symbolic checks over the formula’s underlying domains. It does not itself prove the bound for the finite-support variant. Natural follow-ups are whether symbolic checking can certify the axioms for types outside the observed support, and whether those certificates can safely enable the paper’s winnow query rewrites on a changing population.

The weak point is substantial: under the paper’s set-based semantics, a type’s mass does not affect these axioms, and winnow itself depends on the support rather than the proportions. This is therefore a credible high-multiplicity interpretation of one named result, but a modest continuization: the paper studies preferences among tuples, not a population whose aggregate mass changes the answer. The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s property-checking result; it does not cover the paper’s preference-query framework as a whole.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The one anchor is Theorem 3.1, and the problem is that its computational object is the preference formula, not a society. It checks whether the relation defined by \(C\) has certain properties across the formula’s domain. The paper explicitly separates those abstract properties from query evaluation on a database instance. For a fixed \(C\), the theorem’s answer is unchanged by every choice of population distribution \(\mu\).

That defeats the proposed Continuous Type-Support Preference-Axiom Testing as a mirror of this result. Restricting the checks to the listed types changes the quantifiers: it tests the relation on one finite set of tuples, rather than testing a property of the formula-defined relation. The resulting check may be useful for a particular query instance, but it is a new instance-validation problem. Its dependence is on which tuple types occur; their masses do no work. Calling those tuples people does not supply the missing population role: in this paper, tuples are the alternatives being compared, and winnow returns the undominated alternatives.

The strongest repair would make the population matter by asking for a mass-weighted measure of axiom violations, or whether the preference relation satisfies an axiom almost everywhere under \(\mu\). That is a coherent computational question, but it replaces Theorem 3.1’s universal logical property with a distributional quality measure. It also no longer certifies the structural conditions the paper uses for winnow and ranking across possible inputs. A plausible service-record scenario could motivate that new measure, but it does not make it a continuous version of the paper’s named result.

So the negative case is that every version faces the same choice: preserve the theorem’s meaning and the population is irrelevant, or make the population relevant and change the theorem’s question. This is a substantial mismatch, not a proof that no useful application could ever be built. The case is weaker if the authors would regard distributional axiom validation as a worthwhile extension of their framework in its own right; the paper gives no basis for ruling that out.
