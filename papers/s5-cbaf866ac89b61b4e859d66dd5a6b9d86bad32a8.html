The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\) rational, finitely supported probability measures \(\nu_i=\sum_{x\in X_i}w_{i,x}\delta_x\) on \(\mathbb{Q}^d\), with binary-encoded coordinates and weights and \(\sum_x w_{i,x}=1\), find a rational hyperplane \(H=\{z:a\cdot z=b\}\), \(a\ne0\), and boundary allocations \(r_{i,x}\in[0,w_{i,x}]\) for \(x\in H\), such that for every \(i\), \(\sum_{a\cdot x>b}w_{i,x}+\sum_{a\cdot x=b}r_{i,x}=1/2\).

The model it lives in

There are \(d\) constituency-specific populations, each a distribution over feature vectors. A type is \((i,x)\) with mass \(w_{i,x}\); the shared decision is the hyperplane \((a,b)\), and boundary-type mass is allocated by \(r_{i,x}\). The feasibility condition is exact half-mass on each side for every constituency, with no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The hard embedding uses equal masses on essentially distinct point types, so it does not establish a hard regime with small support \(\tau\); the mirror’s population-compression payoff remains unshown.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the fair-hyperplane search of Theorem 1.8 in the weighted setting. It leaves the Consensus-Halving and Necklace-Splitting results and the paper’s reduction details unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the rational weighted problem lie in PPA?
  • How does its complexity vary with dimension \(d\), support size \(\tau\), and input length \(L\), especially for fixed \(d\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a mass Ham Sandwich problem. It gives the paper’s Discrete Ham Sandwich result a direct population mirror: take \(d\) large constituencies, each described by a distribution \(\nu_i=\sum_{x\in X_i}w_{i,x}\delta_x\) over rational feature vectors \(x\in\mathbb Q^d\). A person’s type is their constituency \(i\) and feature vector \(x\); the mass at that type is \(w_{i,x}\). The instance has support size \(\tau=\sum_i |X_i|\) and binary input length \(L\).

The task is to find a rational hyperplane \(H=\{z:a\cdot z=b\}\), with \(a\ne0\), that divides every constituency’s mass equally between its two sides. For each atom on \(H\), the solution also gives how much of its mass goes to each side. In particular, if \(r_{i,x}\) is the mass at a boundary atom assigned to the side \(a\cdot z\ge b\), then for every \(i\),
\[ \sum_{a\cdot x>b} w_{i,x}+\sum_{a\cdot x=b}r_{i,x}=\frac12, \qquad 0\le r_{i,x}\le w_{i,x}. \]
There is no cost to minimize: this is a total search problem for a fair dividing rule.

The population regime is plausible when many residents in each constituency share the same measured feature vectors, so the number \(N\) of people can greatly exceed \(\tau\). Those repetitions are summarized by masses. This is natural for a common linear rule meant to divide large constituencies fairly. The hard cases need not be small-support: one can repeat every represented profile many times while keeping the compressed instance and its \(\tau\) types fixed.

The anchor is Theorem 1.8, proved in this paper: Discrete Ham Sandwich is PPA-complete. Its input is \(n\) sets of integer-coordinate points in \(n\) dimensions; its output is a hyperplane that splits every set into equal-size parts, with points on the plane allowed to be assigned to either side or split arbitrarily. The mass problem above contains that discrete problem: for each point set \(S_i\), give each point mass \(1/|S_i|\). A solution hyperplane and its boundary allocations then give a valid discrete solution under the paper’s boundary convention. So I expect hardness to transfer—at least PPA-hardness—even when the population is given as rational masses. This is a Class B mirror: the result’s hardness persists through a discrete subfamily, and the difficult structure can lie in the dimension and geometry rather than in population multiplicity.

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous form. The paper explicitly places Discrete Ham Sandwich alongside the continuous Ham Sandwich theorem. That theorem already supplies an existence result for measures; it is relevant prior art, but it does not make the paper’s discrete model already a continuous population model. The computational question here is to find a fair hyperplane from a finite binary description of the population. I would not extend this particular argument to Necklace-Splitting: continuizing beads would make the goods continuous, rather than the society.

The second anchor is Theorem 1.5, also proved here: for some inverse-polynomial \(\varepsilon\), approximate Consensus-Halving is PPA-complete. Its input is \(n\) agents with normalized, piecewise-constant valuation measures on an interval; its output is a partition made using \(n\) cuts and alternating positive and negative labels, such that every agent’s value discrepancy is at most \(\varepsilon\). PPA membership was already known from the cited work [37].

A corresponding type-level question takes a society \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) valuation types \(t\), where each \(t\) is a normalized rational step-function valuation. Ask for at most \(\tau\) cuts and alternating labels so that
\[ \left|v_t(A^+)-v_t(A^-)\right|\le\varepsilon(\tau) \]
for every positive-mass type, using the inverse-polynomial precision from Theorem 1.5. I expect this version to be PPA-complete on the same hard regime of distinct valuation types: it is the paper’s fair-partition problem with one constraint per distinct type. Here the masses describe the population, but do not weight the fairness condition; that is faithful to a requirement imposed on every agent. This is a weaker mirror than the Ham Sandwich one because the cut budget must be tied to the number of types to remain finite, and the masses themselves do not change feasibility.

The weakest point in my lead is the interpretation of geometric feature vectors and boundary splitting as a social-choice population. That is a specialized regime, though the paper itself treats the Ham Sandwich problem as relevant to social choice and permits boundary points to be split. Also, the hardness transfer shows that continuization need not make the problem tractable; it does not show that multiplicity drives its hardness. The next questions are whether the rational weighted version is in PPA, what happens for fixed \(d\), and how complexity varies with \(d\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is against the Consensus-Halving anchor. With \(\tau\) distinct valuation types, the proposed condition is just ordinary Consensus-Halving on those \(\tau\) valuations: every type must be satisfied, and the number of cuts is tied to the support size. Changing the positive masses while keeping the support fixed changes nothing. Keeping the original population-sized cut budget instead leaves the problem parameterized by the number of people, even when many are duplicates. A mass-weighted average discrepancy would make \(\mu\) matter, but it changes the guarantee: a small type could be left entirely unsatisfied. That is a different fairness problem, not a population version of Theorem 1.5.

The Ham-Sandwich anchor is harder to defeat. Its strongest formulation is coherent: model each constituency as a distribution over measured feature vectors, find a common hyperplane that bisects each distribution, and allocate boundary mass fractionally. That uses population mass directly, matches the paper’s boundary convention, and gives a plausible scenario such as dividing neighborhoods by a common policy threshold.

The strongest objection is that the PPA-hard instances in Theorem 1.8 do not compress people into a small set of types: each listed point becomes its own feature type with mass \(1/|S_i|\). On that hard subfamily, the “continuization” is empirical-measure notation for the point sets the paper already studies. Cloning each point many times leaves the normalized distribution and the hyperplane question unchanged. This makes the anchor a weak demonstration of what a population limit adds.

But that objection does not rule out the better mirror. Arbitrary rational masses give a weighted finite-support problem whose input and feasibility depend on the relative mass at each type. The paper’s theorem does not settle its full complexity, and the known continuous Ham-Sandwich existence result does not answer the computational question. The mirror is also recognizably about a population: points can describe people’s features, and mass represents how many share them. Calling it only computational geometry or only outcome-space continuity would be an unreliable veto.

So the negative case is strong for Consensus-Halving, but weak on the lead anchor. The most I can argue is that mass Ham Sandwich opens a specialized geometric-partition direction rather than a broad population-complexity programme. I cannot honestly rule out that it is a worthwhile mirror of Theorem 1.8.
