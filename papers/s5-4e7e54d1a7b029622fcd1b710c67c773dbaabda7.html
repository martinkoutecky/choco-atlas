What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the per-election winner comparisons behind Questions 1 and 2; it leaves out their dataset-level frequencies, Question 3’s Condorcet analysis, and the ballot-sampling experiment.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no anchor that meets the requested criterion: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Its empirical percentages are reported in tables and prose, not as named computational results.

The closest numbered result is Remark 2.4: in an \(m\)-candidate election, as the truncation level varies from \(1\) to \(m\), there are at most \(m-1\) different RCV winners. The paper presents this as a previously observed fact, pointing to Kilgour, Grégoire, and Foley and to Tomlinson, Ugander, and Kleinberg; it specifically credits the latter with showing the bound is sharp for every \(m\). This is a structural result about outcomes, though, not a complexity result, so it is only a near-anchor.

The strongest honest continuous mirror I can make from it is Continuous Truncation Winner-Range. An instance has \(m\) candidates, a finite support of \(\tau\) ballot types, a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each type with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a fixed tie-breaking rule. A type is a ballot as represented in the paper; under the weak-order model, candidates omitted from a ballot are tied at the bottom. For every cap \(\ell\in\{1,\dots,m\}\), truncate each ballot at \(\ell\), run RCV using the paper’s transfer and exhaustion convention, and output the winner \(w_\ell\). The task is to compute the winner sequence—and thus identify which caps change the winner relative to \(w_m\). Here the input is a distribution over voter types, mass is the fraction of the electorate of each type, and the cap is applied uniformly. There is no optimization variable or objective: the question is what RCV returns.

A plausible regime is a citywide or national election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballot types: for example, hundreds of thousands of voters and a much smaller \(\tau\). Voters sharing a type have the same ballot and therefore the same response to each imposed cap. This is a reasonable high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s profile-by-ballot-type representation already points toward it. The mirror covers the per-election winner comparisons underlying Questions 1 and 2. It does not continuize the paper’s ballot-sampling procedure or its dataset of elections; those would put the distribution at a different level.

For rational masses, Remark 2.4’s winner-count bound carries over: multiplying by a common denominator gives an integer profile with exactly the same RCV outcomes at every cap. The winner sequence also appears tractable directly: simulate at most \(m\) rounds for each of \(m\) caps, aggregating mass across at most \(\tau\) types per round. This gives polynomial-time computation in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), so I would expect this mirror to be Class A. A further question is how much mass must change ballot type to change a winner at a specified cap; that would turn the paper’s winner-sensitivity theme into a robustness problem.

The weak point is substantial: this is a clean continuous-population formulation, but it mostly normalizes and compresses the preference profiles the paper already uses. The paper does not establish a hard problem whose complexity might change in the continuum, and the direct computation is simple. So this supports the plausibility of a population-continuous question about the paper’s subject; it does not provide the required named complexity anchor or a strong case for a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its three questions report empirical frequencies, and the ballot-sampling section estimates how those frequencies might vary under resampling. None gives a complexity claim or an algorithmic problem. Remark 2.4 is the closest anchor, but it is a bound on the number of possible winners, credited to prior work—not a computational result.

The proposed Continuous Truncation Winner-Range is well-defined, but it does not supply the missing anchor. Given masses on \(\tau\) ballot types, run RCV at each of the \(m\) truncation levels. Each run takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). More fundamentally, for rational masses, multiplying by a common denominator gives an integer profile with exactly the same tallies and winners. The paper already represents elections as profiles grouped by ballot type, so the continuous formulation preserves the answer without opening a new complexity question. The winner-count bound transfers immediately too.

The strongest repair would ask for a population-level stability radius, such as
\[ \inf_{\nu:\,W_\ell(\nu)\ne W_m(\nu)} \tfrac12\|\nu-\mu\|_1, \]
the smallest mass shift from an observed electorate that makes the winner depend on the truncation level. That is a coherent computational question about continuous population mass. But it adds an optimization objective and a perturbation model absent from the paper, which holds preferences fixed. The authors explicitly leave voter adaptation for future work, so I cannot honestly rule out that they would recognize this as a worthwhile follow-up. It weakens the categorical claim that *no scenario* could yield a good mirror; it does not rescue the proponent’s winner-range anchor as a computational result of this paper.

My strongest case against is therefore narrower: the paper’s actual contributions are empirical, and its closest per-election mirror is just polynomial-time RCV evaluation on normalized ballot counts. That defeats the anchor offered here. I cannot substantiate the universal claim that every continuous population question suggested by the paper would be unworthwhile.
