The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a finite support \(T\) of partial rankings each with at most two undetermined pairs, rational masses \(\mu_t \geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a target \(c^\star\), is there an allocation \(x_{t,\pi} \geq 0\) of each type's mass among its linear extensions \(\pi \in \mathrm{Ext}(t)\) such that \(\sum_{\pi \in \mathrm{Ext}(t)} x_{t,\pi} = \mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,\pi} x_{t,\pi} B(\pi,c^\star) \geq \sum_{t,\pi} x_{t,\pi} B(\pi,a)\) for every candidate \(a\), where \(B(\pi,a)=m-\mathrm{rank}_{\pi}(a)\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over partial-ranking types. Decision variables \(x_{t,\pi}\) assign each type's mass to admissible complete rankings, with each unit of mass receiving one ranking; the goal is feasibility of the Borda co-winner inequalities for \(c^\star\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Borda case of Theorem 8 with at most two undetermined pairs per type. It leaves the theorem's other scoring-rule cases and the paper's Copeland, maximin, and Bucklin results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For Borda and types with an unbounded number of undetermined pairs, can the completion feasibility problem be solved in polynomial time using a pricing or separation algorithm over linear extensions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s Borda result. Its anchor is Theorem 8, proved in the paper: for a smooth Borda-like scoring rule, if its score-difference vector contains consecutive entries \(\langle 1,1\rangle\), Possible Winner is NP-complete even when each vote has at most two undetermined pairs; if the rule eventually avoids that pattern, Possible Winner is in P under the same bound. Borda is in the hard case: its difference vector consists entirely of ones. The paper proves the hardness result by reduction from Three Dimensional Matching.

Here is a continuous version that keeps the underlying question intact. Fix \(m\) candidates and Borda scoring. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\) of partial rankings, each with at most two undetermined pairs; a rational mass \(\mu_t\geq 0\) for each \(t\in T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); and a target candidate \(c^\star\). A type is one partial ranking: all agents of that type have the same reported information and the same admissible completions. Ask whether the mass of each type can be divided among its complete-ranking extensions so that \(c^\star\) has at least as much total Borda score as every other candidate. A solution is the resulting mass assignment to extensions.

Precisely, let \(\mathrm{Ext}(t)\) be the linear extensions of type \(t\), and let \(x_{t,\pi}\) be the mass assigned to extension \(\pi\). Require \(x_{t,\pi}\geq 0\), \(\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{Ext}(t)}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\), and, for every candidate \(a\),
\[ \sum_{t,\pi}x_{t,\pi}B(\pi,c^\star) \;\geq\; \sum_{t,\pi}x_{t,\pi}B(\pi,a), \]
where \(B(\pi,a)=m-\operatorname{rank}_{\pi}(a)\).

This is Borda Continuous Possible Winner with Two Undetermined Pairs. Each type has at most four extensions, so the question is a linear-program feasibility problem with at most \(4\tau\) variables, where \(\tau=|T|\). It is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. I expect this to be Class A: the paper’s discrete hardness dissolves when identical information types can divide their mass among completions.

The population regime is a large electorate formed from repeated ballot or survey types—for example, a large preference survey where respondents share one of a relatively small number of partial rankings. Then the number of people can greatly exceed the number of distinct partial rankings. Each person still has one complete ranking; the continuous variable records what fraction of a type’s mass receives each admissible completion. This is a deterministic completion of an atomless population, not a lottery over election outcomes. The authors should recognize it as their Possible Winner question: the candidate, Borda rule, incomplete information, and co-winner condition are unchanged.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the Borda hardness boundary in Theorem 8, not the paper’s full classification across voting rules. It also raises a natural next question: when a type has an unbounded number of undetermined pairs, can the resulting LP still be solved efficiently through a pricing algorithm over its linear extensions?

The weak point is that fractional mass can make the continuous answer differ from the answer for a fixed finite electorate. A finite type may have to assign whole voters to completions, while the continuum can split its mass at any rational proportion. That is the intended high-multiplicity relaxation, but it means the paper’s discrete NP-hardness does not itself imply hardness for this mirror. A further caveat is that a paper reduction may have nearly as many distinct partial ballots as voters; the high-multiplicity scenario is a property of the proposed application, not of every hard instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative argument is that this changes what counts as a type. In the paper, a partial vote is an information constraint on an individual ballot, not a realized preference ranking. Treating it as a population type and splitting its mass among completions assumes that voters with the same report may have different underlying rankings. If the uncertainty instead comes from one shared unknown preference, or from a common constraint on how ballots are completed, that split can invent completions the original uncertainty model rules out.

That objection does not defeat the proposed mirror as stated. Possible Winner allows each partial vote to be completed independently, so mass assigned to different completions can represent distinct subpopulations, with each person receiving one complete ranking. At most two undetermined pairs give each type at most four extensions; the resulting feasibility question is a linear program with at most \(4\tau\) variables. For a large survey or electorate with repeated partial-ballot patterns, that is a coherent continuous version of the paper’s Theorem 8. The discrete-to-continuous difference is the intended relaxation, not a defect.

The paper’s hardness reductions also do not establish that hard instances have high multiplicity: their numbers of distinct types may grow with the reduction. But that limits what those reductions say about the mirror’s value; it does not rule out a credible high-multiplicity regime. Adding shared-resolution constraints might produce a different question, but there is no basis here for saying the authors would reject the independent-completion version.

So I cannot honestly make a case that defeats this anchor. The mirror appears well-posed and recognizable. The negative case is weak precisely where it must be strong: neither the mismatch between reports and underlying preferences nor the likely simplicity of the continuous feasibility problem rules out a worthwhile mirror.
