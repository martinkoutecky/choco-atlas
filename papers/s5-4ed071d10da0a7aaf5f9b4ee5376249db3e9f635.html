The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchored positive case: the paper contains no named computational result of the kind this task requires. Its numbered results are structural or axiomatic. For example, Proposition 3, proved here, says that \(AT(v,L)=\alpha\) exactly when, for every cone \(K\), the total payoff to members of \(K\) is
\[ \sum_{i\in K}\alpha_i = v(K)+\frac{|K|}{n}\bigl(v(N)-v(K)-v(K^c)\bigr). \]
That characterizes the games yielding a given payoff vector; it does not establish a complexity bound for computing one. Propositions 1 and 2 give a kernel basis and direct-sum decompositions, while Proposition 4 gives an axiomatic characterization. None asserts tractability, hardness, or another computational classification. The Average Tree formula in the paper is attributed to earlier work and is not a numbered computational result here.

The nearest *possible research direction* would be a large communication network made of repeated agents: types would encode an agent’s role and all payoff-relevant features, mass would be each type’s population share, and the output would be a per-type payoff based on averaging marginal contributions over roots. But the paper defines neither that continuous model nor a computational question about it. Its tree is a graph on finitely many agents, and its TU-game can assign arbitrary worths to coalitions, so grouping agents into types may erase differences the model uses. A convincing version would need additional assumptions about repeated structure and coalition worths; I would not present that construction as the paper’s continuous mirror.

So there is no lead anchor and no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. The paper may inspire a new high-multiplicity study of tree-game allocations, but its results do not supply the required computational foothold.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a continuous mirror starts with a missing computational anchor. The paper’s Propositions 1–4 establish a kernel basis, direct-sum decompositions, an inverse characterization of the Average Tree solution, and an axiomatic characterization. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The formula for \(AT(v,L)\) is attributed to earlier work; the paper does not make its computation a complexity result. So there is no named computational claim here for a continuous-population question to mirror.

The proposed direction—many agents in repeated tree roles, with a per-type payoff obtained by averaging marginal contributions over roots—does not supply that missing anchor. The paper’s input is a fixed tree on individually identified agents and an arbitrary worth for every coalition. Those worths are not properties of individual agents that can simply be grouped into types. A complete type description would have to preserve which coalitions each agent belongs to and which edge cuts define its cones; in general, that leaves the agents distinct. A workable mass model would need new restrictions, such as repeated tree structure and worths determined by type counts and boundary cuts.

The strongest version of that proposal is coherent: take a sequence of symmetric trees with repeated roles, specify a structured class of coalition worth functions, and define a type-level allocation by averaging rooted marginal contributions. One could then ask computational questions about computing allocations or solving the inverse problem. But that would introduce the computational problem, input representation, and structured domain that this paper does not study. Its finite-dimensional results concern games on a fixed tree, with a space of dimension \(2^n-1\); they do not establish a high-multiplicity computational problem whose continuous relaxation could be charted.

This supports rejecting a continuous mirror of this paper under ChoCo’s computational remit. It does not prove that no worthwhile continuous theory of tree-game allocations can be built. Repeated-tree scenarios make that broader claim hard to defend. The negative case is strongest when limited to the paper itself: a plausible extension would be new research, not a continuization of a computational result established here.
