The continuous mirror question

For \(m\) candidates and a fixed scoring rule \(f\) with \(f(m)=(\alpha_3^{m-3},\alpha_4,\alpha_5,\alpha_6)\), where \(\alpha_3>\alpha_4\ge\alpha_5\ge\alpha_6\), take a society with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) on each ranking type \(t\) in a support \(S\), with \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\). Given a target \(p\) and a mass budget \(\kappa\), decide whether there are transfers \(x_{t,r}\ge0\), from \(t\in S\) to any complete ranking \(r\), such that \(\sum_r x_{t,r}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,r}x_{t,r}\le\kappa\), and \(p\) is a winner under the resulting mass distribution \(\mu'_r=\mu_r-\sum_q x_{r,q}+\sum_t x_{t,r}\), taking \(\mu_r=0\) outside \(S\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity election given by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, with unit cost per transferred mass. The decision variables \(x_{t,r}\) move mass from original ranking types to replacement rankings; the objective may minimize \(\sum_{t,r}x_{t,r}\), or the decision version may bound it by \(\kappa\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a continuous version of the paper’s deletion and bribery problems for its three-tail scoring rules. The paper’s Theorem 4.4 states that if \(f(m)=(\underbrace{\alpha_3,\ldots,\alpha_3}_{m-3},\alpha_4,\alpha_5,\alpha_6)\), with the coefficients in nonincreasing order and \(\alpha_3>\alpha_4>\alpha_6\), then \(f\)-CCDV is NP-complete. Theorem 5.5, also proved in the paper, states that bribery under the same scoring-rule condition is NP-complete. The first proof builds on a cited CCAV hardness result and constructs a CCDV reduction; the second handles the additional freedom to choose replacement ballots. Both are hardness results proved here, and both reduce from three-dimensional matching. A concrete member of the family is \(f(m)=(0,\ldots,0,-1,-2,-3)\). [Theorem 4.4 and Theorem 5.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.05264)

For Mass-CCDV, take \(m\) candidates, a target \(p\), and a support \(S\) of complete rankings. The society is \(\mu_t>0\) for \(t\in S\), with \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\) and binary-rational masses. Given a deletion budget \(\kappa\), choose \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass deleted from each ranking type. The question is whether \(\sum_t z_t\le\kappa\) and, among the survivors, \(p\) is a winner. Equivalently, for every candidate \(c\),
\[ \sum_{t\in S}(\mu_t-z_t)\bigl(s_f(t,p)-s_f(t,c)\bigr)\ge 0. \]
The remaining mass can be normalized to one; that common scaling does not change the winner set. A solution is the deletion vector \(z\). The optimization version minimizes \(\sum_t z_t\).

For Mass-Bribery, use the same input and budget, but choose transfers \(x_{t,r}\ge0\) from each initial ranking \(t\in S\) to any complete replacement ranking \(r\). Require \(\sum_r x_{t,r}\le\mu_t\) for each source type and \(\sum_{t,r}x_{t,r}\le\kappa\). The resulting society has mass \(\mu_r-\sum_q x_{r,q}+\sum_t x_{t,r}\) on ranking \(r\), taking \(\mu_r=0\) outside \(S\). Ask whether \(p\) wins. A solution is the transfer plan; equivalently, minimize its total transferred mass. Every voter still casts a complete ranking. Only the population and the action budget become continuous.

These are plausible mirrors in an anonymous, high-multiplicity election: for example, a large association choosing among candidates from a shortlist, with hundreds of thousands of members but only dozens or hundreds of repeated full-ballot types. In both paper problems, voters with the same ranking have the same unit status and action cost; there are no named-voter prices or budgets to preserve. Mass is the share of the membership, and \(\kappa\) is the share that may abstain or change its ballot. For a finite election with \(N\) voters and \(n_t\) of type \(t\), the direct dictionary is \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(\kappa=k/N\). Discrete actions embed as mass actions whose amounts are multiples of \(1/N\).

I expect Class A for both mirrors. Mass-CCDV is a linear program with \(\tau=|S|\) deletion variables and one winner constraint per candidate, so it is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). Mass-Bribery gives an LP with variables for source types and possible replacement rankings, so it has exponentially many columns. Its pricing problem is manageable: for fixed dual weights on candidates, the best replacement ranking assigns the scoring positions to candidates in sorted order. That is polynomial-time, giving the usual route to an ellipsoid or column-generation algorithm. This is exactly the kind of optimization question the continuous model makes available.

The paper’s hardness reductions encode the choice of an integral set of voters that realizes a 3DM cover. Fractional mass removes that indivisibility. In the regular 3DM instances used here, the natural exact-cover constraints already admit the fractional assignment \(1/3\) to every tuple, whether or not an integral cover exists. The paper’s specific vote gadgets would need to be analyzed under the mass model, but their discrete NP-hardness does not by itself transfer.

My lead is Mass-CCDV from Theorem 4.4: its action is the cleanest mass relaxation, and its LP makes the predicted change in complexity explicit. Theorem 5.5 gives a useful second anchor because it tests whether the extra freedom of bribery remains manageable through pricing. This scope covers those two hardness results; it does not claim a continuous classification of every result in the paper.

The weakest point is that fractional control may be too permissive for a finite election in which one ballot can be pivotal. A referee could argue that this changes the operational question. The reply is that each person remains indivisible in the model: \(z_t\) selects a measurable share of an interchangeable voter type, which is the natural action in a nonatomic, high-multiplicity regime. The regime matters: the mirror is most convincing when \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\), and the paper’s hardness requires the number of candidates to grow.

The next question is how closely the continuous optimum tracks the discrete optimum: when can a mass solution be rounded to whole-voter deletions or bribes, and what bounds the additive budget or score loss in terms of \(m,\tau,L\), and the score margins? That would turn the direct embedding into the formal two-way bridge the programme calls for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can make a case against the *value* of these mirrors, but I cannot honestly make the universal claim that neither is worth asking. Both anchors are well-posed continuous population problems in a credible high-multiplicity setting.

For Theorem 4.4’s CCDV result, the natural mass version has one variable \(z_t\) per supported ballot type. Its winning constraints are
\[ \sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\bigl(s_f(t,p)-s_f(t,c)\bigr)\ge 0 \]
for each candidate \(c\), with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and a linear deletion budget. That is a polynomial-size LP. A large association with many members and a relatively small set of repeated ballots is a plausible setting for deleting a share of each ballot type. Requiring every deletion to be an integer number of people would restore the discrete problem, not defeat this mirror. So the strongest objection is that the continuous answer is almost immediate for *every* scoring rule; it does not illuminate the paper’s dichotomy or require the continuous optimization machinery ChoCo hopes to bring into the field. That is a case for low research payoff, not a fundamental modeling failure.

Theorem 5.5’s bribery mirror has the same limitation. The transfer variables describe how much mass of each original ballot type is changed into each replacement ranking. With uniform unit bribe costs, the winner constraints and budget are linear. The exponentially many replacement rankings do not seem to create a hard pricing problem: for fixed dual weights on candidates, the best ranking assigns scoring positions according to sorted candidate weights. This is a coherent mass-bribery question in the same association setting, but the proposed Class A result appears to follow from routine LP machinery. A more ambitious version could study rounding back to whole voters or approximation guarantees for discrete elections; that could be interesting, but it would be an additional bridge result, not a reason the mass mirror itself fails.

The case for “not worth ChoCo’s time” is therefore that both anchors collapse to the same generic observation: linear scores plus divisible actions give an LP. The paper’s detailed reductions and scoring-rule distinctions may contribute little to that analysis. The proponent’s \(1/3\)-per-tuple observation is not itself enough to show that the paper’s gadgets become easy fractionally, but the generic LP argument makes that particular observation unnecessary.

That negative case is weak against the programme’s stated standard. A simple tractable mirror is still a computational result, and the paper supplies two named hardness results whose high-multiplicity counterparts are sensible questions. The gap between the fractional optimum and a discrete action may also be worth studying, especially near ties, where rounding can affect who wins. I would not claim that CCDV or bribery is already continuously solved, or treat existing high-multiplicity work as a collision; neither supports that claim. On the supplied evidence, I cannot defeat either anchor as a well-posed mirror. My honest case against is only that the first results may be too routine to justify priority on their own.
