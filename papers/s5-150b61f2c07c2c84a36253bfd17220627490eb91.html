The continuous mirror question

Given \(B,\delta\), a finite family \(\mathcal K\) of \(\delta\)-approximate candidate sets, rational masses \(\mu_{K,\theta}\) on types \((K,\theta)\) with \(\theta\in K\), and a target \(\alpha\), compute whether the \(N\to\infty\) limit of \(M_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\delta)}\)—with each bidder's allocation probability and expected payment multiplied by \(N\)—has welfare ratio at least \(\alpha\) for every undominated bid assignment; characterize the limiting allocation and payment intensities and undominated bids as part of the computation.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass society has rational masses \(\mu_{K,\theta}\) of bidders who know \(K\) and whose realized value is \(\theta\). Bids lie in \(\{0,\ldots,B\}\); aggregate bid shares determine allocation intensities \(q_b(\rho)\) and payment intensities \(p_b(\rho)\), with \(\sum_b \rho_b q_b(\rho)\leq 1\). Dominance comparisons use intensity payoff \(\theta q_b(\rho)-p_b(\rho)\), and the welfare benchmark is \(\operatorname{ess\,sup}\theta\).

The objection that survived

An atomless auction gives each bidder zero ordinary expected utility, so the proposed \(N\)-scaled payoff intensity is needed; whether its limiting undominated strategies faithfully extend the paper's solution concept remains unproved.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

This covers Theorem 4's explicit probabilistic mechanism and its welfare guarantee in a population limit. It leaves Theorems 1–3 and 5, and the paper's structural lemmas, outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the sequence of \(N\)-bidder allocation and payment rules converge for every rational mass profile, including profiles with tied bids?
  • Can the limiting undominated bids and worst-case welfare ratio be computed in time polynomial in \(B,\tau\), and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. This paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in \(P\), or parameterized tractable. Its closest computational anchor is the explicitly constructed mechanism in Theorem 4, whose allocation and payment computations are discussed in §11.6. I would use that as an algorithmic anchor, with Theorem 5 as a companion welfare bound—not describe either as a complexity classification.

A credible regime is a high-volume auction for one indivisible licence or franchise, with many bidders but relatively few recurring valuation-knowledge profiles. For example, many firms may use the same standard business assumptions and therefore have the same candidate-valuation set \(K_t\), even though the actual value within that set is uncertain. Let \(N_t/N\) approach a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and support size \(\tau\ll N\). Each type \(t\) has a complete strategic description including \(K_t\), where \(K_t\) is \(\delta\)-approximate; the realized values \(\theta(a)\in K_t\) remain adversarial, as in the paper. The designer knows \(B\) and \(\delta\), but not which bidders have which \(K_t\).

Here is the continuum model I would hand to a prover. Society is a unit-mass space \(A\), with type masses \(\mu_t\). A mechanism has a finite message set \(S\). If \(\rho_r\) is the population share sending message \(r\), the anonymous mechanism specifies an allocation density \(q_r(\rho)\) and an expected-payment density \(p_r(\rho)\), with \(\sum_r\rho_rq_r(\rho)\leq1\). In an \(N\)-bidder approximation, a person reporting \(r\) wins with probability \(q_r(\rho)/N\) and has expected payment \(p_r(\rho)/N\). Multiplying utility by \(N\) preserves every finite game’s dominance comparisons and gives the limiting payoff \(\theta q_r(\rho)-p_r(\rho)\). The good remains indivisible: \(q\) describes the lottery over who receives it, not fractional bundles.

A continuum strategy profile assigns messages or mixed messages to agents of each \(K_t\)-type. A strategy is undominated under the paper’s criterion: no mixed message gives at least as much utility for every \(\theta\in K_t\) and every opponents’ report distribution, with a strict improvement somewhere. Welfare is \(\int_A\theta(a)q_{r(a)}(\rho)\,da\), and the high-multiplicity benchmark is \(\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{a\in A}\theta(a)\). The computational task is to produce a finite description of the allocation and payment rules, with complexity measured in \(B,\tau\), and the input bit length. The rules must work for every rational type-mass profile of support at most \(\tau\), while remaining independent of that profile, just as the paper’s designer does not know the \(K_i\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(n\), \(\delta\in(0,1)\), and \(B\), there is a mechanism \(M_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\delta)}\) such that for every \(\delta\)-approximate candidate-set profile \(K\), every true-value profile \(\theta\in K\), and every undominated strategy profile \(v\), expected welfare is at least
\[ \frac{(1-\delta)^2+4\delta/n}{(1+\delta)^2}\,\mathrm{MSW}(\theta). \]
The paper constructs the mechanism and says its allocation and expected-price functions are efficiently computable in §11.6. This is the strongest positive anchor: the paper provides a concrete mechanism and a worst-case welfare guarantee, not just an analogy to population models.

The corresponding continuous question, Continuum Knightian Guarantee Synthesis, is: given \(B,\delta,\tau\), an allowed finite universe of \(\delta\)-approximate sets, and a target \(\alpha\), find a finitely described anonymous mechanism—or prove none exists—that guarantees welfare ratio at least \(\alpha\) for every rational-mass society with support at most \(\tau\), every admissible realized-value function, and every undominated strategy profile. The lead target is \(\alpha=((1-\delta)/(1+\delta))^2\), the limit of Theorem 4’s guarantee as \(n\) grows. The expected outcome is Class A: the paper’s explicit single-good allocation rule is evidence that the type-mass version may admit a compact, efficiently computable rule. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 4; one must still establish that the finite mechanisms have a suitable limiting rule and that undominated strategies pass to the limit.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. For every \(n\geq1\), \(\delta\in(0,1)\), \(B\geq5/\delta\), and every deterministic or probabilistic mechanism, it gives a context and an undominated strategy profile whose expected welfare is at most
\[ \left(\frac{(1-\delta)^2+4\delta/n}{(1+\delta)^2}+\frac{4}{B}\right)\mathrm{MSW}(\theta). \]
This is an upper bound over all mechanisms, so it motivates the separate question Continuum Knightian Optimality: for given \(B,\delta,\tau,\varepsilon\), can any such continuum rule beat \(((1-\delta)/(1+\delta))^2+4/B+\varepsilon\) on every admissible society and undominated profile, or can one prove that some society defeats every rule? The expected classification is again Class A for this one-good setting: an upper-bound certificate completing the constructive guarantee seems plausible, though the infinite family of deviation constraints could instead make the problem continuum-specifically hard. The paper supplies no computational hardness result that would support a hardness-transfer prediction.

Theorem 5’s witness is also the main caution. Its bad context singles out one bidder against the others, so that bidder’s type has mass \(1/n\). In a fixed atomless limit that type vanishes; the theorem’s upper bound therefore does not automatically transfer. Allowing arbitrarily small positive rational masses may recover the obstruction as an infimum, but that needs a convergence argument for both the allocation rule and the undominated-strategy sets. It is a real question, not an objection I would wave away. The proposed scope is only welfare-optimal single-good auction design under Knightian values and undominated strategies; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s other mechanism-design results or to settle general auction complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main objection is that neither anchor is a computational result in the sense ChoCo is trying to mirror. Theorem 4 constructs a mechanism with a welfare guarantee; Theorem 5 bounds the guarantee of every mechanism. Neither studies the complexity of a problem, gives an algorithm for finding an optimal mechanism, or classifies a search problem. Section 11.6 checks that the paper’s explicit mechanism can be evaluated efficiently. The proposed “Continuum Knightian Guarantee Synthesis” and “Continuum Knightian Optimality” add a new task: search over a class of mechanisms and measure the cost of that search. That could be a worthwhile project, but it is not a computational question formulated or answered by these theorems.

The strongest setting for a mirror is a high-volume auction in which many bidders share a small number of candidate-valuation sets. That is plausible, and the paper’s social-welfare objective does not track the identity of the winner. But the proposed continuum auction needs a special payoff interpretation. With one indivisible good and an anonymous atomless population, each individual has probability zero of winning. Their ordinary expected utility differences are zero, so the paper’s strict-improvement condition for undominated strategies becomes vacuous. The proponent rescales probabilities and payments by \(N\), then takes a limit. That preserves dominance comparisons at each finite \(N\), but it defines a payoff intensity for the limiting game, not the payoff of an individual in an ordinary continuum auction. It may be a coherent model; the paper supplies no computational problem or limit result for it.

There is also a type-aggregation gap. The candidate set \(K_i\) determines which strategies are undominated, while the realized value \(\theta_i\) determines welfare. Bidders with the same \(K_i\) can have different realized values and can use different undominated strategies. A society described only by masses of \(K\)-types therefore does not determine the welfare-relevant population. A stronger mirror could include realized values and allow arbitrary measurable assignments of undominated strategies within each \(K\)-type. That is possible to define, but it makes the model a new worst-case continuum game; it does not supply the missing computational target.

Theorem 5 has the same problem as an anchor. Its finite bound is a welfare impossibility statement, not a complexity result. The proof’s bad context singles out one bidder, and the proponent correctly notes that this does not itself establish a continuum upper bound. A careful limit analysis could be a real mathematical question. Yet the proposed task—optimize over all finitely described continuum mechanisms—still needs a specified mechanism representation and a complexity measure before it becomes a ChoCo algorithmic problem. The theorem gives no such formulation.

So the negative case is strongest against these *computational* anchors: the paper’s central contributions are strategic welfare theorems, and turning them into a mechanism-synthesis problem risks inventing the ChoCo question after the fact. The universal claim is weaker. Large auctions with recurring bidder profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a rescaled continuum auction could be an object its authors recognize. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous research question in every scenario; I can argue that this paper does not provide a strong computational anchor for one.
