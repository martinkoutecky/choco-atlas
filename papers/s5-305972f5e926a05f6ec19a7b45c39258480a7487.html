What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered by a mirror. Proposition 1 gives an impossibility about mechanism properties, Proposition 2 characterizes axiomatic feasibility, and Corollaries 1–2 give existence results; none states a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named results are structural mechanism-design results, not claims that an underlying computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest anchor is Proposition 2, proved here. It says three conditions are equivalent: some multi-stage schedule makes the system non-wasteful when each stage’s mechanism is individually rational and non-wasteful; some such schedule makes the system straightforward when each stage’s mechanism is also strategy-proof and minimally non-bossy; and the preference domain is contained in a tiered domain. Proposition 1 gives the full-domain impossibility, while Corollaries 1 and 2 give schedule and mechanism examples. None formulates or classifies a computational problem.

A population-mass version of Proposition 2 could be imagined for a large admissions cohort: student types would need to include preferences and the relevant priority information, masses would represent cohort shares, capacities would scale with population, and the designer would choose a schedule and assignment rules to seek non-wastefulness and straightforwardness. But that would be an axiomatic feasibility mirror, not a computational result of this paper. It would also need a careful account of strict individual priorities and individual truthfulness in a continuum model. So I can’t honestly give the requested per-anchor computational questions or a complexity prediction for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not count Proposition 2 as a surviving computational anchor. It characterizes when a multi-stage system can satisfy non-wastefulness and straightforwardness; Proposition 1 and Corollaries 1–2 are also impossibility or existence results about mechanism properties. None asks for, or proves, a complexity bound or algorithm for a computational problem. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, that is decisive: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror.

The strongest charitable mirror would model a large admissions cohort by masses of complete agent types, including preferences and whatever priority information the mechanism uses, while scaling school capacities per capita. One could then ask whether a schedule and suitable stage mechanisms can produce a mass-level non-wasteful, incentive-compatible assignment. Scaling capacities and allowing type-level averages are legitimate parts of such a mirror. But the paper’s result remains an axiomatic existence characterization. In its setting, the tier condition tells the designer when a suitable schedule exists; detecting that condition and ordering tiers is not a complexity landscape for bribery, control, or robustness.

Making this a computational question requires extra structure. With the paper’s strict individual priorities, agents with different priority positions are different complete types; grouping by preference alone discards information used by the mechanisms. A continuum of priority scores or priority classes could be sensible, but then the model must specify how those priorities, individual deviations, and assignments work in the limit. In particular, a unilateral report by an agent of zero mass cannot change aggregate quantities, so the paper’s individual truthfulness condition does not carry over just by replacing counts with masses. A well-defined limit model may be possible, but its incentive constraints would need their own formulation.

There may be a worthwhile *new* computational project in designing mechanisms for such a model. That possibility keeps the universal claim from being airtight: I cannot rule out every useful reformulation. But it would not rescue an anchor in this paper. The honest negative case is that the paper supplies a structural mechanism-design theorem, while turning it into ChoCo’s computational question requires adding a different problem and new strategic primitives.
