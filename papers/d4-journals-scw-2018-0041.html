The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) alternatives and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over their complete strict rankings, with rational masses encoded in binary, decide whether there exists a center ranking \(r_0\) such that for every pair \(r',r\), \(\mu(r')>\mu(r)\) implies \(d(r',r_0)\le d(r,r_0)\); return such a center or report that none exists. The comparison ranges over all rankings, including those outside the support with mass zero.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types, with support size \(\tau\) and binary rational masses of total encoding length \(L\). The decision variable is a center ranking \(r_0\); the objective is to find one satisfying the Flexible Consensus inequalities or determine that none exists. The natural target is running time polynomial in \(K,\tau,L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the recognition results in Theorems 4 and 1. It leaves aside the empirical findings, the impartial-culture probability results, and the stability guarantees themselves.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can Flexible Consensus recognition be implemented in time polynomial in \(K,\tau,L\), including the constraints from rankings omitted from the support?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Flexible Consensus Detection for a ranking distribution. The paper contains no named hardness result, so its recognition algorithms are the right anchors.

Let \(A\) be \(K\ge 3\) alternatives, and let each type \(r\) be a complete strict ranking of \(A\). An instance is a distribution \(\mu\) with finite support \(S\) of size \(\tau\): each supported ranking has a positive rational mass, the masses sum to \(1\), and every ranking outside \(S\) has mass \(0\). The input lists the supported rankings and their masses in binary; let \(L\) be their total bit length. The distance \(d(r,r')\) is the number of alternative pairs they rank differently.

Lead: Flexible Consensus Detection. Given \(\mu\), decide whether there is a center ranking \(r_0\) such that, for every pair of rankings \(r',r\),
\[ \mu(r')>\mu(r)\ \Longrightarrow\ d(r',r_0)\le d(r,r_0). \]
A solution is a center \(r_0\) satisfying the condition, or “no.” This mirrors Theorem 4, proved in this paper: Algorithm 3 decides whether a finite profile has Flexible Consensus and returns a witness, in time \(O(n'^2K\sqrt{\log K}+n'^2\log n')\), where \(n'\) is the number of distinct rankings in the profile. The continuous version should be tractable in \(\operatorname{poly}(K,\tau,L)\): the algorithm’s comparisons become comparisons of rational masses, and the paper’s Mahonian-number count handles rankings outside the listed support without enumerating all \(K!\) rankings.

This is a credible population-scale regime: for a fixed agenda of five alternatives, perhaps hundreds of thousands of participants submit complete rankings, but there are at most \(5!=120\) types. Agents with the same ranking are indistinguishable for this paper’s property, and their mass is their share of the population. Flexible Consensus then says higher-mass rankings lie no farther from a common center than lower-mass rankings. The paper’s stability motivation carries over: the center’s top alternative is a weak Condorcet winner, and it scores at least as highly as every other alternative under every scoring rule.

A second, narrower anchor is Level-1 Consensus Detection. Ask whether there is a ranking \(r_0\) such that for all rankings \(r,r'\),
\[ d(r,r_0)\le d(r',r_0)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mu(r)\ge\mu(r'), \]
and there is at least one pair with \(d(r,r_0)<d(r',r_0)\) and \(\mu(r)>\mu(r')\). Return a witnessing center or “no.” Theorem 1, also proved here, gives Algorithm 1 for this recognition problem in \(O(n'^2K\sqrt{\log K}+n'^2\log n')\) time and says the runtime is polynomial in profile size. The paper defines \(n'\) as the number of distinct rankings, bounded by \(\min(K!,n)\). The continuous analogue should likewise be in \(\operatorname{poly}(K,\tau,L)\). This stricter mirror requires rankings on each distance shell to respect the frequency ordering strictly, including the constraints imposed by absent, zero-mass rankings.

Theorem 4 is my lead: Flexible Consensus is the paper’s proposed weakening and its central new property. Both questions preserve the full ranking types, the inversion metric, and the consensus predicates; they do not simplify the task to plurality or top-choice data. The paper itself uses frequencies \(\mu_\pi(r)\), so replacing voter counts by population shares is a natural model change. The mirror covers these two recognition results, not the paper’s empirical findings or its asymptotic probability propositions.

The main weakness is that these are algorithmic anchors, not cases where continuity might dissolve discrete hardness. Indeed, any rational \(\mu\) can be multiplied by a common denominator to give integer voter counts with exactly the same consensus answer. The difference is that this discrete profile may be exponentially larger than its binary-encoded mass description. So the mirror offers a meaningful high-multiplicity, succinct-input question, but likely no new fractional phenomenon in the yes/no property itself.

Natural follow-up questions are how much total population mass must move to create or destroy either form of consensus, and whether the recognition results extend to other distances or approximate consensus.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does have named algorithmic results, so “there is no computational result to mirror” is not a sound objection: Theorems 1 and 4 give recognition algorithms. The stronger objection is that, for these two results, changing to a continuous society changes the input encoding but not the mathematical problem.

For any finite profile, let \(n_r\) be the number of voters with ranking \(r\). Its normalized mass is \(\mu(r)=n_r/n\). Both consensus predicates are unchanged by this normalization: they depend on which masses are equal or larger, and, for Level-1 Consensus, whether a strict inequality exists. Conversely, every binary-rational distribution in the proposed mirror can be multiplied by a common denominator to produce a finite profile with exactly the same answer. Even allowing real masses would not add new yes/no cases: these predicates use only comparisons and equalities, so a rational vector can reproduce any such pattern.

This is especially direct for the two anchors. Flexible Consensus checks whether higher-mass rankings are no farther from a candidate center; Level-1 Consensus checks the corresponding shell ordering, including its strictness and the zero-mass rankings outside the support. The paper’s algorithms already aggregate voters into ranking frequencies, then do their work over the distinct rankings and the inversion-distance shells. Supplying binary-rational masses instead of integer counts requires exact comparisons with cost polynomial in their bit length. That is a straightforward adaptation of the stated algorithms, not a new computational landscape. The proposed large survey over a fixed agenda is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, but these theorems already reduce such a survey to its ranking histogram.

A potentially stronger proposal is to ask how much population mass must move to create or destroy consensus, or to overturn the winner guarantee. That may be worth studying, but it adds an intervention objective and transfer costs that the paper’s recognition theorems do not provide. It is a new robustness problem, not a more faithful continuous version of Theorem 1 or Theorem 4. The impartial-culture asymptotics do not supply a separate deterministic continuum anchor either: replacing sampled profiles by the uniform limiting distribution removes the frequency fluctuations those results analyze.

So the case against the stated mirrors is that they restate scale-invariant properties of a ranking histogram, and the paper’s algorithms already operate on that histogram. The weakness is real: this does not rule out a worthwhile ChoCo project built around a new mass-transfer robustness question. I would not use that possible extension to defend these particular recognition mirrors, but I cannot honestly claim that no continuous-population question prompted by the paper could be worthwhile.
