The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant-type and position-type sets \(I,J\), rational applicant masses \(\mu_i\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), rational position masses \(\nu_j\), mutual acceptability \(E\subseteq I\times J\), and strict preference orders over types, maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\) over rational allocations \(x_{ij}\ge 0\). Require \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\mu_i\), \(\ell_j\nu_j\le\sum_ix_{ij}\le u_j\nu_j\), and no positive-mass envy: there are no \(i,k,j,r\), with \(r\in J\cup\{\bot\}\), such that \(x_{ir}>0\), \(x_{kj}>0\), \(j\succ_i r\), and \(i\succ_j k\). For the ONE-ONE-LQ case, \((\ell_j,u_j)\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}\); report the maximum or that no feasible envy-free allocation exists.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity lower-quota market in which each type represents interchangeable agents or positions, with rational mass and rankings between types. The decision variables \(x_{ij}\) are aggregate assignments; individuals still receive integral assignments, while cohort mass can split across assignments. Position rankings form priority tiers over applicant types, which is the extension from the paper's strict individual rankings.

The objection that survived

The type model removes within-cohort strict tie-breaks, which can erase envy and may require splitting cohorts by priority to preserve the paper's comparisons; the proponent's category-based regime makes this a bounded extension risk, but does not establish how common that regime is.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers MAXEFM and MAXRSM, including the hardness questions anchored by Theorem 1 and Corollary 1; it leaves the kernelization and FPT results in Theorems 2 and 3 without continuous counterparts.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the Independent Set reduction behind Theorem 1 retain its obstruction when each type's mass can split fractionally?
  • Does type-level envy create a tractable continuous optimization formulation, or does its disjunctive structure make the mass problem hard?
  • How much does preserving individual priority information increase the support size in plausible placement markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror for the paper’s two maximum-matching problems. The strongest is MAXEFM. The mirror makes the *population* continuous: people and positions occur in preference cohorts with rational masses. Matching remains integral for each person; only the aggregate mass assigned between cohorts is fractional.

A plausible regime is a large centralized placement market, such as medical training positions filled by successive large cohorts. Many applicants share the same eligibility and ordered program list; many positions share the same priority order over applicant cohorts and the same quota status. The number of applicants and positions can be in the tens of thousands while the number of distinct applicant and position types is much smaller. A type includes the complete preference, acceptability, and quota information relevant to the problem. This is a high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every matching market is one.

Lead mirror: maximum mass envy-free matching. An instance has finite applicant-type and position-type sets \(I,J\), a mutually acceptable relation \(E\subseteq I\times J\), applicant masses \(\mu_i\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and position masses \(\nu_j\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\). A position type specifies a strict ranking of applicant types and a quota pair \((\ell_j,u_j)\). In the paper’s ONE-ONE-LQ setting, use \((\ell_j,u_j)\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}\). The allocation is a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), with \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(E\), satisfying
\[ \sum_j x_{ij}\le \mu_i,\qquad \ell_j\nu_j\le \sum_i x_{ij}\le u_j\nu_j. \]
Unmatched applicant mass is \(x_{i\bot}=\mu_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\).

The question is to maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\) among feasible allocations with no positive-mass envy. Precisely, there must not be types \(i,k\), a position type \(j\), and an option \(r\in J\cup\{\bot\}\) such that \(x_{ir}>0\), \(x_{kj}>0\), \(j\succ_i r\), and \(i\succ_j k\). This captures the paper’s envy pair: positive mass of type \(i\) would prefer a position occupied by type \(k\), and that position prefers \(i\) to \(k\). A solution is an optimal rational allocation matrix. This is a mass matching, not a fractional outcome for any individual: it describes how a nonatomic cohort is divided among integral assignments.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: MAXEFM is W[1]-hard parameterized by \(q\), \(n_d\), or \(d\), and para-NP-hard parameterized by \(t\), even in ONE-ONE-LQ instances. Here \(q\) is the number of lower-quota resources, \(d\) is stable-matching deficiency, \(n_d\) is the number of deficient resources, and \(t\) measures shared non-lower-quota resources in pairs of agents’ preference lists. The paper proves the result by reduction from Independent Set. This anchor is a good fit because the continuous question still asks for the largest allocation satisfying the same quota and preference-based envy conditions. The flow constraints are linear, but the no-envy condition introduces complementarity: for each forbidden envy pattern, at least one of the two relevant masses must be zero. So the mirror is not obtained by simply taking a maximum-flow LP.

Second mirror: maximum mass relaxed-stable matching. Use the same type-based instance and feasibility constraints. Let \(v_j=\nu_j-\sum_i x_{ij}\) be vacant position mass. A positive-mass block exists when applicant mass on option \(r\in J\cup\{\bot\}\) prefers \(j\) to \(r\), and \(j\) has positive vacant mass or positive mass assigned to a type \(k\) that it ranks below \(i\). The allocation is relaxed stable if no such block involves applicant mass currently unmatched or assigned to a non-lower-quota position type. The question is to maximize total matched mass among feasible relaxed-stable allocations.

Its anchor is Corollary 1, proved here from hardness work cited as [13]: MAXRSM is para-NP-hard parameterized by \(d\) or \(n_d\), even in ONE-ONE-LQ. The reduction gives hardness with \(d=n_d=0\), so the stable matching can be feasible and the difficulty can remain even without stable-matching deficiency. This is a distinct question from MAXEFM: relaxed stability permits some blocking by agents already assigned to lower-quota resources, rather than forbidding every envy pair.

For both mirrors, my tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness (Class C), not an assertion that the paper’s reductions transfer. Splitting cohorts into mass can dissolve the discrete choices in an Independent Set reduction, so the finite hardness result alone does not settle the continuous case. But the continuous problems retain quota feasibility and disjunctive preference constraints; those can preserve combinatorial difficulty. The first things to investigate are whether the reduction survives mass splitting, whether fractional assignments yield a tractable relaxation, and whether an integrality gap can be bounded. Natural parameter questions include the number of lower-quota resource types, preference-list overlap between agent types, and deficiency measured as mass. For deficiency parameters, one would first need a suitable stable-mass-matching theorem to establish that the measure is well-defined and invariant.

The mirror covers the paper’s MAXEFM and MAXRSM optimization problems and the hardness claims above. It does not mirror the paper’s kernelization and FPT results, which would need separate continuous questions.

The main weakness is that the paper ranks individual agents and resources strictly, while a high-multiplicity model ranks *types*: members of a cohort are exchangeable. That is a real modeling change and the authors might regard identity-based priority as essential. Still, for a market in which decisions are made from shared categories and profiles, type-level rankings are a natural description, and the mirror preserves the central object the paper optimizes. The aggregate assignment becoming fractional is the expected consequence of continuizing the population, not a change to divisible outcomes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both mirrors is that the proposed type compression removes information used by the paper’s central fairness tests. In the paper, resources rank individual agents strictly. Take two applicants with the same preference list, \(b_1 \succ b_2\), while \(b_1\) ranks \(a_1 \succ a_2\). If \(a_2\) gets \(b_1\) and \(a_1\) gets \(b_2\), \(a_1\) envies \(a_2\). Merge the applicants into one type and that distinction disappears: the resource either ties members of the type or the type must be split according to their priority positions. The former changes the strict-priority model; the latter can leave as many types as applicants in the contested part of the market.

That is a direct problem for the proposed Theorem 1 / MAXEFM mirror. Envy-freeness depends on which particular applicant a resource prefers, not just on applicant demand or the mass assigned to each option. A cohort model can define a sensible envy condition using priorities between types, but it then studies a model with tied or coarsened priorities. A more faithful model can retain rank positions as part of each applicant’s type, but then the type count may grow with the population, weakening the high-multiplicity rationale.

The same issue defeats the proposed Corollary 1 / MAXRSM mirror. Relaxed stability still asks whether an applicant blocks with a resource, and that depends on the resource’s strict ranking of that applicant against its current match. The lower-quota exception does not remove the identity-sensitive comparison. In a type-level version, one must decide how within-type priority and partial assignment affect blocking; that can be modeled, but it is an additional change to the paper’s fairness notion.

The best reply is that real placement systems sometimes use categories and shared priority tiers, so a maximum mass envy-free or relaxed-stable allocation could be a worthwhile problem in its own right. I cannot rule that out. But it is weaker evidence for these particular anchors than the proponent suggests: their medical-placement story supports a cohort model only where positions treat cohort members alike or where the tie-break is discarded. The paper’s defining examples and results use strict two-sided rankings, where that tie-break determines whether a matching is admissible.

So the negative case is a model-fidelity objection, not a claim that mass matching is meaningless or that the continuous problems would have an uninteresting answer. Both proposed questions are coherent, and suitable tie-based versions could be worth studying. I would not claim the universal negative is airtight; its force depends on whether the authors regard type-level indifference as a natural mirror of their strict-priority problems.
