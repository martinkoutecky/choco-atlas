What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; the paper's numbered theorems establish existence.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the rent-splitting result, but it is a lead for a new computational question, not an eligible computational anchor. Theorem 1.5, proved in this paper, says that if for every room-price distribution every group of \(n-k+1\) potential tenants contains someone willing to rent a room, and preferences are closed, then there is a price distribution and \(k\) tenants willing to rent distinct rooms. It is a topological existence theorem. The paper has no named result asserting a complexity classification or giving an algorithm, and its arbitrary closed preference sets have no finite input representation. So there are no anchors of the kind the brief requires.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large housing market with many repeated apartment blocks. A type \(t\) is a tenant preference type: the same willingness to rent each room at each price distribution. The society is a finite set \(T\) of such types with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Room supply scales with the population, so each room class has positive capacity \(q_j\). This makes a mass assignment meaningful while each individual still receives one whole room.

One possible follow-up is Mass Rent-Splitting. Given \(k\), the masses \(\mu_t\), capacities \(q_j\) with \(\sum_j q_j\le 1\), and each type’s closed, rational-polyhedral willingness sets \(W_j^t\subseteq\Delta^{k-1}\), find a price distribution \(p\) and assignment masses \(y_{tj}\ge 0\) such that
\[ \sum_t y_{tj}=q_j,\qquad \sum_j y_{tj}\le\mu_t,\qquad y_{tj}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ p\in W_j^t. \]
For fixed \(k\), I would expect this version to be tractable: partition the price simplex into regions where willingness is constant, then check each region by a flow computation. With variable \(k\), that argument does not settle the complexity. Natural next questions include robustness to losing a given mass of tenants and whether finite repeated markets approximate the mass solution.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 1.5 assigns \(k\) individual tenants to the \(k\) rooms of one apartment. With fixed \(k\), those recipients are vanishing mass in a continuum population. Positive room capacities require replicated supply, which is a plausible scaling for large housing markets but an added modeling choice. The mirror is credible as a direction the authors might recognize; the paper itself does not establish its computational case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.5 is the strongest candidate, but it is an existence theorem, not a computational result: it assumes arbitrary closed preference sets and quantifies over every price distribution and every large subset of tenants. The paper gives no finite representation of those sets, no input model, and no algorithmic problem whose complexity could be classified. That is the central obstacle for a ChoCo mirror.

The proposed Mass Rent-Splitting problem gives the idea its best chance by adding finite types, rational polyhedral willingness sets, and room capacities. Those additions also change what the theorem guarantees. At a fixed price, Theorem 1.5 only ensures that at least \(k\) tenants are willing to rent some room; its conclusion assigns one tenant to each of \(k\) rooms. With \(k\) fixed as the population grows, those \(k\) recipients have zero mass in the limit. For a finite type support, any positive-mass type supplies arbitrarily many individual tenants, so the original theorem’s condition can become a support-level existence condition while the actual masses play no role.

Positive capacities \(q_j\) make the outcome aggregate, but the theorem does not ensure enough willing mass to fill them. Guaranteeing those capacities requires a stronger mass-demand condition and a fractional matching constraint. That could define a worthwhile computational problem, but its key content comes from the added capacity and mass-balance model; it is not the computational question posed or answered by Theorem 1.5. Letting capacities vanish recovers the theorem’s finite selection, with a vanishing allocation.

The other existence results do not repair this mismatch. The secretive guest remains a single named person, and resilience to excluding a fixed number of guests disappears at population scale. Scaling the number of exclusions can preserve a mass threshold, but the theorem still selects only \(k\) recipients.

Repeated tenants are a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, and a capacity-aware market problem might interest the authors. The negative case is weaker against such a new project than against a mirror of this paper: it cannot rule out every useful follow-up. It does show that the paper supplies no computational anchor, and that making its conclusion genuinely about mass requires changing the problem’s inputs and output.
