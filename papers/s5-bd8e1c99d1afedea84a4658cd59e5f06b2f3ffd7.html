The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and position type sets \(I,J\), rational type masses, compatible pairs, strict rankings over counterpart types, and nonnegative rational rewards \(w_{ij}\), represent a matching by masses \(x_{ij}\) that can be realized by assigning each individual unit mass to one partner. Agents are indifferent among members of the same counterpart type and compare matchings by their assigned partner type, with being unmatched last. A matching is popular if, against every measurable re-matching of the same population, the total mass preferring it is at least the total mass preferring the challenger. Compute a popular matching maximizing \(\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}x_{ij}\), or give an approximation algorithm, with complexity measured in the finite type input and rational bit length.

The model it lives in

An atomless population represented by finitely many types and rational masses, with preferences strict over counterpart types and tied within each type. The matching is a finite mass matrix \(x\); popularity compares the same agents under every competing matching, expressible through finite type-level transition masses, and the objective is \(\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}x_{ij}\).

The objection that survived

The paper assumes strict preferences over named partners, while the finite-type mirror ties partners within each type; whether that preference change preserves the target market regime is the main modelling concern.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 14's maximum-weight popular-matching approximation threshold; it leaves the paper's other hardness, algorithmic, and bounded-treewidth results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the universal popularity condition be separated or dualized using finitely many type-level transition variables to optimize the reward?
  • What additive guarantee, if any, does this model give for discrete instances with moderate type support?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 14, proved in this paper. It says that for a bipartite graph with strict preference lists and nonnegative edge weights, unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), maximum-weight popular matching has no polynomial-time approximation with ratio better than \(1/2\); a polynomial-time \(1/2\)-approximation exists. The paper proves the hardness using its forced/forbidden-edge construction. The approximation uses prior results on stable and dominant matchings and algorithms for finding maximum-weight matchings in those classes.

A plausible mirror is a large, recurring one-to-one placement market: many applicants and many individual positions, drawn from a small number of preference and eligibility types. A type records everything relevant to the problem: its compatibility, strict preference list, and any edge weights. For example, applicants of a type might share a ranking of job classes, and positions of a type might share a priority ranking of applicant classes. To retain strict rankings among individuals within a class, give each counterpart a public rank coordinate and order first by class, then by that coordinate. Members of a type then have the same strict preferences over all individuals. The regime is millions of agents but relatively few supported types, \(\tau\); when costs or preferences differ, those agents belong to different types.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. The instance consists of finite type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) encoded in binary, compatible type pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\), strict preference orders of each type over counterpart types, and nonnegative rational rewards \(w_{ij}\) for compatible pairs. Realize each type’s population as an atomless interval of the stated mass, with the public coordinate order within each type. A feasible matching \(M\) is a measurable, measure-preserving bijection between matched subsets of the two populations, pairing only compatible types. Each unit of mass receives an integral assignment: one partner or none. Its reward is \(W(M)=\int_{a\text{ matched}}w_{i(a),j(M(a))}\,d\lambda(a)\).

For two feasible matchings \(M,N\), let each individual vote for the matching that gives them their preferred partner, with being unmatched last. Define \(\Delta(M,N)\) as the mass on both sides who prefer \(M\) to \(N\), minus the mass who prefer \(N\) to \(M\). Call \(M\) popular if \(\Delta(M,N)\ge 0\) for every feasible \(N\). The mirror of Theorem 14 is to compute the maximum reward among popular measurable matchings, or approximate that optimum, with complexity measured in \(\tau\), the input graph size, and the bit length \(L\). A solution is a popular matching attaining the value, or an algorithm returning a popular matching with a stated approximation guarantee. Producing a finite representation of such a matching is part of the algorithmic question.

I would expect the discrete \(1/2\) barrier to dissolve in this regime, and lean toward Class A: reward is linear in matched mass, while the mass supplies invite continuous optimization. A tractable result would need to characterize popularity with a finite set of constraints or a separation procedure over competing matchings; identifying that pricing problem is a natural further question. Other useful questions are whether the continuous optimum is the limit of high-multiplicity discrete optima, and what additive approximation it gives when a discrete instance has moderate \(\tau\). I am not claiming an LP follows automatically: the paper’s barrier may survive in a different form.

The weak point is that a type-to-type mass matrix alone does not determine the votes between two matchings. Which particular members of a type receive which partners can change the comparison, so the precise mirror uses assignments on a shared individual space. This makes the model more demanding than a finite transport LP, and the public within-type priority is a constructed scenario whose plausibility should be judged. Still, the question preserves the paper’s central object: bilateral matchings under pairwise majority over the same agents. It covers Theorem 14’s weighted popular-matching result, not every result in the paper. The paper’s discussion of fractional and mixed matchings is relevant background, but those relax the matching or outcome representation in finite instances; they do not by themselves answer this continuous-population question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is against Theorem 14’s proposed high-multiplicity encoding. Its public rank coordinate is doing essential work: it makes preferences over individual partners strict. But rank also affects who is desirable to the other side, and therefore who can be matched to whom. Under ChoCo’s definition of type as a complete description for the problem, agents at different ranks are not interchangeable. Treating every rank as a separate type makes the support grow with the population; keeping only finitely many types means collapsing distinct ranks.

Collapsing ranks gives a plausible market with strict preferences over *types* but indifference among individual partners of the same type. That is the better mirror to consider, and it avoids the coordinate problem. Yet a type-to-type mass matrix does not capture Theorem 14’s voting rule. When two matchings are compared, an agent’s vote depends on whether that same agent receives a better partner in one matching. The two matrices record the marginal assignments, not which individuals move between them. A valid formulation therefore needs to represent couplings between matchings on the shared population, or otherwise prove that those couplings can be compressed. The proponent’s measurable-bijection model retains them mathematically, but does not supply a finite input and output representation or a complexity measure in \(\tau\) and \(L\). “Find a finite representation” names this missing step; it does not settle it.

This is a substantive weakness in the anchor as stated: its finite-type input and its strict, identity-sensitive preferences pull in different directions. But it is not a decisive case against every mirror. A prover could define a natural weak-preference population model and discover a finite representation of the relevant couplings. If that model preserves the central question in the authors’ view, it would be a worthwhile continuous mirror even if its answer differed from Theorem 14’s. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the proposed formulation has not yet shown that it is a finite-support computational problem, but the material here does not justify ruling out a better one.
