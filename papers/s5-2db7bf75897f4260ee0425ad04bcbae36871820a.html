What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue covers Proposition 2's global restrictions and Proposition 15's local restrictions for complete profiles; it does not cover the paper's other structural comparisons. Neither anchor supplies a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are mathematical guarantees about when propositionwise majority preserves logical consistency; none classifies an algorithmic problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest result is Proposition 2, proved in the paper: for a profile of consistent judgment sets, single-plateauedness relative to an order of the propositions guarantees a consistent majority outcome; single-canyonedness also guarantees consistency and is preserved by majority. Proposition 15, also proved here, gives a local version for complete and consistent profiles. These are meaningful results, but they assert consistency under structural domain restrictions—not computational complexity.

A natural population analogue would take types to be consistent judgment sets \(A\subseteq X\), with rational mass \(\mu_A\) for each type. The group accepts proposition \(p\) when \(\sum_{A\ni p}\mu_A>1/2\). One could ask whether the resulting majority judgment set is logically consistent, particularly when the supported types are single-plateaued under a common proposition order. A large deliberative body with many members but relatively few recurring, coherent judgment types is a plausible regime, and the authors would recognize this as their aggregation question. The consistency guarantee appears to extend by the same majority-intersection argument.

That is a plausible continuous-population extension, but it is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. There are therefore no qualifying anchors, no per-anchor prover questions, and no honest basis for assigning a Class A, B, or C expectation from the paper’s results. The positive case is limited to the subject’s natural fit with population masses; the computational landscape would be new work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor is a computational result. Proposition 2 and Proposition 15 give conditions under which majority preserves consistency. They do not analyze an algorithmic problem or its complexity. The proposed mass version therefore may be a sensible extension of the paper’s mathematics, but it does not supply the computational question this programme is looking for.

For Proposition 2, let \(\mu_A\) be the mass of each judgment type \(A\), and let the group accept \(p\) when \(\sum_{A\ni p}\mu_A>1/2\). A continuous proof follows the same structural argument: two majority sets of types must overlap, and a type accepting the endpoints of an interval accepts everything between them. That is a clean continuous analogue, but it remains a consistency guarantee. Proposition 15 has the same limitation: its local conditions can be stated for masses of complete judgment types, with an appropriate convention for ties, and the conclusion is still a consistency guarantee.

The population change adds no computational parameter to these results. With a finite agenda \(X\), there are at most \(2^{|X|}\) judgment types. For rational masses, a common denominator \(D\) turns \(\mu_A\) into counts \(D\mu_A\), giving a finite profile with exactly the same majority outcome; conversely, every finite profile gives rational masses. Aggregation itself just sums masses. If the agenda or logic is an input, consistency checking may be hard, but that difficulty comes from the logic or agenda, not from making the population continuous. Adding costs and asking for the cheapest mass transfer, or asking for the nearest profile satisfying the paper’s restrictions, could create computational problems—but those introduce objectives absent from this paper and would be new projects, not mirrors of Propositions 2 or 15.

So the mirrors fail as *computational* anchors, not as population models. A large deliberative body with repeated judgment types is quite plausible, and the paper’s authors could reasonably recognize the mass formulation as their aggregation question. That makes the universal claim—no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario—hard to establish honestly. The narrower negative case is stronger: these results support a continuous consistency theory, but they do not furnish a computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
