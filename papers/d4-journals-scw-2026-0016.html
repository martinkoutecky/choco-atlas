What the mirror covers

The paper samples utility pairs from a continuous distribution but keeps a finite committee; its Section 4 extension also retains finite-\(n\) random pairing. The proposed mirror covers trade offers and aggregate welfare, but its nonatomic incentives do not preserve the equilibrium result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors under the rule you set. The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which characterizes non-trivial equilibria of the finite-\(n\) game by equations (2)–(9), and Corollary 1, also proved here, which establishes that such an equilibrium exists. Corollaries 2 and 3 give symmetry and multiplicity results. None asserts a computational complexity classification such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or FPT. The paper’s equilibrium analysis is not a named complexity result.

The strongest plausible positive direction is a follow-on problem about the paper’s group-wide vote trading. Let types be rational utility pairs \(t=(u_1,u_2)\) in a finite support \(T\), with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. In a nonatomic population, types are randomly paired; each type chooses a distribution over the paper’s trade offers, and trades are resolved by its rule. The resulting masses of yes and no votes determine each issue’s majority outcome. The problem would be: given \(\mu\), find a symmetric type-dependent Nash equilibrium of trade offers and compute its aggregate welfare \(\int (I_1u_1+I_2u_2)\,d\mu\). A large committee with many repeated utility profiles is a plausible regime, and the paper’s Section 4 already studies random pairing and group-wide trading, though approximately.

That is a recognizable extension of the authors’ question, but its weak point is fundamental: in a nonatomic society, one voter’s trade cannot change a majority outcome. The pivotal-vote incentives that drive Theorem 1 can therefore disappear, leaving a degenerate equilibrium problem unless the continuum model adds some treatment of near ties or aggregate fluctuations. That makes this a poor basis for claiming the paper has a strong continuous-computational mirror. It is a candidate research question, not an anchor-backed case; I would not assign it a credible tractable, hardness-transfers, or continuum-specifically-hard classification from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper has no qualifying computational result to anchor a mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibria of the finite-\(n\) game by equations; Corollary 1 proves one exists. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The numerical equilibrium calculations and the ANES density estimate do not supply such a result either. Although voter utilities are drawn from a continuous distribution, the society in the game is still a finite sample of voters.

The strongest proposed mirror—compute a type-dependent equilibrium and its welfare from rational type masses—uses a plausible high-multiplicity regime. That is not the problem. In a nonatomic society, an individual’s trade changes zero mass, so it cannot change either majority outcome. The pivotal probabilities that drive the paper’s incentives are finite-\(n\) effects. Once they disappear, an individual is indifferent among trade offers: any strategy profile is an equilibrium, while aggregate trade choices can still change group outcomes. The equilibrium concept therefore no longer selects the trades whose welfare the paper evaluates.

Near-tie fluctuations or a finite committee sampled from the type distribution might preserve interesting pivotal incentives, but they retain finite-population randomness; they do not restore the proposed nonatomic equilibrium. Positive-mass trading blocs or a welfare-maximizing flow could produce a nontrivial aggregate problem, but they replace individual voters with blocs or a planner. Those may be worthwhile follow-up models, but they are not mirrors of the paper’s strategic result.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo anchor: there is no computational result to mirror, and its central incentive vanishes when the population itself becomes nonatomic. It is not a proof that no related continuous question could be worthwhile; finite-size asymptotics are the clearest possible follow-up. That possibility does not give this paper a nondegenerate continuous-population computational mirror.
