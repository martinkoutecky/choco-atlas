What the mirror covers

The proposed profile-mass task covers evaluation of the allocation families in Theorem 1 and Theorem 2; it leaves their axiomatic characterizations and the Twitch findings without a complexity analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is a streaming platform with many subscribers but relatively few repeated subscription and consumption profiles. Let a type be a subscriber’s complete profile \(t=(p_t,c_{1t},\ldots,c_{mt})\), where \(p_t\) is their fee and \(c_{it}\) is their consumption of category \(i\). A finite-support distribution \(\mu\) then records each profile’s fraction of subscribers. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime when those profiles really recur; the paper’s Twitch example, with users recorded individually, does not itself establish that regime.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes rules satisfying equal treatment of equals, neutrality, scale invariance, non-advantageous transfer within a subscriber, and composition: they are exactly the \(\beta\)-mixtures of equal division and subscriber-proportional allocation, for \(\beta\in[0,|N|/(|N|-1)]\). Theorem 2 gives the parallel characterization with sharing proofness in place of composition and the proportional rule in place of subscriber-proportional allocation. These are axiomatic characterizations, not claims about complexity or algorithms for an underlying computational problem. The empirical calculations apply the rules, but do not supply a named complexity result either.

One can define a continuous task from the formulas: given \(m\) categories, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) subscriber profiles, and a chosen \(\beta\), compute the resulting allocation vector. For example, normalized total revenue is \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\), and the subscriber-proportional allocation to category \(i\) is \(\sum_t\mu_t \frac{c_{it}}{\sum_j c_{jt}}p_t\). This task is directly computable from the support in \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations, but that bound is an inference from the formula, not a result proved by the paper. It is also just rule evaluation; it does not turn the paper’s axiomatic question into the computational complexity question ChoCo studies.

So the population mirror is plausible as a model of streaming subscribers, but this paper offers no qualifying anchor and no substantive computational mirror question per the stated criteria. That is the weak point I cannot honestly repair without inventing a new problem detached from the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 3 characterize which allocation rules satisfy specified axioms. Corollaries 2 and 4 characterize unique rules. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result. A continuous version of those statements would still be an axiomatic characterization, while ChoCo’s target is the computational landscape created by a continuous population.

The proponent’s subscriber type \(t=(p_t,c_{1t},\ldots,c_{mt})\) is a credible high-multiplicity model when complete fee and consumption profiles recur. That is not where this case is strongest. In such a model, the paper’s rules translate directly into sums over the profile distribution: equal division uses \(\frac{1}{m}\sum_t\mu_t p_t\), and subscriber-proportional allocation uses \(\sum_t\mu_t\frac{c_{it}}{\sum_j c_{jt}}p_t\). Replacing subscriber sums with profile-mass sums gives a natural population formulation, but the questions it puts forward remain about axioms and rule selection.

The best computational candidate is to take rational masses on \(\tau\) profiles and compute the allocation vector. The displayed formulas make that a direct \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic task. That task is compatible with the paper’s application, but it is not a computational result the paper states or analyzes; it is a new evaluation problem attached to explicit formulas. The empirical Twitch section likewise applies the rules without posing a complexity question. Adding an optimization task, such as choosing \(\beta\), would introduce a new decision problem rather than mirror one of the paper’s named results.

So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo mirror anchored in this paper: a plausible continuous society exists, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built from streaming revenue allocation. The case rests on the programme’s stated scope and the paper’s actual results, not on showing that every possible high-multiplicity model is implausible.
