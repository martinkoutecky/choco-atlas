The case FOR (proponent)

Caulfield’s paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its tables report calculations on 58 students’ ballots, and Arrow’s theorem is mentioned as an external axiomatic result, not proved here. So there is no qualifying result to anchor a continuous mirror to.

The strongest positive angle is an exploratory mirror of the paper’s central activity. Consider a large movie-voting group whose members share a relatively small set of ballot types. A type is a complete ranking of the \(m\) films together with the approval cutoff; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the group with type \(t\). Given the supported types and their rational masses, ask for the winner set under plurality, Borda, approval, Condorcet, and instant runoff, using a specified tie-breaking rule. The solution is the winners under each rule and whether they disagree. This preserves the paper’s question—how the group’s choice changes with the voting method—and fits a regime with many voters but moderate support size \(\tau\). It appears tractable by direct aggregation and elimination.

That is a plausible population-continuous version of the activity, but it is not an anchor-backed complexity contribution from this paper. Its weakest point is that the article studies an educational comparison of voting methods, not a computational bottleneck; in the proposed mirror, the calculations remain straightforward.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The direct mirror is easy to state: take each complete ballot type to include a ranking and an approval cutoff, assign types rational masses, then compute the winners under the five rules. That is a sensible continuous-population model. A large electorate can have many people per type, and the normalized masses preserve the contrasts the activity is meant to show. I see no honest objection based on identity, multiplicity, or a degenerate limit.

The problem is that this mirrors the classroom exercise, not a computational result in the paper. The article reports one tabulation of 58 ballots and uses it to prompt discussion; it proves no theorem about winner computation or any other computational problem. The proposed mirror repeats the tabulation with weighted types. Even if that is a valid question to ask, it gives ChoCo no result from this paper whose computational landscape it could chart.

The stronger variants expose the same gap. Mapping which mass distributions make two rules disagree would turn the activity into a profile-space or axiomatic geometry question. Asking for the least mass that must change to switch a winner, or for a way to campaign toward a desired outcome, adds robustness or strategic intervention absent from the article. Those could be worthwhile research questions in their own right, but they are not continuous mirrors of this paper’s results. So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo research contribution, though weak against the claim that the activity itself has a natural continuous formulation.
