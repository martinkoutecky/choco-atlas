What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation covers computing \(WUt\) on a majority relation generated from a continuous society; it leaves \(SUt\) and the paper's structural comparisons unaddressed. Neither the paper's prose complexity claim nor its numbered structural results provide a qualifying anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case has a limit: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result, so it has no qualifying named anchor under your rules. It does make a computational claim: Section 4 presents Algorithm 1 and concludes that membership in both \(WUt(R)\) and \(SUt(R)\) is in P. The algorithm computes \(WUt\) using the predecessor and transitive-closure characterization, with the analogous substitution for \(SUt\). But that P claim is not a numbered result of the required kind. The numbered propositions and lemmas give structural relationships, not complexity classifications. The paper also has no hardness result to anchor a continuization whose point would be to ask whether hardness dissolves.

The continuous problem I find plausible, though it is unanchored, is Continuous Majority-Relation WUt. An instance has a finite alternative set \(A\), a list of \(\tau\) voter types, and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) is one agent’s complete description of their pairwise judgments: a reflexive binary relation \(R_t\), which may leave alternatives incomparable. For each pair, form the strict-majority relation
\[ xR_\mu y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{t:\,xR_t y}\mu_t>\tfrac12. \]
Because each \(R_t\) is reflexive, \(R_\mu\) is reflexive and hence a pseudo-tournament. The task is to output the exact set \(WUt(R_\mu)\); equivalently, return membership bits \(z_x\) with \(z_x=1\) exactly when, for every \(y\in A\), either \(y\) does not strictly beat \(x\) in \(R_\mu\), or \(x\) reaches \(y\) through the asymmetric part of \(R_\mu\). This is a choice-set computation, so there is no optimization objective.

A plausible regime is a very large membership organization or public consultation where millions of participants assess the same candidates using a relatively small set of recurring judgment patterns, including “incomparable.” The type masses are population fractions; \(\tau\) counts distinct patterns, not people. Any rational instance also corresponds to a finite replicated population by choosing a common denominator for the masses.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: compute the pairwise majority relation from the \(\tau\) types, then apply the paper’s WUt algorithm. That gives polynomial time in the number of alternatives, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. This is an inference from the paper’s Section 4 claim, not a named result in it, and it does not reveal whether discrete hardness dissolves—the paper establishes no such hardness.

The weakest point is that the paper takes a binary relation as its input; it does not define a population of judges or a majority aggregation rule. The proposed model adds that layer. The authors may recognize the same Untrapped choice problem on a relation generated from a continuous population, while still judging the aggregation wrapper to be a new application rather than a mirror of their stated problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative begins with the anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its numbered results characterize relationships among choice sets; Section 4 gives Algorithm 1 and concludes in prose that membership in \(WUt(R)\) and \(SUt(R)\) is in P. So Continuous Majority-Relation WUt is not a continuization of a named complexity result.

The proposed model also adds little computational structure if voter types are arbitrary reflexive relations. A society with one type of mass \(1\) already represents any pseudo-tournament \(R\) the paper accepts. With several types, their masses are used to form an aggregate relation \(R_\mu\), and the output depends only on that relation. The task is then weighted pairwise aggregation followed by the paper’s existing reachability computation. That is a population-based input encoding, but it leaves the paper’s computational problem unchanged.

Restricting types to rankings makes the population scenario more convincing: a large electorate with recurring ballots is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Still, the task factors into majority aggregation and computing \(WUt\) on the resulting relation. That may be a useful applied extension, but it is not a mirror of a named complexity result in this paper.

The negative case is therefore strong against the proposed anchor, but weak as a universal claim. The ranking-profile version is coherent, and the programme’s rules allow a well-posed mirror even when its answer is already easy to infer. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario; I can say that this paper offers no qualifying named computational result, and its most natural population formulation appears to add an aggregation layer around the relation-level problem.
