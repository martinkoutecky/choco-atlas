The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: the paper has a natural continuous-population analogue, but it has no qualifying computational anchor. None of its numbered results proves NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or any other complexity classification. Theorem 1 is axiomatic, Proposition 1 is a core characterization, Proposition 2 is a rule equivalence, and Theorems 2 and 3 are structural identities. Thus the mirror below is substantively credible, but it cannot be presented as a standard ChoCo case anchored in a computational theorem.

The best regime treats the teams themselves as the population. Consider a large broadcasting association with \(n\) teams and only \(\tau\ll n\) team types. A type completely specifies a team’s audience behaviour against every opponent type: its home and away audiences, fan-market characteristics, and any other parameter used by the sharing rule. Let \(T\) be the finite type set, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of teams of type \(t\), and let \(a(t,u)\) be the normalized audience for a home game between types \(t\) and \(u\). The normalization is important: one may set the finite audience to \(a(t_i,t_j)/(n-1)\), so total league revenue is \(O(n)\), not \(O(n^2)\). This is the natural per-capita version of the paper’s model.

Define

\[ B(\mu)=\sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_t\mu_u a(t,u) \]

and

\[ \alpha_\mu(t)=\sum_{u\in T}\mu_u\bigl(a(t,u)+a(u,t)\bigr). \]

Here \(B(\mu)\) is total broadcasting revenue per unit mass of teams, while \(\alpha_\mu(t)\) is the audience contribution of one type-\(t\) team.

My lead mirror is Continuous Concede-and-Divide Regression. It is anchored in Theorem 3, proved in this paper. The theorem says that, after removing any team \(k\) to eliminate collinearity, the authors regress each audience entry on a generic intercept and team effects, assign the estimated generic component equally, assign each team effect to that team, and split residuals equally between the two teams. The resulting allocation is independent of the removed team and equals

\[ R_i^{b,k}(N,A) = \frac{(n-1)\alpha_i-\lVert A\rVert}{n-2} = CD_i(N,A). \]

The continuous problem is:

Given rational \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), and a rational audience kernel \(a\), compute the per-type revenue allocation induced by the paper’s regression procedure.

Use the normalization \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t=0\) and solve

\[ \min_{b_0,b} \sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_t\mu_u \bigl(a(t,u)-b_0-b_t-b_u\bigr)^2. \]

Writing the residual as

\[ \varepsilon_{tu}=a(t,u)-b_0-b_t-b_u, \]

the per-capita version of the paper’s three allocation procedures asks for

\[ \rho_t = b_0+2b_t+ \frac12\sum_{u\in T}\mu_u \bigl(\varepsilon_{tu}+\varepsilon_{ut}\bigr). \]

A solution is the exact vector \((\rho_t)_{t\in T}\), together with the least-squares coefficients. The normal equations give

\[ b_0=B(\mu),\qquad b_t=\frac{\alpha_\mu(t)}2-B(\mu), \]

and hence

\[ \rho_t=\alpha_\mu(t)-B(\mu). \]

This is precisely the per-capita limit of the paper’s concede-and-divide formula. It also preserves an important feature of the original problem: some \(\rho_t\) may be negative. The allocation is budget-balanced because

\[ \sum_t\mu_t\rho_t=B(\mu). \]

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains round-robin pairwise audiences, the same regression objective, the same decomposition into generic fans, team fans, and joint-game residuals, and the same concede-and-divide rule. Only the population representation and the necessary per-capita normalization change.

A second, cleaner mirror is anchored in Theorem 2, also proved here. It states that for every finite broadcasting problem, the equal-split allocation equals the Shapley value of the associated cooperative game

\[ v_A(S)=\sum_{\substack{i,j\in S\\i<j}}(a_{ij}+a_{ji}). \]

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Equal-Split/Shapley Allocation:

Given \(T\), \(\mu\), and \(a\), compute the common per-team award \(\rho_t\) for every team of type \(t\), where the award is the high-multiplicity limit of the finite Shapley/equal-split allocation.

The answer is

\[ \rho_t=\frac{\alpha_\mu(t)}2. \]

It satisfies

\[ \sum_t\mu_t\rho_t=B(\mu). \]

Thus the continuous object is genuinely the population of teams and its type distribution, not merely the divisibility of money. The finite-to-continuous dictionary is direct: if \(n_t/n\to\mu_t\) and the normalized audience matrix is generated by \(a(t_i,t_j)/(n-1)\), then the finite equal-split and concede-and-divide awards converge to the two formulas above.

Both mirrors are expected to be Class A. With \(T\) explicitly represented, the required sums are polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length; the regression version requires only a finite linear system. There is no discrete NP-hardness result in this paper to transfer, and no obvious continuum-specific hardness. Further questions would include whether the continuum pairwise game has a useful nonatomic core/value theory, how hybrid performance-based schemes should be optimized over type masses, and what happens when audiences depend endogenously on the allocation.

The weakest point is substantial. The paper’s sports setting usually has only about twenty teams, and team-specific audience matrices can make every team its own type. The high-multiplicity regime is convincing only for a larger ecosystem of near-exchangeable clubs or esports franchises with repeated market types. More importantly, the resulting continuous problems are closed-form aggregation problems, not a new complexity frontier. So the paper supports a plausible Class A mirror, especially through Theorem 3, but under the programme’s strict anchor rule it does not provide a compliant computational case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational anchor to continuize. None of its numbered results classifies an algorithmic problem: Theorem 1 is axiomatic, Proposition 1 concerns the core, Proposition 2 identifies rules, and Theorems 2 and 3 prove algebraic identities. Thus the proposed mirrors do not relax a computational problem from the paper; they add one.

Theorem 3 is the strongest candidate, and its proposed population regime is not nonsense. One can imagine many clubs or franchises sharing a finite set of audience types, with pairwise audiences scaled per capita. Under that model, the continuous least-squares calculation is legitimate: with
\[ B(\mu)=\sum_{t,u}\mu_t\mu_u a(t,u) \]
and
\[ \alpha_\mu(t)=\sum_u\mu_u\bigl(a(t,u)+a(u,t)\bigr), \]
the normalized normal equations do give
\[ b_0=B(\mu),\qquad b_t=\frac{\alpha_\mu(t)}2-B(\mu), \]
and hence the concede-and-divide density
\[ \rho_t=\alpha_\mu(t)-B(\mu). \]
That is a valid high-multiplicity identity, and it should not be rejected merely because audiences or awards are fractional.

But it is not a ChoCo computational mirror. The finite theorem does not pose a problem whose complexity is studied; it shows that a prescribed regression-and-allocation procedure equals a closed-form rule. The continuous version merely evaluates type-weighted sums and solves a rank-one least-squares system. Calling this “Continuous Concede-and-Divide Regression” gives the calculation a new name, not a new computational question. A genuinely harder variant—optimizing the type distribution, designing the league, imposing capacity constraints, or making audiences depend on the allocation—would be a new problem absent from the paper.

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. Its Shapley result is likewise a structural identity, not a complexity result. In a nonatomic league there is no ordinary player-level Shapley value of the finite game: individual players have zero mass, and the proposed \(\rho_t=\alpha_\mu(t)/2\) is a limiting payoff density introduced by an additional convention. One can define a nonatomic cooperative game and study positive-mass coalitions, but that becomes new continuum game theory. The equal-split density itself remains an entirely natural object; what does not survive as a paper-derived computational anchor is the finite Shapley characterization.

The team-type story is also only conditionally persuasive. If arbitrary named teams and arbitrary audience matrices are retained, each team is effectively its own type, so there is no high-multiplicity gain. If exact repeated audience types are imposed, one obtains a sensible block-structured restriction, but not the empirical La Liga problem as presented. This is a weakness of the regime, not a claim that teams can never be repeated types.

So the honest negative conclusion is narrower but strong: the paper supports mathematically respectable continuous analogues, especially of Theorem 3, yet neither is a worthwhile ChoCo mirror in the programme’s computational sense. The universal claim that no interesting continuous model can ever be formulated would be overstated; the defensible claim is that this paper supplies no named computational result whose high-multiplicity complexity landscape the programme can study.
