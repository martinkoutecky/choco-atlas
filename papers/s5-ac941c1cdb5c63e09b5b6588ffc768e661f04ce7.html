The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many store types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), holding and penalty costs \(h,p\), and a finite encoded set of common-shock scenarios \(\omega\) with rational probabilities \(\pi_\omega\) and nonnegative per-capita demands \(d_{t\omega}\), compute type rates \(a_t\) in the core of the resulting continuum cost game: for every submass vector \(0\le\nu\le\mu\), let \(D_\omega(\nu)=\sum_t\nu_t d_{t\omega}\) and \(c_\mu(\nu)=\min_{q\ge0}\sum_\omega\pi_\omega[h(q-D_\omega(\nu))_++p(D_\omega(\nu)-q)_+]\); find \(a\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=c_\mu(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t\nu_ta_t\le c_\mu(\nu)\) for all such \(\nu\), or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity retail chain represented by finitely many complete store types with masses \(\mu_t\), under an encoded common-shock demand model. The decision variables are per-store cost rates \(a_t\); the objective is to find rates meeting total-cost equality and every subcoalition’s core constraint.

The paper permits correlated demands but does not specify a finite encoding of coalition demand laws; under independent demands, per-capita costs may vanish in the large-population limit.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational complexity result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The paper’s numbered results concern convergence of allocation processes. The closest is Theorem 5.2, proved here: for nonnegative stochastic cooperative games whose coalition costs converge almost surely, pointwise, to a balanced cost game \(c\), every accumulation point of the diagonal \(R2\) allocation sequence belongs almost surely to \(\operatorname{core}(c)\). Theorem 5.1 gives the corresponding convergence to a least-square value. These are meaningful results about iterative processes, but they give no complexity classification or running-time bound. Theorem 3.2’s related finite-game result is cited from Lehrer (2002a).

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the subject: a large retail chain with many stores but relatively few complete store types, where a type records the demand law and all cost-relevant parameters. If \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of stores of type \(t\), a coalition can be represented by a mass vector \(\nu\le\mu\), and a type-level cost allocation by rates \(a_t\). Its core constraints would be \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=c(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t\nu_ta_t\le c(\nu)\) for every \(\nu\le\mu\). That is a sensible continuous cooperative-game model. But making it a computational question—such as finding such an \(a\), or proving a continuous version of \(R2\) convergence—would be a new extension, not a result this paper establishes. I can’t honestly assign it a complexity class from these anchors.

There is also a problem with relying on the paper’s newsvendor-specific convergence results: Lemma 4.1 identifies \(g(\mathbb E[X])\) with \(\mathbb E[g(X)]\), which is false in general. Since Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 use that lemma to connect the dynamic realization game to the expected game, they are especially weak foundations for a positive mirror case without a repair. The strongest honest positive claim is that the store setting admits a natural continuous-population formulation; the paper itself supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is at the gate: this paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.1 proves convergence of a finite-player allocation process to a least-square value; Theorem 5.2 proves that accumulation points lie in a finite-player core. In both, the limit is the number of repetitions \(T\), while the player set \(N\) stays fixed. Each update gives the budget to one named player, and the core is defined by constraints for the coalitions of that finite set. Neither theorem gives an algorithm, a running time, or even a rate at which a finite-stage allocation approaches its limit. The general hypothesis \(c_t(S)\to c(S)\) almost surely supplies no such rate. Theorem 3.2 is cited from Lehrer; it does not add a computational result from this paper.

The proposed type-mass core is the best candidate for a continuous question: given \(\mu\), compute type-level cost rates \(a_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=c_\mu(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t\nu_ta_t\le c_\mu(\nu)\) for every \(\nu\le\mu\). A large chain with repeated store types is plausible, and that question could be worthwhile on its own. But it is not supplied by these theorems. The paper gives no finite encoding of \(c_\mu(\nu)\): it permits general demand distributions and correlated demands, while specifying neither how coalition demand laws are represented nor how to compute their costs. With independent idiosyncratic demand and per-capita scaling, aggregation washes out the uncertainty and the limiting cost can collapse to zero. A common-shock model can preserve nonzero costs, but choosing and encoding that dependence structure is an added modeling commitment.

The allocation-process version faces a related gap. \(R1\) and \(R2\) select a single player for each update. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass; replacing the update with one that allocates to a type or to a positive-mass group would define a new process. The type-level core problem and such a mass-allocation process may both be sensible research directions, but neither is a complexity question established by the paper’s convergence results. Recasting Theorem 5.1 as optimization over type rates, or Theorem 5.2 as continuous-core computation, gives a new problem rather than a computational mirror of their results.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo target: its central contribution is convergence in time for fixed finite games, not computational analysis of a population problem. It does not prove the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A structured demand model with common shocks could make a continuous core problem meaningful. The honest limitation is that this would require enough new assumptions and formulation that its value cannot be ruled out from this paper alone.
