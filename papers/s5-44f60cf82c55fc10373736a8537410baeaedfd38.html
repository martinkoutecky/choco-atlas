The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result: no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or gives an algorithm or complexity classification. So it has no qualifying anchor under the programme’s rules. Its strongest anchor-like result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper, but it is a structural equivalence, not a computational result: for positive-externality games, the set of strong Nash equilibrium coalition structures in the exclusive-membership \(H\)-game equals the set of \(\alpha\)- and \(\beta\)-core-stable coalition structures. The proposition establishes both inclusions; Lemma 1 supplies the positive-externality reduction to deviations where outsiders break into singletons. I would not present Proposition 1 as a complexity anchor.

There is still a plausible *new computational question* inspired by that result. Call it Finite-Type Continuum \(H\)-Stability Search. Consider an industry with many firms, perhaps thousands, whose cost, strategy, and payoff attributes fall into a fixed set \(T\) of types. An instance gives each type’s rational population share \(\mu_t\), and a finite rational description of type \(t\)’s per-firm payoff as a function of its coalition’s type-mass vector and the resulting coalition structure. Require positive externalities: merging coalitions weakly benefits firms outside the merger. A coalition is represented by its vector of member masses; any mass not assigned to a nontrivial coalition is in singleton coalitions. For a finite output representation, restrict attention to structures with finitely many nontrivial coalitions.

The question is: does there exist a coalition structure with no profitable \(H\)-deviation, and, if so, find one? A deviation selects masses from existing coalitions and singletons, partitions those masses into new coalitions, and leaves outsiders as singletons, as in the paper’s \(H\)-game punishment. It is profitable if every positive-mass group of deviators weakly improves and some positive mass strictly improves. The solution is a stable coalition structure; there is no social-welfare objective in the paper’s problem. This preserves its central concern—coalition formation and stability under externalities—while making population mass, rather than the number of firms, the quantity being partitioned.

The regime is credible for large industries with many firms sharing a small number of production or R&D profiles, which the paper itself identifies as examples of coalition formation with spillovers. It is not a mirror of the paper’s country-level environmental example. With a suitable definition of mass deviations, Proposition 1 suggests asking whether the continuum \(H\)-stable structures still coincide with the corresponding continuum core-stable structures. That would be a structural question, however, not a complexity result.

For the finite-bloc search problem, my tentative expectation is Class C: the stability test ranges over mass selections and partitions, which could introduce hardness specific to the continuum model. That is only a research guess; the paper establishes no such hardness, and more structured payoff functions might instead permit optimization methods. Natural follow-ups are whether stable structures exist, whether the Proposition 1 equivalence survives, whether the finite model embeds in this one, and how the answer changes when the number of types is fixed.

The weakest point is that this is a computational agenda built *around* the paper’s equilibrium concept, not a computational problem the authors solve. The finite-bloc representation and payoff input format also need justification, and the paper’s named propositions provide no complexity result for the mirror to continue. That makes this a defensible lead for a new question, but not a strong anchor-based case under the programme’s stated test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is also the clearest: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 equates two sets of stable coalition structures; Lemma 1 and Propositions 2–4 give structural characterizations and inclusions. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or even a computational problem with a finite input and output. There is no analogue here of a mass-transfer LP whose tractability turns on a pricing problem. Under the programme’s stated test, Proposition 1 is not a computational anchor.

The proposed Finite-Type Continuum \(H\)-Stability Search is the best replacement, but it is a new problem built around the paper, not a computational mirror of a result in it. Its payoff input is described only as a “finite rational description,” without specifying a representation that would make complexity meaningful. And its finite-bloc output restriction does substantial modelling work: the paper’s \(H\)-game allows arbitrary partitions and uses agreement on complete coalition-structure announcements to determine which coalitions form. Restricting the continuum to finitely many nontrivial blocs makes mass vectors usable as an output, but the paper gives no reason to privilege that restriction. A more general continuum model would need to represent a distribution over coalition compositions and define how measurable groups’ announcements induce outcomes. That could be formalized, but doing so would be a substantive new modelling and computational agenda.

The multiplicity story itself is plausible: many firms sharing production or R&D profiles could form a high-multiplicity population. So I would not reject the idea on the ground that firms are inherently individuated, or that the paper’s economic examples cannot scale. Nor does the absence of a computational theorem show that no useful computational question can be developed around coalition stability. It shows that the proposed question is not anchored in a computational result this paper establishes.

I would therefore vote against treating this as a worthwhile continuous mirror *of this paper*. The negative case is strong on the programme’s anchor test and weaker as a universal claim: a carefully specified finite-type continuum coalition game could become a legitimate computational project, but the paper itself does not supply the result or input model that would make it a mirror.
