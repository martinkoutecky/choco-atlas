The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of ranking types over candidates \(C\) and rational societies \(\mu,\nu\in\Delta(T)\), decide whether the set \(\{\lambda\in\Delta(T):D(\lambda,\mu)=D(\lambda,\nu)\}\) has nonempty relative interior in \(\Delta(T)\), where \(D(\lambda,\mu)=\frac12\sum_{t\in T}|\lambda_t-\mu_t|\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over ranking types \(T\); \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are benchmark societies, and \(\lambda\) ranges over possible population compositions. The decision question is whether total-variation distance to the two benchmarks ties throughout a relatively open region of the population simplex.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the simplex-restricted tie-region complexity question suggested by Proposition 5.10; it leaves the quotient-distance and Wasserstein results, hyperplane-rule results, and other decisiveness claims untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether the tie set has nonempty relative interior in \(\Delta(T)\), as a function of \(|C|\), \(|T|\), and the rational input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Proposition 5.10, proved in this paper. It says that LARGE-BISECTOR is NP-complete: given \(x,y\in\mathbb{Q}_+^M\), decide whether their \(\ell^1\) bisector—the points \(z\) equidistant from \(x\) and \(y\)—contains an open ball. The paper proves hardness by reducing PARTITION, and uses its preceding subset-sum characterization to establish membership in NP. This is a hardness anchor, and the paper’s own discussion connects large bisectors to indecision in distance-rationalizable rules.

My lead mirror is Population Tie-Region. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of admissible ranking types over candidates \(C\), and two rational societies \(\mu,\nu\in\Delta(T)\), given by their supported types and masses. For a possible society \(\lambda\in\Delta(T)\), use the normalized Hamming distance between societies, which is total variation: \(D(\lambda,\mu)=\frac12\sum_{t\in T}|\lambda_t-\mu_t|\). Decide whether there is a relatively open subset \(U\) of the population simplex such that every \(\lambda\in U\) ties between the two benchmark societies: \(D(\lambda,\mu)=D(\lambda,\nu)\). A yes-instance has an open tie region as its solution; a no-instance does not. Equivalently, the benchmarks are singleton consensus sites for a two-outcome distance-rationalized rule, and the question asks whether its tie boundary contains a positive-volume region.

The regime is a large electorate with many voters sharing a modest number of ballot types: for example, millions of voters in a district where a restricted ballot domain permits only a few dozen rankings. The masses are type fractions, and the society \(\lambda\) is the population composition being assessed. Its distance to each benchmark is the average normalized cost of changing voters’ rankings. The input has size governed by the candidate and type counts, support sizes, and rational bit lengths—not the number of individually named voters.

The normalized version appears to retain the anchor’s hardness, by adapting the paper’s PARTITION reduction. Given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) with sum \(A\), take \(N=n+2\) ranking types and define signed mass differences \(\delta_i=\epsilon a_i\) for \(i\le n\), and \(\delta_{n+1}=\delta_{n+2}=-\epsilon A/2\). Choose a small rational \(\epsilon\), and set \(b=(1-\epsilon A)/N\), \(\mu_i=b+\max(\delta_i,0)\), and \(\nu_i=b+\max(-\delta_i,0)\). These are positive rational distributions, each of total mass one, and their entries can be made arbitrarily close to \(1/N\).

For a proper nonempty set \(S\) of types, consider societies \(\lambda\) that put each \(i\in S\) below both benchmark masses and each \(i\notin S\) above both. Such a region intersects the simplex when the benchmarks are sufficiently close to uniform. Throughout it, the difference between the two distances is constant and equals \(2\sum_{i\in S}\delta_i\). Thus it is a tie region exactly when the selected differences sum to zero. With these \(\delta_i\), a proper nonempty zero-sum subset exists exactly when some subset of the \(a_i\) sums to \(A/2\), which is the PARTITION condition. Intermediate-coordinate regions cannot supply an open tie region in this construction: the distance difference varies with a nonzero slope there. This gives a credible NP-hardness route for the population-simplex problem. I would expect NP-completeness: a sign-cell certificate can be checked by verifying an affine identity and the feasibility of the cell’s rational strict inequalities.

I expect this mirror to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C). Its question concerns whether ties occupy an open region of the continuous society simplex; an individual finite electorate cannot witness that property. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the number of types and their binary-encoded masses, rather than the number of agents. Further questions include whether hardness persists under natural restrictions on consensus sites, whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the joint support size, and what changes when total variation is replaced by a ranking metric such as Kendall distance.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Proposition 5.10’s bisector-complexity result, not the paper’s geometric treatment of distance rationalization or all its claims about decisiveness. The authors should recognize the question: it uses their vote-distribution simplex, their normalized Hamming geometry, and their concern with large tie boundaries.

The weak point is substantial. The paper already treats vote distributions on a simplex, so this is not a new continuous-population model; it is a new computational question posed inside their existing model. Also, the reduction uses two arbitrary, interior benchmark societies as sites. A referee insisting on natural, neutral consensus sets—such as unanimity sites on the simplex boundary—could reject the scenario, and the paper notes that large bisectors may disappear there. The case survives as a precise, population-based complexity question closely tied to their own large-bisector result, but it should be graded as a narrow mirror rather than a broad new continuization of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 5.10 is already a computational result about continuous societies. The paper identifies a society with its distribution over ranking types, represents those distributions in the vote simplex, and shows that normalized Hamming distance becomes total variation distance. Its LARGE-BISECTOR problem then asks whether two such points have a tie region with interior. Population Tie-Region gives that same geometry a population interpretation; it does not make the population continuous for the first time.

The proposed reduction may establish a useful refinement: Proposition 5.10 asks about an open ball in the ambient space, while the mirror asks about relative interior inside the probability simplex. Those are not literally identical questions, and the normalized restriction can make the problem nontrivial. But that is a restricted complexity variant within the paper’s existing continuous-population model, not a new continuization. The reduction’s Partition argument strengthens that variant; it does not supply the missing discrete-to-continuous bridge.

Making the sites more natural weakens the anchor further. The paper’s hyperplane-rule results show that standard unanimity-based versions have tie boundaries contained in finitely many hyperplanes, so they cannot have an open tie region. The hardness case instead uses arbitrary interior singleton sites, precisely the kind of consensus the paper describes as unnatural. The case against is therefore strong on novelty, though not airtight: the simplex-restricted complexity question itself is not proved by Proposition 5.10 and could be a worthwhile follow-up to this paper. It is just a narrow continuation of work already carried out in the continuous model, rather than a ChoCo mirror.
