The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchor-based positive case for this paper: it contains no named computational result of the required kind. Theorem 4.1 proves that the Colomer–Martínez index is uniquely characterized by EFF, NP, SYMw, and DPMw; Theorem 5.1 gives the analogous characterization of the Holler–Colomer–Martínez index using HCMw. Neither asserts a complexity bound, an algorithm, or a computational encoding that the paper uses to solve an underlying problem. The Ecuador tables are applications, not named computational results.

A possible future question would be how to compute a power index when many members fall into a small number of identical types. But that would not mirror a named result here, and the continuous version needs a careful definition: the indices rely on minimal winning coalitions, whose meaning does not carry over directly to nonatomic masses. The paper’s contribution is axiomatic, so a continuous population formulation would continuize the wrong thing for this programme’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied a computational anchor to mirror. Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 5.1 characterize power indices from axioms; neither gives an algorithm or a complexity result. The Ecuador tables are calculations, not computational theorems. By ChoCo’s stated scope, continuizing those characterizations would be an axiomatic project, not a continuous-computational mirror.

The strongest adjacent idea is to ask how to compute aggregate Colomer–Martínez or Holler–Colomer–Martínez power when many players fall into a few weight types. That is more plausible than insisting on the paper’s six named parliamentary benches: a large voting body could have many members of each type, and type-level power could preserve a useful output. But it is a new computational question, not a mirror of a result this paper proves. The authors themselves leave methods for calculating the indices to future work.

There is also a deeper modeling gap. Both indices, and the mergeability properties used to characterize them, count minimal winning coalitions. In a nonatomic population, a coalition can be represented by a vector of type masses; the minimal winning coalitions at a quota generally form a continuum. The paper’s uniform average over a finite set of coalitions then has no canonical counterpart. Choosing a measure or sampling rule over that continuum could produce a computable index, but it would add a substantive new modeling choice. Meanwhile, an individual of measure zero cannot change the outcome by joining or leaving, so per-player pivotal power disappears; aggregating power by type is possible, but does not resolve how to weight the continuum of coalitions.

That makes the negative case strong for this paper: its named contributions are axiomatic, and its most natural computational extension needs new primitives before there is a well-defined continuous problem. The universal claim is harder to establish. A carefully designed asymptotic, type-level power index might be worth studying; the case against is that this would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
