The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(p\le -1\), take sequentially arriving divisible items with aggregate supplies \(s_i\) and a society of total mass \(N\), represented by \(\tau\) binary valuation profiles \(t\) with shares \(\mu_t\). Each profile has unit monopolist utility, \(\sum_i s_i v_{ti}=1\). Values are revealed item by item; online allocations must depend only on revealed histories, assigning equal per-member allocations to profiles with identical revealed histories. What is the infimum worst-case competitive ratio for maximizing mass-weighted \(p\)-mean welfare, \(\bigl(\sum_t\mu_t U_t^p\bigr)^{1/p}\), against the clairvoyant offline optimum, as a function of \(\tau\), \(N\), and input bit length?

The model it lives in

The population has type masses \(N\mu_t\). At each arrival, a nonanticipating policy chooses per-member allocations \(q_{ti}\) from revealed histories, subject to \(\sum_t N\mu_t q_{ti}\le s_i\); type \(t\)'s utility is \(U_t=\sum_i v_{ti}q_{ti}\). Compare its mass-weighted \(p\)-mean welfare with the offline optimum over allocations that know all values.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 22's lower bound for binary valuations and \(p\le -1\), when support size \(\tau\) grows. It leaves the algorithmic results, including Theorem 9, and the other \(p\)-regimes aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a matching upper bound depend on support size \(\tau\) rather than total mass \(N\), particularly when \(\tau\) is fixed and the type masses are unequal?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is Theorem 22, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(p\in[-\infty,-1]\), every online algorithm for \(p\)-mean welfare maximization has competitive ratio at least \(n^{1/2-o(1)}\), even when valuations are binary and every agent’s monopolist utility is \(1\). This is a lower bound on online performance, rather than an NP-hardness result. I would make it the sole anchor: it is a substantial hardness result, and the continuum question it suggests is precise.

The natural regime is a large cloud service or ad platform serving many accounts in recurring workload or demand classes. Accounts in a class share their value profile over resource opportunities; the number of accounts is much larger than the number of profiles. The divisible items and their online arrival remain as in the paper. The continuization is the population: each profile represents a mass of otherwise identical agents.

I would call the mirror Continuum-Cohort Online \(p\)-Mean Welfare. An instance has total population mass \(N\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) complete valuation types, and type shares \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Type \(t\) therefore has mass \(N\mu_t\). There are \(m\) divisible items with rational supplies \(s_i\); type \(t\)’s binary value \(v_{ti}\) for item \(i\) is revealed when that item arrives, not before. Require \(\sum_i s_i v_{ti}=1\) for every type, matching the unit monopolist-utility assumption.

At item \(i\), the policy chooses a per-agent allocation \(q_{ti}\) for each type, with \(0\le q_{ti}\le s_i\) and \(N\sum_t\mu_t q_{ti}\le s_i\). The resulting type utility is \(U_t=\sum_i v_{ti}q_{ti}\), and welfare is \(\bigl(\sum_t\mu_t U_t^p\bigr)^{1/p}\), interpreted as the geometric mean at \(p=0\) and the minimum at \(p=-\infty\). The offline optimum sees all values. A solution is an online policy, using only revealed values, with the smallest worst-case competitive ratio; the computational question is to determine that ratio as a function of \(\tau\), \(N\), and the input bit length. The society’s full profiles define types for analysis, while the online policy sees value revelations only as they arrive.

This is close to the authors’ problem: it retains the same online allocation decisions, information pattern, welfare objective, and monopolist normalization. The policy treats members of a type equally, which is natural for identical agents and divisible items. The mirror covers Theorem 22’s negative-welfare regime only.

There is a direct hardness-transfer reason to take this question seriously. Set \(\tau=n\), give each type share \(1/n\), and set \(N=Kn\), so every type has mass \(K\). If \(q_t\) is the continuum allocation per member, then \(Kq_t\) is a feasible allocation for the corresponding finite representative agent: the continuum item constraint becomes \(\sum_t Kq_t\le s_i\). Utilities in the continuum instance are the finite utilities divided by \(K\), so both online welfare and offline optimum scale by the same factor. The competitive ratio is unchanged. Theorem 22’s construction has \(\Theta(n)\) distinct profiles—the private makeup items distinguish many of the agents—so its lower bound yields a \( \tau^{1/2-o(1)} \) lower bound even when every type has arbitrarily many identical members. I expect hardness to transfer in the number of types, not to arise specifically from the continuum.

The weakness is also the boundary of that argument: the hard construction needs \(\tau\) to grow with \(n\). It says nothing about whether the competitive ratio can grow with \(N\) when \(\tau\) is fixed. In the cloud example, the result supports a mirror when workload classes themselves are numerous; it does not establish a worsening guarantee merely because more accounts join the same few classes. That leaves a useful next question: can the lower bound be matched by a policy whose guarantee depends on \(\tau\) rather than total mass, and what happens for fixed \(\tau\) with unequal type masses?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only defensible objection I can find is that Theorem 22 is an online competitive-ratio lower bound, not a running-time or encoding-complexity result. Its continuum counterpart would ask for the best competitive ratio of an online policy. That is less directly aligned with ChoCo’s complexity landscape than a mirror about the complexity of a population-level optimization problem.

I can’t make that objection decisive. Competitive algorithms are still computational results, and the paper pairs its lower bounds with online algorithms. A careful mirror can preserve the arrival information: at each step, the policy sees the values revealed so far and allocates the arriving item across the mass of agents with each observed history. It need not know agents’ future type labels. The fact that the hard instance creates many distinct full valuation profiles—including through private makeup items—means the transferred bound concerns a regime with growing support \(\tau\). That narrows the question, but does not defeat it: one can duplicate every constructed profile many times and scale total supplies accordingly, leaving a high-multiplicity society whose support grows with the construction.

The proposed formalization does need a normalization fix. With total supply \(s_i\), the constraint \(N\sum_t\mu_tq_{ti}\le s_i\) does not give each agent unit monopolist utility when \(\sum_i s_i v_{ti}=1\); total supplies must scale with \(N\), or be stated as per-capita supplies. That is repairable, and the programme explicitly allows this kind of co-modification.

So my negative case is weak. I do not see an honest way to defeat this anchor, including its stronger version. The paper supports a plausible high-multiplicity online-allocation question; I cannot justify the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
