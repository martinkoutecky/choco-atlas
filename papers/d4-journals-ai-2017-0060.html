What the mirror covers

The proposed fleet scenario aggregates the individual choices computed by Algorithm 1; a collective outcome or evidence-change intervention would require a decision model not studied in the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: when the distinguished preference criteria have a strict total order, the framework’s choices from a choice experiment coincide with the maximizers of a rational preference relation. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that the resulting choice structure satisfies WARP. Lemma 1 establishes that the epistemic component implements a rational preference relation. These are choice-theoretic results, not computational complexity results. Algorithm 1 computes acceptable alternatives, but the paper gives no complexity guarantee, and its argumentation component remains abstract.

A possible population extension would model a large fleet of robots in standardized warehouse settings. A type could specify a robot’s alternatives, evidence about their attributes, and ordered preference criteria; mass would be the fraction of robots with that complete decision context. One could compute the mass selecting each alternative by applying Algorithm 1 to each supported type. But this mostly aggregates independent single-agent choices. Adding an intervention objective—such as minimizing the mass whose evidence must change to alter the aggregate choice—would introduce a new problem the paper does not study.

So I cannot provide a continuous problem anchored in a qualifying named computational result. The robot-fleet lift is a possible extension, but it is too weakly connected to the paper’s computational contribution to count as a defensible positive case under the stated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s three anchors are the paper’s Lemma 1, Theorem 1, and Theorem 2, and none states a computational result. Lemma 1 says the epistemic component implements a rational preference relation; Theorem 1 relates the framework’s choices to preference maximization; Theorem 2 establishes WARP. Each characterizes the choices of one decision maker. None asks how hard a computational problem is or gives an algorithmic guarantee.

The best nearby computational candidate is Algorithm 1, which computes acceptable alternatives. A fleet of robots in repeated warehouse settings could plausibly supply high multiplicity: a type would include the robot’s full decision context, including its alternatives, evidence, and preference criteria. That is a more credible type definition than treating named robots as interchangeable. But applying the algorithm to each type and reporting the mass selecting each alternative is a distributional summary, not a computational problem about collective choice. The paper defines no rule for turning those individual choices into a social outcome.

Adding such a rule—for example, a collective winner—or adding costs for changing agents’ evidence to reach a target outcome could produce a research problem. But either move supplies the missing collective decision or intervention model; neither is a population version of a problem this paper studies. Algorithm 1 also does not rescue the anchors: its argumentation component is abstract, with no common input encoding or complexity guarantee. In a concrete instantiation, complexity would depend on the chosen argumentation formalism.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: its named results are choice-theoretic, and its algorithm does not yield a clear continuous computational mirror without adding a new collective problem. The case is not airtight: a distributional study of repeated decision contexts could still be useful, but it is hard to defend as the continuous-population complexity programme’s mirror of this article.
