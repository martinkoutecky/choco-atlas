What the mirror covers

The proposed workforce model covers the paper’s task-group and coalition optimization problem; the paper has no named computational result for that mirror to carry across.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive opening is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s task-group coalition problem. Imagine a large service workforce where many agents share the same capability vector, conversion costs, and communication-cost parameters. A type \(q\) represents that complete shared profile, and \(\mu_q\) is its fraction of the workforce. Keep the tasks and precedence relation discrete; choose a partition of the tasks and, for each task group \(G\), allocate type mass \(x_{q,G}\) to its coalition, maximizing gross income subject to the group’s capability requirements. That makes the population continuous while retaining the paper’s central decision: which tasks to combine and which coalitions to form.

But this is only a candidate mirror, not an anchor-based case. The paper has numbered Definitions, Remarks, and Algorithms 1 and 2, but no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithm 2 describes a search and reports the best income found; the simulations do not establish a complexity class or a formal guarantee of optimality. So there are no qualifying named results to restate, and no continuous question per anchor to hand to a prover. The main weakness in the candidate mirror is that the paper does not fully specify the value of a coalition serving a task group or whether agents can be reused across groups. Those details are needed for a precise, faithful optimization problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper offers no named computational result to carry across. Algorithms 1 and 2 are proposals, not complexity or approximation theorems. Algorithm 2 searches the pruned task-group graph, calls earlier coalition-formation algorithms, and returns its best solution if time permits. Its stated comparison with earlier methods is not a guarantee of optimality or running time. The simulations on small task sets do not supply one either. So there is no proved result here whose continuous counterpart the programme can classify.

The workforce mirror can be made more precise than the proponent’s sketch. Let \(q\) describe agents’ capabilities and all task-relevant costs, let \(\mu_q\) be their population share, and let \(x_{q,G}\) allocate type mass to task group \(G\). Supply constraints, aggregate capability requirements, and a net-income objective would define a coherent high-multiplicity optimization problem. With suitable scaling, it could be a sensible research question. Repeated capability types are plausible; identity and multiplicity are not the obstacle.

But that formulation would require choices the paper leaves open, including how coalition value and communication costs depend on team composition and whether agents can serve multiple groups. Resolving them could yield worthwhile new work, but it would formalize a new problem rather than mirror a result proved in this paper. That is the strongest paper-specific negative case. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the proposed problem may be valuable precisely as a new, well-specified study of the paper’s central optimization task.
