Why no mirror

Proposition 2 gives a useful analytic bridge from repeated supportability to the stage-game core, and the proposed mass-market extension is plausible. But the paper states no named computational result for that question to mirror, so the required computational anchor is absent.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Proposition 2's secret-transfer supportability result in a type-mass matching setting and could extend the private-wage conclusion of Proposition 3; it leaves the paper's other repeated-game results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Proposition 2's finite-agent public-PCE/core equivalence extend when blocks can move divisible type masses and public histories record those masses?
  • If it does, can feasibility of the resulting mass-core payoff set be decided by an LP with complexity polynomial in the number of types and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest natural setting is a large labor market with repeated hiring: many workers and positions, but relatively few worker skill types and position types. Agents within a type share the same productivity, preferences, and wage observability rules; the type masses are rational proportions. A one-to-one assignment market is a clean special case of the paper’s labor-market application.

My exploratory lead would be Mass Secret-Wage PCE Supportability. An instance gives finite type sets \(\mathcal F,\mathcal W\), rational masses \(p_f,q_w\), pairwise match surpluses \(s_{fw}\), a discount factor \(\delta\), and target per-agent payoffs \(a_f,b_w\). A feasible period assignment is a mass flow \(x_{fw}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_w x_{fw}\leq p_f\) and \(\sum_f x_{fw}\leq q_w\). Ask whether there is a public plan in the repeated mass market that delivers the target payoffs and cannot be profitably blocked at any public history. A block can reroute positive masses of firm and worker types and redistribute their surplus through secret wages; the public plan observes the assignment and the blocking masses, but not those private transfers. A solution is a yes or no, with a supporting mass assignment and plan in the yes case.

This is plausible as their problem: it retains repeated matching, coalitional blocking, and the role of wage secrecy. For the one-to-one transferable-utility case, I would expect a tractable answer if supportability reduces to checking the mass assignment core, whose feasibility conditions have an LP form. That is a conjecture about the continuous model, not a result of this paper.

The nearest result is Proposition 2, proved in the paper’s Supplementary Appendix: if every essential coalition can make secret transfers, then for every \(\delta\geq 0\), a public PCE supports a discounted payoff vector if and only if that vector lies in the stage-game core. This is a useful bridge from dynamic supportability to a static object. It is an equilibrium characterization, though; it gives no complexity classification. The paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a comparable class. So Proposition 2 cannot serve as an anchor under the stated rules, and there is no eligible anchor for this proposed mirror.

The weakest point is the move from finite coalitions to mass coalitions. In the paper, plans can respond to the identity of a blocking coalition; in the mirror, they would need to respond to the masses and types in a block. That change may alter which punishments are credible. The candidate is worth studying, but the computational question and its likely tractability are new work suggested by Proposition 2, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is categorical: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–5 and the labor market propositions characterize which payoffs can be supported in a repeated game; they do not give an algorithm or a complexity classification for a finite-input problem. The proponent’s only anchor, Proposition 2, says that public PCE supportability is equivalent to membership in the stage-game core. That is an equilibrium characterization, not a computational result.

A stronger version of their proposal is quite plausible: use finitely many firm and worker types with rational masses, and ask whether a target payoff profile is supportable in the mass market. With secret transfers, Proposition 2 suggests reducing the question to membership in a type-level core; for one-to-one matching with pairwise surpluses, that may have a clean LP formulation. This is not defeated by the multiplicity objection: a large labor market with repeated agents of the same type is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the per-type mass model is a natural co-modification.

But the algorithmic question would be new work built on an analytic characterization. The paper supplies no finite computational problem whose complexity can be compared with the continuous version. Under ChoCo’s stated standard for an anchor, the proposal therefore does not qualify as a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

That makes the case against strong as a judgment about this paper’s fit for the programme, but weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous project exists in any scenario. The mass-market problem could be worth studying on its own; I cannot honestly argue that it is an unpromising model.
