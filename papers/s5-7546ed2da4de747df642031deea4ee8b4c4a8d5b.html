The continuous mirror question

Given α ∈ (1,2] and ε > 0, can any deterministic anonymous mechanism F map each finite-support rational distribution μ over location types t ∈ [0,1] and prediction ŷ to a facility location y ∈ [0,1], be strategyproof under every finite refinement of μ, and satisfy both improved bounds? Define u_y(t) = 1 - |y - t|, ER(y,μ) = max_{t ∈ supp(μ)} u_y(t) / min_{t ∈ supp(μ)} u_y(t), OPT(μ) = min_{z ∈ [0,1]} ER(z,μ), and mid(μ) = (min supp(μ) + max supp(μ))/2. The bounds are ER(F(μ, mid(μ)), μ) ≤ (α - ε)OPT(μ) and ER(F(μ, ŷ), μ) ≤ (α/(α - 1) - ε)OPT(μ) for every ŷ.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support rational distribution μ over residents' ideal locations t ∈ [0,1]. The prediction ŷ is the forecast of the midpoint of the support extremes; envy ratio uses the maximum and minimum utilities among positive-mass types, not a mass-weighted average. The mechanism outputs the α-BIM clipping of ŷ to [1 - 1/α, 1/α]. Require individual strategyproofness on each finite refinement of μ, where one resident's deviation shifts mass 1/N between types in a population of size N.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the deterministic prediction results in Section 3, especially Theorems 3.2 and 3.3. It leaves the randomized mechanisms and prediction guarantees in Section 4 untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the consistency-robustness frontier change when resident locations are restricted to a specified finite grid?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible positive mirror here, though it is a limited one. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. Its closest anchors are mechanism guarantees and an impossibility bound. My lead is the explicit mechanism result.

A credible regime is siting a public facility along a city corridor. Each resident’s location is their ideal facility location; residents at the same representative neighborhood location have the same preferences and utilities. A city might have millions of residents but only hundreds of such location types. The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\subseteq[0,1]\), with rational mass \(\mu_t\) at each location type. The prediction \(\hat y\) is a forecast of the optimal facility location.

For a facility at \(y\), define \(u_y(t)=1-|y-t|\) and
\[ \mathrm{ER}(y,\mu)= \frac{\max_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}u_y(t)} {\min_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}u_y(t)}. \]
Let \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\min_y\mathrm{ER}(y,\mu)\). As in the paper’s Lemma 2.3, attributed to Ding et al., an optimal location is the midpoint of the smallest interval containing the support. To preserve the paper’s individual strategyproofness meaning, define a report profile’s society as its empirical distribution: one resident’s deviation moves mass \(1/N\) between types in a population of size \(N\). This keeps the incentive constraint meaningful at every finite population size.

Lead — Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper. For each \(\alpha\in[1,2]\), the \(\alpha\)-BIM is anonymous and strategyproof, \(\alpha\)-consistent, and \(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\)-robust with respect to envy ratio. At \(\alpha=1\), the robustness bound is unbounded. The mechanism returns the prediction when it lies in \([1-1/\alpha,1/\alpha]\), and otherwise returns the nearer endpoint of that interval.

Its continuous question is Measure-\(\alpha\)-BIM: given a finite list of location types with rational masses, a prediction \(\hat y\), and \(1<\alpha\le2\), return
\[ M_\alpha(\mu,\hat y)= \min\!\left(\frac1\alpha,\max\!\left(1-\frac1\alpha,\hat y\right)\right). \]
The required guarantee is \(\alpha\)-consistency when \(\hat y\) is the midpoint of the support extremes, and \(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\)-robustness for every prediction, using \(\mathrm{ER}(y,\mu)/\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\). This is an explicit, tractable mechanism: finding the support extremes takes \(O(\tau)\) comparisons, followed by the stated calculation. Since it ignores the report distribution, its individual strategyproofness also holds in every finite refinement.

Theorem 3.3, also proved here, supplies a second, matching question. For any \(\alpha\in(1,2]\) and \(\epsilon>0\), the paper proves that no deterministic, strategyproof, anonymous mechanism can be both \((\alpha-\epsilon)\)-consistent and \((\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}-\epsilon)\)-robust. The continuous problem is Measure-\(\alpha\)-Frontier: does any deterministic anonymous mechanism on finite-support societies, strategyproof under all finite refinements, achieve both improved bounds? The answer should be no. Every finite location profile gives a rational empirical measure, so the paper’s lower-bound instances carry into this model. This is a transferred mechanism impossibility, not a computational-hardness classification; on a fixed restricted type grid the precise frontier could depend on which locations that grid contains.

The mirror covers the deterministic, prediction-based results in Section 3. It does not claim a mirror for the randomized results in Section 4 or for the paper as a whole. Further questions include how the frontier changes on a particular finite location grid, and what happens for randomized mechanisms with predictions under the expected-utility definition.

The weak point is substantial: envy ratio takes a maximum over agents, so the masses \(\mu_t\) do not affect it once the support is fixed. The mirror makes the population a distribution, but these results use only which locations occur, not how much population has each location. And in a purely atomless model, a single resident’s report would not change the distribution; the finite-refinement definition is needed to retain the paper’s incentive concept. So this is a recognizable continuous-population version of their facility-location mechanism problem, but it does not open a rich new high-multiplicity complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s siting scenario is credible, and I would not reject it on the ground that large populations cannot have repeated types. The weakness is that the paper’s guarantees barely use the population at all. For a society \(\mu\), the envy ratio depends on the largest and smallest utilities among types in its support; the masses of those types do not enter. The optimum is determined by the support’s extreme locations. So the proposed Measure-\(\alpha\)-BIM is exactly the paper’s clipping rule applied after scanning for those extremes. It runs in \(O(\tau)\), but the paper’s result neither needs nor exploits high multiplicity: changing a type’s mass from nearly all the population to an arbitrarily small positive fraction changes none of the guarantees.

The finite-refinement definition preserves individual strategyproofness, but it also reveals how little changes. A rational mass vector can be scaled into the corresponding finite profile, and a one-person deviation remains a \(1/N\) change on that finite grid. In a genuinely atomless model, an individual deviation has no effect; making positive-mass cohorts the strategic agents would instead change the incentive concept. The proponent’s model is coherent, but its continuous population is principally a new encoding of the same finite profiles, while the mechanism ignores their reports.

Theorem 3.3 fares no better as a computational mirror. Under the proposed requirement that strategyproofness hold for every finite refinement, restricting a measure mechanism to any fixed population size gives a deterministic, anonymous, strategyproof mechanism to which the paper’s impossibility theorem applies. The lower bound is inherited directly. If the model is broadened to real-valued masses and cohort deviations so that this reduction no longer applies, the paper’s theorem no longer supplies the claimed frontier. Either way, this is an extension of a mechanism impossibility result, not a new complexity question about computation on continuous societies.

One could make the problem mass-sensitive by replacing individual envy with a quantile or group fairness objective, or by asking how much mass must move to change a guarantee. Those may be worthwhile projects, but they alter the objective or add a new intervention problem; neither is what these theorems establish. Computing the prediction from the population would likewise add a learning problem absent from the paper, which treats the prediction as input.

So I can make a strong case against spending ChoCo’s complexity effort on these particular mirrors: they yield an immediate support-extrema calculation and an inherited impossibility, with no new computational landscape. But I cannot honestly defend the literal universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The siting model is plausible, and the mechanism guarantees do transfer. The negative case is strongest as a claim about low computational value, not as proof that the paper has no recognizable continuous-population counterpart.
