The continuous mirror question

Given colleges \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), applicant types \(t\in T\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and each type’s strict college preferences and integer scores \(s_{tj}\), let rational per-capita quotas be \(q_j\). A threshold vector \(\theta\) assigns each type to its most-preferred eligible college with \(s_{tj}\ge\theta_j\), or leaves it unmatched; let \(\ell_j(\theta)\) be the assigned mass at college \(j\). Compute the componentwise least and greatest threshold vectors satisfying \(\ell_j(\theta)\le q_j\) for every \(j\), with lowering any positive \(\theta_j\) by one making that college’s assigned mass exceed \(q_j\); give an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and input bit length \(L\), or show that no such algorithm exists unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The model it lives in

Types carry their full college preferences and score vectors, with mass \(\mu_t\); thresholds determine each type’s placement, and colleges have per-capita quotas \(q_j\). The task is to compute the least and greatest stable threshold vectors and their induced type masses.

The objection that survived

The score updates appear to transfer almost directly to cohorts, potentially giving a routine \(O(m^2\tau)\)-type bound; the proponent acknowledges the lack of a hardness collapse but does not establish a new algorithmic phenomenon.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the computation of the extreme score-stable threshold vectors and their induced enrollments, using the guarantees in Theorems 5 and 6 alongside Theorem 7’s runtime bounds. It does not address the paper’s separate lattice and stability-equivalence results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the extreme stable threshold vectors remain exactly computable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the binary input length when masses and per-capita quotas are rational?
  • Can the score-update bounds be stated independently of the numeric score range when scores are encoded in binary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror here is the score-stability algorithm. In the supplied paper, Theorem 7, proved by the authors, says that the score-decreasing algorithm runs in \(O(m^2 n)\) steps and the score-increasing algorithm in \(O(mn)\), so both run in polynomial time. Their output guarantees are established by Theorems 5 and 6, also proved here: starting from any valid score vector, the decreasing algorithm returns the greatest stable vector below it; starting from any critical score vector, the increasing algorithm returns the least stable vector above it. From the standard starting vectors, these are respectively the maximum stable score vector, which is applicant-pessimal, and the minimum stable score vector, which is applicant-optimal. The paper gives no named hardness result, so this algorithmic theorem is the appropriate anchor.

I would call the mirror Continuous Score-Limit Extremes. An instance has \(m\) colleges and a finite list of applicant types \(T\). A type \(t\) specifies a strict preference order over the colleges it applies to and its integer score \(s_{tj}\) at each such college. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). College \(j\) has a rational per-capita quota \(q_j\). A threshold vector \(\theta\in\{0,\ldots,M+1\}^m\) determines each type’s assignment: it goes to its most-preferred college \(j\) with \(s_{tj}\ge\theta_j\), or remains unmatched if there is none. Thus the mass enrolled at \(j\) is \(\ell_j(\theta)=\sum_{t:\,t\text{ assigned to }j}\mu_t\).

A solution is a pair of stable threshold vectors and their induced assignments: the least and greatest stable vectors. Stability means \(\ell_j(\theta)\le q_j\) for every college, and, for every \(j\) with \(\theta_j>0\), lowering only \(\theta_j\) by one makes \(\ell_j\) exceed \(q_j\). The question is whether both extremal vectors can be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and the binary input length \(L\). I expect Class A: the paper’s cutoff updates and monotonicity argument have a natural aggregate analogue, and with explicit types each type’s assignment can change only among its listed colleges. A type’s mass remains a cohort of applicants, each receiving one placement; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

The regime I find credible is a large centralized admissions round with many applicants, bounded integer score bands, and a restricted set of recurring application rankings. Types group applicants with the same full preference order and score vector; if those combinations recur, \(N\) applicants can have \(N\gg\tau\). This grouping is faithful to the problem because preference and score are the information its assignment rule uses. The paper itself notes continuum-student work by Azevedo and Leshno, which supports the plausibility of this regime; its discussion distinguishes that work’s setting from the tied-score cutoff mechanism studied here.

The mirror covers the computation of the two extreme score-stable cutoffs and their enrollments. Per-capita quotas are the natural co-modification when population is normalized to mass one. The weakest point is that the paper already has a polynomial algorithm in the individual applicant count, so this mirror does not promise a discrete hardness collapse. Its value is the exact high-multiplicity formulation and the question whether the extreme solutions can be found in time polynomial in the number of distinct types and the bit length of their masses and quotas. A natural follow-up is whether the paper’s lattice results for stable score vectors carry over under rational cohort masses and capacities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about yield, not whether the mirror is well posed. The proposed instance has a finite list of complete applicant types, each with a rational mass. Run the paper’s score updates on those types: each college’s enrollment is a sum of type masses, and each type’s assignment moves through at most \(m\) colleges. That gives the same monotone argument with roughly \(O(m^2\tau)\) or \(O(m\tau)\) update bounds, plus polynomial bit complexity for the rational sums and comparisons. The result looks like a compressed execution of the paper’s algorithm, rather than a new use of continuous optimization.

That makes this a relatively low-yield anchor for a programme looking for a new complexity landscape. But it does not defeat it. Theorem 7 is an explicit algorithmic runtime result, and Theorems 5 and 6 give the extremal guarantees. The type-compressed version asks for exact computation in the support size and input bit length, a genuine high-multiplicity question. A large admissions round with recurring preference and score profiles is plausible, and the per-capita quota is a natural scaling of capacity.

The cited continuum-matching work does not close the gap: Azevedo and Leshno study stable matching for a continuum of students through cutoff and supply-demand analysis, with comparative statics and large-market results; that is useful background, but it does not answer the computational question for the paper’s tied-score cutoff rule. ([Azevedo and Leshno](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/687476))

So I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case here. The mirror’s likely algorithm is easy to anticipate, which weakens its novelty, but “the answer may be a direct extension” is not a valid reason to reject a well-posed high-multiplicity computation. This anchor survives.
