The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a continuous extension of the paper’s stability question, but the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for a ChoCo anchor. Its closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: if every agent’s preference has a substitutable sub-preference, then a pairwise stable allocation exists. Theorem 2, also proved here, shows that if one hospital’s preference is not pseudo-substitutable, there is a market with another hospital and two doctors, all other agents having pseudo-substitutable preferences, in which no pairwise stable allocation exists. Corollary 1 states the resulting maximality of the pseudo-substitutable domain. These are existence and domain results, not algorithmic or complexity results. So none qualifies as an anchor under your rule, and there is no paper-grounded basis for assigning a continuous problem to the tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hard class.

The nearest positive lead is a population version of Theorem 1, which is a structural existence result rather than a valid computational anchor. A plausible regime would be a large medical labor market with many resident physicians and hospital service units, where many agents share the same complete preferences, contract terms, and feasible bundles. The number of agents could be very large while the number of distinct preference types stays modest.

One precise extension would take finite doctor and hospital type sets, masses for each type, and a finite set of contract classes linking types. A type specifies a strict ranking over feasible bundles of contract classes, including the relevant capacity and unitary constraints. Let \(z_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\). A solution must assign all mass, balance the mass of each contract class on its two sides, and be individually rational. It must also have no blocking contract: for no contract class \(x\) may there be positive mass on both sides assigned bundles \(B,C\) that omit \(x\) and for which both types choose \(x\) when offered it alongside their current bundle. The question is to decide whether such a \(z\) exists and, if so, return one. Individuals still receive discrete bundles; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

This is recognizable as a population version of their pairwise-stability problem: it preserves their deviation concept and makes the society, rather than the bundles, continuous. For rational masses in a model that exactly reproduces the paper’s finite markets by cloning agents, Theorem 1 gives a route to existence on the pseudo-substitutable slice. Extending the result to a general type-mass model, and determining the computational complexity of finding \(z\), would require new work. The paper does not establish either a complexity bound or a hardness result for that question.

The weakest point is therefore also decisive for the formal case: this is a credible continuous existence extension, but it is not a mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is also the cleanest: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves existence of a pairwise stable allocation on the pseudo-substitutable domain. Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 characterize how far that existence guarantee extends. Propositions 1–3 establish structural properties of preferences and stable sets. None gives a complexity bound, an algorithm, or a computational problem with a stated input and output. So the proposed type-mass question may be interesting, but it cannot anchor a continuous *computational mirror* of a result in this paper.

The medical-market scenario is plausible. Large cohorts can share preferences and contract terms, and a model with type masses \(\mu_t\) and assignment variables \(z_{t,B}\) can ask for individually rational assignments with no blocking type pair. That is a sensible neighboring model; I would not reject it simply because the population is continuous or because bundles remain integral for individuals.

The difficulty is preserving what the paper’s results concern. A type here must capture an agent’s complete preferences over feasible sets of contracts, and a contract identifies particular parties. If contract classes replace counterpart identities, then the model must specify how the original restriction—at most one contract with each counterpart—works when many counterparts share a class. Bundles over classes can silently change that feasible-set structure. If identities remain explicit, the preference and bundle descriptions grow with the number of agents, undermining the finite-support compression. A carefully restricted type model can resolve this, but those restrictions define a new matching model; they are not supplied by the paper’s maximality theorem.

More decisively, even after fixing that model, asking whether a stable mass assignment exists or can be found is a new computational question. The paper’s existence proof does not establish its complexity, and its maximality result is not a hardness result. The continuous question might yield worthwhile research, but any complexity classification would be a new contribution rather than a result carried over or sharpened from this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated criterion: no proposed anchor is a computational result in the paper. It is weaker as a universal claim that no continuous matching project could be worthwhile. The type-mass extension is coherent enough to pursue as adjacent theory; what it lacks is a paper-grounded computational anchor.
