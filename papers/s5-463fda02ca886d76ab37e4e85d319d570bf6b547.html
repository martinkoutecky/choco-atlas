The anchor — hardness

Theorem 5.2 (NPhardness)

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite conflict graph \(G=(O,E)\), rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_o\ge 0\), and finitely many types \(a\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\) and nonnegative additive valuations \(v_a\), decide whether masses \(x_{a,S}\ge 0\) exist for independent schedules \(S\subseteq O\). Require \(\sum_S x_{a,S}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_{a,S:o\in S}x_{a,S}\le\beta_o\), and, for each type \(a\), every pair of schedules \(S,T\) with positive mass to satisfy either \(T=\emptyset\) or \(v_a(S)\ge v_a(T\setminus\{o\})\) for some \(o\in T\). Require mass maximality as well: if capacity for \(o\) is slack, every positive-mass schedule contains \(o\) or a neighbor of \(o\). The anchor-aligned restriction has one type.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic cohort with finitely many complete agent types, where each person receives an integral independent schedule, \(x_{a,S}\) is the mass assigned to schedule \(S\), capacities scale per capita, and feasibility requires type-wise EF1 and no compatible positive-mass schedule when capacity is slack.

The objection that survived

A finite instance can have one unused seat, giving per-capita slack \(1/N\) that vanishes as \(N\) grows; mass maximality may therefore identify sequences distinguished by the paper's itemwise maximality.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the NP-hard existence result, especially its identical nonnegative additive valuation regime; it leaves the two-agent algorithms, the path-graph \(\mathrm{EF}[1,1]\) result, and the tree result untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding feasibility for type masses, per-capita capacities, support-wise EF1, and mass maximality?
  • Does the discrete NP-hardness persist in this model, or does mixing over integral schedules make the existence question tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a per-capita version of the paper’s existence problem. Think of a large cohort of students choosing course schedules. Course sections are item types; an edge joins two sections that conflict in time. Every student receives an integral schedule, so their bundle is still an independent set. Students with the same preferences and eligibility form one type. The population is represented by type masses \(\mu_a\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\), and each course type \(o\) has a rational seat supply \(\beta_o\) per student. As the cohort grows, seat supply scales with it. A large cohort may have \(N\) students but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with \(N\gg\tau\).

My anchor is Theorem 5.2 (NPhardness; proved here). It says that deciding whether a maximal EF1 allocation exists is NP-hard in each of these settings: three agents with identical monotone valuations; any fixed \(n\geq2\) with identical additive valuations; and any fixed \(n\geq4\) with identical nonnegative additive valuations. I rely most on the last case: the hardness holds for every fixed number \(n\geq4\), even when all agents have the same nonnegative additive valuation. The theorem is proved in this paper, via a reduction from Independent Set.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Per-Capita Conflict-Constrained EF1-Maximal Existence. An instance consists of a finite conflict graph \(G=(O,E)\), rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_o\geq0\), and finitely many agent types \(a\), each with rational mass \(\mu_a\) and a nonnegative additive valuation \(v_a\). A bundle is an independent set \(S\subseteq O\). Choose nonnegative masses \(x_{a,S}\), where \(x_{a,S}\) is the share of society of type \(a\) receiving bundle \(S\). Require
\[ \sum_{S}x_{a,S}=\mu_a \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{a,S:o\in S}x_{a,S}\leq\beta_o \]
for every type \(a\) and item type \(o\). Every two bundles \(S,T\) used by positive mass must satisfy EF1 for the first type’s valuation: either \(T=\emptyset\), or there is an \(o\in T\) with
\[ v_a(S)\geq v_a(T\setminus\{o\}). \]
Finally, if course type \(o\) has unused supply, every bundle used by positive mass must already contain \(o\) or contain a course conflicting with \(o\). Thus no remaining seat can be added to any student’s schedule. The question is whether such a distribution \(x\) exists; the solution is the distribution itself. This is an existence decision problem, not an optimization problem. For the anchor-aligned subproblem, take one agent type with an identical nonnegative additive valuation. The natural complexity parameters are \(|O|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\); the bundle set can be exponential.

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the anchor’s reduction places its combinatorics in the conflict graph and the independent sets that remain the feasible bundles, not in a large number of agents. Continuous mass lets us mix among integral schedules, but it does not make an individual schedule fractional. A likely bottleneck is pricing over those schedules, which includes maximum-weight independent set on general graphs. That is a reason to investigate hardness, not a proof that this continuous existence problem is hard.

The authors should recognize the mirror: it keeps their conflict-feasible bundles, EF1, maximality, and identical-additive goods regime, while interpreting recipients as a large cohort and supplies as scaling with population. This case covers the existence-hardness result, not the paper’s two-agent algorithms, its path-graph EF\([1,1]\) result, or its tree result.

The weak point is the supply model. The paper allocates a finite set of named items, each at most once; the mirror gives each item type a per-capita supply of seats. That change is natural for course sections and necessary for a growing population to receive nonempty bundles, but it is a real change to the allocation model. The central bridge question is whether mixing over schedules erases the paper’s hardness or preserves it. That is exactly what the proposed mirror makes precise.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to the anchor is that maximality does not pass cleanly to an atomless population. The paper’s condition is itemwise: if an item is unallocated, no agent can add it. In the proposed model, an unused share of course capacity can be assigned to an arbitrarily small positive mass of compatible students. Thus maximality becomes a condition on every bundle used by positive mass, rather than on every individual schedule.

That change can matter even along a natural high-multiplicity sequence. If a cohort has \(N\) students and one seat remains unused, its per-capita slack is \(1/N\). The seat is addable in every finite instance, but the slack tends to zero. At the limit, the capacity may be fully used, and maximality imposes no condition for that course. The mirror can therefore lose distinctions the paper’s theorem treats as consequential.

A more faithful repair would keep a smallest assignable mass, such as \(1/N\), so one remaining seat still matters. But that retains the population scale the continuum was meant to remove. Alternatively, one can adopt “no positive mass can be assigned another item” as the efficiency notion. That is coherent, but it is a new capacity-constrained fair-scheduling problem; it does not preserve the paper’s exact itemwise maximality. Changing type spaces or keeping each student’s bundle integral does not resolve this issue: the gap is between a single unused item and a vanishing share of population.

This does not defeat the anchor outright. The paper does contain a computational result to mirror: its NP-hardness theorem for deciding whether a maximal EF1 allocation exists. The cohort setting is plausible, the identical-valuation case is compatible with a single agent type, and per-capita supplies are a natural way to scale course seats. The proposed model also keeps each student’s bundle integral, so it does not reduce EF1 to comparisons of average bundles.

So the negative case identifies a real semantic break in the efficiency condition, but it does not show that authors would reject the mass-based version or that the resulting question is unworthy. Calling this a decisive failure would overstate the evidence. The honest negative case is weak: the maximality caveat remains, but this is a strong candidate for a continuous mirror.
