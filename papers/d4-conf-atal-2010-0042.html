The strongest positive angle I can defend is an unanchored research question about the paper’s continuous double auction. Consider a large prediction market where many accounts are instances of a finite catalog of agent profiles. A type records everything relevant to an agent’s behavior: its beliefs about the event outcomes, risk and return preferences, budget, intention, information source, and order schedule. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\). Millions of accounts deployed from hundreds or thousands of fixed profiles would make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The contracts and outcomes remain finite; the continuous object is the population.

One possible problem is CDA Mass Execution. An instance gives a finite set of contracts, rational type masses, an opening order book, and a finite sequence of type-specific buy and sell orders. Each order specifies a contract, side, limit price, and per-agent quantity; when a type submits it, the order book receives mass \(\mu_t\) times that quantity. Under a fixed published price and priority rule, the question is to compute the execution masses between orders, their transaction prices, and the residual book. The variables are the rational execution masses \(x_{ab}\); they must respect order quantities and prices and replay the auction’s matching priority, allowing partial fills. The market’s resulting quotes or last-trade prices are the output. This covers the paper’s CDA mechanism, not its opinion-extraction pipeline or Oscar-prediction experiments. A solver would likely find a polynomial replay algorithm in the number of order templates, contracts, types, and input bit length: a tentative Class A question, not a hardness-transfer claim.

The paper describes CDA orders and matching, so its authors could recognize this as an algorithmic subproblem of their market architecture. But it is not a theorem they prove, nor does it formalize the computational cost of matching as a research question. The mirror’s weakest point is that exact type-level order schedules and cohort arrivals may smooth away the individual timing and strategic variation central to continuous trading. If those distinctions must be preserved, the number of types can approach the number of agents. A synchronized cohort market is a plausible co-modification, but it makes this a modest mechanism-computation question rather than a mirror of the paper’s main empirical claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma or corollary asserting a computational result. Its contribution is an IAM architecture and simulations comparing how well market prices predict Oscar winners. The winning outcome is external ground truth; the market estimates it rather than choosing it. There is no complexity result in the paper to mirror.

CDA Mass Execution is a coherent subproblem if a large market has many agents following a finite catalog of order templates. But with the book, template sequence and priority rule fixed, execution is a deterministic replay: match the best compatible orders, subtract the smaller mass and continue. Each match exhausts at least one order, so the number of match steps is linear in the number of orders; sorting and rational arithmetic remain polynomial in the input size. The question may be useful for implementing the market, but it does not expose the kind of computational challenge the programme is looking for. The continuous model replaces repeated identical orders with mass and leaves the matching computation essentially unchanged.

A stronger mirror might ask how much population mass must change to move a quoted price or reverse the market’s forecast, or how a market designer should allocate mass across agent types to improve accuracy. Those could become substantial optimization problems. But the paper defines no such intervention, cost model or objective: its agents’ information processing and trading behavior are prescribed, and its accuracy claims are empirical. Adding an adversary or designer would create a new strategic market problem rather than continue a computational question the paper poses. A continuum model of aggregate market dynamics would be another possible direction, but it would be an analytic market model, not the missing complexity analysis of this paper.

So the offered execution question does not survive as a worthwhile mirror of the paper, and the paper supplies no computational result anchor. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that no continuous-population market problem could be worthwhile: price robustness or market design may merit study in their own right. The case here is that those would be adjacent projects, not mirrors of this paper.
