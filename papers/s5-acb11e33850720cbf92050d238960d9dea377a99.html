What the mirror covers

The candidate covers only Proposition 2’s aggregation rule; Propositions 1 and 3 and the domain characterizations receive no computational coverage.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational result in this paper. Proposition 1, proved here, characterizes the domain and rule induced by an even-sized strategy-proof, tops-only, anonymous, unanimous social choice function under richness. Proposition 2, also proved here, shows that a semilattice single-peaked domain admits such a function for every population size: it chooses the supremum of the distinct top-ranked alternatives. Proposition 3 gives an odd-population extension under an additional invariance condition. None classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class; the paper contains no qualifying anchor.

The closest positive seed is Proposition 2’s aggregation rule. For a finite semilattice of alternatives and a finite set of preference types, let each type be a complete ranking and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of society. A natural continuous extension chooses the semilattice supremum of the top alternatives of all types with positive mass. This fits a plausible setting such as a large electorate with relatively few ballot types, and it extends the proposition’s rule from a finite list of voters to a distribution.

But that extension is not a continuous computational mirror of a named result. In an atomless society, one person’s report does not change the distribution, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous; and the paper supplies no optimization objective or complexity question for this rule. The positive case therefore has a recognizable aggregate-rule analogue, but no anchor that meets the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is unusually direct: the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–3 characterize which preference domains and social choice functions fit stated axioms. They give no computational problem, input encoding, objective, algorithm, or complexity bound. A social choice function being a map does not itself make its characterization a computational result.

Proposition 2 is the closest seed. For a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many preference types, one could define
\[ F(\mu)=\sup_{\nu}\{t(R):\mu_R>0\}. \]
That is a sensible high-multiplicity extension of the rule, but it replaces a finite list of voters with the support of a distribution. The actual masses do not affect the result. If the semilattice operation is available, computing \(F\) takes at most \(\tau-1\) successive suprema. If the alternatives or semilattice are represented implicitly and those operations are hard, the paper gives no representation or result that would make this a computational mirror of Proposition 2.

The more ambitious version exposes a further mismatch. In an atomless society, one person’s report changes the distribution on a set of measure zero, so the paper’s individual strategy-proofness condition becomes vacuous. A nontrivial question about manipulating the outcome would need to let a positive-mass group change types, and specify costs or a robustness objective. That could be a worthwhile new control problem, but none of the paper’s propositions answers or constrains it. For a rule depending only on support, even a tiny positive cohort can change the outcome; under mass-proportional costs, introducing a favorable type can therefore have zero infimum cost unless the model adds a threshold or fixed cost.

So none of the proponent’s proposed anchors survives as a continuous *computational* mirror. This does not prove that no adjacent computational project could be worthwhile; such a project could be designed by adding a new objective and model. The negative case is strongest—and honest—at the paper’s boundary: its results concern axiomatic existence and characterization, not computation.
