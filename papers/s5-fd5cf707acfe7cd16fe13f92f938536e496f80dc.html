The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many site types \(t\), each with \(n_t\) samples per site and rational population share \(\mu_t\), and positive parameters \(\mu_e,\sigma^2\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to one macroscopic federation; the remaining mass learns locally. What choice minimizes limiting weighted error per site, \(\sum_t[(\mu_t-x_t)\mu_e+x_t n_t\sigma^2]\)?

The model it lives in

A continuous population of interchangeable learning sites, with type \(t\) carrying mass \(\mu_t\) and each site retaining its integral sample bundle \(n_t\). Assign mass \(x_t\) of each type to one federation whose total sample supply scales with its population; assign the rest to local learning. Minimize \(\sum_t[(\mu_t-x_t)\mu_e+x_t n_t\sigma^2]\), the limiting weighted error per site.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 1’s optimal-partition calculation in the large-population limit. It leaves aside the finite-coalition corrections and the paper’s stability, core-stability, and Price of Anarchy results, including Theorem 2.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove convergence of the normalized finite-population optimum to the mass-allocation optimum when \(M_t/M\to\mu_t\), including finite-size effects at types with \(n_t\sigma^2=\mu_e\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s optimal coalition partition result, in a regime with many interchangeable learning sites and few sample-size classes. Think of a large network of clinics or schools doing the same mean-estimation task. Each site has a fixed number \(n_t\) of local samples, and all sites face the paper’s common noise and heterogeneity parameters \(\mu_e\) and \(\sigma^2\). A type is this complete description for the model: sites with the same \(n_t\) are interchangeable. If there are \(M\) sites, type \(t\) has fraction \(\mu_t\) of them, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); the regime is \(M\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of sample-size types. The individual sites remain the agents choosing whether to learn locally or federate.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that an optimal partition can be found by starting with local learning, adding players to a federation in ascending order of sample size, and stopping when the next player would increase its error by joining; the resulting partition minimizes the paper’s total weighted error. The theorem presents an efficient constructive algorithm, though it does not label the problem “in P.” Sorting and checking the joining condition gives a polynomial-time algorithm on the explicit list of players. This is the paper’s best anchor for a continuous mirror. The paper gives no named hardness result to anchor instead.

Here is a continuous version of that underlying optimization problem. The input is a finite support of types \(t\), each with positive integer sample count \(n_t\) and rational population share \(\mu_t\), together with positive rational parameters \(\mu_e,\sigma^2\). Choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to federation; the remaining mass learns locally. The federated mass forms one coalition. The objective is to minimize the limiting weighted error per site:

\[ \min_{0\le x_t\le \mu_t} \sum_t\left[(\mu_t-x_t)\mu_e+x_t n_t\sigma^2\right]. \]

The solution is the vector \(x\); its objective is the society’s cost. This is the large-population limit of the paper’s error model for macroscopic federations. If type mass \(x_t\) represents \(M x_t+o(M)\) sites in a federation, its total sample count grows proportionally to \(M\). In Equation 2, the sampling-noise term then vanishes, and a member’s expected error tends to \(\sigma^2\). A local learner of type \(t\) still has error \(\mu_e/n_t\), so its contribution to the paper’s sample-weighted objective is \(\mu_e\) per site; a federated member contributes \(n_t\sigma^2\). This produces the objective above. A solution can be computed with \(\tau\) comparisons: federate all of type \(t\) when \(n_t\sigma^2<\mu_e\), keep it local when \(n_t\sigma^2>\mu_e\), and either choice is optimal at equality. The expected outcome is tractable. The cutoff is the limiting form of Theorem 1’s rule: small-sample types join the growing federation, while types whose federation error would exceed their local error stay local.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous, high-multiplicity version of their weighted-error partition problem. It retains the sites, federation coalitions, their sample-weighted errors, and the choice between local and joint learning. The co-modification is that coalition sizes and their sample supply scale with the population, while the input records type shares instead of naming every site. The paper’s own model already lets sample counts vary and makes them central to the optimum.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: Theorem 1’s optimal-arrangement problem only. It does not mirror the paper’s results on individual stability or its Price of Anarchy bound, which depend on deviations by particular agents.

The main weakness is that this limit removes finite-coalition effects. In particular, it loses the finite-size corrections in Equation 2 and restricts attention to federations with positive limiting mass; it does not represent coalitions whose size stays bounded as \(M\) grows. That makes the continuous optimization much simpler than the finite theorem’s problem. A natural follow-up is to prove convergence rates from the discrete optimum to the cutoff solution, including what happens at the threshold \(n_t\sigma^2=\mu_e\), and to study whether limits that retain smaller coalitions preserve any of the theorem’s richer structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed \(x_t\) model is too narrow to stand for the whole partition problem. It places each type’s mass either in local learning or in one federation of positive limiting mass. The finite model also permits many separate coalitions, and its cost includes a \(\mu_e\) term for every coalition. For bounded-size groups, that term remains relevant per site; in the proposed macroscopic federation it disappears after normalization. So the proposed objective captures a leading-order regime, not the finite-coalition corrections or every way a high-multiplicity society could be partitioned.

That objection does not defeat Theorem 1’s anchor, though. The theorem itself says an optimal finite partition has a particularly simple form: a size-ordered federation and local learners. With finitely many sample-size types, the proposed mass cutoff is a credible limit of that result. And there is a stronger formulation if retaining finite coalitions matters: let \(k\in\mathbb{N}^{\tau}\) describe a coalition’s counts by type, and let \(z_k\) be its number per unit population. With \(N(k)=\sum_t k_t n_t\), its cost is
\[ F(k)=\mu_e+\sigma^2 N(k)-\sigma^2\frac{\sum_t k_t n_t^2}{N(k)}. \]
The constraints \(\sum_k k_t z_k=\mu_t\) and objective \(\sum_k z_kF(k)\) give a type-mass configuration model. It keeps agents grouped in discrete coalitions while making the population of each type continuous. That is a natural high-multiplicity optimization question, and its pricing or limiting behavior could be studied computationally.

The remaining skeptical point is about payoff: the paper already gives an efficient algorithm, and its type-compressed version appears to inherit the same cutoff structure. The continuous problem may therefore add little algorithmically. But under the programme’s stated standard, a simple or already tractable answer does not make a well-posed mirror unworthy. The clinic and school scenarios are plausible, and sample count is a complete type for the paper’s expected-error model.

So I can challenge the proponent’s particular formulation as incomplete, but I cannot honestly defeat the stronger mirror. Theorem 1 survives as a worthwhile continuous-population question; the case against a universal mirror is weak.
