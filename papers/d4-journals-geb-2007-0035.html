I can’t make an honest continuous-mirror case anchored in this paper’s results: it contains no named computational result of the kind your criteria require. Theorem 1 says every implementable outcome function can be implemented with direct, truthful messages. Theorem 5 says that, under evidentiary normality, any outcome function implementable by a dynamic mechanism is also implementable by a static one. Theorem 6 says any dynamic implementation can be replaced by a special three-stage mechanism with truthful reporting in equilibrium, with a simplification under complete information. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its Theorems 1, 5 and 6 concern which mechanisms and equilibria suffice for implementation. The model specifies finite players, states, evidence constraints and preferences, but no encoded family of computational instances, complexity measure, or algorithmic task. A continuous version could be mathematically interesting, but it would not fill the programme’s computational gap as posed.

The best repair would replicate the strategic parties into a population of evidence types and ask whether direct truthful reporting, static mechanisms, or the three-stage construction still suffice for an aggregate outcome. That changes the strategic problem. If the public outcome depends on the distribution of reports and evidence, an individual in a nonatomic population has measure zero, so their deviation leaves that outcome unchanged. The incentive tests that drive the finite-player results then become vacuous. Treating each type group as a bloc whose deviation moves its whole mass restores strategic force by changing the equilibrium concept; keeping pivotal parties as atoms retains the finite-player structure. Individualized outcomes could preserve pointwise incentives, but that leads to a new continuum mechanism-design model with its own outcome and measurability choices.

Theorem 1’s strongest mirror would be a direct-revelation result for continuum agents with hard evidence. Such a result may be worth proving, but it remains a structural characterization; the paper supplies no computational objective or input representation for it. Theorem 5’s static-versus-dynamic question has the same problem: in an aggregate-only nonatomic model, static sufficiency may follow simply because individual deviations cannot move the outcome. In a model with individualized consequences, the question may be substantive, but it is a new implementation theorem rather than a computational mirror. Theorem 6 is more tied to finite players: its construction uses a player-by-player order of evidence moves and selects the first player whose evidence conflicts with their report. A mass distribution does not supply that ordering or selection rule; adding one would be part of designing a new model.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit: none of the raised theorems is a computational claim about a population input, and the most natural continuum extensions change the strategic object or remain analytic. The broader claim that no continuum implementation project could be worthwhile is weaker; the paper does not establish that.
