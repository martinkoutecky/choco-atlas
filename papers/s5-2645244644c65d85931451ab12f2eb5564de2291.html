The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive construction I can defend is a population version of the paper’s news aggregation question. But the required anchor condition rules out a fully compliant positive case: the paper contains no named computational result—no theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, hard, FPT, or otherwise. Its numbered propositions establish learning and polarization claims, not computational complexity.

The nearest substantive result is Proposition 9, proved here. It says that for a society with echo chambers \(e\) and information quality \(q\), if long-run polarization is positive and \(\bar q_{\mathrm{LR}}=\max_i q_{\mathrm{LR}}(e_i,\gamma,\hat\gamma)\), then replacing signals with the paper’s block-majority aggregator makes long-run polarization zero whenever the odd block size \(M\) satisfies
\[ M>-\frac{2\ln(1-\bar q_{\mathrm{LR}})}{(2q-1)^2}. \]
That is a useful anchor for a *research direction*, but it is not the kind of computational anchor your instructions require.

Here is the concrete mirror I would propose. A type is a normal news consumer’s echo chamber \(e=(d_A,d_B,n)\): the counts of A-dogmatic, B-dogmatic, and normal friends. The instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with binary-rational masses, together with common rational parameters \(q,\gamma,\hat\gamma\), where \(\hat\gamma<\gamma\). In a large platform or media audience, many consumers could share each exposure type, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types remains moderate. The action is a common odd aggregation window \(M\), using the paper’s block-majority aggregator; the objective is to find the smallest \(M\) for which every type with positive mass learns the true state almost surely. Equivalently, for each type \(e\), the aggregator’s induced signal quality must exceed \(q_{\mathrm{LR}}(e,\gamma,\hat\gamma)\), the paper’s long-run threshold. A solution is that minimum \(M\), or a certificate that a proposed \(M\) succeeds for every supported type.

I would expect this finite-support window-selection problem to be tractable: the constraint is monotone in \(M\), the typewise thresholds are computed independently, and Proposition 9 gives an explicit sufficient bound. Further questions include heterogeneous aggregation costs by type and whether those costs make the optimal policy hard. The mirror covers the paper’s aggregation intervention and resulting long-run learning, not its full account of short-run belief distortion, endogenous sharing, or climate opinions.

The case’s strength is that a distribution over echo-chamber types fits the paper’s own objects, and its long-run learning result makes the aggregation objective precise. Its weakness is decisive for this assignment: Proposition 9 is an analytic threshold result, not a computational result. Optimizing \(M\) reframes the paper’s policy question as an algorithmic one, and the authors might accept the connection, but I cannot honestly present it as a required named complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is at the required anchor gate: this paper states no named computational result. Its propositions characterize learning, polarization, and the effects of aggregation; none gives the complexity of a computational problem or an algorithmic classification. Proposition 9 is the closest candidate, but it proves a sufficient analytic bound on an aggregation window \(M\). It does not define an optimization problem or analyze its computational complexity.

The proposed continuous society over echo-chamber types is plausible: a large audience could contain many consumers with the same \(e=(d_A,d_B,n)\). But for the proposed goal—finding one \(M\) that makes every supported type learn correctly—the masses \(\mu_e\) do not enter. Only the support and the largest type-specific threshold matter. A mass-weighted objective, such as choosing aggregation to make at least \(1-\varepsilon\) of the audience learn correctly, is a better population question. Yet it adds a planner’s objective and an intervention choice to Proposition 9; it is a new optimization problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper. The same is true of adding type-specific aggregation costs or budgets.

That is the fundamental limit of the mirror: the paper studies beliefs in a stochastic learning model, not the computational complexity of a social choice problem. Its appendix adds a policy vote, but its results there concern equilibrium sharing and gridlock, not computational questions. A continuous distribution can represent the audience and summarize polarization; making it the input to a computational task requires adding a separate algorithmic objective and decision problem.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated scope: none of the proponent’s anchors meets the requirement of a named computational result, and making Proposition 9 algorithmic requires extending the paper. The case is weaker against the broader claim that a continuous, algorithmic follow-up on information aggregation could be worthwhile. Large populations with repeated echo-chamber types are a sensible regime; that possibility supports a new project, even though it does not supply the required mirror here.
