The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a finite antichain \(\mathcal V\subseteq\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) defining a coalition profile \(q\) as losing exactly when \(q\le v\) for some \(v\in\mathcal V\), take two losing input coalitions encoded by \(\lambda_{t,\sigma}\), the mass of type \(t\) appearing in exactly \(\sigma\subseteq\{1,2\}\), with \(\sum_{\sigma}\lambda_{t,\sigma}=\mu_t\). Decide whether there are rational masses \(z_{t,\sigma,A}\ge0\), for \(A\subseteq\{1,2\}\) with \(|A|=|\sigma|\), satisfying \(\sum_{A:\,|A|=|\sigma|}z_{t,\sigma,A}=\lambda_{t,\sigma}\), such that the output profiles \(q^i_t=\sum_{\sigma,A:\,i\in A}z_{t,\sigma,A}\) are both winning.

The model it lives in

An atomless society has types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\); a monotone simple game is represented by its finite list \(\mathcal V\) of maximal losing mass profiles. Given two losing coalitions, decide whether their type-mass slices can be reassigned to two winning coalitions while preserving each participant's number of appearances.

The objection that survived

The finite antichain \(\mathcal V\) is a representation choice supplied by the mirror, and many natural continuous winning boundaries may not have this form; this limits the mirror's domain but not the stated problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 2.3's \((\mathcal L^M,\mathcal L)\) 2-trade case and Proposition 2.5's parameterized analogue with \(K=|\mathcal V|\); it leaves the other representation cases, fixed-\(j\) results, and open problems unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the complexity of this rational feasibility problem in \(\tau=|T|\), \(K=|\mathcal V|\), and input bit length \(L\), including whether it is NP-hard.
  • Determine whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(K\), with running time \(f(K)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is a continuous two-trade problem. Picture a large federation whose members fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of rule-relevant roles: members of the same type have the same voting power, eligibility, and effect on whether a coalition wins. There may be millions of members but only dozens or hundreds of types. A coalition is described by the mass it draws from each type. The decision is whether two losing coalitions can be reorganized into two winning ones while preserving every member’s total number of appearances across the pair.

Here is a finite input and certificate for that question. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\), and let \(\mathcal V\) be a finite antichain of rational vectors \(v\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), representing maximal losing mass profiles. A profile \(q\) loses exactly when \(q\le v\) coordinatewise for some \(v\in\mathcal V\); equivalently, \(q\) wins when, for every \(v\in\mathcal V\), some type \(t\) has \(q_t>v_t\). Give two losing coalitions by \(\lambda_{t,\sigma}\): the mass of type \(t\) whose members belong to exactly the input coalitions in \(\sigma\subseteq\{1,2\}\). The input consists of these rational masses and \(\mathcal V\).

Ask whether there are nonnegative rational masses \(z_{t,\sigma,A}\), where \(A\subseteq\{1,2\}\) is the pair of output coalitions a mass slice joins, such that
\[ \sum_{A:\,|A|=|\sigma|}z_{t,\sigma,A}=\lambda_{t,\sigma} \quad\text{and}\quad q^i_t=\sum_{\sigma,A:\,i\in A}z_{t,\sigma,A} \]
for \(i\in\{1,2\}\), with both \(q^1\) and \(q^2\) winning. The equal-cardinality constraint preserves each member’s number of appearances; because each type is an atomless cohort, the mass slices can be assigned to actual members. A solution is such an allocation \(z\). This keeps the paper’s two losing coalitions, two replacement winners, monotone winning condition, and conservation rule. It makes the population continuous while allowing the winning boundary to be described by mass profiles.

My lead anchor is Proposition 2.3, proved in this paper. It says that \(\OTR{\alpha}{\beta}\) is NP-complete when \(\alpha(\Gamma)=\mathcal L^M\) and \(\beta(\Gamma)=\mathcal L\): given a simple game by its maximal losing coalitions and two losing coalitions, decide whether there are two winning coalitions that complete a 2-trade, with every player appearing equally often on both sides. The authors prove membership in NP and reduce from SAT. For the continuous version above, I would expect the general problem to remain hard, possibly for continuum-specific reasons: each listed losing boundary imposes disjunctive constraints on the two replacement profiles. But that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of their reduction.

A second anchor is Proposition 2.5, also proved here, with an imported ingredient: the authors show that the same \((\mathcal L^M,\mathcal L)\) 2-trade problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(k=|\mathcal L^M|\), reducing it to SetSplitting and using the cited FPT result for that problem. Its continuous counterpart is the same decision question parameterized by \(K=|\mathcal V|\). I would investigate whether it is FPT in \(K\), with running time \(f(K)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. That is a plausible continuation of the paper’s parameterized result, but it needs a new argument.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: the two-trade, losing-to-winning case represented by maximal losing profiles. I am not claiming a mirror for the paper’s other representations, its fixed-\(j\) results, or its other complexity entries. The strongest point is that the central object being traded remains the same; only the population and coalition coordinates become mass-valued. The weakest point is substantial: the SAT reduction in Proposition 2.3 relies on indivisible literal players. In the natural profile lift, each literal role can be split between the two output coalitions; both may then receive positive mass of every literal, making the constructed coalitions win even when the formula is unsatisfiable. So the paper’s hardness result motivates this continuous question but does not settle it. That possible loss of hardness is precisely why the mirror is worth asking.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2.3 is the strongest target, but its NP-hardness is built from individually distinct literal players. Each literal carries part of the SAT assignment, and the trade has to distribute those players integrally between the output coalitions. In a genuinely high-multiplicity regime, identical participants must share a type; preserving the gadget’s distinctions makes the number of types grow with the formula. That weakens the case that the theorem itself points toward a useful continuous model.

The proponent’s stronger version repairs this weakness: a large federation with a few exchangeable roles, a finite list \(\mathcal V\) of maximal losing mass profiles, and atomless mass slices gives a coherent continuous two-trade question. I do not see a fundamental objection to it. The reservation is that \(\mathcal V\) carries the game’s entire winning structure. This is a carefully chosen language for continuous monotone games, rather than a limit supplied by the paper; many natural continuous boundaries would not have a finite list of maximal profiles. That makes the mirror’s scope representation-dependent, but does not make the stated version invalid.

Proposition 2.5 is a weaker anchor. Its FPT result reduces an indivisible SetSplitting instance to a partition of a finite universe. With atomless types, mass can be cut at arbitrary fractions, so that reduction does not transfer directly. Parameterizing by \(K=|\mathcal V|\) is coherent, but it asks about a new fractional feasibility problem whose input parameter measures the boundary description, not the population multiplicity. Again, that is a reason to doubt how much of the paper’s result is being mirrored, not a reason the question cannot be asked.

So the best negative case is about priority: the paper’s results are representation-sensitive certificates about finite simple games, and their link to high-multiplicity societies is less direct than the proposed framing suggests. But I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Large federations with repeated roles are plausible, and the proposed mass-profile model is a sensible object. Under the programme’s stated standard, that continuous two-trade question survives.
