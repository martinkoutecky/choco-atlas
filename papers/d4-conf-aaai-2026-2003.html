The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a committee bound \(k\), and \(\tau\) voter types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\) and rational ballots \(u_t:C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), candidates arrive in a uniformly random order. When \(c\) arrives, a policy observes \(u_t(c)\) for each type and irrevocably accepts or rejects \(c\), selecting at most \(k\) candidates. For \(W\subseteq C\), define \(\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_t\mu_t\log(1+\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c))\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}=\max_{|W|\le k}\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)\). Does there exist a policy running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length, such that \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)]\ge ((1-1/e)/7)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity online election with \(\tau\) complete cardinal-ballot types carrying masses \(\mu_t\). The policy's decisions are integral accept/reject choices for arriving candidates, the committee has size at most \(k\), and welfare is the mass-weighted Nash objective \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(1+\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c))\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6's Online Nash Rule guarantee. It does not address Theorems 1–5, the empirical results, or the offline Nash-welfare hardness cited from prior work.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the guarantee be implemented in bit-polynomial time for arbitrary binary-rational masses and ballots?
  • What approximation ratio is achievable as a function of \(\tau\)?
  • How does the guarantee change under other candidate-arrival models?

The case FOR (proponent)

I’d make a focused case around continuous-population online Nash-welfare selection, anchored by Theorem 6. Theorem 6, proved in this paper, says that Online Nash Rule returns a feasible committee whose expected Nash Welfare is at least \((1-1/e)/7\) of the offline maximum. The paper’s offline NP-hardness statement for Nash Welfare is attributed to Ramezani and Endriss (2009), rather than proved in a numbered result here, so I would not use it as an anchor or claim this mirror dissolves a hardness result.

The scenario is a large membership organization selecting a fixed-size slate from candidates shown in a randomized order. Members have cardinal evaluations, but many share the same complete evaluation profile. For example, a platform might have millions of members and a few dozen recurring preference types. This is a regime claim about that organization, not about hiring committees in general; the paper’s two-person committee example is too small to motivate a population limit.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. There are \(m\) candidates, a committee limit \(k\), and \(\tau\) voter types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a complete cardinal ballot \(u_t:C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\). Candidates arrive in a uniformly random order. When candidate \(c\) arrives, the policy sees \(u_t(c)\) for each type and must irrevocably accept or reject \(c\), selecting at most \(k\) candidates. For a committee \(W\), define continuous Nash Welfare by \(\mathrm{NW}_\mu(W)=\sum_t\mu_t\log(1+\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c))\), and let \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\max_{|W|\le k}\mathrm{NW}_\mu(W)\). The problem, Continuous-Population Online Nash-Welfare Selection, is to find a bit-polynomial online policy with \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NW}_\mu(W)]\ge (1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}_\mu/7\), where the expectation is over arrival order and any policy randomness. The policy’s solution is an online accept/reject rule; the committee remains integral.

This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. If a finite electorate has \(n_t\) members of each type and \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), then \(\mathrm{NW}_\mu(W)\) is exactly the paper’s Nash Welfare divided by \(n\). Thus the approximation ratio is unchanged, and grouping repeated ballots preserves the objective exactly. The population is what becomes continuous; candidates, arrival decisions, and committee slots do not. I’d expect the tractable side of the trichotomy: Online Nash Rule’s welfare calculations can be written as type-weighted sums, so the theorem’s argument appears to carry over. The natural follow-up questions are whether the guarantee holds for arbitrary rational masses in the standard bit model, what the best achievable ratio is as a function of \(\tau\), and how much the result depends on random arrival.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps cardinal ballots, irrevocable online selection, committee size, and the Nash objective intact. The paper also presents Online Nash Rule as a polynomial-time algorithm, so this is a meaningful algorithmic mirror rather than a fractional relaxation of the committee problem. My weakest point is that the paper’s result already gives an approximation for finite electorates. The continuous formulation may mainly provide a concise type-weighted representation and a clearer high-multiplicity regime, rather than expose a new complexity boundary. The required scenario also assumes a small set of complete, shared evaluation profiles and randomized candidate order, both of which need defending in an application. This case covers Theorem 6 only.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that its stated guarantee appears to transfer with almost no new proof. For rational masses, choose a common denominator \(N\) and set \(n_t=N\mu_t\). Then

\[ \mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_t n_t\log\!\left(1+\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c)\right). \]

The factor \(1/N\) changes neither committee comparisons nor the secretary rule’s choices, and the random-arrival argument is unchanged. The welfare calculations can also be performed by summing over the \(\tau\) types. So Theorem 6’s guarantee seems to yield the proposed continuous guarantee directly. That makes the anchor look incremental: it adds a compact representation, but no new algorithm or complexity boundary for that bound.

This does not defeat the mirror. Compact high-multiplicity inputs are part of ChoCo’s stated subject, and the large membership organization is a plausible setting for recurring evaluation types. The population remains the continuous object, the per-capita Nash objective is well-defined, and neither candidate decisions nor committee slots need to become fractional. A sharper question about bit complexity in \((m,\tau,L)\), the best ratio as \(\tau\) varies, or other arrival models could go beyond the direct transfer.

So the case against is weak: the advertised approximation guarantee may be an immediate corollary, but that alone does not make the continuous question unworthy. I can’t honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists for this anchor.
