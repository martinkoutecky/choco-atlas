The continuous mirror question

Given finite good classes \(G\), lexicographic types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and type valuations \(v_t\) over integral class-count bundles \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{|G|}\), determine whether a finite-support lottery over feasible mass allocations exists and, when it does, compute one. In each state \(\omega\), masses \(x^\omega_{t,B}\ge 0\) must satisfy \(\sum_B x^\omega_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x^\omega_{t,B}B_g=q_g\) for each \(g\in G\). Every state must be EFX across all occupied type-bundle pairs: if \(x^\omega_{t,B},x^\omega_{s,C}>0\), then \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(C-\mathbf e_g)\) for every class \(g\) with \(C_g>0\). Every state must also be Pareto optimal: there is no mass-preserving reassignment, represented by a coupling from assigned to new bundles, that weakly improves every unit of mass for its type and strictly improves a positive mass. Across the lottery, require \(\sum_\omega p_\omega\sum_B x^\omega_{t,B}v_t(B)/\mu_t\ge \frac{9}{10}\sum_\omega p_\omega\sum_C x^\omega_{s,C}v_t(C)/\mu_s\) for every pair \(t,s\). What is the existence and computational complexity of this problem in \(|G|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\), independently of the replica scale?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fair-allocation model with a distribution \(\mu\) over lexicographic preference types, integral bundles of interchangeable goods from fixed classes, and per-capita supply \(q_g\). The decision variables are lottery probabilities \(p_\omega\) and type-bundle masses \(x^\omega_{t,B}\); the task is feasibility, or equivalently finding any lottery that meets the supply, ex-ante fairness, ex-post EFX, and mass-Pareto-optimality conditions. It is an extension because goods and the allocation are represented by class counts and type masses.

The objection that survived

In a replica with \(K\) recipients, the construction can assign \(\Theta(K)\) goods to one recipient, leaving mass \(1/K\) on a bundle whose size grows with \(K\); thus its outputs need not converge to a finite-bundle mass lottery. This remains a concrete limit on transferring Theorem 2’s guarantee, even though the continuous problem can seek a different construction.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for every instance with lexicographic valuations, the authors give a polynomial-time algorithm that returns a lottery over allocations which is ex-ante \(\frac{9}{10}\)-EF and ex-post EFX and Pareto optimal. The paper has no named NP-hardness result to anchor on, so this algorithmic theorem is the best fit.

Consider a large population of recipients choosing among standardized indivisible goods. A type is a complete lexicographic valuation template; all recipients of that type have the same preferences and other relevant parameters. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a small set \(T\) of types. Supplies scale with population: for each good class \(g\), \(q_g\) is the number of indivisible copies available per unit of population. At any finite replica scale, each recipient still gets an integral bundle. Only the aggregate mass assigned to each type–bundle pair can be fractional.

Here is the continuous problem I would ask:

Continuous Lexicographic Best-of-Both-Worlds Allocation. Given finite good classes \(G\), type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and complete lexicographic valuation templates \(v_t\), find a finite-support lottery over mass allocations \(x^\omega_{t,B}\). In each state \(\omega\), \(x^\omega_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\). Require
\[ \sum_B x^\omega_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B}x^\omega_{t,B}B_g=q_g\quad\text{for every }g\in G. \]
Thus every recipient receives an integral bundle and every good is allocated; fractions describe only how much population receives each bundle.

For each state in the lottery’s support, every occupied pair of bundles must satisfy EFX: if type \(t\) receives \(B\) and type \(s\) receives \(C\), then \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(C\setminus\{g\})\) for every good copy \(g\in C\). The state must also be Pareto optimal: there is no feasible reassignment of the same population that weakly improves every recipient and strictly improves a positive mass of them. Across the lottery, require ex-ante \(\frac{9}{10}\)-EF for every pair of types:
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_B\frac{x_{t,B}}{\mu_t}v_t(B)\right] \;\ge\; \frac{9}{10}\, \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_C\frac{x_{s,C}}{\mu_s}v_t(C)\right]. \]
The computational question is whether such a lottery always exists and, if so, how to find one in time measured by \(|G|\), \(\tau=|T|\), the input bit length, and the representation of the valuation templates.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: for example, many households receiving standardized relief goods, with a modest number of recurring preference profiles and inventory growing with the recipient population. The unit of analysis is a fraction of households of a given type, not a named household. That makes the ex-ante fairness comparisons and per-capita supply constraints natural. It also keeps the paper’s core question intact: fairness and efficiency over indivisible bundles, with randomization across integral outcomes.

My initial expectation is Class A, but as a conjecture for the continuous problem, not a consequence of Theorem 2. A configuration LP would use type–bundle masses, with ex-ante fairness and supply constraints linear. The central algorithmic question is whether one can price or separate over states that are both EFX and Pareto optimal. The paper’s lexicographic structure and picking-sequence characterizations give a reason to look for such a method. The further questions are whether the \(\frac{9}{10}\) guarantee survives at arbitrary rational masses, whether it can be improved to EF, and whether the complexity can depend on \(\tau\) rather than the total replica population.

The scope is Theorem 2 alone. I would not claim this mirrors the paper’s monotone- or subadditive-valuation results, whose charity guarantees need additional modelling choices.

The weak point is the passage from named agents and distinct goods to types and scaled supplies. Cloning goods while preserving strict lexicographic preferences needs a precise convention, and Pareto optimality for a mass assignment must mean no improvement for any recipient, not merely no improvement in type averages. The continuous model above makes those choices explicit, but Theorem 2 does not itself establish the continuous guarantee or a polynomial-time algorithm in \((|G|,\tau,L)\). That unresolved transfer is the research question, and the paper’s authors should recognize it as a high-multiplicity form of their allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to the proposed mirror is a failure of the direct large-population limit when supplies scale with population. Take \(K\) copies of a fixed recipient population and scale the goods accordingly. In the paper’s Theorem 2 construction, each recipient first gets one good, then the entire remaining “tail” is assigned to one recipient in each outcome. If the supply is \(q>1\) goods per recipient, that tail can contain \(\Theta(K)\) goods. In the type-by-bundle description, its contribution is mass \(1/K\) at a bundle of size \(\Theta(K)\): the mass vanishes, but the goods it carries do not. Thus the construction has no ordinary limit as a distribution over finite bundles. Keeping supply fixed while \(K\) grows avoids this escape only by making supply per recipient vanish, so the resulting fairness question can become vacuous.

That is a real flaw in this particular translation, but it does not defeat a better mirror. One could allow a singular resource-bearing agent in the limit, or require bundles to remain bounded and study a diffuse allocation regime. The first adds an object with zero population mass to a model whose society is supposed to be the distribution over types; the second changes which outcomes are allowed. Neither choice is forced by the paper, and the second may still be a fair-division problem the authors would recognize. The tail behavior is also a feature of their construction, not evidence that every ex-post EFX+PO lottery must concentrate its goods this way.

The broader negative case is weak. Theorem 2 is a named polynomial-time result, and repeated recipient types with per-capita supplies are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. For rational type masses and supplies, scaling to a finite replica gives an instance of the paper; symmetrizing among identical recipients yields a natural type-level lottery. That establishes finite-instance existence, though it does not give the desired running time in the bit length of the masses. Finding an algorithm whose complexity depends on \(|G|\), \(\tau\), and input length rather than on the replica size remains a genuine computational question. I cannot honestly support the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
