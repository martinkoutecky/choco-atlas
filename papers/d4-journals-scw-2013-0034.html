What the mirror covers

The proposed mass model covers rationing allocations with baselines, claims, and per-capita endowment; the paper's axiom-preservation and characterization results, including Theorems 1–6, have no computational mirror here.

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible continuous population setting exists. Consider a large water district or public health system where people are grouped by identical per-person baseline \(b_t\) and claim \(c_t\). Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s share of the population and \(e\) the available resource per person. An allocation assigns each type an amount \(x_t\), with \(0\le x_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=e\). This is a natural mass version of the paper’s rationing model; scaling the endowment with population is part of that model, not a change of subject.

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: a rule satisfies baseline truncation, truncation of excessive claims, baseline invariance, and polar baseline self-duality if and only if it is obtained through the Baselines First extension operator. That is an axiomatic characterization of allocation rules. It does not assert that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper’s other numbered theorems likewise concern preservation or characterization of axioms.

So the positive case has a firm limit: the paper contains no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under the requested criteria. One could study how to compute allocations for a specified rule on a distribution of types, but that would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. Its axiomatic questions also fall outside ChoCo’s stated computational scope. The population setting is sensible; the paper does not give us an eligible anchor for a continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The water district is a credible high-multiplicity setting: a type can be a pair \((b_t,c_t)\), with mass \(\mu_t\), and the resource can scale per capita. That makes a continuous population model sensible. It does not, by itself, give this paper a computational result to mirror.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 5, but it characterizes which allocation rules satisfy four axioms. Theorem 6 gives a related characterization, and Theorems 1–4 concern preservation of axioms. None classifies the complexity of computing an allocation. In a mass model, the Baselines First formula still calls the chosen standard rule on transformed claims and endowments. The paper gives no computational representation of an arbitrary rule in that class, so there is no defined uniform algorithmic problem or input size to analyze.

A stronger proposed mirror would ask for the exact allocation on a finite-support distribution of \((b,c)\) types. That is coherent, but it adds a computation task rather than continuizing a result in the paper. For a fixed rule, the answer depends on how that rule is represented; for the whole axiomatically defined class, the paper supplies no finite representation to compute over. Turning the axioms into a search or verification problem would require choosing a new encoding and a new task.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated criterion: the population mirror is plausible, but the paper offers no named computational finding for it to mirror. Its weak point is that one could develop a worthwhile computational study of rationing rules independently; the paper’s results do not make that study a continuous mirror.
