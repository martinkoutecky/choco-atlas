What the mirror covers

No computational result is mirrored; the bloc construction repackages Theorem 1's analytical impossibility, and the other named results are analytical characterizations or incentive claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s discussion of collective decisions by groups such as states or parties. A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large electorate organized into constituencies whose members share the same cardinal utilities, with each constituency acting through a representative.

That yields a precise Mass-Bloc PE–BIC question. Let \(X\) be a finite set of alternatives, and let the society have \(\tau\) blocs with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A realized society is \(\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{u_t}\), where each bloc’s type \(u_t\in[0,1]^m\) is its members’ common payoff vector. Assume the blocs’ types have an absolutely continuous, full-support joint distribution. A direct mechanism is a Borel map \(F:([0,1]^m)^\tau\to\Delta(X)\). Each bloc representative reports its type; the mechanism chooses a lottery. Call \(F\) efficient if no other mechanism raises every bloc’s expected per-capita payoff and strictly raises one’s, and BIC if truthful reporting is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium for the bloc representatives. The question is whether a non-dictatorial efficient, BIC mechanism exists; a solution would be such a mechanism or a proof none exists.

The nearest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for finite \(I\) and \(X\), with \(|I|,|X|\ge2\), and a joint type distribution absolutely continuous with full support over \([0,1]^{mn}\), an SCF is ex ante Pareto efficient and BIC if and only if it is dictatorial. Applied to bloc representatives, the theorem says the Mass-Bloc question has a negative answer under the stated assumptions: every efficient, BIC mechanism is dictatorial. Weighting each bloc’s welfare by its positive mass does not change Pareto comparisons.

That is the best positive *population* interpretation I can make: mass represents the constituency size, and the paper’s own group-decision discussion gives it some plausibility. But it is not a valid computational anchor under the programme’s criteria. Theorem 1 is an analytical characterization, not a result about polynomial time, hardness, parameterized complexity, or an algorithm. Propositions 1 and 2 and Corollary 1 are also characterizations or incentive claims, not computational results. The paper has no named computational result of the required kind. The proposed Mass-Bloc problem is therefore a motivated extension, not a question anchored in a computational result of this paper; the prior also has no finite encoding here, so no complexity class follows.

The main weakness is the change in who acts. In this model a bloc representative controls the reports for a whole mass of people. If the mechanism instead gives each voter an individual report in a nonatomic population, one voter’s report has zero effect on the aggregate, and individual BIC can become vacuous. So the group scenario makes the extension plausible, but it does not establish that the paper’s individual-agent incentive question survives continuization. The mirror covers only Theorem 1’s efficiency–incentive impossibility, not a computational landscape, and it yields no A/B/C classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only serious anchor, and it has no computational problem to continuize. It characterizes all Borel social choice functions for a class of priors; it gives no finite input, algorithmic output, or complexity question. Its quantification over arbitrary mechanisms is especially important: to ask for an algorithm, one would first have to choose a finite representation of the prior and restrict the mechanism class. That could define a worthwhile mechanism-design project, but the choices would supply the project’s computational content; they are not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

The Mass-Bloc construction is the strongest population-based repair. It is coherent for constituencies whose members share cardinal utilities, and the paper’s discussion of groups gives it some motivation. But its strategic actors are bloc representatives, so it preserves the finite-player model while attaching masses to players. Those masses do no substantive work in Theorem 1: multiplying each bloc’s expected payoff by its positive mass preserves Pareto comparisons, and multiplying a representative’s utility by a positive constant preserves their incentives. The theorem transfers, but the population has not become the continuous object.

Making the individuals themselves nonatomic does put the population into the model, but an anonymous mechanism that depends on the distribution of reports cannot be changed by one person’s report. Individual BIC then loses the strategic tradeoff the theorem studies. A version with positive-mass blocs restores that tradeoff by making blocs the players; a version based on coordinated positive-mass deviations changes BIC into a coalition criterion. Either could motivate new research, but neither is the paper’s individual-agent theorem continued into a continuum.

So the negative case is strong at the programme’s actual gate: there is no named computational result here to mirror, and the most plausible continuization either leaves the population finite in strategic terms or changes the incentive concept. I would not claim that no interesting computational project could grow out of the paper. I would say that such a project would need to introduce its own encoded problem and computational results, rather than charting a complexity landscape already present in this paper.
