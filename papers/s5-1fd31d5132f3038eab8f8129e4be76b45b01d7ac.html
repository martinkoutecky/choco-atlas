What the mirror covers

The candidate analogue covers only Theorem 6.1's good-choice outcome guarantee; the paper's protocol and ranking procedure have no named computational result or complexity analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a real population-continuum regime for redistricting. Take a large state whose residents fall into a modest number of geographic and voting strata. A type records a resident’s district-relevant location and voting forecast; its mass is the fraction of the population in that stratum. A map assigns mass to \(n\) equal-population, geographically viable districts, and a party’s objective can be its additive rating of those districts, including the number it expects to win. Millions of residents across a relatively small number of recurring types is plausible here. The continuum is the population, not the map’s outcome space.

The nearest named result is Theorem 6.1 (Good Choice Property). The paper cites it from [6]; it does not prove it here. It says that for any voting model \(V\) and rating system \(R\), one of the choices offered by the protocol achieves an outcome at least as good for the party as its \(k\)-split geometric target. That is a useful fairness guarantee, but it is not a computational-complexity result: it establishes an outcome property, not that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The paper has no named result of the required computational kind, so it supplies no eligible anchor under your instructions.

If Theorem 6.1 were accepted as a weaker, non-complexity anchor, its continuous analogue would be a Continuous Good-Choice Attainment question. The instance would give a polygonal state with rational population density, a finite set of voter strata and their forecasts, a number \(n\) of districts, and a nested sequence of equal-mass geographic splits. Viable maps would partition the population into connected districts of mass \(1/n\). For party \(P\), let \(\mathcal V(S_k)\) be the viable maps respecting split \(S_k\), and define its target as
\[ G_P(S_k)=\frac{\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal V(S_k)}R_P(\pi)+\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal V(S_k)}R_P(\pi)}{2}. \]
The question is whether one of the protocol’s two assignments of map-drawing authority across the split yields a viable map \(\pi\) with \(R_P(\pi)\ge G_P(S_k)\); a solution is the assignment and map. This mirrors the paper’s guarantee, but the theorem does not establish it for nonatomic populations or provide an algorithm for finding the map. I would leave its computational classification open: the paper does not specify a finite encoding of the geometric constraints from which to infer one.

So the positive case is that a continuous population model is sensible and closely tracks the paper’s fairness question. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested format: there is no named computational result to anchor the proposed continuous problem to. The mirror is plausible as a research direction, but this paper cannot support the requested anchored complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper offers no computational result to continuize. Its only named theorem, Theorem 6.1 (the Good Choice Property), is an outcome guarantee, cited from [6]. It says one of two assignments of map-drawing authority gives a party a rating at least as high as a geometric target. It makes no claim about the complexity of finding a map, evaluating that target, or running the protocol.

The proposed continuous good-choice question has the same limitation. For a nonatomic population on a polygonal state, it can ask whether one of the two choices attains the midpoint of the best and worst ratings over viable maps. That is a reasonable fairness question, but it is still an existence guarantee, not a computational problem. The paper’s stated argument for the guarantee is an averaging identity; for a continuous version, the same argument applies whenever the two choices and their corresponding viable maps are defined. It does not, by itself, yield a new algorithmic question.

One could upgrade the proposal to ask for an efficient algorithm that constructs the map or computes the continuous target. But then the computational task depends on choices the paper does not make: how geography and density are encoded, what district boundaries are allowed, and how connectivity, equal population, and other constraints are checked. Those choices define a new optimization problem over geographic partitions; they are not supplied by Theorem 6.1. That could be worthwhile research, but this paper does not provide a computational result to anchor it.

I would not claim that continuous-population redistricting is inherently an insensible regime. Residents can plausibly be grouped by location and voting forecast, and real states have large populations. So the negative case is strong against this paper as a computational mirror, but weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The obstacle here is the paper’s noncomputational contribution, not a demonstrated failure of population continuization.
