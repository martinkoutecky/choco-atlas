The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational result to anchor a mirror on. Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 2.2 are axiomatic characterizations of the Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf indices, and their proofs are attributed to earlier work. The paper’s Monte Carlo findings are empirical results from one ten-player example, not a named complexity or algorithmic guarantee. So the proponent’s proposed question is unanchored: it cannot be a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The strongest repair is to fix a finite set of weight types \(t\), with per-player weights \(a_t\) and population fractions \(\mu_t\), replicate that electorate, and ask for limits and algorithms for each type’s aggregate Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf power. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime; treating common vote weights as types is not a distortion. But it changes the paper’s computational object. Both indices count an individual player’s pivotality. In an atomless society, an individual has zero mass, so direct pivotality vanishes or is undefined. Aggregating power over types can preserve a nontrivial quantity only through a specified finite-replication limit, where the pivotal events still depend on discrete coalition and permutation structure. The paper does not study that limit or establish that it is determined by \((\mu_t,a_t,q)\).

That leaves a credible new asymptotic question, especially for large bodies with standardized weight classes, but not a continuous mirror grounded in this paper’s named results. The case against that *new* question is weaker: the paper’s interest in large commissions gives it some motivation, and no universal argument rules out a useful limit theory. My strongest defensible negative is therefore that this paper supplies no qualifying anchor, and its best repaired mirror would launch a different research project.
