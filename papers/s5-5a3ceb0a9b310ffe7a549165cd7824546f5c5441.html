The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying numbered computational result in this paper: it contains no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic classification. Section 4.1 defines the allocation problem, §4.2 gives a greedy rule, and §7 reports synthetic performance. The half-block convergence bound in §7.3 is not presented as a numbered result. I can’t honestly supply a named anchor or claim that the paper proves its allocation problem is in P.

The best positive case is therefore an unanchored research question about the allocation problem in §4.1. Call it *Continuous Ranked-Wish Shift Allocation*. Consider a large facility collaboration with many members but repeated combinations of institution, ranked shift wishes, availability, rest constraints, block limits, and incompatible duties. A type \(t\) is that complete combination; its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of eligible members. Let each shift class \(k\) have a supply \(\sigma_k\) per eligible member. A feasible bundle \(A\) for type \(t\) contains only wished-for blocks and obeys that type’s individual constraints.

Use \(x_{t,A}\geq 0\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(A\), with \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\). For every shift class, require \(\sum_{t,A}x_{t,A}n_k(A)\leq\sigma_k\), where \(n_k(A)\) counts its blocks in \(A\). Compute each institution’s allocated points \(a_i\) and its share-plus-carryover quota \(q_i\). The question is to find a feasible mass allocation that first maximizes the institutions’ capped fill rates \(\min(1,a_i/q_i)\) in leximin order, then maximizes rank satisfaction using the paper’s \(u(r,n)=1-(r-1)/n\). Individuals still receive whole feasible bundles; only the distribution over identical types is continuous. This is an exact-optimization comparator for the goal in §4.1, not a claim that the paper’s greedy procedure in §4.2 is leximin-optimal.

I would expect this question to be Class A when the paper’s time-based rest and duty restrictions define the feasible bundles: a configuration LP has one column per feasible bundle, and its pricing step appears reducible to weighted interval scheduling. The paper itself says an exact formulation would be a natural comparison, which makes this question plausibly recognizable to its authors. The continuous model also uses the programme’s allowed co-modification: block supply scales with population, while each person’s bundle remains integral.

The weak point is substantial. This is not an anchor from the paper, and the leximin objective replaces a rule the authors chose for explainability over optimality. Also, the paper’s \(256\)-member, \(48\)-institution example does not establish a high-multiplicity regime: individualized wish lists and availability may make the number of complete types approach the number of members. So there is a plausible mirror of the allocation problem, but no named computational result here to support an anchor-based case for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying result to anchor a mirror on. The paper states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its contribution is an explainable allocation workflow, an independent checker, and quota rules; the half-block bound concerns cumulative fairness, not algorithmic complexity. So the proposed continuous problem would not mirror a result established by this paper.

The proponent’s allocation question is also less faithful than it first appears. Its capped leximin objective is a new policy choice; the paper deliberately chooses a retraceable priority rule over optimization, though it calls an exact formulation a natural comparison. A closer comparator could optimize the paper’s own priorities over admissible allocations, but that would still be a new computational question, not a complexity claim the paper makes.

There is a practical obstacle to making that question high multiplicity in the paper’s stated setting. A complete type would include an institution, wishes for specific dated seats, availability, incompatible duties, and relevant personal constraints. The synthetic case generates wishes at the member level, so its complete-type support may be close to its \(256\) members. The period also has only \(72\) concrete seats. Growing the membership alone leaves seat supply fixed and per-member opportunity shrinking; keeping the allocation nondegenerate would require scaling the seats too. That can be modeled, but it moves toward a replicated schedule or multi-facility scenario that the paper neither studies nor demonstrates.

Those objections weaken the mirror; they do not defeat its best version. Repeated member profiles and a seat supply that scales with population make a coherent continuous model. Its configuration LP could have a useful pricing problem, and the paper’s invitation to compare with an exact formulation gives the authors a plausible reason to recognize it. I cannot honestly claim that no such scenario is worthwhile. The strongest negative case is narrower: this paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its own example does not establish a useful high-multiplicity regime. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is weak.
