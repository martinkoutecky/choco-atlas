The continuous mirror question

Given finite seller and buyer value supports \(S\) and \(B\), rational type-mass vectors \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), and a threshold \(\rho\), compute the seller- and buyer-offering price schedules that maximize their respective expected profits under these masses, using the paper’s tie-breaking convention. With market-type mass \(\alpha_s\beta_b\) for each pair \((s,b)\), compute first-best gains \(\mathrm{OPT}=\sum_{s\in S,b\in B}\alpha_s\beta_b(b-s)^+\) and random-offerer gains \(\mathrm{GFT}_{RO}=(\mathrm{GFT}_{SO}+\mathrm{GFT}_{BO})/2\), then decide whether \(\mathrm{GFT}_{RO}<\rho\,\mathrm{OPT}\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of independent bilateral markets, with seller types \(s\in S\) carrying masses \(\alpha_s\) and buyer types \(b\in B\) carrying masses \(\beta_b\). Pair types independently, so market mass is \(\alpha_s\beta_b\); each market retains one indivisible item. The decision variables are the price schedules by type, and the objective is the aggregate gains from trade under the random-offerer mechanism, compared with first-best gains.

The objection that survived

The cohort interpretation adds no population-level computation: repeating the market scales both welfare quantities equally, and the stated calculation is already the paper’s distributional calculation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.1 and its explicit \(H=1000\) witness in Lemma 2.4; it does not address the note’s broader comparisons with second-best or best-of mechanisms.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s random-offerer performance result, interpreted over a large cohort of bilateral markets. It is an approximation-performance anchor, not a complexity-theoretic one: the paper contains no named result classifying a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

Take many independent one-seller, one-buyer markets, each with one indivisible item. A seller’s type is their value \(s\), and a buyer’s type is their value \(b\). Let \(\alpha_s\) and \(\beta_b\) be the fractions of sellers and buyers of each value. Pair them independently, so the mass of markets with values \((s,b)\) is \(\alpha_s\beta_b\). As the cohort grows, its size can become arbitrarily large while these type supports stay fixed. These are genuine repeated traders: mass records how many traders have a type, not uncertainty about the value of one named trader.

For each seller type \(s\), the seller-offering price is a maximizer of
\[ (p-s)\sum_{b\ge p}\beta_b. \]
For each buyer type \(b\), the buyer-offering price is a maximizer of
\[ (b-p)\sum_{s\le p}\alpha_s. \]
Use the paper’s tie-breaking convention. Each offer mode trades only the mass of markets whose two values accept its price; the random-offerer mechanism allocates half the market mass to each mode. First-best gains from trade are
\[ \mathrm{OPT}(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{s,b}\alpha_s\beta_b(b-s)^+. \]
The mechanism’s gains are the average of the two offer modes’ aggregate gains.

The continuous problem is Cohort Random-Offer Gap Evaluation: given a finite value range, rational seller and buyer mass vectors, and a threshold \(\rho\), determine whether the random-offerer mechanism’s aggregate gains are below \(\rho\) times first-best gains, and return the maximizing price schedules and ratio. That is the same performance question as in the paper, now asked of a cohort whose mass fractions remain meaningful as the number of traders grows. Each pair still trades an indivisible item; fractional quantities arise only in the aggregate mass across markets.

The anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this note. It states that there are finite discrete-support seller and buyer distributions for which random-offerer gains are less than \(0.495\) of first-best gains. The paper gives an explicit witness in Lemma 2.4, also proved here: take \(H=1000\), with seller and buyer distributions
\[ \Pr[S\le m]= \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2(H-1)}, & m=0,\\ \frac{1}{H-m}, & 1\le m\le H-1,\\ 1, & m=H, \end{cases} \qquad \Pr[B\ge m]= \begin{cases} 1, & m=0,\\ \frac{1}{m}, & 1\le m\le H-1,\\ \frac{1}{2(H-1)}, & m=H. \end{cases} \]
These become the seller and buyer mass fractions in the cohort model. Writing \(H_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}1/i\), the ratio for this witness is
\[ \frac{\frac{H_{999}}{999}-\frac{7}{12\cdot999}+\frac{1}{2\cdot999^2}} {\frac{2H_{1000}}{1001}-\frac{998}{1000\cdot999}} <0.495. \]
Thus the cohort question has a concrete yes-instance: the paper’s bad distribution is a mass population of repeated trader types, with the same price incentives and the same per-market welfare calculation.

For fixed finite support, I expect this evaluation problem to be tractable: enumerate candidate prices for each type, compute the best responses, then sum the resulting gains over type pairs. That is a Class A expectation for the fixed-instance mirror, not a complexity result proved by the paper. The theorem’s worst-case statement is already answered by its explicit witness; it does not establish the complexity of searching over all mass vectors. Natural follow-ups are to characterize the minimum ratio over all such populations, determine how small an extremal support can be, and compare the random-offerer and “best-of” mechanisms with the second-best mechanism in the cohort setting. This mirror covers Theorem 1.1’s gap result, not the note’s broader mechanism-design questions.

The weak point is that the original paper already represents uncertainty with value distributions and evaluates expected gains from trade. Reinterpreting those distributions as cohort proportions leaves the formulas unchanged, so the mirror offers little new computational content and a referee could regard it as a population reading of an existing stochastic model. The positive case is that the population is genuinely different in the intended high-multiplicity sense: many actual traders share each value type, and the aggregate question concerns their per-capita welfare. But this is a credible mirror of the performance question, not a strong new complexity problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor offered is Theorem 1.1, with Lemma 2.4 as its explicit witness. It is a meaningful welfare guarantee, but not a computational-complexity result. The proposed cohort is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many markets can share seller and buyer value types. The problem is that the cohort adds no population-level computation to the theorem.

With seller and buyer masses \(\alpha_s\) and \(\beta_b\), the product mass of markets of type \((s,b)\) is \(\alpha_s\beta_b\). A seller of value \(s\) still chooses a price to maximize \((p-s)\sum_{b\ge p}\beta_b\), and a buyer of value \(b\) still chooses one to maximize \((b-p)\sum_{s\le p}\alpha_s\). The cohort’s per-market expected gains are therefore exactly the paper’s expected gains under those same distributions. For \(N\) independent markets, expected aggregate gains are \(N\) times the paper’s quantity, so the ratio is unchanged. This is not a new population-dependent objective or algorithm; it is the existing distributional calculation expressed as a cohort average.

The proposed gap-evaluation problem is also straightforward for finite rational supports. Each type’s best price can be found by checking the relevant breakpoints in the other side’s support; then the gains can be summed over seller–buyer type pairs and compared with the threshold. The explicit \(H=1000\) witness is already a distributional instance of that calculation. This does not make the mirror invalid because its answer is known; it means the mirror supplies no new computational structure for the programme to chart.

The stronger repair would be to ask for the worst ratio over all mass vectors, or to compare random-offerer, “best of,” and second-best mechanisms across cohorts. Those could be worthwhile mechanism-design questions, but the masses are just the distributions \(F_s,F_b\) already used by the paper. The search is over priors, not over a newly continuized population problem. Likewise, extending the value distributions to continuous support is about uncertainty over one seller and one buyer; the paper itself says its result extends to that case. If instead many sellers and buyers compete over one item, or markets are coupled by a shared allocation constraint, that may create a genuine computational problem—but it changes the mechanism and no longer mirrors Theorem 1.1.

So the cohort interpretation survives as a sensible story, but the anchor does not survive as a worthwhile computational mirror: independent repetition leaves the paper’s distributional problem intact, while adding market-wide interaction creates a different problem. The limitation of this negative case is that it cannot rule out every useful population model inspired by bilateral trade; it rules out these natural continuizations as mirrors of the result the other analyst chose.
