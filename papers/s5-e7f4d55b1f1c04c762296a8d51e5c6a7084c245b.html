The continuous mirror question

Given a compactly supported location distribution \(\mu\) with an exact CDF or quantile representation and feasible capacity shares \((q_1,q_2)\), choose a permutation \(\pi\in S_2\) and feasible quantile levels \(v_1\le v_2\) minimizing the limiting maximum-cost ratio. The numerator is \(\min_{\lambda_j\le q_{\pi(j)},\,\sum_j\lambda_j=1} W_\infty\!\left(\mu,\sum_{j=1}^2\lambda_j\delta_{F_\mu^{-1}(v_j)}\right)\); the denominator is the optimal mass-allocation cost \(\min_{\sigma\in S_2,\,\zeta=\sum_{j=1}^2\zeta_j\delta_{y_j},\,\zeta_j\le q_{\sigma(j)},\,\sum_j\zeta_j=1} W_\infty(\mu,\zeta)\). Return \((\pi,v_1,v_2)\).

The model it lives in

A society is a compactly supported mass distribution \(\mu\) on the line, with facility capacity shares \(q_1,q_2\). The decision is a feasible ERM specified by its facility permutation and quantile levels; its objective is the limiting Bayesian maximum-cost ratio against optimal mass allocation. The selected ERM remains truthful for each finite population, while its performance is evaluated at the population limit.

The objection that survived

A nonatomic individual has zero mass, so the mirror cannot claim that the mass allocation itself preserves individual truthfulness; it optimizes finite-agent ERMs by their limiting performance.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the optimal two-facility ERM for limiting maximum cost, while Theorem 8 gives a uniform-distribution social-cost case and Lemma 1 formulates the underlying allocation problem; it leaves arbitrary mechanisms, general \(m\), and finite-\(n\) complexity bounds open.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact bit complexity for a fixed finite encoding of \(\mu\), such as a rational piecewise-polynomial density?
  • Can the projection characterization extend to more than two facilities?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper makes capacitated facility location a mass-allocation problem and computes exact answers for particular continuous populations. It does not provide the usual complexity classifications, and it does not support a claim about every result in the paper.

The natural mirror has location \(x\) as an agent’s type and \(\mu(A)\) as the fraction of society located in a region \(A\). A facility’s capacity becomes a population share \(q_j\). A solution chooses facility locations \(y_1,\ldots,y_m\) and an assignment flow \(\gamma\) from locations to facilities, with at most \(q_j\) mass assigned to facility \(j\). The social-cost objective is \(\int |x-y|\,d\gamma(x,y)\); for maximum cost, it is the largest distance used by the flow. A plausible regime is a large service area or transit corridor summarized by its distribution of residents: the number of residents is very large relative to the number of location types or regions in the input. This scaling of capacities is a natural part of the mirror.

Lemma 1, proved in this paper and used in its later analysis, is the clearest bridge to that model. For a finite profile with empirical measure \(\mu_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\), it states that the optimal social cost equals the minimum \(W_1(\mu_n,\zeta)\) over facility-capacity assignments: \(\zeta\) has at most \(m\) ordered support points, and its mass at point \(j\) is at most the capacity assigned there by some permutation \(\sigma\). It also states that an ERM’s social cost is the minimum \(W_1(\mu_n,\lambda)\) over feasible masses \(\lambda_j\) at that ERM’s selected order-statistic locations. This is a formulation result, not a polynomial-time classification; the paper computes with the transport formulation in its later results.

The corresponding continuous problem, Mass-CFLP-SC, is: given rationally described location types \(x_1<\cdots<x_\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to one, and capacity shares \(q_1,\ldots,q_m\) with total capacity at least one, choose facility locations and a nonnegative assignment flow that sends all mass to facilities without exceeding their capacities, minimizing total distance. A solution is the locations and the flow. I expect Class A: on a line, optimal assignments have strong ordering structure, suggesting an algorithm based on ordered groups of types. Lemma 1 supplies the right objective and capacity constraints, but does not establish that running-time claim; a proof would need to handle the facility capacities in the algorithm.

For the mechanism-design side, Theorem 8, proved here, gives a concrete social-cost anchor. For two facilities, a uniform population on \([0,1]\), and \(q_1\ge q_2\), it says the optimal ERM is unique and uses \(\pi=\mathrm{Id}\), with quantile vector \(\mathbf v=(1/4,3/4)\) if \(q_2\ge3/4\); \(\mathbf v=(1-q_2,q_1)\) if \(3q_2-2-q_1\le0\); and \(\mathbf v=(1-q_2,1-q_2/3)\) otherwise. My mirror question, Uniform-Optimal-ERM-SC, takes the two rational capacity shares as input and asks for the feasible truthful ERM minimizing its limiting Bayesian social-cost ratio, returning its permutation and quantile parameters. Theorem 8 gives the answer directly, so this is Class A. It is a narrow but meaningful case of choosing a truthful facility mechanism for a very large population.

My lead algorithmic anchor is Theorem 10, proved here. For a compactly supported distribution \(\mu\) and two capacities, it takes an optimal two-facility measure \(\nu\) for the maximum-cost transport benchmark, projects its two support points onto the capacity-feasible quantile intervals for each of the two facility permutations, then selects the permutation whose projected points have the smaller maximum distance to the support endpoints. The optimal ERM uses quantile parameters \(p_i=F_\mu(s_i)\) at those projected points. The paper’s Theorem 9 provides the benchmark measure through a projection routine. The associated continuous question, Optimal-ERM-MC(2), takes \(\mu\) and the two capacity shares as input and asks for a feasible ERM minimizing the limiting maximum-cost ratio; the solution is its permutation and quantile parameters. I expect Class A for this two-facility problem: the result reduces the search to a constant number of projections and comparisons. That expectation assumes an efficient exact representation of the distribution—such as a rational polynomial density of fixed degree, or an exact quantile oracle. The theorem does not supply a bit-complexity analysis for arbitrary distribution descriptions.

Theorem 10 is the strongest computational anchor; Lemma 1 is the strongest evidence that a high-multiplicity version of the underlying allocation problem is a sensible object. These mirrors cover the social-cost mass-allocation problem, the uniform two-facility social-cost mechanism, and the two-facility maximum-cost ERM. They do not cover arbitrary mechanisms, arbitrary numbers of facilities, or all the paper’s Bayesian results. Natural follow-up questions are whether the ordered structure yields a polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\), how the maximum-cost result extends beyond two facilities, and how these continuous solutions relate to finite instances after rounding rational masses into agents.

The weak point is that the paper is already unusually close to the continuous-population model: it studies a law \(\mu\), uses Wasserstein mass formulations, and takes \(n\) to infinity. Its contribution is largely analytic and mechanism-specific, rather than a new computational-complexity landscape. It has no named NP-hardness, P, FPT, or similar classification theorem; the anchors here are a transport formulation and explicit optimization results. The positive case is therefore that the high-multiplicity input and its bit complexity remain meaningful computational questions, not that this paper has left the continuous population itself untouched. Also, Theorem 9’s Algorithm 1 appears to print \(y_1=z/2\) for support \([a,b]\), where its midpoint construction seems to require \(y_1=(a+z)/2\). I would verify that formula before relying on the routine as printed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has already made the population continuous. It models locations by a common distribution \(\mu\), scales facility capacities as population shares \(q_j\), and studies the limit as \(n\) grows. Its Wasserstein formulation and limiting results are the continuous-population analysis a mirror would otherwise supply. The missing piece is a finite-input complexity analysis; that is a real gap, but it is different from a gap in continuization.

Lemma 1 does not establish an algorithmic result: it rewrites a finite instance as an optimal-transport problem. But the proposed Mass-CFLP-SC is already the object behind the paper’s continuous benchmark. Theorem 3 identifies the limiting optimal cost through a minimization over measures, and Theorem 4 does the same for other costs. Asking for a polynomial-time algorithm on rational finite-support inputs could be worthwhile, but it would add a complexity analysis to a model the paper already formulates continuously. It would not be a new continuous mirror of Lemma 1.

Theorem 8 is an even closer collision. Its input is the uniform population and two capacity shares; its output is the optimal ERM, including explicit quantile parameters in each capacity regime. Uniform-Optimal-ERM-SC asks for that same answer. Extending the computation to a richer distribution encoding could be a separate project, but it does not rescue this anchor. And if the proposed replacement is a nonatomic mechanism model, individual truthfulness becomes vacuous: one person’s report changes no population mass and hence no outcome determined by that mass. Preserving the paper’s nontrivial incentive constraint requires retaining finite agents, or modeling strategic blocs—neither is the proposed continuum of individuals.

Theorem 10 also already treats the continuous question as stated. It gives a projection procedure for choosing the optimal two-facility ERM from a compactly supported \(\mu\) and capacity shares; Theorem 9 supplies the benchmark construction. Optimal-ERM-MC(2) restates that procedure. The paper does not give a bit-complexity bound, and arbitrary distributions have no canonical finite encoding. Fixing a representation could make runtime analysis precise, but that would formalize the complexity of an existing continuous algorithm, not reveal a missing continuous population model. A broader finite-support or many-facility problem might be worth studying, but it moves beyond this anchor.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. Facility location has a convincing high-multiplicity story: many residents can share a location type, and population shares are natural. A computational study for rational finite-support populations may be valuable. My case is narrower: none of the three proposed anchors survives as a *new continuous mirror*. The paper already works with a continuous population and answers those particular limiting optimization questions; what remains open is their computational treatment under a chosen finite input representation.
