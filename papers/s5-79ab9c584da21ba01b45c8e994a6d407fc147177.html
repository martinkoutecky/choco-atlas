The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many block templates \(B\), each with residents \(I_B\), empty houses \(U_B\), bounds \(ub_B\), and legal integral housings \(\mathcal P_B=\{P\subseteq U_B: |N_B(i)\cap P|\le ub_B(i)\text{ for every }i\in I_B\}\), along with rational template masses \(\mu_B\) summing to \(1\) and a target refugee load \(\rho\), do there exist \(x_{B,P}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_B}x_{B,P}=\mu_B\) for every \(B\) and \(\sum_B\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_B}|P|x_{B,P}=\rho\)?

The model it lives in

The population consists of repeated copies of block templates. Each copy receives an integral legal housing \(P\); \(x_{B,P}\) gives the mass of copies of type \(B\) using \(P\), and feasibility asks whether their total housed refugee mass equals \(\rho\).

The objection that survived

The model assumes independent block copies with no neighborhoods crossing block boundaries, and the paper does not establish that this is common in actual resettlement layouts.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper’s inhabitants-respecting housing feasibility problem, including Theorem 1’s planar maximum-degree-\(3\) hardness result. It leaves the relaxed-violation problem and the proposed strategic extensions untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can useful algorithms arise when template size, the number of resident-role types, or structural parameters are small?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a Continuous Block ARH-UB model for a large resettlement area built from repeated housing-neighborhood templates. Think of many copies of a standard estate or village layout. Residents in the same position in the same layout have the same neighborhood and upper-bound; each copy has the same vacant-house pattern. The number of copies can be enormous while the number of distinct resident-role types stays fixed. Refugees are homogeneous, and the supply of vacant houses scales with the number of copies.

A resident type is a template and a position within it, including the resident’s full neighborhood and upper-bound. The template keeps the graph correlations that matter to this paper; the mass of a template represents the fraction of repeated copies with that layout. For a template \(B\), let \(I_B\) be its residents, \(U_B\) its empty houses, and let
\[ \mathcal P_B=\{P\subseteq U_B:\ |N_B(i)\cap P|\le ub_B(i)\text{ for every }i\in I_B\} \]
be its legal local housings. Given rational template masses \(\mu_B\), summing to \(1\), and a target refugee load \(\rho\) per unit of template mass, ask whether there are \(x_{B,P}\ge 0\) such that
\[ \sum_{P\in\mathcal P_B}x_{B,P}=\mu_B\quad\text{for each }B, \qquad \sum_B\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_B}|P|x_{B,P}=\rho. \]
Here \(x_{B,P}\) is the mass of copies using housing \(P\). Each copy still assigns refugees to whole houses and respects every resident’s bound; only the distribution of copies across legal housings is continuous. This is a feasibility question, as in the paper. Its natural optimization companion maximizes the refugee mass housed.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: ARH-UB is NP-complete even when the topology is planar and has maximum degree \(3\). The proof reduces from Independent Set: each original graph edge is subdivided by a vertex occupied by a resident with upper-bound \(1\), the original vertices remain empty, and the refugee target is \(k\). A legal housing set is exactly an independent set of size \(k\). That full construction also gives the continuous mirror a direct interpretation: one template of mass \(1\), with target \(\rho=k\), is feasible exactly when the source graph has an independent set of size at least \(k\). If the continuous solution mixes legal housings, an average of \(k\) still requires some housing of size at least \(k\); because legal housings remain legal when houses are removed, a housing of exactly \(k\) then exists.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. The continuous formulation permits mixtures across repeated copies, but the hard choices live inside the marked topology of each copy. In the reduction, deciding whether a copy can house enough refugees is already the Independent Set question. Equivalently, maximizing the local number housed has an NP-hard pricing problem. So this is not a case where making the population continuous removes the combinatorics; it is a faithful high-multiplicity version that exposes where those combinatorics remain.

The scenario is plausible because large housing estates can contain many repetitions of a local design, and describing a refugee load as a share of that repeated stock is natural. The model also keeps the paper’s core question intact: can refugees be placed in available houses while every nearby resident’s upper-bound is respected? It covers the paper’s inhabitants-respecting housing feasibility result, especially Theorem 1; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s strategic extensions or relaxed-violation results.

The main follow-up is whether useful algorithms emerge when the number of resident-role types, template size, or structural parameters such as feedback-edge set are small. In this model the continuous quota is easy to mix once each template’s maximum legal housing size is known; computing those maxima is the hard part.

My weakest point is the repeated-template regime. The paper studies one finite topology, not many copies of a neighborhood template, and its hard instances need templates whose graph structure grows with the input. The role-type support can therefore grow too. This mirror is strongest as a plausible large-city regime and as a precise high-multiplicity lift of the paper’s problem; it does not promise that the regime makes the problem easier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the topology carries information a population distribution over resident attributes cannot preserve. Residents with the same upper bound and degree may face different empty houses, and overlapping neighborhoods determine which housings are legal. Grouping them by those attributes loses the problem. A configuration distribution can restore the lost information, but in the proposed model its units are whole block layouts and legal housing patterns.

That makes the Theorem 1 mirror depend on repeating an entire topology as a block. To retain the reduction, the block must contain the graph encoding Independent Set; repeating many copies then gives high multiplicity, but the copy boundaries must leave neighborhoods independent. Real housing neighborhoods often overlap across buildings or blocks. If the block instead stays small and fixed, the reduction’s growing graph is no longer represented within it; if it grows, the topology and its role types carry the instance.

This is a substantial limit on how broadly the mirror describes refugee housing. It does not defeat the anchor, though. Repeated estates are plausible, and repeating the whole construction really does give every resident role many identical copies. Its continuous formulation is a valid high-multiplicity model, and I cannot rule out a more realistic repeated-network variant that preserves overlapping neighborhoods.

So the case against is weak at its hardest point: Theorem 1 has a defensible continuous mirror. The narrowness of the repeated-block scenario is a reason to question its priority for ChoCo, but under the programme’s stated criteria it is not enough to dismiss it.
