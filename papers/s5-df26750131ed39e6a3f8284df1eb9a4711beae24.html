The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that an exchange rule satisfying strategy-proofness, anonymity, continuity, simplicity, and non-bossy non-traders must be a continuous Barberà–Jackson rule. That is a characterization of rules, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 2, also proved here, says that the specified Collapsing Uniform Rules are strategy-proof, anonymous, continuous, and voluntarily participating. It likewise establishes properties of rules, not an algorithmic or hardness result. Proposition 1 concerns topological dimension. None of these named results asserts a problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The most plausible setting for a population mirror would be a large exchange with two divisible goods: each type is a complete preference relation, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that preference, and \(x_t\) is the per-person bundle assigned to that type, with feasibility \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=\omega\). A city allocating a fixed per-resident supply among many residents drawn from a relatively small set of preference types is a conceivable high-multiplicity regime.

But the paper’s central question is which *rules* satisfy incentive, fairness, continuity, and message-space conditions. Turning that into an existence or characterization question for a continuum of agents would be an axiomatic mirror, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Turning it into an algorithm for computing a type-level allocation would require choosing a particular rule; the paper does not give a named complexity result for that computation. There is also a substantive obstacle: with atomless individuals, one person has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness can become vacuous; treating each type as a strategic bloc would change the incentive concept.

So the honest positive case is only a plausible high-multiplicity setting for a related exchange problem. There is no continuous computational question I can tie to a qualifying named result here, and I would not claim a mirror for this paper under the programme’s criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes a class of exchange rules. Proposition 2 establishes properties of one constructed family, and Proposition 1 is about topological dimension. None states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm whose complexity could be mirrored. The paper’s finite-dimensional message restriction is about the number of real variables needed to encode information; it allows arbitrary precision and does not define bit complexity. The “communication complexity” label does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The best version of a population mirror would take a distribution over complete preference types and ask for a type-level exchange allocation, with per-capita bundles satisfying \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=\omega\). That is a plausible market setting, and the paper’s equal per-person endowment scales naturally to it. But applying Theorem 1’s conditions to such a society remains an axiomatic characterization, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Asking for an algorithm instead means selecting a particular rule and specifying how its preferences, parameters, and real-valued inputs are represented. The paper’s theorem selects neither a unique rule nor a finite input representation; making those choices would define a new computational problem rather than mirror its result.

The same gap applies to Proposition 2. One could ask how to compute a Collapsing Uniform Rule’s allocation for finitely many preference types with given masses. That may be a sensible question, but the proposition proves strategic and fairness properties, not a computational claim. A continuum version of Proposition 1 would likewise concern topology, not algorithms.

There is also no single automatic way to carry the incentive conditions over: if a mechanism sees only the aggregate distribution, an individual report has zero mass; preserving meaningful individual incentives requires specifying how a person’s own report affects their allocation. That is a modeling choice the paper does not settle. It does not rule out a worthwhile continuous computational study of exchange. It does leave this paper without a qualifying result to anchor one, which is the strongest case against counting it as a ChoCo mirror.
