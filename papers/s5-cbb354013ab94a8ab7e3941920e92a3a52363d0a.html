The case FOR (proponent)

The paper gives me no qualifying numbered computational anchor. Lemma 1 states normative properties, Lemma 2 characterizes competitive demand, and the central result is printed simply as “Theorem,” without a number. None claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. I won’t invent a number or present a structural characterization as a complexity result.

The closest useful result is that unnumbered Theorem. In full, it says: a problem is positive exactly when it has a competitive division with positive budget, and then competitive allocations maximize the product of the positive utilities over feasible profiles that are positive for \(N^+\) and zero for \(N^-\); the resulting utility profile is unique. A problem is negative exactly when it has a competitive division with negative budget, and then competitive allocations correspond to critical points of the product of absolute utilities among efficient, strictly negative feasible profiles; all agents’ utilities are negative. A problem is null exactly when it has a competitive division with zero budget, and then competitive allocations have the zero utility profile.

My strongest positive case is a continuous-population version of the theorem’s positive branch. Call it Positive Continuous Competitive Division. Let \(A\) be a finite set of divisible items and \(T\) a finite support of complete utility types. Each type \(t\) has a rational utility vector \(u_t\in\mathbb Q^A\), and a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). An allocation gives each member of type \(t\) a bundle \(x_t\ge0\), with one unit of each item available per unit of population mass:
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{ta}=1\qquad(a\in A). \]
The type’s utility is \(U_t=u_t\cdot x_t\). The instance is promised positive: some feasible profile gives every type with a positive valuation strictly positive utility and gives the remaining types utility zero.

The problem is to compute a competitive division and supporting prices: find \(x,p\) such that each type’s bundle maximizes its utility among bundles \(y\ge0\) with \(p\cdot y\le1\), with the paper’s least-expenditure tie-break, and the weighted supplies clear. The target utility profile is the maximizer of
\[ \sum_{t\in T_+}\mu_t\log U_t, \]
equivalently of \(\prod_{t\in T_+}U_t^{\mu_t}\), over the feasible positive profiles. This weighting follows the discrete model: if \(n_t\) agents share type \(t\), then \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the displayed objective is \(1/n\) times the log of the discrete Nash product \(\prod_t U_t^{n_t}\).

A plausible regime is a large cooperative or public service system dividing divisible amenities and burdens among many residents or workers. A type is the person’s full utility vector across those items; mass is the share of the population with that vector. The intended case has \(n\gg\tau\): many people, relatively few distinct utility types. Scaling item supplies with population keeps per-person supplies meaningful. This still asks the paper’s question about the Competitive Rule with additive utilities, mixed goods and bads, equal incomes, and market-clearing prices. Divisible outcomes are already in the paper; the proposed continuization is specifically of the population.

I expect the positive problem to be Class A, at least for approximation: it is a concave weighted-log objective over a feasible polytope with roughly \(m\tau\) allocation variables. The paper itself does not establish that algorithmic result, and exact bit complexity may need separate treatment if the output requires representing irrational optima. Natural next questions are whether the negative branch’s weighted critical points can be computed or represented efficiently, and how exactly the continuous model relates to finite instances when supplies scale with population.

The soft spot is substantial: this is a mirror of a structural theorem, not a numbered complexity result, and the positive branch’s tractability looks like standard convex optimization. The paper supplies no discrete hardness here whose disappearance in the continuum could be demonstrated. So I think the positive-population mirror is sensible and recognizable to the authors, but it is a modest ChoCo case rather than a strong hardness-dissolution result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper has no computational result to continue. Its central Theorem characterizes competitive divisions by the geometry of feasible utility profiles; the lemmas establish demand and fairness properties. None states a complexity bound or formulates an algorithmic problem. So the proposed mirror is not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper. It adds a new task: compute the rule’s outcome.

The proponent’s positive-population model is coherent. With \(\tau\) listed utility types and per-capita supplies, its objective is a weighted Nash welfare program, \(\sum_t \mu_t\log U_t\), over allocations with roughly \(\tau|A|\) variables. Cohorts sharing full utility vectors could make this a sensible high-multiplicity regime; aggregation need not lose information about the rule’s utility profile. I would not reject it on grounds of identity, fractional allocations, or an implausible population story.

That leaves a narrower case against spending ChoCo effort here: the paper gives no computational landscape whose boundary the mirror could chart. The positive branch turns its characterization into a standard optimization task. A broader mirror of the negative branch would need to decide whether the task is to find one competitive division or represent all critical points; the paper’s theorem does not specify a computational problem of either kind. Those could become worthwhile questions, but they would be new algorithmic projects attached to the paper, not continuations of its computational results.

So the negative case is strongest as a priority judgment, not as a claim that no worthwhile population model exists. The positive mirror survives the modelling test. What it lacks is a clear connection to a computational contribution made by this paper.
