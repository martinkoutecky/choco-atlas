The continuous mirror question

Given customer and provider types, rational arrival masses over \(H\) rounds, a population mass scale \(N\), and unknown fixed utilities in \([-1,1]\) for each ordered type pair, choose each round’s mass matching and zero-sum transfers to minimize expected cumulative Subset Instability. A matched mass \(x_{ij}\) yields aggregate utility feedback with variance proxy \(1/x_{ij}\); determine whether a policy achieves regret \(O\!\left(\tau\sqrt{nH\log(NH)}\right)\), where \(\tau\) is the number of types and \(n\) is the maximum arriving mass per round.

The model it lives in

A typed mass-market bandit: type masses can split across partner types through a capacitated flow, each unit of mass receives one partner or remains unmatched, and transfers balance per matched unit. The policy learns unknown type-pair utilities from mass-scaled noisy feedback and minimizes cumulative instability over the horizon.

The objection that survived

The mass-feedback variance proxy assumes a fixed sampling intensity per unit of count-equivalent mass; if feedback instead scales with every individual in an expanding market, normalized aggregate noise vanishes. The proponent specifies the former model but does not establish that it is the unique limit of the paper’s feedback process.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers typed preferences with transferable utilities and the primal-dual equilibrium computation in Theorem A.1. It leaves the unstructured and separable-linear regret results, non-transferable utilities, and the search-frictions revenue result outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a UCB policy with capacitated matching and mass-scaled feedback achieve regret \(O\!\left(\tau\sqrt{nH\log(NH)}\right)\)?
  • How does the regret change when feedback intensity scales with population size or when the arriving type proportions vary across rounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for the paper’s typed-preference setting. The paper has no named NP-hardness, P, or parameterized-complexity result; its main algorithmic results are about statistical regret. So the lead anchor is an algorithmic learning theorem, and the complexity predictions below are questions for the mirror, not claims the paper already proves.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1.3 (Typed Preferences, Informal), proved in the paper’s formal typed-preference theorem in Section 5, with proof in Appendix C.2. It says that when each agent has a context in a finite set \(\mathcal C\), and an agent’s utility for a potential partner depends only on the two agents’ contexts, a UCB-style algorithm can learn stable matchings with transfers. With \(n\) the maximum number of agents arriving in a round and \(H\) rounds, its expected cumulative Subset Instability is \(O\!\left(|\mathcal C|\sqrt{nH\log(|\mathcal A|H)}\right)\). The feedback is noisy utility feedback from matched pairs. The result shows that learning cost depends on the number of types rather than the number of possible individual pairings.

A precise mirror is Typed Mass-Market Instability Learning. Its input is a finite set of customer and provider types, rational type masses for each round, a horizon \(H\), and unknown fixed utilities in \([-1,1]\) for each ordered type pair. A type is a complete description for this model: agents of that type have the same relevant preferences. In round \(r\), the decision is a mass matching \(x^r_{ij}\), with \(\sum_j x^r_{ij}\le a^r_i\) and \(\sum_i x^r_{ij}\le b^r_j\), plus a zero-sum transfer per unit of matched mass. Here \(a^r_i,b^r_j\) are the available masses of the two types. The flow lets a type’s mass split across several partner types, while every individual unit of mass still receives one partner or remains unmatched.

The objective mirrors the paper’s Subset Instability: for the outcome \((M^r,z^r)\), take the supremum over measurable coalitions \(S\) of the coalition’s best total utility from rematching within \(S\), minus its total utility under the chosen outcome, including current transfers. The learning objective is to choose outcomes online to minimize expected cumulative instability over \(H\) rounds. To make feedback explicit for fractional mass, let matching mass \(x^r_{ij}\) yield an aggregate observation of each side’s type-pair utility with variance proxy \(1/x^r_{ij}\); no observation arrives when \(x^r_{ij}=0\). A solution is an adaptive policy producing each round’s mass matching and transfers. The target is a regret bound of order \(O\!\left(\tau\sqrt{nH\log(NH)}\right)\), where \(\tau\) is the number of types and \(N\) is the population mass scale.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large recurring service marketplace in which many customers and providers share a small number of location, service, and preference types. Then \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\) remains moderate. The paper explicitly studies typed preferences and describes them as standard in theoretical market models, so this mirror preserves its central assumption and question rather than simplifying them away. I would expect the finite-type version to be tractable: its per-round allocation is a capacitated matching problem, and its unknown parameters are the finite type-pair utilities. Whether the stated regret bound survives this continuous feedback and mass-allocation model is the prover’s question. Further questions include the sharp dependence on \(\tau\), how to round mass policies back to finite high-multiplicity markets, and whether population imbalance changes the regret rate.

A supporting anchor is Theorem A.1 (\(\cite{SS71}\)), attributed to Shapley and Shubik and recapitulated with proof in the paper’s Appendix A. It states that a stable matching with transfers induces an optimal primal-dual pair for the maximum-weight matching LP, with dual prices equal to agents’ utility under the outcome; conversely, an optimal primal-dual pair whose primal solution is an extreme point yields a stable matching with transfers.

Its mass analogue is Stable Matching for Typed Masses. Given rational customer and provider type masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), and known utilities \(u_{ij},v_{ji}\), output a stable mass assignment \(x_{ij}\) and supporting prices \(p_i,q_j\). The underlying allocation maximizes \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}(u_{ij}+v_{ji})\), subject to the type-mass capacity constraints. Its dual minimizes \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i+\sum_j\nu_jq_j\), subject to \(p_i+q_j\ge u_{ij}+v_{ji}\) and nonnegative prices. For a matched unit on edge \(ij\), the customer can receive transfer \(p_i-u_{ij}\); dual tightness makes the provider’s net utility \(q_j\), with the transfer balanced between them. This is a polynomial-size rational LP in the number of types and input bit length, so I expect this static mirror to be tractable. The mass flow’s fractional entries are appropriate: they describe how an atomless type population is divided among integral partner assignments.

The lead is Theorem 1.3; Theorem A.1 supplies the equilibrium-computation machinery. The mirror covers only typed preferences with transferable utilities, not the paper’s unstructured or separable-linear settings, its non-transferable-utility extension, or its revenue result.

The weakest point is that the paper’s central theorem is about finite named agents receiving individual bandit feedback, while the proposed mirror replaces that feedback with an aggregate mass oracle and allows fractional type flows. Those are meaningful co-modifications, not consequences already proved in the paper. The paper also studies statistical learning rather than worst-case computational complexity. Still, the typed market and its primal-dual equilibrium problem remain recognizable, and they give a concrete continuous-population question to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is about what ChoCo is trying to classify. Theorem 1.3 is a genuine learning result: utilities are unknown, feedback arrives from matched agents, and the guarantee measures cumulative instability over \(H\) rounds. It does not give the complexity of computing an outcome from a supplied society. In the typed case, the unknown object is already a finite matrix of type-pair utilities; the roster sizes affect how quickly the platform learns that matrix. A mass-matching version can be well-posed, but its main question is how an observation model scales with population mass, not what becomes computable when the society is given as a distribution.

The typed mirror is also the strongest case against my position. With \(N\) individuals and type proportions \(\mu\), a flow of mass \(\mu_{ij}\) corresponds to \(N\mu_{ij}\) matched individuals and therefore \(N\mu_{ij}\) noisy observations. Its aggregate variance scales as \(1/(N\mu_{ij})\). In the population limit, any positive flow reveals that type-pair utility with vanishing noise; per-capita regret vanishes as well. The proponent’s \(1/x\) noise model is a sensible way to retain finite information when \(x\) is count-equivalent mass, but then the learning question depends on that population scale and the chosen feedback normalization. That could be a worthwhile project in continuous stochastic market learning. It is a substantial extension of the paper’s learning model, rather than a complexity mirror of the kind ChoCo is organized to study.

Theorem A.1 does not repair that mismatch. It recapitulates the Shapley–Shubik primal-dual characterization; the mass version is the type-capacitated assignment LP. It is a useful equilibrium-computation fact, but not a paper-specific complexity result. Scaling rational type masses to integer copies recovers an ordinary finite matching instance, and the matching polytope is integral. The mirror therefore supplies no distinct computational task from this anchor.

I would not argue that typed preferences lack multiplicity: they are exactly where a high-multiplicity regime is most plausible, and the paper itself motivates them as standard. Nor can I rule out a useful mass-bandit paper. My negative case works only if ChoCo stays focused on computational complexity for a society supplied as an instance; under that scope, the substantive anchor is a statistical-learning theorem, and the static anchor is a standard LP characterization. If ChoCo includes regret analysis for online learning in mass markets, the typed anchor survives, and the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is weak.
