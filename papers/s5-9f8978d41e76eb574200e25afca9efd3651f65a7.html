The continuous mirror question

Given finite complete type sets \(T_A,T_B\), a finite outcome set \(O\), rational masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\), utilities \(u_A,u_B\), and threshold \(G\), consider a high-multiplicity population of independent two-role units with pair mass \(\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s)\). Does there exist a deterministic mechanism \(f:T_A\times T_B\to O\) satisfying the Bayes–Nash truthfulness inequalities \(\sum_{s\in T_B}\mu_B(s)u_A(t,f(t,s))\ge\sum_{s\in T_B}\mu_B(s)u_A(t,f(t',s))\) and the analogous inequalities for role \(B\), while achieving \(\sum_{t,s}\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s)(u_A(t,f(t,s))+u_B(s,f(t,s)))\ge G\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity population of independent two-role units, with complete role-specific types \(t\in T_A\) and \(s\in T_B\), masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\), one shared deterministic mechanism \(f:T_A\times T_B\to O\), Bayes–Nash or dominant-strategy constraints, and mass-weighted average social welfare.

The objection that survived

Even for Theorem 2, the masses can be viewed as the prior already present in the finite-agent model, and the mechanism does not respond to a shared aggregate report, so the population interpretation may be only a relabelling of expectations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 1–3: deterministic dominant-strategy hardness, deterministic Bayes–Nash hardness, and randomized dominant-strategy LP tractability. It leaves Theorem 4 and the illustrative randomization example unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a formal dictionary characterize when repeated local units constitute a genuine high-multiplicity population model rather than merely a relabelling of priors \(P_i\)?
  • Does allowing correlated pair masses \(\mu_{t,s}\) instead of product masses change the complexity landscape?
  • Can an aggregate-report mechanism over a continuum of agents preserve nonvacuous strategic incentives and yield a distinct complexity problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a modest but valid one: continuize a large population of repeated two-role decision units, while retaining the paper’s finite strategic interaction inside each unit. This avoids the atomless-population problem in which one individual’s report has zero effect on a single global outcome.

Take a platform running millions of identical two-role decisions—for example, requester–provider or planner–executor pairs—with a finite set \(T_A\) of complete type descriptions for role \(A\) and \(T_B\) for role \(B\). A type includes the role, utility function over outcomes, and every parameter relevant to incentives. Let \(\mu_A\) and \(\mu_B\) be the rational population masses of the two role-types. A random pair therefore has type \((t,s)\) with mass \(\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s)\). The same mechanism is deployed in every pair, and its objective is average welfare over the whole population. The outcome set \(O\) remains discrete; population, not outcomes, is continuous.

My lead anchor is the paper’s Theorem 1, proved in the paper:

“2-agent DETERMINISTIC-MECHANISM-DESIGN with dominant strategies implementation (DMDDS) is NP-complete, even with the social welfare function as the objective function.”

The corresponding problem is \(\mathrm{DMDDS}_\infty\). Its input is finite \(T_A,T_B,O\), rational utilities \(u_A(t,o)\) and \(u_B(s,o)\), rational masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\), and a rational threshold \(G\). A solution is a deterministic mechanism
\[ f:T_A\times T_B\to O \]
such that truthful reporting is dominant:
\[ u_A(t,f(t,s))\ge u_A(t,f(t',s)) \]
for all \(t,t'\in T_A\) and \(s\in T_B\), with the analogous inequalities for role \(B\). It must also satisfy
\[ \sum_{t,s}\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s) \bigl(u_A(t,f(t,s))+u_B(s,f(t,s))\bigr)\ge G. \]

This is a genuine population question: does one nonmanipulable mechanism achieve the required average welfare across a high-multiplicity society of repeated decision units? It is not merely a new encoding of a fractional outcome.

I expect hardness to transfer, placing this in Class B. The reduction in Theorem 1 uses two agents with uniformly distributed types. Replace those distributions by actual type masses \(\mu_A(t)=1/|T_A|\) and \(\mu_B(s)=1/|T_B|\). Clearing denominators produces a finite society of repeated pairs with exactly the same feasible mechanisms and welfare threshold. The graph combinatorics remain in the type utilities and the mechanism table, not in the number of named agents. Thus the continuous population does not dissolve the independent-set hardness.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in the paper:

“2-agent DETERMINISTIC-MECHANISM-DESIGN with Bayes-Nash implementation (DMDBN) is NP-complete, even with the social welfare function as the objective function.”

The continuous problem \(\mathrm{DMDBN}_\infty\) has the same input and deterministic mechanism \(f\), but replaces the paper’s prior distributions by actual role-population masses. Truthful reporting must satisfy
\[ \sum_s\mu_B(s)u_A(t,f(t,s)) \ge \sum_s\mu_B(s)u_A(t,f(t',s)) \]
for every \(t,t'\), and symmetrically
\[ \sum_t\mu_A(t)u_B(s,f(t,s)) \ge \sum_t\mu_A(t)u_B(s,f(t,s')) \]
for every \(s,s'\). The objective is the same mass-weighted social welfare threshold.

This too should be Class B. Theorem 2’s knapsack reduction already uses the item weights as probabilities \(w_j/W\). In the mirror they become population masses. Choosing which type classes receive the favorable outcomes still requires selecting an integral subset of types; replacing counts by rational masses does not fractionalize that mechanism choice. Hence the knapsack hardness survives in the high-multiplicity regime.

The paper’s positive counterpart is Theorem 3, proved there:

“2-agent RANDOMIZED-MECHANISM-DESIGN with dominant strategies implementation as the solution concept is solvable in polynomial time by linear programming, for any polynomially computable objective function.”

The continuous problem \(\mathrm{RMDDS}_\infty\) asks for lotteries
\[ p_{t,s}\in\Delta(O) \]
for every reported type pair. Dominant-strategy constraints become
\[ \sum_o p_{t,s}(o)u_A(t,o) \ge \sum_o p_{t',s}(o)u_A(t,o), \]
and the analogous constraints for \(B\). For any polynomially computable objective \(g(t,s,o)\), maximize
\[ \sum_{t,s}\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s) \sum_o p_{t,s}(o)g(t,s,o). \]
This is a polynomial-size LP in \(|T_A||T_B||O|\) variables. I therefore expect this mirror to be Class A. The lottery is not the continuous object; it is the same randomized mechanism allowed by the paper. The continuous object is the population measure weighting the repeated interactions.

The authors should recognize these as their problems: same no-side-payment setting, same mechanism table, same dominant-strategy or Bayes–Nash requirement, and the same welfare objective. What changes is that the two-agent setting is interpreted as one finite interaction repeated over a large, type-repetitive population. The high-multiplicity dictionary is exact for rational masses: clearing denominators yields a finite population of cloned decision units, while the mass formulation is the compact representation.

The weakest point is that the mechanism is local to each two-role unit. If ChoCo requires one mechanism producing one global outcome for an atomless society, individual manipulation becomes vacuous and this paper is a poor direct fit. A stronger follow-up would study a genuinely aggregate mechanism \(M(\mu)\), with positive-mass type blocks able to alter the reported distribution; that would be a new extension rather than a direct theorem transfer. Other natural questions are whether hardness persists with a fixed number of types, whether correlated pair masses \(\mu_{t,s}\) change the LP, and whether aggregate mechanisms admit continuum-specific tractable or hard cases.

This covers Theorems 1–3. I would not claim more: Theorem 4 is an obvious Bayes–Nash randomized counterpart, but the three anchors above already capture the paper’s central deterministic-hard versus randomized-LP contrast.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s repeated-pair construction is the strongest possible rescue, but it does not actually continuize the paper’s computational object. It creates a continuum of independent copies of a two-agent mechanism-design instance. The mechanism never observes, aggregates, or acts on the population measure. The measure is only an exogenous weighting of outcomes.

This is decisive for Theorem 1. In the proposed \(\mathrm{DMDDS}_\infty\) problem, the dominant-strategy constraints are

\[ u_A(t,f(t,s))\ge u_A(t,f(t',s)), \]

and similarly for \(B\). These constraints do not contain \(\mu_A\) or \(\mu_B\) at all. The feasible mechanisms are exactly the same finite tables as in the paper. The masses appear only after the mechanism has been selected, in the objective

\[ \sum_{t,s}\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s) \bigl(u_A(t,f(t,s))+u_B(s,f(t,s))\bigr). \]

Thus \(\mu\) is not the society on which the mechanism operates; it is merely a weighting of the paper’s existing type contingencies. Repeating the two-agent game millions of times changes the interpretation of the expectation, but not the computational object. Theorem 1 already permits those distributions as priors.

Theorem 2 does not repair this defect. Its Bayesian incentive constraints use the prior over the other agent’s type:

\[ \sum_s P_B(s)u_A(t,f(t,s)) \ge \sum_s P_B(s)u_A(t,f(t',s)). \]

Replacing \(P_B\) by a population mass \(\mu_B\) is therefore not a high-multiplicity transformation; it is a relabelling of the probability distribution already present in the theorem. A population interpretation is possible, but only because each local pair samples a partner from that distribution. The mechanism still acts separately on each pair and never faces a strategic population.

The knapsack reduction consequently does not establish a population-level phenomenon. Its subset \(S\) is selected through entries of a finite mechanism table, not through divisible or indivisible masses of agents. Calling the probabilities “fractions of a population” supplies a story for the same coefficients; it does not make population mass a decision variable or a constraint-bearing object.

The same problem is even clearer for Theorem 3. In the proposed randomized mirror, the incentive constraints are

\[ \sum_o p_{t,s}(o)u_A(t,o) \ge \sum_o p_{t',s}(o)u_A(t,o), \]

which again contain no population masses. The \(\mu_A(t)\mu_B(s)\) factors occur only in the objective coefficients. The LP is therefore precisely the paper’s finite randomized-mechanism LP, with a different interpretation of its expectation. Its tractability comes from allowing lotteries over outcomes, not from making the population continuous. Treating fractions of the population as receiving different outcomes instead would be continuization of the outcome allocation, which is outside the programme’s scope and is not the paper’s randomized mechanism.

A genuinely population-level repair would require a mechanism \(M\) that takes the aggregate report distribution as input and produces a common outcome, or an assignment of outcomes across the population. But then an individual in an atomless population changes the reported measure by zero. Dominant-strategy constraints become vacuous: unilateral misreports cannot affect \(M\). To recover nontrivial incentives one must impose positive-mass coalition deviations, finite cohorts, or some other new solution concept. None is the paper’s dominant-strategy or Bayes–Nash implementation problem.

Alternatively, one could retain per-unit outcomes but let them depend on the aggregate population report. That would avoid vacuity, but it is a new mechanism-design problem with a new outcome function and new incentive constraints. The paper’s Theorems 1–4 would no longer be anchors for it. The local repeated-pair version is mathematically legitimate, but its population dimension is inert; the genuinely aggregate version is potentially interesting, but is not a continuization of the paper.

So the negative case is not that the proposed mass formulations have uninteresting answers. It is that none of the three anchors makes population mass part of the mechanism-design problem itself. The paper studies uncertainty about the types of finitely many agents and optimizes a mechanism table. A continuum of independent repetitions merely turns expectations into average frequencies. That is a plausible application story, but not a worthwhile continuous-population mirror in the sense required by ChoCo.
