The continuous mirror question

Given finite worker-type and firm-type sets \(I,J\), rational type masses \((\alpha_i)_{i\in I}\) and \((\beta_j)_{j\in J}\), and explicitly represented path-independent piecewise-linear choice maps on mass bundles, compute the least stable upper bound and greatest stable lower bound of two stable matching matrices under the continuous firm order, or report that either bound does not exist.

The model it lives in

A matching is a nonnegative matrix \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{I\times J}\), with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding type masses. Type-level choice maps determine individual rationality and whether a positive mass of a worker-firm type pair blocks. The model specifies a union operation on mass bundles; lattice operations are extremal stable matrices under the resulting continuous Blair order.

The objection that survived

For markets derived from duplicated labelled agents, type-level marginals do not determine partner-set unions or the coupling of individual bundles across matchings; the paper supplies no canonical mass-valued choice or union rule.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s lattice-operation construction over an anonymous mass market. It leaves the paper’s quasi-stability lattice and operator results outside that stated computational question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the stable set remain a lattice under the specified mass-valued choice and union rules?
  • Can the lattice operations be computed with a bound polynomial in the number of supported types, input bit length, and choice-map description size?
  • For which clone markets do the labelled lattice operations descend to operations on type-level distributions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. The strongest computational anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: given two stable matchings \(\mu,\mu'\), construct \(\lambda_{\mu,\mu'}\) by having each firm choose from the union of its partners in the two matchings; then iterate \(T^F\) from \(\lambda_{\mu,\mu'}\) to its fixed point \(F^F(\lambda_{\mu,\mu'})\). That fixed point is their join under the firms’ Blair order. Symmetrically, construct \(\gamma_{\mu,\mu'}\) by having each worker choose from the union of their partners; iterating \(T^W\) gives \(F_W(\gamma_{\mu,\mu'})\), their meet under the firms’ order. This is a constructive computation theorem, but it gives no running-time bound.

A plausible setting is a large freelance market: many contractors and firms, with relatively few complete types—say, combinations of skills, availability, and contract preferences on one side, and procurement rules and capacity on the other. A type includes the entire choice rule and every parameter relevant to it. The number of people \(N\) could be very large while the number of supported types \(\tau\) is much smaller. This is a reasonable high-multiplicity regime for many-to-many work, where contractors can hold several engagements and firms can hire several contractors.

Here is the mirror I would ask authors to assess. Mass-Lattice Operations takes finite worker-type and firm-type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) summing to one on each side, and explicitly represented, bounded rational piecewise-linear choice maps for each type. A type’s choice map acts on vectors of offered partner mass and is path-independent on the mass bundles in the model. The matching variable is a matrix \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{I\times J}\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of relationships between types \(i\) and \(j\). Thus the per-capita partner bundles are \(x_{i\cdot}/\alpha_i\) and \(x_{\cdot j}/\beta_j\); these can be fractional averages of underlying integral assignments.

Call \(x\) stable when both sides’ type-level bundles are individually rational and there is no positive mass \(q\) of new \(i\)-to-\(j\) relationships that both types’ choice rules accept when that fresh mass is offered. Define the firm order by the continuous analogue of the paper’s choice-from-union test: \(y\succeq_F x\) when each firm type chooses its bundle in \(y\) from the pool-union of its bundles in \(x\) and \(y\). The input includes a specified union operation on mass bundles—for example, typewise maximum for anonymous, nested opportunity pools—so the operation on aggregated bundles is well-defined.

Given two stable mass matchings, the problem is to return their least stable upper bound and greatest stable lower bound under \(\succeq_F\), or report that an operation does not exist. The outputs are exact matching matrices. This mirrors Theorem 3’s lattice operations and re-equilibration, not merely the fact that matching outcomes can be fractional. The complexity parameters would be \(\tau\), the mass bit length \(L\), and the choice-map description size, rather than the number of people \(N\).

My lead expectation is Class A on a bounded rational-polyhedral subclass: once the population is represented by type masses, a fixed-point or separation-based continuous-optimization approach might compute the operations without dependence on \(N\). That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 3; its finite iteration says nothing about how many steps a mass-valued iteration would need. The follow-up questions are whether the continuous stable set remains a lattice, whether the fixed-point computation has a polynomial bound in \(\tau,L\), and how its answer relates to operations in large finite clone markets.

The mirror covers the lattice-operation result only. Its weakest point is also central: Theorem 3’s unions are unions of sets of named partners. A type-average matrix does not generally retain enough information to reconstruct those unions or each individual’s acceptance. The mass-valued choice maps and pool-union operation are therefore a substantive co-modification, not a free consequence of the paper’s finite-agent model. I think the authors could recognize this as their problem for markets where partner opportunities are genuinely anonymous and divisible at the aggregate level; without that feature, this is only a nearby matching problem, not a faithful mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real anchor: it gives a constructive way to obtain a join and meet by re-equilibrating a quasi-stable matching. The negative case cannot dismiss it as merely axiomatic. Its weakness as a ChoCo anchor is that the construction operates on a finite, labelled market; the paper gives no input encoding or running-time analysis. Any complexity mirror must first say what information an aggregated market retains.

The proposed mass matrix does not retain enough to carry out the paper’s operation. For each firm, \(\lambda_{\mu,\mu'}\) uses the set union of its partners in two matchings. If a firm has three workers of a given type in each matching, the union could contain three, four, five, or six workers of that type, depending on how much the two groups overlap. The aggregate counts in the two matchings do not reveal that overlap. A typewise maximum assumes perfect overlap; adding the counts assumes none. Neither is generally the union used by Theorem 3. The subsequent operator has the same issue: it depends on individual workers’ accepted firms and individual firms’ choice from actual partner sets. Path-independence alone supplies no choice rule on fractional vectors that recovers those facts.

A stronger mirror could represent the distribution of each type’s complete partner bundles, together with the coupling that says which individuals’ bundles in \(\mu\) are paired with which bundles in \(\mu'\). That preserves the information needed for the union and re-equilibration. But the state is then a distribution over bundles and their pairings, rather than the proposed matrix of type-to-type masses. Its description can grow with the number of possible bundles, and the paper’s path-independence assumption does not by itself give a finite-dimensional or polyhedral representation. This is a substantial obstacle to the claim that continuization exposes the operation to tractable continuous optimization.

That objection does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. In a market with genuinely anonymous, divisible opportunities, authors could define mass-valued choice and union rules directly; in a clone market, one could study whether the labelled lattice operations descend to type-level distributions. Either could be a sensible question. They would need additional structure beyond the paper’s model, though, and the clone-market version must address the missing coupling rather than infer it from marginal masses. So the strongest case against is that the proposed tractable mirror is not yet a faithful high-multiplicity formulation, and repairing it may restore much of the individual-level complexity. The universal negative remains weak: a carefully chosen anonymous market could still yield a coherent, author-recognizable mirror of Theorem 3.
