The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 6.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 6.1 — survived: The opponent’s truthfulness objection limits coverage, but this theorem itself is the Round-Robin allocation guarantee the proposed flow mirrors.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational type shares \(\mu_t\), additive valuations \(v_t\) over \(d\) item categories, exact continuum MMS benchmarks \(\eta_t\), and one unit of each category per unit mass, with \(v_t(g)\leq\eta_t/2\) for every type and category, can an algorithm output a rational flow \(x_{t,b}\u005c) in time polynomial in d,\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)? Here each \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^d\) has \(\sum_g b_g=d\), each unit of mass receives one indivisible bundle, \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=1\) for every category \(g\), and every bundle with \(x_{t,b}>0\) satisfies \(v_t\cdot b\geq\eta_t/2\)?

The model it lives in

A rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) additive valuation types, with per-capita supply one of each of \(d\) categories and indivisible bundles of \(d\) items. The decision variables \(x_{t,b}\geq 0\) describe the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\); find a feasible flow meeting type masses, category supplies, and the per-type MMS/2 guarantee using the supplied benchmarks \(\eta_t\).

What the mirror covers

It mirrors only the small-item Round-Robin MMS guarantee in Theorem 6.1; it leaves out Plant-and-Steal truthfulness, prediction consistency and robustness, noisy-prediction bounds, and experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the feasible flow be computed in time polynomial in \(d,\tau\) and \(L\), for example using a configuration LP with an efficient pricing method?
  • Does the discrete-to-continuous correspondence hold without assumptions on rational denominators or discrete MMS agreement?
  • Can the compressed allocation framework preserve the truthful prediction-based mechanism from Section 6?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 6.1, which the paper cites as Lemma 2 of Amanatidis et al. (2023), based on Theorem 3.5 of Amanatidis et al. (2017). It says: for \(n\) agents and goods \(M\), if every agent \(i\) values every good \(j\) at most \(\mu_i^n/2\), then Round-Robin gives every agent at least \(\mu_i^n/2\), where \(\mu_i^n\) is that agent’s \(n\)-agent MMS. The cited analysis also permits changing the allocation order between rounds. The paper invokes this result in its consistency proof. It is an algorithmic approximation guarantee, not a complexity classification; the supplied paper has no numbered NP-hardness, P, W[1]-hardness, or FPT result to anchor.

A natural regime is a large course allocation with many students and repeated seat categories: each of \(d\) course categories has one seat per student on average, and students have additive values for categories. A type \(t\) is a complete value vector over those categories; \(\mu_t\) is its share of the cohort. The regime has \(N\) students, \(N\) copies of each category, and \(\tau\ll N\) distinct types. This preserves the paper’s indivisible goods and gives the population and supply a common scale. The small-item condition says that no one course seat is worth more than half a student’s fair-share benchmark.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: High-Multiplicity Round-Robin MMS. The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) additive valuation types on \(d\) item categories, along with each type’s exact continuum MMS value \(\eta_t\). Define \(\eta_t\) as the best minimum value in a unit-mass partition of the per-capita inventory: a finite-support distribution \(\lambda\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d\), with \(\sum_b\lambda_b=1\) and \(\sum_b\lambda_b b=\mathbf 1\), maximizes the minimum \(v_t\cdot b\) among bundles with positive mass. The input is promised to satisfy \(v_t(g)\le\eta_t/2\) for every type \(t\) and category \(g\).

A solution is a rational mass flow \(x_{t,b}\) over bundles of exactly \(d\) indivisible items. It must satisfy \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=1\) for each category. Every bundle receiving positive mass must give its type value at least \(\eta_t/2\). Thus \(x_{t,b}\) says what fraction of type \(t\) receives bundle \(b\); each person still receives a whole bundle. The computational question is whether one can produce such a flow with polynomially many bits and support, in time polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and the input bit length, without expanding the cohort into \(N\) named students.

The case for this mirror is concrete. For rational instances whose MMS-optimal bundle frequencies have rational denominators, choose \(N\) large enough to realize both the type masses and those frequencies. The discrete MMS then agrees with \(\eta_t\). Run the cited Round-Robin theorem on that \(N\)-student instance and aggregate its allocations by type and bundle. The resulting empirical flows satisfy the stated constraints and guarantee. Since there are finitely many size-\(d\) bundles, a convergent subsequence gives a continuous flow. This establishes feasibility on that realizable regime; the theorem itself does not give a compact algorithm in \((d,\tau,L)\).

I would expect this bounded-item slice to be tractable, with the aggregate allocation described by a configuration LP and the main algorithmic question being whether its bundle-pricing step has an efficient solution. The eligible columns are integral bundles meeting \(v_t\cdot b\ge\eta_t/2\); efficient pricing over them is the relevant boundary to investigate. Further questions are whether the discrete-to-continuous dictionary holds without denominator or slack assumptions, whether the pricing problem admits a polynomial algorithm, and whether the same compressed approach can handle the truthful prediction-based mechanism in §6.

The authors should recognize the connection: Theorem 6.1 is explicitly the multi-agent Round-Robin fact used to prove their \(n\)-agent result. The mirror covers that bounded-item fairness guarantee, with item supplies scaling per capita. Its weakest point is that the numbered anchor is a cited lemma, and finite replication already gives a feasibility argument. The new computational question is compressed implementation in the support size and bit length; that is a credible continuization question, though it does not by itself transfer the paper’s full mechanism-design contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is the weakest kind of result to build this paper’s continuous mirror around: Theorem 6.1 is explicitly attributed to earlier work, and it states a guarantee for a specific Round-Robin algorithm. It does not pose or classify a computational problem. The paper’s own contribution is the truthful, prediction-based mechanism; the cited theorem is a fairness lemma used in its consistency proof.

The course-cohort example is coherent, and scaling the seats with the cohort avoids the obvious supply problem. But the proposed flow \(x_{t,b}\) asks only whether anonymous type masses can be assigned whole bundles meeting a threshold. It drops the feature that makes this paper distinctive: an agent’s report determines their own allocation while the mechanism remains truthful. The flow records neither an individual’s report nor how their bundle would change after a deviation. A more faithful continuous mechanism would need to specify type-contingent individual allocations and incentive constraints; the anonymous flow does not provide those.

There is a real computational question in compressing the flow. But the discrete Round-Robin result already supplies feasible allocations on rationally replicated instances, while the compressed formulation introduces a new configuration problem. Supplying \(\eta_t\) as input sidesteps computing the benchmark; computing it as part of the task creates another problem beyond the cited theorem. The exponential bundle space may make either version difficult, but difficulty alone does not show that the question is unworthy.

So this is a case about priority, not a fundamental obstruction. The mirror is plausible as a high-multiplicity fair-allocation problem, and the replication argument gives it substance. I cannot honestly rule it out in every sensible scenario. The stronger negative point is that this mirror strips away the paper’s central mechanism-design contribution, and the proposed flow task is not itself a complexity result the paper establishes. That may weaken its claim to be a mirror of this paper, but it probably does not defeat the anchor under the programme’s one-result standard.
