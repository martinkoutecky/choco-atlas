The continuous mirror question

Given finite schools \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of student types, rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings \(P_t\), weak school priorities over types, and rational capacity shares \(q_c\) with \(\sum_c q_c\ge 1\), compute a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_c x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,c}\le q_c\). Require stability, meaning there are no \(t,u,c,d\) with \(x_{t,d}>0\), \(x_{u,c}>0\), \(cP_t d\), and \(t\succ_c u\), and require that no other stable mass assignment gives every type a weakly first-order stochastically better school distribution and some positive-mass type a strict improvement. Realize \(x\) as a type-symmetric lottery with per-person probabilities \(p_{t,c}=x_{t,c}/\mu_t\); determine whether this lottery is ex ante stable and is not ordinally dominated by any ex ante-stable lottery, including lotteries asymmetric within types.

The model it lives in

A school-choice cohort is represented by a distribution over complete student types, where each type specifies preferences and priorities at every school. Rational type masses and per-capita capacity shares define feasible assignments; a mass assignment gives each type’s school probabilities, which can be realized by random measurable assignments respecting capacity in each realization. Stability and ordinal dominance are then stated over type probabilities, while comparison lotteries may still treat members of a type differently.

The objection that survived

In an atomless type, equal treatment is built into the type-level assignment, so the finite reassignment among named students becomes automatic; finding the stable, efficient mass assignment adds a bottleneck absent from Theorem 1.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s ETE reassignment guarantee; it leaves Proposition 2’s ex-post-stability guarantee and rank-minimizing selection problem outside the surviving anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the stable, first-order-stochastically undominated mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in the number of schools, support size, and input bit length?
  • Does Theorem 1’s nondominance argument extend from finite student permutations to exchangeable lotteries over measurable assignments, even against asymmetric lotteries?
  • How does this mass problem relate to finite instances with proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and quotas scaled by \(n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity school-choice mirror. The paper’s equal-treatment groups already identify natural types: students with the same preferences and the same priority at every school. In a large district, many students can share each such type, so the relevant input is the mass of each type, with \(\tau\) distinct types potentially far fewer than the number of students. School quotas become per-capita capacities. The paper’s lotteries over matchings are already part of its outcome model; that is not the continuity claim. The mirror makes the population a distribution over types.

There is an important qualification about the anchors. The paper has no named hardness result and no numbered theorem that formally states a running-time bound. Its abstract calls the method polynomial-time, but the main theorem is conditional on having a constrained efficient matching. I would use that theorem as a constructive algorithmic anchor, while being clear that it is not itself a formal complexity classification.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says: if \(\mu^\ast\) is a constrained efficient matching—stable and not Pareto dominated by another stable matching—then the ETE reassignment of the point lottery on \(\mu^\ast\) is ex ante stable, satisfies equal treatment of equals, and is not ordinally dominated by any other ex ante stable lottery. This is a genuine construction with a substantial welfare guarantee, though computing the constrained efficient matching is a separate step.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Let \(C\) be a finite set of schools and \(T\) a finite support of student types. Each type \(t\) has a strict school ranking \(P_t\); each school \(c\) has a weak priority order over types. Let \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\) be the type masses, summing to \(1\), and let \(q_c\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) be capacity shares with \(\sum_c q_c\ge 1\). A mass assignment is a matrix \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_c x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,c}\le q_c\). Call it stable if there are no \(t,u,c,d\) with \(x_{t,d}>0\), \(x_{u,c}>0\), \(cP_t d\), and \(t\succ_c u\). Call it constrained efficient if no other stable mass assignment gives every type a weakly better school distribution in the first-order stochastic-dominance sense, and strictly improves some type.

Continuous ETE-Stable-Lottery Construction asks, given \((C,T,\mu,P,\succsim,q)\), for a constrained efficient stable mass assignment \(x\) and its type-symmetric lottery. The lottery gives each member of type \(t\) school probabilities \(p_{t,c}=x_{t,c}/\mu_t\); it can be realized by randomly rotating the members of each type through the type’s assignment partition, preserving capacities in every realization. A valid output must be ex ante stable, satisfy ETE, and not be ordinally dominated by any ex ante stable lottery, including one that treats members of a type differently. This is the continuous counterpart I think the authors could recognize: their equals groups become masses, while the central stability and lottery guarantees remain. My expectation is Class A, using type-level stable matching structure and continuous optimization. The paper does not establish that classification for this mass model. The natural follow-ups are whether the output can be represented and computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and how it relates to finite instances with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and quotas scaled by \(n\).

A second, distinct anchor is Proposition 2, proved here using Okumura’s 2025 claim. It says that if \(\bar\mu\) is a stable matching minimizing the sum of students’ rank positions among stable matchings, then its ETE reassignment is ex ante stable, satisfies ETE, and is not ordinally dominated by any ex post stable lottery. The paper explicitly leaves efficient identification of such a matching open.

Its continuous mirror is Rank-Minimum Stable Mass Assignment. On the same input, find a stable mass assignment minimizing \(R(x)=\sum_{t,c}x_{t,c}r_t(c)\), where \(r_t(c)\) is type \(t\)’s rank for school \(c\), and return its type-symmetric lottery. The guarantee to investigate is that no ex post stable lottery ordinally dominates that output. I tentatively expect tractability when the support \(\tau\) is moderate, since the objective is linear in masses; the stable-assignment structure could still make the general problem hard. The key further question is whether this objective admits an LP or pricing formulation, or whether the stability choices create a genuine source of hardness.

The soft spot is that the paper’s contribution is primarily a market-design theorem, not a complexity result. Theorem 1 supplies an algorithmic guarantee only after a constrained efficient matching has been found, and Proposition 2’s selection step is expressly open. Also, although the authors’ equals groups make the type interpretation unusually natural, the mass model changes capacities and permits fractional type-level assignments. That co-modification needs their buy-in. So this is a credible case for a focused mirror, not a claim that the paper already contains a continuous computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that the two anchors are results about the quality of lotteries, not complexity results. Theorem 1 assumes a constrained-efficient matching is already in hand; it proves what its ETE reassignment guarantees. Proposition 2 assumes a rank-minimizing stable matching exists, then explicitly leaves finding one efficiently open. The abstract calls the method polynomial-time, but the paper gives no numbered complexity result with an input encoding and running-time analysis. So either proposed mirror adds a computational problem the paper does not solve, or it mirrors only a structural guarantee.

That distinction is sharpest for Theorem 1. Given a stable, constrained-efficient mass assignment, equal treatment is already expressed by giving every member of a type the same school probabilities, \(p_{t,c}=x_{t,c}/\mu_t\). Computing those probabilities is simple. The interesting finite operation is redistributing assignments among named students; in an atomless type, symmetry can instead be built into the assignment. To make the mirror computationally substantial, the proponent adds the task of finding the stable, efficient mass assignment. That may be a worthwhile problem, but it is not the computational content of Theorem 1. A stronger mirror could also compare against asymmetric ex ante-stable lotteries, but that makes the task about optimizing over lottery supports and identity-level assignments, not about continuizing the theorem’s ETE reassignment.

Proposition 2 resists this objection more strongly. Its best mirror is to minimize \(R(x)=\sum_{t,c}x_{t,c}r_t(c)\) over stable mass assignments, then ask whether the resulting symmetric lottery resists domination by ex-post-stable lotteries. That is a recognizable high-multiplicity optimization problem. But the paper itself identifies finding the finite rank-minimizing matching as open; the continuous question therefore extends that bottleneck rather than continuing a solved algorithmic result. And while the objective is linear, stability depends on which type-school pairs receive positive mass, so the proposed mirror does not automatically give the continuous-optimization advantage the programme seeks.

These objections do not establish that the mirrors are uninteresting. Large school-choice cohorts with repeated preference and priority profiles are plausible, and proportional capacities are a natural co-modification for that regime. Proposition 2 in particular yields a clean mass-based question. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the proponent’s strongest computational questions are new optimization problems motivated by the paper, rather than computational results the paper has actually established. I cannot make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; this is a scope-and-novelty caution, and it is weakest against the Proposition 2 mirror.
