The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(X\), a finite family of nonempty menus \(\mathcal B\), and rational population shares \(p_{xB}\) for \(x\in B\), decide whether there is a distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(X\) such that \(p_{xB}=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}_t(B)=x}\mu_t\) for every \(B\in\mathcal B\) and \(x\in B\). Each individual chooses one alternative; the shares are the fractions of the population making each choice.

The model it lives in

Use rankings as types and nonnegative masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. The decision variables are the type masses; the objective is feasibility of the linear share equations. There are \(|X|!\) potential types, while any feasible instance has a witness supported on at most \(1+\sum_{B\in\mathcal B}(|B|-1)\) rankings.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 8’s preference-maximization application as aggregate choice-share feasibility; leaves Theorem 1’s general projection result, Proposition 10, Proposition 11, and the other applications alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which restricted menu families admit polynomial-time feasibility or pricing algorithms?
  • How do algorithms scale with the number of alternatives, menus, input bit length, and a bound on the support size?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Population Random-Choice Rationalizability\(_\infty\), a population version of the choice problem in Proposition 8. The paper defines rationalizable choice as choice from each budget set \(B\) equal to the set of alternatives maximal under one weak order. Proposition 8, proved here, says that this theory is universally and recursively enumerable axiomatizable relative to the theory of general choice data. In other words, the hidden preference relation can be eliminated to obtain effective universal restrictions on observable choices. This is a computability result, not a polynomial-time bound. The paper has no named NP-hardness, P, or parameterized-complexity result; Proposition 8 is the strongest usable algorithmic anchor.

Here is the mirror. Let \(X\) be a finite set of alternatives and \(\mathcal B\) a finite collection of nonempty menus. The input is a table of rational choice shares \(p_{xB}\), where \(p_{xB}\) is the fraction of the population choosing \(x\) from menu \(B\), and \(\sum_{x\in B}p_{xB}=1\). A type is a strict ranking \(t\) of all alternatives in \(X\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, with rational masses summing to one. Write \(\operatorname{top}_t(B)\) for the highest-ranked alternative in menu \(B\). The question is whether there is a \(\mu\) such that, for every \(B\in\mathcal B\) and \(x\in B\),

\[ p_{xB}=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}_t(B)=x}\mu_t. \]

A solution is a finite list of supported rankings and their masses. This is a feasibility question, so there is no objective to minimize or maximize. Each person still chooses one whole alternative; the fractional shares arise from averaging across the population. I use strict rankings to avoid adding a tie-breaking model; they are a natural restriction of the weak-order choice problem in Proposition 8.

The regime is a large customer or member population facing the same menus, with stable preferences. A million people may share a comparatively small number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: the type records the preference information the problem uses, and mass records what fraction of people have that type. For \(q=|\mathcal B|\), the choice-share vectors have dimension at most \(d=\sum_{B\in\mathcal B}(|B|-1)\), so a feasible instance has a witness using at most \(d+1\) types. The mirror therefore has a concrete continuous optimization shape: an LP over the \(m!\) possible rankings, with an exponential number of potential columns. Its pricing problem is to find a ranking maximizing a weighted sum of its top choices across menus.

I expect the unrestricted problem to sit on the hardness-transfers side of the trichotomy, though that is an inference, not a result of this paper. Pairwise menus already make the pricing problem a weighted ranking-optimization problem, so the agenda’s ordering combinatorics remain when the population becomes continuous. The population change may remove fixed-electorate integrality constraints, but it does not remove the alternative-ranking structure. Structured menu families could still yield tractable cases. Further questions include which menu families admit efficient pricing, how the continuous solution relates to exact fixed-size electorates, and whether small-support witnesses can be found efficiently.

The high-multiplicity dictionary is especially clear here: any rational \(\mu\) can be multiplied by a common denominator to produce a finite electorate with exactly those choice shares, and any finite electorate gives a rational \(\mu\). So this formulation is a sensible population version of the authors’ hidden-preference rationalizability question; it does not claim the continuous version is novel or automatically easier.

The weak point is that Proposition 8 studies one choice correspondence rationalized by one weak order, while this mirror explains aggregate shares through a distribution of strict preferences. That adds population heterogeneity and fractional observations. I think the extension survives the authors’ plausibility test because the underlying behavior is still individual preference maximization, and the fractional data are exactly the average forced by describing many such agents through type masses. This mirror covers Proposition 8 only; it does not purport to continuize the paper’s group-preference or Nash-equilibrium applications.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Proposition 8 does not establish a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize. It says that rationalizable choice data have a universal, recursively enumerable axiomatization. The paper defines “effective” as having a Turing machine enumerate the axioms; it imposes no running-time bound. So Proposition 8 does not ask how feasibility scales with \(m\), the number of menus, or the input bit length.

The faithful population version has little room to change that problem. Proposition 8 concerns one choice function rationalized across menus by one weak order. Replicating that decision maker gives a point mass on one type, so choice shares remain \(0\) or \(1\). The proponent’s fractional shares require a distribution over different rankings. That is a coherent model of a population, but it changes the question from whether one hidden order rationalizes choices to whether a mixture of orders rationalizes aggregate choice probabilities.

That changed question is standard stochastic rationalizability, or the random utility model. Computational work formulates it as an LP over preference orders and develops column-generation tests; for pairwise menus, the associated rationalizability problem is NP-complete via the linear ordering polytope. Later work develops computational tools for nonparametric random-utility testing as well. [Smeulders et al. (2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6224134/) and [Smeulders, Cherchye, and De Rock (2021)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA17605). Under the programme’s prior-art rule, that history alone does not invalidate a well-posed mirror; it does mean the generic mirror already has a substantial computational literature.

I would not argue that the proposed population regime is implausible. Large groups facing common menus, with rankings as complete types, fit high multiplicity well, and the paper’s group-choice focus gives the authors a plausible reason to recognize the extension. The negative case is therefore limited: there is no clear population continuization of Proposition 8 that preserves its single-order problem and yields a new complexity question. The more compelling heterogeneous-population version changes the problem and has a close existing computational counterpart. That makes this a weak case against a worthwhile follow-on, rather than a convincing case against every possible mirror.
