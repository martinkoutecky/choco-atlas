The anchor — hardness

Theorem 1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 1 — survived: Grouping identical ballots into types with masses equal to their row frequencies preserves every pairwise outcome and the strict issue majorities.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(t\) equally weighted binary issues and a finite support \(S\subseteq\{-1,+1\}^t\) of complete ballot types with positive rational masses \(\mu_v\) summing to \(1\), where every issue has a strict population majority, decide whether there exists a Condorcet-winning proposal \(p\in\{-1,+1\}^t\): whether, for every challenger \(q\in\{-1,+1\}^t\), \(\sum_{v\in S}\mu_v\,\operatorname{sgn}\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^t v_j(p_j-q_j)\right)\geq 0\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over complete issue-opinion types \(v\in\{-1,+1\}^t\), with rational mass \(\mu_v\); the decision variable is a proposal \(p\in\{-1,+1\}^t\), and it wins if its mass-weighted pairwise vote margin against every other proposal is nonnegative.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's co-NP-hardness for unweighted odd electorates. It leaves the single-switch recognition and forbidden-subprofile algorithms, weighted results, and majority-supported proposal bounds unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the problem be solved efficiently when the support size \(\tau\) is fixed or small?
  • What algorithms are available parameterized by the number of issues \(t\) or support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 1: deciding whether an unweighted instance with an odd number \(n\) of voters admits a Condorcet-winning proposal is co-NP-hard. The paper proves this result. Since odd \(n\) gives a strict majority on every issue, the issue-wise majority proposal is unique; the paper further shows that deciding whether this proposal is a Condorcet winner is co-NP-hard. Equivalently, it is NP-hard to find a proposal that defeats it. [Theorem 1 in the published paper](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2025/pdfs/p179.pdf)

Here is a direct continuous version, Continuous Unweighted Condorcet Existence. An instance has \(t\) binary issues and a finite support \(S\subseteq\{-1,+1\}^t\). Each type \(v\in S\) is a complete issue-opinion vector and has a positive rational mass \(\mu_v\), with \(\sum_{v\in S}\mu_v=1\). All issues have equal weight. Assume each issue has a strict population majority, so there is a unique IWM proposal.

For proposals \(p,q\in\{-1,+1\}^t\), define the population’s pairwise margin by
\[ M_\mu(p,q)=\sum_{v\in S}\mu_v\, \operatorname{sgn}\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^t v_j(p_j-q_j)\right). \]
A proposal \(p\) is a Condorcet winner exactly when \(M_\mu(p,q)\geq 0\) for every proposal \(q\). The problem asks whether such a \(p\) exists; a yes-instance has a Condorcet-winning proposal as its solution.

A natural setting is a large referendum or policy consultation where many people answer the same slate of equally weighted questions. People with the same complete answer vector form a type, and its mass is the fraction of respondents with that vector. The number of respondents can be much larger than the number of distinct answer patterns. This preserves the paper’s underlying comparison rule: each person prefers the proposal closer in Hamming distance, and society compares proposals by majority. The outcomes remain binary; the society is what becomes a distribution.

The hardness transfers directly. Given a paper instance with odd \(n\), group identical rows into types and assign each type mass equal to its multiplicity divided by \(n\). Every population pairwise margin is then the discrete vote margin divided by \(n\), so all pairwise outcomes—and therefore the Condorcet winners—are unchanged. The rational masses have polynomial-size encodings. Thus this continuous problem is co-NP-hard under the strict-IWM promise, and I expect Class B: discrete hardness transfers. The main follow-up questions are whether the problem has useful algorithms parameterized by \(t\) or support size \(\tau=|S|\), and whether a proposed IWM can be checked efficiently by finding or ruling out a defeating challenger.

This mirror covers Theorem 1 alone, in its unweighted, odd-electorate setting. Its weakest point is also clear: the reduction establishes hardness for the support-and-mass representation, but does not establish hardness when the support size is fixed or especially small relative to the population. So this is a faithful high-multiplicity formulation with inherited hardness, rather than evidence of a new continuum-specific phenomenon.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong case that this mirror is not worth asking. The anchor is a named computational hardness result, and its natural population version is unusually clean: treat each complete issue-opinion vector \(v\in\{-1,+1\}^t\) as a type with rational mass \(\mu_v\). Then the continuous pairwise margin is
\[ M_\mu(p,q)=\sum_v \mu_v\,\operatorname{sgn}\!\left(\frac1t\sum_j v_j(p_j-q_j)\right). \]
Given any paper instance with odd \(n\), set \(\mu_v\) to the fraction of rows equal to \(v\). The margin is exactly the discrete margin divided by \(n\), so every pairwise result and every Condorcet winner is preserved. The IWM is unique, and the paper’s result that a Condorcet winner must be an IWM carries over. This gives co-NP-hardness on valid rational-mass instances with polynomial-size encodings.

The best attempt to narrow the mirror does not defeat it. Asking whether the unique IWM is a Condorcet winner is equivalent to asking whether a Condorcet winner exists here; asking for a defeating proposal is the complementary search question already underlying the paper’s reduction. Restricting support size \(\tau\) would be a useful further question, but the reduction only establishes hardness when \(\tau\) can grow. That leaves a parameterized landscape to investigate; it does not make the unrestricted mirror ill-posed. Ballot vectors are complete types, and repeated ballots in a large referendum or policy consultation give a credible high-multiplicity regime.

The strongest negative point is about expected novelty: this theorem’s continuous version inherits its hardness directly from the discrete problem, so the paper alone gives no reason to expect a tractability gain from fractional masses. But inherited hardness is Class B in the programme’s own trichotomy, and the programme explicitly treats identifying where continuization stops helping as a result. High-multiplicity work supports the regime, and the paper supplies no continuous computational treatment that collides with this question.

So the negative case is weak. The mirror is faithful, well-posed, and computational; I see no honest obstruction that defeats it.
