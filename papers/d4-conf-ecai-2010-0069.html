The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named theorem saying that finding a kernel\(^+\)-stable payoff is polynomial-time solvable or hard; its conclusion explicitly leaves the computational complexity open. The best named computational anchor for a positive mirror is Theorem 1 (Convergence), proved in this paper. It says that Algorithm 2’s sequence of side-payments converges to a payoff distribution in the kernel\(^+\): the agents’ strengths move toward equality, subject to the singleton-payoff floor. That is an algorithmic existence result, not a tractability result. The algorithm repeatedly evaluates strengths over coalitions, and the paper notes that this requires exponentially many coalition values in the finite model.

My lead is the Continuous Kernel\(^+\) Payoff Problem. Consider a large workforce or multiagent population whose members form coalitions to produce value. A type records everything relevant to coalition value and payoff: for example, the agent’s skills, costs, outside options, and bargaining parameters. Let \(T\) be the finite set of types, with mass distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). The intended regime has many agents and relatively few types: perhaps millions of agents drawn from tens or hundreds of recurring role profiles.

Let \(q\in[0,\mu]\) describe a coalition’s composition by type, and let \(V(q)\) be its worth. The instance includes the per-capita value \(W^\star\) of an optimal coalition structure, as in the paper’s two-stage setup: first select an optimal structure, then distribute its worth. Write \(a_t\) for a type-\(t\) agent’s singleton worth. A proposed payoff \(p_t\) is the payoff per unit of type-\(t\) mass. Define the normalized strength of type \(t\) as \(\sigma_t(p)=\sup_{q\in[0,\mu],\,q_t>0}\bigl(V(q)-\sum_s q_s p_s\bigr)\): the greatest excess among coalition compositions containing positive mass of that type.

The problem is: given \((T,\mu,V,W^\star,(a_t)_{t\in T})\) and a precision \(\epsilon>0\), find payoffs \(p_t\ge a_t\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=W^\star\), and for every pair \(t,s\), both \(\sigma_t(p)+\epsilon\ge\sigma_s(p)\) or \(p_t=a_t\), and \(\sigma_s(p)+\epsilon\ge\sigma_t(p)\) or \(p_s=a_s\). In words, allocate the optimal structure’s value across the population so that types are in approximate global equilibrium, except where a type is already at its singleton floor. A solution is the payoff vector \(p\), with enough information to verify the budget balance and the strength comparisons. The feasibility condition is the objective: as in the paper, the task is to find a stable distribution, not maximize a second quantity.

This is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s underlying payoff problem, not a change to fractional outcomes or to the stability criterion. Finite populations with \(n_t\) agents of each type give proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); the coalition value and payoff sums can be normalized per population member. The continuous question asks whether the payoff guarantee from Theorem 1 survives in that limit and how to compute it. The type-composition strength is the limiting counterpart of the paper’s “best excess over coalitions containing this agent.” It also exposes the central computational issue: evaluating strengths means optimizing over coalition compositions, the continuous analogue of the paper’s exhaustive scan over coalitions.

For this stated problem, my tentative expectation is Class A when the type-composition strength problem has an efficient optimization method. The payment dynamics in Theorem 1 suggest a route: use a strength oracle to find imbalanced types, then transfer payoff while respecting singleton floors. With fixed or moderate \(\tau=|T|\), continuous optimization may make those strength calculations practical. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1; the theorem gives no polynomial bound. For unrestricted value representations, pricing or equilibrium computation may instead be hard. There is no hardness result in this paper to support a Class B prediction.

The mirror covers the paper’s convergence result for computing a kernel\(^+\)-stable payoff, conditional on an optimal coalition structure. It does not claim to mirror coalition-structure generation, the externality extension, or Lemma 1’s inclusion of the core. Useful follow-up questions are whether a stable payoff always exists in the atomless model, whether an \(\epsilon\)-algorithm can be bounded in \(\tau\), input length, and \(1/\epsilon\), and how its guarantees relate to finite high-multiplicity instances.

My weakest point is the passage from individual agents to a continuum. Kernel\(^+\) compares named agents and permits them to have different payoffs, whereas the mirror assigns a common payoff to members of each type and defines strength through positive coalition mass. A single infinitesimal agent does not change a coalition’s mass profile, so this is a limiting version of the paper’s strength, not an identity already proved by it. The case survives as a credible question because it keeps the paper’s optimal-welfare target, coalition excesses, singleton floors, and cross-coalition transfers. It should be presented as a proposed high-multiplicity extension of Theorem 1, not as a result the authors have already established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a named algorithmic convergence result, so I would not dismiss this anchor as purely axiomatic. Its content is still limited: Algorithm 2 converges in the limit, while the paper gives no finite running-time bound and leaves complexity and approximation to future work. A continuous computational problem could be posed around it, but the proponent’s proposed version loses the theorem’s central stability condition under the usual high-multiplicity scaling.

Take a natural regime with finitely many anonymous types, coalition worth scaling with population size, and a continuous per-capita value function \(V(q)\), where \(q\) is the coalition’s type composition. For a payoff vector \(p\), write \(H_p(q)=V(q)-\sum_s q_s p_s\). The proponent defines type \(t\)’s strength as \(\sup_{q_t>0}H_p(q)\). Since an arbitrarily small positive mass of type \(t\) can be added to any feasible composition, continuity gives

\[ \sigma_t(p)=\sup_{q\in[0,\mu]}H_p(q) \]

for every type \(t\). Thus all types have the same strength for every payoff vector \(p\). The global-equilibrium condition holds automatically; it does not select stable payoffs or require a sequence of side-payments. Budget balance and singleton floors can still be imposed, but the stability test contributes no further constraint. In the finite model, a coalition containing one type-\(t\) agent has mass at least \(1/n\); that distinction disappears in the normalized limit.

A stronger rescue would preserve the finite-\(n\) differences between strengths. Those differences can depend on order-one changes in coalition value that vanish from \(V\). For example, a bonus triggered by having at least one type-\(t\) member can affect finite strengths while contributing nothing to the per-capita limit. Keeping such effects requires extra, sequence-specific information beyond the continuous society. Other repairs change the object: requiring a coalition to contain at least a fixed mass of type \(t\) measures bloc strength, while using a derivative of \(V\) measures marginal productivity. Either could motivate a new study, but neither is the paper’s single-agent maximum-excess criterion.

This is a strong case against the stated mirror and its regular, continuously scaled version. It does not prove that no worthwhile version exists: threshold technologies or a carefully defined block-stability concept could yield a sensible mass-based question. Those scenarios make the universal negative claim weaker, though they would need to justify why their stability notion is the authors’ problem. On the paper and proposal as given, I would argue against treating Theorem 1 as a surviving continuous mirror.
