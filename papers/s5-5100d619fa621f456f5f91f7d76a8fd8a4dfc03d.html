The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 1 and 2 characterize rules for two claimants; Theorem 3 is an axiomatic characterization for arbitrary claimant sets, cited from earlier work; and Theorem 4 characterizes the random-arrival rule using Pareto optimality, scale covariance, symmetry, conditional monotonicity, and truncation invariance. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper also gives no finite encoding for its estate \(E\), which can be an arbitrary compact set, so there is no input length \(L\) or stated algorithmic task to carry into a continuous model. Aziz’s computational study of the transferable-utility rule is relevant prior work, but it is neither a result of this paper nor the same model.

Theorem 4 is the strongest candidate the proponent raises. A more compelling version than simply “put many claimants into types” would define a sequence of anonymous NTU estates for repeated claimant types, scale claims and feasible payoffs per capita, and ask for an algorithm to compute the limiting random-arrival allocation. That could be a coherent computational project. But its central ingredients—the estate family, how feasibility scales, and what random arrival means for nonatomic claimants—must all be supplied. The paper’s theorem neither specifies nor analyzes them. Its characterization would survive only as an axiomatic question about a new rule, which the programme excludes; a complexity result for the newly specified mass model would be adjacent research, not a mirror of Theorem 4.

The same problem defeats Theorems 1 and 2: their two-claimant characterizations contain no population axis, and extending them to populations of claimant types requires replacing the finite-dimensional estate with a new aggregate model. Theorem 3 cannot anchor a mirror of this paper’s contribution either, since it is an external axiomatic result. This case does not rely on high-multiplicity work already existing, or on predicting whether continuity changes an answer.
