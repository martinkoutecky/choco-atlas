The continuous mirror question

Given a simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) and integer \(K\ge2\), with \(|V|\ge K\), does there exist a mass vector \(\mu\) such that \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\), \(0\le\mu_v\le1/K\), and \(K^2\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v\sum_{w\in N(v)}\mu_w\ge K(K-1)\)?

The model it lives in

A unit mass of identical agents with utility \(u(x)=x\) is assigned across location classes indexed by \(V\). Each class has capacity \(1/K\), and a class's neighboring classes are specified by \(G\). The mass at class \(v\) is \(\mu_v\), an agent there receives \(U_v(\mu)=K\sum_{w\in N(v)}\mu_w\), and aggregate welfare is \(K\sum_v\mu_v U_v(\mu)\). The population has one preference type; the decision variable is its mass allocation across locations.

What the mirror covers

Covers the USW threshold hardness in Theorem 9 for \(u(x)=x\); leaves its ESW and NSW results and all stability and Pareto results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the same hardness transfer hold for other utility functions or welfare thresholds, and which restrictions on the location graph change the classification?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the welfare hardness in Theorem 9, proved in this paper. It says that deciding whether an allocation has utilitarian social welfare at least \(\zeta\) is NP-complete and W[1]-complete when parameterized by the number of agents, even with binary, linearly increasing, or linearly decreasing utilities; the same classifications hold for egalitarian and Nash welfare under those utility restrictions. Its linear-increasing USW reduction uses \(K\) identical agents with \(u(q)=q\), sets \(\zeta=K(K-1)\), and uses the input graph of \(K\)-Clique as the topology. I would anchor on that slice alone.

A plausible regime is a large campus or compute cluster with many similar people or jobs and repeated location classes: each class contains many interchangeable slots, with adjacency between classes inherited from a finite graph. Everyone has the same complete preference type, \(u(q)=q\), so the number of types is \(\tau=1\) even as the population grows. Repeating the slots as the cohort grows is the co-modification that makes population mass meaningful while preserving the paper’s local-neighborhood question.

My lead mirror is Continuous Network USW at the clique threshold. Its input is a simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) of location classes and an integer \(K\ge2\), with \(|V|\ge K\). Let \(\mu_v\) be the fraction of the society’s unit mass assigned to class \(v\), with \(\sum_v\mu_v=1\) and \(0\le\mu_v\le1/K\). The cap represents per-capita slot supply: in a finite clone with \(qK\) agents, each class has \(q\) slots. A person in class \(v\) gets utility \(U_v(\mu)=K\sum_{w\in N(v)}\mu_w\), the neighboring population in agent-equivalent units. The question is whether a real mass assignment \(\mu\) exists with total welfare

\(\mathcal W_K(\mu)=K\sum_v\mu_v U_v(\mu)=K^2\sum_v\mu_v\sum_{w\in N(v)}\mu_w\ge K(K-1).\)

A solution is the mass vector itself. This is the underlying welfare-allocation problem, not a continuous encoding of it. Rational masses can be realized by replicating each location class enough times and clearing denominators.

This threshold still captures the graph hardness despite allowing fractional mass. Set \(y_v=K\mu_v\); then \(0\le y_v\le1\), \(\sum_v y_v=K\), and \(\mathcal W_K=y^\top A_Gy\). A \(K\)-clique gives welfare \(K(K-1)\) by placing one unit on each of its vertices. Conversely, for \(p=y/K\), the Motzkin–Straus bound gives \(p^\top A_Gp\le1-1/\omega(G)\). If \(G\) has no \(K\)-clique, then \(\omega(G)\le K-1\), so welfare is at most \(K^2(1-1/(K-1))<K(K-1)\). Thus this continuous threshold problem is equivalent to \(K\)-Clique: it is NP-complete and W[1]-complete parameterized by \(K\). I expect Class B, hardness transfers. The combinatorics remain in the graph’s adjacency structure, and fractional assignment cannot cross this threshold without a \(K\)-clique.

The mirror covers only Theorem 9’s USW, linearly increasing branch; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s stability results or its ESW and NSW results. It raises natural next questions: how arbitrary thresholds and other utility functions behave under fractional assignment, and whether topology restrictions such as bounded treewidth make the mass problem tractable.

The weakest point is that this is an extension using replicated location classes and divisible mass, rather than a literal continuation of a fixed graph of indivisible offices. An author who considers individual vertices essential might reject that co-modification. The case for it is that the paper permits arbitrary topologies, and the extension preserves its defining ingredients—agents at locations, utility from occupied neighbors, and aggregate welfare—while giving the growing population a concrete slot-supply interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror does substantial modelling work. Theorem 9 takes \(K\) agents and a graph \(H\); the mirror turns the vertices of \(H\) into location classes, gives each class capacity \(q\), and makes the mass assignment \(\mu_v\) the optimization variable. The input society is a single preference type, while the nontrivial distribution is over locations. The result is a nonatomic quadratic facility-allocation problem on a specially structured, replicated topology. Motzkin–Straus shows that its threshold question retains the clique problem exactly. That limits the mirror’s reach and makes its computational answer look familiar.

Those limitations do not defeat this anchor under the programme’s criteria. A society of interchangeable workers or jobs with \(u(x)=x\), assigned to repeated slots in a network, is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Replicating each location class \(q\) times gives a finite realization, and the mass model is its large-cohort limit. The topology still matters because neighbor mass determines utility; the mass vector describes where the continuous population is placed. The paper’s anonymous agents and aggregate welfare objective make this a natural extension, and one surviving result is enough.

The Motzkin–Straus connection may reduce the amount of new complexity theory needed: the mirror appears to have a direct Class B answer. But the programme explicitly counts a discrete-to-continuous identity as a worthwhile result, and an outside solution does not invalidate a well-posed mirror. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case rests on the mirror applying to a particular replicated-topology regime, and the proponent has given a plausible regime of exactly that kind. The case against the paper is therefore weak.
