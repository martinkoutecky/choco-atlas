The continuous mirror question

Given a connected planar type graph \(H=(T,F)\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational edge weights \(w_{tu}\), and a rational threshold \(B\), partition the atomless mass at each type into coalitions whose positive-mass type supports induce connected subgraphs of \(H\). Decide whether the supremum of \(J=\sum_{\{t,u\}\in F} w_{tu}\sum_{A}\lambda(A\cap I_t)\lambda(A\cap I_u)\) is at least \(B\), where \(I_t\) is the type-\(t\) mass interval.

The model it lives in

Each type is a network site carrying an atomless cohort of interchangeable agents. Adjacent sites interact all to all; in an \(N\)-agent realization, each cross-site pair contributes \(w_{tu}/N\), and zero-value within-site links can make connected type support correspond to connected coalitions. A partition assigns mass slices to coalitions, and its normalized edge value is the sum of \(w_{tu}\) times the within-coalition mass products.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s planar edge-sum hardness result. It leaves the separator bounds and polynomial-time results for special graph classes, including Theorems 2, 3, and 4, untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the supremum be represented by a finite description and attained for rational input?
  • Does the planar edge-sum problem remain hard when mass can split among coalitions, or does the fractional structure yield an efficient optimization method?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a continuous version of the paper’s edge-sum coalition problem on a network of large, interchangeable cohorts.

The anchor is Theorem 5, proved in Section 4.2. It says that finding a maximum-value connected coalition structure is NP-complete even when the graph is planar and the valuation is edge-sum: a coalition’s value is the sum of the weights of the edges between its members. The theorem also gives a reduction from 3-SAT with \(m\) clauses to such a problem on a planar graph with \(O(m^2)\) vertices. That is the result I would mirror.

Consider a network whose vertices are sites or roles, with one large cohort of interchangeable agents at each site. A type specifies the site and the agents’ full interaction profile: everyone of that type has the same network links and the same pairwise values. Let \(H=(T,F)\) be the planar graph of type sites and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the population at site \(t\). A plausible regime is a large modular service or communications network: there may be many sites, but vastly more agents than sites, so the number of agents \(N\) grows much faster than the number of types \(\tau=|T|\). Each coalition must occupy a connected set of sites. This keeps the paper’s connected-coalition question while treating the population at each site as divisible mass.

Here is the resulting problem, Planar Mass Edge-Sum Coalition Generation. The input is a connected planar type graph \(H=(T,F)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational edge weights \(w_{tu}\), and a rational threshold \(B\). Represent the society by disjoint intervals \(I_t\) of lengths \(\mu_t\). A feasible solution is a measurable partition \(\mathcal P\) of these intervals, up to null sets, where every positive-mass coalition \(A\) has a type support
\[ S(A)=\{t:\lambda(A\cap I_t)>0\} \]
whose induced subgraph in \(H\) is connected. Its normalized edge-sum value is
\[ J(\mathcal P) = \sum_{\{t,u\}\in F} w_{tu} \sum_{A\in\mathcal P} \lambda(A\cap I_t)\lambda(A\cap I_u). \]
The decision question is whether \(\sup_{\mathcal P} J(\mathcal P)\ge B\). The optimization version asks for this supremum and a partition attaining it when one exists. The rational input’s bit length is \(L\); complexity should be measured in \(\tau\) and \(L\), not in the number of agents represented by the mass. The product of masses is the natural normalized pair value: if each cross-site pair contributes \(w_{tu}/N\), then the total edge value divided by \(N\) is exactly the expression above.

This is a high-multiplicity version of the authors’ problem, not a claim that their planar hardness proof already applies to it. It preserves connected coalitions and additive pairwise edge value; mass splitting is the central change. The type graph is the network over which coalitions connect, and agents at one site can be linked through a shared local hub. That makes it a reasonable object for authors who motivate graph coalition formation with networked agents and resources.

For this problem, I would ask a prover to investigate Class C: hardness for continuum-specific reasons. The type graph and its coalition-consistency constraints remain part of the input as \(\tau\) grows, while the objective couples the masses assigned to each coalition. But I would not claim hardness transfer from Theorem 5: its reduction assigns individual vertices to coalitions, and a type’s mass can now be split among several coalitions. Establishing hardness would require a new reduction that survives that splitting. A finite-dimensional LP or convex reformulation could instead make the problem tractable.

The weakest point is the network coarsening itself. The paper’s planarity applies to its graph of individual vertices; here planarity applies to the type-site graph, while a many-agent network built from cohorts need not be planar at the individual level. The mass-product valuation also assumes that interactions between adjacent cohorts aggregate pairwise. Those are defensible modeling choices for a network of interchangeable agents, but they are not forced by Theorem 5.

This mirror covers only the planar edge-sum hardness result. It does not claim to mirror Theorem 3’s separator bound or Theorem 4’s polynomial cases. The next questions are whether optimal partitions admit a finite description, whether rational inputs yield an effectively representable optimum, and whether the paper’s planar gadgets can be adapted to prevent fractional splitting from erasing their logic.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the one anchor, and the strongest case against it is that a type-site model changes the paper’s network in a substantial way. In the paper, vertices are the individual elements being partitioned, and each edge and its weight describe a particular relation between two such elements. In the proposed model, a type edge stands for every cross-cohort pair: with \(N\mu_t\) agents at site \(t\), it creates \(N^2\mu_t\mu_u\) ties to site \(u\), each weighted \(w_{tu}/N\). That mean-field scaling does give the proposed objective after dividing by \(N\), but it turns the graph into a dense block network. The paper’s communication-capacity and trust examples more naturally have particular, sparse links; their vertices generally do not come in large groups with identical interaction profiles. The SAT gadget’s vertices are likewise distinct logical roles, not repeated members of cohorts.

The best version of the mirror would make that block-network assumption explicit: cohorts are types, adjacent types interact all-to-all, and within-type links can be added with zero value so that coalition connectivity matches connected support. This is mathematically coherent and makes population mass meaningful. But it is a new regime imposed on the graph, rather than an obvious high-multiplicity version of the sparse individual-level instances behind Theorem 5. The theorem’s reduction does not establish hardness there: its gadgets use indivisible vertices, and mass splitting may erase their logic. That leaves a real question about the new model, but the case against is that its relevance to the paper’s graph problems depends on whether the authors would recognize dense cohort networks as their problem.

That objection is limited. Theorem 5 is a named computational result, and a planar type graph of large interchangeable cohorts is plausible enough that I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror. The negative case is therefore weak: it challenges the connection to the paper’s motivating networks, but does not defeat the type-site formulation as a continuous computational question.
