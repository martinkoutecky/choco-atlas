The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a properly anchored positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as NP-hardness or tractability. Its results are simulation findings about coalition performance, not complexity results.

The most plausible continuous-population angle would be a large organization running many parallel projects. Types could describe workers’ expertise, capability sets, learning propensity, and utility weights; mass would be the workforce share of each type. A decision problem could ask how to reassign that mass over time among three-role teams to minimize the aggregate performance gap on the paper’s NK landscapes.

But that changes the paper’s central setup: it studies one coalition of exactly three selected agents at a time. In that setup, type mass mostly says whether a bidder type is available; it does not naturally determine the performance of the selected team. Making mass consequential by adding many simultaneous coalitions is a substantial extension, and the authors might not recognize it as their problem. So this is a possible research direction, not a defensible mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Its four findings—about \(K\), interaction structure, learning probability \(p\), and reorganization frequency \(\tau\)—come from simulations of performance. The paper asks how those parameters affect a team’s outcomes; it does not formulate a computational problem or establish an algorithmic or complexity result. A continuous-population analysis of expected performance could be useful, but it would be a new analytic study of the simulation model, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

The best version of the proposed mirror would describe a large pool by a distribution over complete agent types: expertise, known capabilities, utility weights, and current state. A mean-field model could track how that distribution changes through learning and forgetting, then model the auction selecting a team. That is coherent, and more defensible than treating the paper’s three team members as three masses. But with exactly one selected agent per expertise, the continuum weakens the role of mass: every type with positive mass is available, and the auction selects by expected utility. Copies of the same type do not change its bid or the team’s outcome. Finite-pool counts can affect order statistics or tie probabilities, but those are finite-sample effects; they do not by themselves supply a computational problem from the paper. This mirror could sharpen predictions about the simulated dynamics, but it does not give ChoCo a complexity question to chart.

Mass becomes more consequential if the model is extended to many simultaneous teams sharing a workforce. That is a plausible organizational setting, but it adds a system of projects and a workforce-allocation problem to a paper explicitly focused on one three-person coalition at a time. If the teams are independent, replicating them does not make their per-team dynamics depend on population mass. If they compete for workers, the coupling is new. Either way, a computational question about assigning workers or choosing a reorganization policy would be a worthwhile extension only if formulated; it is not one of this paper’s results.

So I would reject a green mirror for this paper under ChoCo’s stated computational remit. The negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational result and weaker as a universal claim about the subject: a large shared workforce could support a valuable continuous coalition-formation model. The point is that such a model would be new work, not a mirror of the results reported here.
