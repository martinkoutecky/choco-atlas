The continuous mirror question

Given balanced applicant and position populations with finite type supports \(A\) and \(P\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_p\) each summing to \(1\), and strict rankings by each type over types on the other side, run synchronous type-mass DA and ADA. Each position type has capacity \(\nu_p\), holds its highest-ranked applicant mass up to capacity, and may split a marginal type; when full, ADA also pre-rejects unmatched mass below its worst-held type. Compute the exact per-applicant proposal masses \(\Pi_{\mathrm{DA}}\) and \(\Pi_{\mathrm{ADA}}\), and determine whether \(\Pi_{\mathrm{ADA}} \leq \Pi_{\mathrm{DA}}\) holds for every such market, with complexity measured in \(|A|\), \(|P|\), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

A large balanced cohort represented by applicant and position types, each with rational population mass and rankings over opposite-side types. Matching is a flow of mass subject to position-type capacities; proposal burden is total proposal mass per applicant.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in Appendix A: for every balanced one-to-one market with complete strict preferences, men-proposing accelerated deferred acceptance uses no more proposals than men-proposing deferred acceptance. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar complexity classification. Theorem 3 is a named computational result, but it compares the operating cost of two algorithms rather than classifying a problem’s complexity.

A plausible mirror is Continuous Proposal-Mass Dominance. Take an atomless applicant population and an equal-mass population of one-slot positions, each described by a finite-support distribution over complete preference types. A type specifies a strict ranking of the types on the other side, and its mass is a fraction of the population. For example, this could represent a large annual hiring or residency cohort with many applicants and positions but relatively few preference and priority templates. The paper itself points to labour and hospital matching as settings where its proposal and round metrics matter.

The continuous algorithms would operate on mass. In each round, unmatched applicant mass applies to its highest-ranked eligible position type. Each position pool has capacity equal to its mass and holds the highest-ranked applicant mass it can accommodate. DA rejects the excess; ADA also pre-emptively rejects applicant mass below the pool’s current acceptance cutoff. Matching flows describe how much of each applicant type is assigned to each position type; they represent shares of a large cohort, with each infinitesimal agent still receiving one position. The instance consists of the two finite supports, their binary-rational masses summing to one on each side, and the rankings over types. The task is to return the exact total proposal mass under each algorithm and decide whether \(\Pi_{\mathrm{ADA}}\leq\Pi_{\mathrm{DA}}\). Total proposal mass is the cohort’s average number of applications per applicant.

This mirrors Theorem 3’s comparison of proposals, rather than claiming that the paper already studies a continuous market. I expect the universal dominance question to be Class A: the paper’s proof is structural, and the mass version should admit a rejection-and-cutoff invariant. A finite-support algorithm for exact proposal totals also looks plausible, though a polynomial bound in support size and input bit length would need to be proved. The mirror generates useful further questions: does the continuous version end at the proposer-optimal stable mass allocation, as the paper’s discrete mechanism does; does the round-by-round dominance of Theorem 5 extend; and what is the exact complexity of computing proposal savings?

My weakest point is the co-modification. The paper’s receivers are individuals with unit capacity, while this mirror treats same-type positions as a capacity pool with a common cutoff. That is a natural model for large cohorts and preserves ADA’s central idea—discarding applications that cannot be accepted—but it changes how individual rejections aggregate. The paper’s conclusion explicitly leaves richer matching environments for future work, which makes this a credible extension; still, authors or a referee could judge it too far from their one-to-one theorem. The case is also modest for ChoCo: it offers an aggregate computational question about proposal burden, not a hardness result whose complexity might dissolve under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and I would not dismiss it as “no computational result”: it is a named theorem about an algorithm’s performance. The narrower objection is that it compares two execution counts; it does not pose a complexity problem. The proposed continuous question turns that comparison into computing total proposal mass, but the mass statistic itself is a normalization of individual proposal counts. It does not, on its own, expose the kind of optimization or complexity boundary ChoCo is trying to chart.

There is a deeper modelling gap. In the paper, each participant ranks named individuals on the other side, and Theorem 3’s proof compares proposals against each man’s particular final partner. A fixed finite support of types across a growing market needs a different representation: participants rank counterpart *types* or pools. That replaces strict rankings of individuals with ties within pools, and replaces one-to-one pairs with mass flows into capacities. ADA’s cutoff and the resulting proposal paths then need new definitions. A hiring cohort with recurring worker and vacancy templates makes this a plausible extension, but it is a capacitated, type-ranked matching model; it is not simply the paper’s population made continuous.

The proponent’s best repair is to define that extension carefully and ask for exact proposal mass or its computation. That is coherent, and mass per applicant is a sensible quantity. But Theorem 3’s per-person comparison does not automatically establish the aggregate result once a type’s mass can split across held and rejected portions. Proving a mass analogue would require new reasoning about those splits; alternatively, retaining strict individual preferences risks making the type space grow with the named identities. The paper does flag richer matching environments as future work, so the authors might recognize the extension—but that makes it a weaker mirror of this theorem, not an obviously faithful one.

The proposed extension of Theorem 5 is weaker still. Round counts depend on synchronous updates and on when the last, possibly tiny, portion of mass finishes. They are sensitive to how types and cutoffs are represented, so their continuous counterpart would need a particularly well-motivated operational interpretation.

This is the strongest negative case I can make, but it does not defeat the hiring-cohort mirror outright. That scenario is plausible, and a well-defined mass matching model could be worthwhile. The case against is that its most natural formulation changes the market structure behind the theorem, while the paper’s clearest result concerns an individual-level algorithmic comparison rather than a complexity question.
