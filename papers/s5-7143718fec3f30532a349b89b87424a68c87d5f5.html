The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant types \(P\) and job types \(Q\), rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_q\), a compatibility graph \(E\), applicant rankings, and two scenarios with the same masses and graph that differ only in the ranking of one positive-mass type \(p^\star\), decide whether there is a single measurable matching \(M\) of the atomless populations such that, in each scenario \(s\), every feasible measurable matching \(N\) satisfies \(\Delta_s(M,N)\ge 0\). Each individual is matched to at most one compatible counterpart or left unmatched; \(\Delta_s\) is the mass preferring their assignment under \(M\) minus the mass preferring their assignment under \(N\), using the paper’s voting and abstention rules.

The model it lives in

Use atomless populations of applicant and job types with rational masses. A type-level flow \(x_{pq}\) along \(E\), with unmatched mass allowed and row and column totals bounded by \(\mu_p\) and \(\nu_q\), describes each matching. Popularity comparisons must also retain how the same individuals are assigned under \(M\) and a challenger \(N\); the decision objective is whether one matching is popular in both scenarios.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 14’s robust-popularity decision under cohort perturbations; it leaves Theorem 8’s one-sided model, Theorem 15’s multi-instance extension, and Theorems 16, 22, and 23 outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the joint assignment information needed for exact vote margins be represented with polynomially many variables in \(\tau\), graph size, and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Is robust popularity under a positive-mass cohort perturbation decidable in polynomial time, or does this extension become hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 14, proved in this paper. It says that, in the two-sided model with one-sided ties, deciding whether two instances admit a common popular matching is polynomial-time solvable when they have the same graph and differ only in one applicant’s preference order. The algorithm checks a popular matching that leaves that applicant unmatched, then uses hybrid instances to test popular matchings containing each possible edge incident to them. Its calls to the popular-matching algorithm of [8] are cited machinery; Theorem 14’s robust-matching result is the paper’s own.

My mirror is Robust Popular Matching for a Typed Continuum Market. The input is a finite set \(P\) of applicant types and \(Q\) of job types, with rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_q\), a compatibility graph \(E\subseteq P\times Q\), and strict rankings by each applicant type over its compatible job types. Every job type treats all compatible applicants as tied, as in the paper. There are two preference scenarios with the same masses and graph; only one applicant type \(p^\star\) changes its ranking. Complexity is measured in \(\tau=|P|+|Q|\), the graph size, and the masses’ bit length \(L\).

Represent each type’s population by an atomless interval. A feasible matching pairs measurable subsets of applicants and jobs along \(E\); each individual is either matched to one partner or left unmatched, though a type’s mass can be split across different assignments. The question is whether there is one such matching \(M\) that is popular in both scenarios. For every alternative feasible matching \(N\), popularity in scenario \(s\) requires
\[ \Delta_s(M,N) =\text{mass voting for }M-\text{mass voting for }N \ge 0. \]
Applicants vote by their rankings; jobs vote for the matching in which they are saturated when saturation differs, and abstain when it does not. The solution is the common matching itself, not a fractional bundle for any individual. The voter comparisons are between the same labelled individuals in \(M\) and \(N\); the type-level masses describe how many such individuals receive each assignment.

The regime is a large placement market with recurring applicant cohorts and job templates: many applicants share the same qualifications, compatibility set, and ranking, while many positions share a template. A change in information or conditions shifts the ranking of one whole cohort between scenarios. As the market grows, the number of people grows while the number of distinct types stays moderate. This is a deliberate cohort-level extension of Theorem 14’s single-applicant perturbation: a single person has zero mass in a nonatomic limit. The paper’s authors should recognize the resulting question as their robust-popularity problem applied to a type cohort, with the same matching feasibility, voting rule, and two-scenario requirement.

I expect this question to be Class A, though that is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 14. For a fixed candidate matching, a strongest challenger can plausibly be found by a weighted matching or flow LP. A type’s mass can be partitioned among its current partner types, giving finitely many assignment classes; the challenger’s votes and matching constraints can then be written over those classes. If that formulation has polynomial size, dualizing its separation problem could give a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau\) and \(L\). The key open step is proving that this finite description preserves all identity-level comparisons and yields a polynomial-size search for the common matching.

The main weakness is exactly that step. Theorem 14 handles one changing individual, not a positive-mass cohort, and its hybrid-instance argument does not immediately show how to handle a cohort split across several assignments. A type-level flow alone also loses which individuals receive which partners, information needed to compare votes. The mirror therefore needs a careful representation of those assignment classes; the finite algorithm is evidence for a tractable direction, not a proof of one. Further questions include whether the cohort version is indeed in P, how it relates to finite markets with many replicated agents, and how complexity changes when several types’ rankings vary. The paper’s cited hardness background does not provide a named hardness theorem for this anchor, so I would not use it to claim a hardness-transfer result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 14 is a genuine computational result, so the negative case cannot rest on there being nothing to mirror. Its most faithful high-multiplicity limit does, however, lose the feature that makes the theorem interesting: one applicant changes preferences. In a population of size \(n\), that applicant contributes at most \(1/n\) to a normalized popularity margin. In the atomless limit their vote has mass zero, so the preference change disappears from the popularity comparison. That limit asks only for ordinary popularity, not whether one matching survives a single applicant’s perturbation.

The proposed cohort version avoids that collapse by making every member of a positive-mass type change preferences. But this is a material change to the perturbation model. The paper treats one applicant and explicitly leaves perturbations by multiple agents as an open extension. A cohort shock is sensible, and the authors may well recognize it as that extension; it is not a limit of the one-applicant theorem. The direct mirror therefore faces a choice: preserve the theorem’s perturbation and get a vanishing effect, or keep a nontrivial effect by asking a broader question.

There is also a real modeling burden behind the proposed flow formulation. Type-level assignment totals do not alone say how the *same people* fare under a matching and a challenger: the vote margin depends on each person’s two assignments. One can define matchings as measurable assignments on a fixed population and track the joint assignment data, but that adds structure beyond the stated type masses. It may still yield a finite formulation; this is a challenge to the proposed tractability story, not a proof that the mirror is unworthy.

I cannot honestly make this a strong case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The cohort question is close to the paper’s stated future-work direction, and the mass version of its unpopularity-factor result also has a natural population interpretation. The defensible negative conclusion is narrower: the paper’s one-applicant robustness theorem has no nontrivial atomless limit without changing its perturbation unit. Whether the resulting cohort or mass-based problems count as worthwhile mirrors remains open, and the proponent’s cohort mirror plausibly survives.
