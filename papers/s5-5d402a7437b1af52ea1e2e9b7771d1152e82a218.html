What the mirror covers

The question covers Proposition 5.1's single-winner average-welfare guarantee; it does not cover the paper's moment-identifiability results, risk-adjusted or Nash welfare, or committee selection.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is welfare selection from a high-volume population of preference types. Imagine a large user base evaluating AI responses in an embedding space. A type is a user’s complete, normalized utility vector \(\theta\in S^{d-1}\); mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with each type. In a ChoCo-style instance, one could use a finite-precision set of utility profiles with rational masses and support size \(\tau\ll N\), where \(N\) is the number of users. A query is a comparison direction \(q\), realizable as a difference between response embeddings, and the user returns \(1\) exactly when \(\theta^\top q\ge 0\). The decision is which candidate \(\phi\) to select, maximizing
\[ \operatorname{SW}_{\mu}(\phi)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\theta_t^\top\phi. \]
This is a plausible population regime, though the paper’s results require the embedding space to realize every query direction.

The closest named anchor is Proposition 5.1, proved in this paper. It says that with one pairwise query per sampled voter and \(T\in O\!\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta\right)\) arriving voters, social welfare can be estimated to additive error \(\varepsilon B\) for every candidate with \(\|\phi\|\le B\); among \(\ell\) candidates, the method selects one within \(2\varepsilon B\) of optimal welfare with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The continuous problem corresponding to that result is: given a finite candidate set, a sampling oracle for users drawn from the society, and one comparison query per sampled user, output a candidate meeting that welfare guarantee. Its solution is the one-query sampling and estimation protocol. The theorem supplies a sample bound; an explicit implementation can estimate the mean vector and scan the candidate list, but the proposition does not state a running-time classification.

I would expect this problem to be tractable in the statistical sense the paper establishes. It does not provide an NP-hardness anchor, a discrete hardness reduction, or a computational-complexity classification of the kind the continuization programme is designed to map. Its scope is Proposition 5.1’s single-winner, average-welfare result; I would not stretch this case to cover the paper’s other objectives.

The central weakness is also decisive for novelty: the paper already models the population as an unknown distribution over voter types and asks how to select candidates from information about that distribution. The proposed question is therefore almost exactly the paper’s own problem, rather than a new continuous mirror of a discrete computational result. Moreover, the paper assumes an absolutely continuous distribution on the sphere for exposition; a finite rational-support, high-multiplicity version is plausible, but it is an adaptation, not the stated regime. Under the requested anchor rule, Proposition 5.1 is a useful near-match, not a named complexity-theoretic anchor. The case is strongest as evidence that this is a sensible continuous-population problem—and weakest as a claim that the paper leaves a continuous mirror to discover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational-complexity result to mirror. Proposition 5.1—the other analyst’s sole anchor—bounds how many sampled voters are needed to estimate welfare and choose a near-optimal candidate. Its resource is sample size, not running time; the input is access to voters drawn from an unknown \(\Theta\), not an explicitly encoded society with support size \(\tau\) and rational masses. The other results likewise concern identifiability, estimation, or approximation from moments. Recasting any of them as a worst-case algorithmic problem would change the question, not continue a discrete computational result.

That change also runs into a collision at the level of the model: the paper already takes the population to be a distribution over voter types and studies social choice objectives under that distribution. Its motivating users are expressly a large, repeatedly sampled population. The proposed finite rational-support version is sensible, and relaxing the paper’s absolute-continuity assumption need not be a serious obstacle. But for Proposition 5.1, giving the mass table replaces the sampling problem with computing a weighted mean and maximizing its inner product over the candidate list. Keeping the sampling interface returns to the problem the paper already studies. The stronger mirrors for variance-aware welfare and committee selection fare similarly: the paper already formulates those objectives over \(\Theta\) and connects them to its moment-recovery results.

One could add constraints on the candidate space or study the runtime of specific estimators. That might lead to worthwhile work, but the paper does not define those computational problems, and their difficulty would come from the added representation or constraints. It would be a new project on top of an already continuous population model, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result. The negative case is strongest on that boundary; it does not show that no useful computational study of this model is possible.
