The case FOR (proponent)

The honest answer on anchors is zero. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says ABNN is incentive compatible for the stated single-unit and diminishing-marginal-utility settings. Theorem 2, also proved here, says its loss never exceeds the auctioneer’s chosen cap \(S\). Theorem 3 says it resists false-name bids when an agent impersonates another agent on the same side of the market; the paper says this guarantee does not extend to impersonating someone on the other side. These are mechanism-property results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or has some other complexity classification. The simulation results are not named complexity theorems either. So I cannot honestly present any of them as a required anchor.

There is still a plausible computational question close to the paper’s own problem. Call it ABNN Threshold Design\(_\infty\). A natural setting is a large permit market: many firms trade permits, and many share the same per-unit value or cost schedule. A type records a firm’s full marginal-value vector or marginal-cost vector, including its unit limits; mass is the fraction of firms of that type. Give the instance a finite list of market scenarios \(\omega\), each with rational buyer and seller type masses, and rational scenario weights \(\pi_\omega\). Let \(\tau\) be the total number of distinct types across those scenarios, with the number of firms much larger than \(\tau\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime, though it is not the only one.

For a precise restricted version, use single-unit firms. In scenario \(\omega\), let \(b_\omega(q)\) be the descending buyer-value quantile and \(s_\omega(q)\) the ascending seller-cost quantile over traded mass \(q\). The decision is a pair of rational, piecewise-constant threshold schedules \(p_b(q),p_s(q)\), each with at most \(K\) pieces, with \(p_b\) nondecreasing and \(p_s\) nonincreasing as in the paper. The induced eligible buyer and seller masses are
\[ d_\omega=\sup\{q:b_\omega(q)\ge p_b(q)\},\qquad r_\omega=\sup\{q:s_\omega(q)\le p_s(q)\}, \]
and the traded mass is \(q_\omega=\min(d_\omega,r_\omega)\). Use the continuum limit of ABNN’s critical-price rule: at the marginal rank, the buyer price is the greater of the next buyer value and the buyer threshold, and the seller receipt is the lesser of the next seller cost and the seller threshold. Ties are split by mass. The instance asks for threshold schedules maximizing expected gains from trade,
\[ \sum_\omega \pi_\omega\int_0^{q_\omega} \bigl(b_\omega(u)-s_\omega(u)\bigr)\,du, \]
subject to a deficit of at most \(S\) in every scenario and nonnegative expected auctioneer budget. A solution is the schedules and their induced traded masses and payments. The design can also require individual truthfulness under the continuum mechanism’s critical-price interpretation.

That question has a real connection to the paper: it formalizes its parameter-setting discussion and its comparison of social surplus and auctioneer revenue over sampled markets. I would initially try for Class A when \(K\) and \(\tau\) are moderate, by reducing the finite-support quantile curves to a finite optimization problem. With unrestricted schedule complexity, continuum-specific hardness is possible. This is a conjectural direction, not a result of the paper.

The paper’s Theorems 1 and 2 would motivate constraints in that design problem, but they do not establish its computational complexity; thus the question is an unanchored follow-on, not a mirror of a qualifying named result. It covers the paper’s ABNN design and parameter evaluation, not Theorem 3’s identity-based false-name guarantee. My weakest point is that unilateral incentive compatibility and false-name manipulation do not automatically survive a nonatomic population model: in particular, the continuum critical-price formulation would need its own precise incentive definition and proof. The lack of any named computational anchor is the larger limitation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves incentive compatibility, Theorem 2 proves a loss bound, and Theorem 3 proves a restricted false-name-resistance property. All three concern what the mechanism guarantees; none classifies the complexity of computing an outcome or solving another computational problem.

The continuum versions of those properties do not supply the missing computational anchor. For Theorem 1, a single participant has zero mass and does not move the market’s aggregate quantiles. One could retain a personal trade decision through critical prices, but that requires formulating a nonatomic, price-taking incentive notion; it is not the paper’s finite-agent deviation statement. Theorem 2 may become an integral budget bound once the cap is scaled for a mass market. That remains a guarantee about the mechanism, not a complexity question. Theorem 3 depends on tracking whether several reports belong to one individual. A distribution over report types loses that ownership information: the same distribution could come from distinct firms or from one firm splitting into aliases. Adding ownership structure would require a different model.

The proposed permit-market scenario is credible. The paper itself names pollution permits, and many firms could share a small set of value or cost schedules. So absence of a plausible high-multiplicity regime is not a sound objection here.

The threshold-design problem is also a plausible follow-on. It builds on the paper’s parameter discussion and simulations, but adds a scenario distribution, scenario-by-scenario loss limits, and a bound \(K\) on schedule complexity. Those choices define a new optimization problem; they do not turn one of the paper’s theorems into a computational result. That may still be worthwhile research, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as a natural extension.

So the negative case is strong if “mirror” means a continuous computational question anchored in a named result of this paper. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists: the threshold-design proposal survives as a plausible new direction.
