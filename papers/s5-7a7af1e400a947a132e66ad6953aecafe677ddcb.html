The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(I\), position types \(J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), strict rankings over opposite-side types and being unmatched, and a feasible baseline mass matching \(q^0\), form the auxiliary market whose acceptable pairs \(E(q^0)\) are exactly the type pairs whose members prefer one another to their baseline assignments, preserving their rankings among those pairs. For feasible mass matchings \(x\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\beta_j\), and \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(E(q^0)\), determine the minimum and maximum of \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E(q^0)}x_{ij}\) over stable \(x\), and return flows attaining both extrema. Stability means there is no pair in \(E(q^0)\) for which positive mass on each side is unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred type.

The model it lives in

A two-sided atomless cohort market: preference and role archetypes carry masses, \(q^0\) is the baseline mass assignment, and \(x_{ij}\) assigns pair mass in the auxiliary market. The continuous measure is the total objection pair mass, \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers finding permissible sets under Proposition 3 and tests the invariant-measure idea behind Proposition 2 by asking whether stable objection mass is unique. It leaves the bargaining-set behavioral interpretation, empirical validation, comparison with all blocking pairs, and later-stage dynamics unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do all stable mass matchings in the auxiliary market have the same total pair mass, as the finite result suggests?
  • Can the minimum and maximum stable objection masses be computed in time polynomial in the number of types and the binary input length \(L\)?
  • What finite-to-continuous correspondence holds when a discrete market contains repeated preference and role types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: Biermann’s measure is a concrete matching quantity, and the paper gives a way to compute it. The lead mirror is the continuous computation of permissible-objection mass.

The best named anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that, for an individually rational unstable matching \(\mu\), a set \(D\subseteq B(\mu)\) is permissible exactly when it is a stable matching in an auxiliary marriage market. In that market, each player ranks the blocking partners above being single, preserving their original order, and ranks nonblocking partners below being single. Section 6 uses this characterization to find permissible sets: compute stable matchings in the auxiliary market, or use Gale–Shapley to find one when only the measure’s cardinality is needed. Those algorithms are cited from elsewhere; Biermann does not state a complexity-class theorem or prove a running-time bound. So this is an algorithmic characterization, not an NP-hardness or “in P” result. The paper’s separate Proposition 2, also proved here, says that all justifiable objections against an individually rational matching involve the same set of players. That supports the interpretation of the measure but is not a complexity anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large entry-level labor market: for example, tens of thousands of applicants and positions, with perhaps hundreds of preference and role archetypes. An applicant type records a strict ranking over position types; a position type records its ranking over applicant types. To evaluate a particular existing matching, refine types as needed so that everyone in a type has the same incumbent partner category as well as the same preferences. The input gives each type’s rational mass and the baseline matching. The number of agents can be far larger than the support size \(\tau\).

Here is the concrete question: Continuous Permissible-Objection Measure. Given those type masses, rankings, and a baseline matching \(q^0\), let \(B(q^0)\) be the type pairs that mutually prefer one another to their respective incumbent partners. Construct the auxiliary preferences as in Proposition 3: preserve rankings among blocking partners, place them above being single, and place nonblocking partners below being single.

A solution is a mass matching \(x=(x_{ij})\) in that auxiliary market. It must satisfy \(x_{ij}\geq0\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\beta_j\), and \(x_{ij}=0\) unless \((i,j)\in B(q^0)\), where \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) are the two sides’ type masses. It must also be stable: for each type pair \((i,j)\), it cannot be that positive mass on both sides would prefer to be matched to the other type rather than to their current assignment, including being unmatched. Ask for the minimum and maximum of \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\) over stable mass matchings, along with flows attaining both values. If those values coincide, the result is a well-defined continuous counterpart to the paper’s measure; if they differ, the range exposes a genuine failure of the finite uniqueness property.

This is population continuization: masses describe the society, and the allocation is the matching decision. Fractional type-level allocations are part of representing a continuum of individually matched agents. I think the authors could recognize the question as an extension of their measure: it asks how much mass can take part in a simultaneously feasible first-stage objection. The derivation of the auxiliary market follows their Proposition 3 closely.

I would expect a Class A result, provided the algorithm runs in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length. Stable-matching structure gives a plausible route to computing such a flow without expanding each type into individual agents. But that is a conjecture about the continuous problem, not a result in this paper. The question also opens the direct analogue of Proposition 2: do all stable mass matchings in the auxiliary market have the same total mass? A finite-to-continuous dictionary and the dependence of the algorithm on \(\tau\) and the rational bit length would be natural follow-ups.

The scope is Proposition 3’s computation of permissible sets and the paper’s measure of matching comparison. It does not extend the paper’s empirical claims, bargaining-set interpretation, or analysis of later market dynamics.

The weak point is that this paper contains no named hardness result and no named theorem classifying a computational problem as tractable. Proposition 3 supplies a route to computation, but the continuous stable-flow algorithm and the uniqueness of its total mass still need to be established. There is also a modeling step from preferences over named individuals to preferences over counterpart types. The large-cohort labor-market scenario makes that step plausible, but it is the part most likely to draw a referee’s objection.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is the only serious anchor. It characterizes permissible sets in a finite market by reducing them to stable matchings in an auxiliary market; Section 6 then invokes existing stable-marriage algorithms to find them. The paper gives no complexity theorem or running-time analysis for that computation. Proposition 2 adds a structural uniqueness fact about which players participate, but it also makes no computational claim. So the positive case reaches beyond the paper’s stated results when it presents continuous stable-flow complexity as their mirror.

The strongest modeling objection is that the market’s input cannot be just a distribution of preference types. It must also encode the baseline matching, since blocking depends on each agent’s incumbent partner. If preferences rank named individuals, faithfully grouping agents may require types that encode very detailed rankings and incumbent categories; the support could then grow until the compression disappears. A broader model with preferences over counterpart categories avoids that problem, but it changes the market’s preference structure.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror, though. A large cohort market in which applicants and positions rank role categories can have a modest type space and a baseline assignment represented as a mass flow. Fractional flows can represent actual pairings in an atomless population, and the authors could recognize the resulting question as an extension of their permissible-objection measure. Proposition 2’s finite claim that all justifiable objections involve the same players does not automatically give an invariant mass in this setting; asking for the minimum and maximum objection mass is a legitimate way to investigate that difference.

So the defensible negative case is narrow: the paper itself does not establish a complexity result, and the continuous-flow question is an extension whose value depends on a suitable type regime. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The cohort-category version survives the strongest objections; rejecting it would require relying on the answer one expects or on denying a plausible high-multiplicity setting.
