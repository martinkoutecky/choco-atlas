The case FOR (proponent)

The closest positive case is the paper’s alternative bankruptcy game. A natural large-population setting is a very large pool of creditors or taxpayers whose claims fall into a modest number of standard bands. Each creditor’s type is their claim \(d_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of creditors in that band. The number of creditors can be large while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) stays small. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the problem the authors study.

The nearest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for the game \(\tilde v(S)=\min(E,\sum_{i\in S}d_i)\), an efficient allocation \(x\), with \(\sum_i x_i=E\), belongs to the anticore exactly when every creditor receives between zero and their claim: \(0\le x_i\le d_i\). This is a clean characterization of the paper’s solution concept, but it is not a named computational result of the kind your instructions require. Theorem 1 gives no complexity claim or algorithm, and none of the paper’s other named results does either: Proposition 1 proves standard convexity, while Proposition 2 proves that increasing marginal contributions fail for bankruptcy games with at least three creditors.

A precise continuous analogue of Theorem 1 would be Continuous Bankruptcy Anticore Selection. An instance gives rational claim types \(d_t\), masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and normalized estate \(e\le\sum_t\mu_t d_t\). The continuum game assigns a measurable coalition \(A\) worth
\[ \tilde v(A)=\min\!\left(e,\int_A d\,d\mu\right). \]
The task is to return a per-creditor payout \(x_t\) for each type such that the total payout is \(e\) and no coalition can claim more than its worth. The natural candidate characterization is
\[ \sum_t\mu_t x_t=e,\qquad 0\le x_t\le d_t\quad\text{for every }t. \]
It preserves the population as the continuous object and mirrors the paper’s alternative game and anticore. A type-homogeneous allocation is sensible when creditors of a type have identical claims and no other distinctions used by the model.

This candidate is tractable: for positive total claims \(D=\sum_t\mu_t d_t\), the proportional payout \(x_t=e d_t/D\) is feasible; if \(D=0\), the instance assumptions force \(e=0\). Finding such a payout takes polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. Follow-up computational questions could optimize a linear welfare objective over these anticore allocations, or study how the feasible set changes under shifts in the claim distribution. Those would be extensions, not results established by this paper.

The weak point is decisive for the requested case: there is no eligible computational anchor. The proposed selection problem is a reasonable computational question extracted from Theorem 1, but the authors present a mathematical characterization, not a complexity result or a computation they analyze. So this is a plausible continuous mirror of their *subject matter*, but not a strong, rubric-compliant positive case for the programme’s computational agenda.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The claim-band scenario is credible: a large pool of creditors or taxpayers can be represented by masses \(\mu_t\) over claim types \(d_t\), with a normalized estate \(e\). The continuous game and the type-level allocation constraints are sensible. The obstacle is the paper’s contribution: Theorem 1 characterizes the anticore; Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 establish properties of games. None states a computational problem, algorithm, running time, or hardness result.

“Continuous Bankruptcy Anticore Selection” turns Theorem 1 into a task of returning an allocation, but that task is newly imposed by the analyst. Likewise, adding a welfare objective or asking for a Shapley allocation would create further computational questions the paper does not study. The paper’s core, anticore, and Shapley results are mathematical characterizations and identities, so their continuous counterparts would chiefly continue that structural analysis rather than chart computational complexity.
