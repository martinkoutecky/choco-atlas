Why no mirror

Theorem 2.2 classifies the probability that an AJR committee exists in sampled finite elections; it does not assert that an object can be computed or that computing it is hard. The proposed AJR feasibility problem over rational masses is coherent, but it cannot supply the missing computational character of this paper’s named result.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers AJR feasibility for exact mass societies and could recover the strict feasibility regions on the Erdős–Rényi mass family. It does not capture the finite-sample likelihoods at the thresholds or the paper’s discussion of other representation axioms and random models.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(m\), what is the complexity of deciding continuous AJR feasibility for an arbitrary rational-mass society?
  • How does feasibility for the exact Erdős–Rényi mass distribution relate to the finite-sample threshold probabilities, especially at the critical points?

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest anchor is Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper. There is no named P, NP-hardness, FPT, or similar complexity result here: the theorem is about the likelihood of AJR existence in random finite elections. It says that, for fixed \(m>k\ge2\) and fixed \(p\), as \(n\to\infty\), the probability that some AJR committee exists is \(1-o(1)\) when \(p< p_1^*=1/k\) or \(p>p_2^*\); it is \(o(1)\) when \(p_1^*<p<p_2^*\); and at either threshold it and its complement are both bounded away from zero. Here \(p_2^*\) is the maximum \(x\in[0,1]\) satisfying
\[ k\bigl(2x(1-x)^k+kx^2(1-x)^{k-1}\bigr)=1. \]
For \(k=2\), the thresholds coincide. This is the strongest relevant result to build on; I would not pad the case with a second anchor.

The mirror I believe in is Continuous AJR Feasibility. An instance has candidates \(M=[m]\), committee size \(k\), and a rational-mass society \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\subseteq M\), given by its positive support and binary rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to one. A type is an approval set: within this problem, that is the complete description of a voter’s preferences. Mass \(\mu_A\) means that fraction of the population has type \(A\).

The question is whether there is a committee \(W\subseteq M\), \(|W|=k\), that satisfies AJR. For a precise test, take any \(\ell\in[k]\) and any \(\ell\)-candidate set \(L\). A potential cohesive group can draw mass \(z_A\) from each type approving \(L\), where \(0\le z_A\le\mu_A\) for \(A\supseteq L\). If at least \(\ell/k\) mass approves \(L\), minimize
\[ \Phi_{L,W}(\mu)= \min\left\{ \sum_{A\supseteq L}z_A|A\cap W|: 0\le z_A\le\mu_A,\ \sum_{A\supseteq L}z_A=\ell/k \right\}. \]
The committee passes this constraint exactly when \(\Phi_{L,W}(\mu)\ge\ell^2/k\). The decision problem asks whether some \(W\) passes every such constraint; a yes-solution is that committee. Taking exactly quota mass is sufficient: among any larger underrepresented group, the lowest-utility quota-sized portion is also underrepresented. This preserves the paper’s underlying question—whether an AJR committee exists—while replacing voter counts with fractional mass. The committee remains an ordinary \(k\)-candidate set.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a very large electorate choosing from a small slate, with voters grouped by their recurring approval ballots. In the paper’s fixed-\(m\) setting there are at most \(2^m\) types, regardless of \(n\); thus \(n\) can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of occupied types. On the Erdős–Rényi family, the exact continuous society is \(\mu_A=p^{|A|}(1-p)^{m-|A|}\). The paper’s finite random elections are samples around that mass vector. For strict ranges of \(p\), the theorem’s threshold division should reappear as deterministic feasibility of \(\mu\). At a threshold, the exact mass society sits at equality in the AJR constraints, while the finite theorem’s mixed probabilities reflect sampling fluctuations around that boundary.

For the paper’s fixed-\(m\) regime, I expect this feasibility problem to be tractable: there are only constantly many committees and sets \(L\), and each \(\Phi_{L,W}\) is a rational LP with at most \(\tau\) variables. This gives an exact algorithm polynomial in the mass encoding length when \(m\) is fixed. It makes no claim about variable \(m\); there, committee selection could make the problem hard, but this paper supplies no hardness anchor for that claim. The mirror covers AJR existence and the Erdős–Rényi threshold phenomenon, not the paper’s other representation axioms or its conclusions for more general random models.

The weak point is real: the proposed question turns a probability about sampled profiles into deterministic feasibility for an exact mass distribution. At the critical points, it cannot preserve the theorem’s nontrivial likelihoods; those depend on finite-sample variation. So this is a strong mirror of the AJR existence predicate and its threshold structure, not a claim that the paper already studies continuous societies or that its full probabilistic theorem transfers unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole strong anchor is Theorem 2.2, and it establishes an asymptotic probability classification, not the complexity of a computational problem. Its input is an i.i.d. random election with fixed \(m,k,p\) and sample size \(n\); its output is the limiting likelihood that some AJR committee exists. No algorithm is given a society and asked to decide or optimize anything. That leaves no computational result here for ChoCo to continuize.

The proposed continuous AJR feasibility problem is well formed. Approval sets are sensible types, and the large electorate plainly has a high multiplicity regime. For the Erdős–Rényi family, the natural mass vector is \(\pi_A=p^{|A|}(1-p)^{m-|A|}\). Testing continuous AJR on \(\pi\) can recover the strict feasibility regions around the paper’s thresholds. But this replaces the random empirical society \(\hat\mu_n\) with its mean \(\pi\). At a transition point, the exact mass society sits on the weak AJR boundary, while the theorem’s distinctive conclusion is that finite elections fall on both sides with probabilities bounded away from zero. The deterministic test loses that result. Retaining it requires modeling the fluctuations of \(\hat\mu_n\) around \(\pi\), which makes sampling noise central again.

The strongest rescue is to study AJR feasibility for arbitrary rational-mass societies. That is a legitimate computational question, but it extends beyond what this paper studies. In the paper’s fixed-\(m,k\) regime, there are only constantly many committees and candidate sets \(L\), and each \(\Phi_{L,W}\) is an LP; the feasibility test is straightforward. Allowing \(m\) to vary could create a broader algorithmic problem, but the paper supplies no complexity result or variable-\(m\) motivation to anchor it.

So the negative case rests on the mismatch between the paper’s probabilistic contribution and ChoCo’s computational aim. A continuous mass model may give a useful deterministic interpretation of the phase diagram. That is the honest weak point in this case: recovering the same strict thresholds could be worthwhile in its own right. It still would be a new AJR feasibility study, rather than a computational mirror of the paper’s main result.
