The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. Lemma 3.1 bounds SENS’s loss over time, and Lemma 3.2 bounds TIME’s loss; both are proved here, but neither classifies a problem’s complexity or establishes an algorithmic guarantee. Algorithm 4.1 proposes a batching procedure without a stated runtime or performance result. The introduction’s claim that kidney exchange is NP-hard is unnumbered background, not an anchor. So there are no per-anchor continuous questions to supply.

There is still a plausible *unanchored* mirror. In a large national or regional exchange pool, many patient-donor pairs could share one of the paper’s 32 blood-pair and sensitization categories, or one of 64 categories after splitting by critical status. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of each type, with per-period arrival, progression, and perishing rates. In each period, choose nonnegative mass \(x_c\) for each compatible cycle \(c\) of length at most three, subject to the constraint that the cycles use no more than the available mass of any type. The resulting unmatched mass undergoes the stated health transitions and arrivals. One could ask for the attainable long-run loss under SENS-style priority (maximize highly sensitized mass matched each period) and TIME-style priority (maximize critical mass matched), and compare those losses.

For a finite horizon and an explicit type-level compatibility model, this is a finite linear optimization problem, so I would expect tractability. The main weakness is that type masses alone may not capture the paper’s random, individual-level compatibility graph: establishing that the fluid cycle allocations represent its large-pool behavior is substantive work. The authors might recognize the question as a fluid version of their clearing and fairness problem, but it cannot be presented as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case begins with the anchors: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 bound losses; they do not establish complexity or an algorithmic guarantee. Algorithm 4.1 proposes a batching heuristic but gives no runtime or performance result. The paper’s mention of kidney exchange being NP-hard is background, not a result about the fairness questions it studies.

The proposed mass model also has a real fidelity problem. Its 32 or 64 categories record blood type and sensitization, but those labels do not determine which pairs can form a feasible cycle: compatibility depends on pairwise crossmatches. In a dynamic pool, an unmatched pair’s actual compatibility neighborhood affects its future options, so category masses alone need not describe the state. A faithful type might have to encode a pair’s compatibility profile against the rest of the pool, making the number of types grow with the pool; alternatively, a stochastic compatibility kernel would require additional assumptions and a new model of how matches affect future states.

There is a tempting repair. Under a dense random compatibility model, one could use a finite kernel and take a fluid limit. But the paper already uses a dense-graph argument to reduce matching outcomes to aggregate counts, then derives loss bounds from those counts. The resulting continuous model may therefore be a compact recurrence or small optimization problem, rather than a new complexity landscape. In sparse pools, where matching structure matters more, the finite category summary is less credible. This leaves a difficult choice between a fluid model that may largely re-express the paper’s calculations and a richer dynamic model whose state and assumptions the paper does not supply.

That is a case against treating the proposed 64-type model as an established mirror. It does not justify the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large exchange pool is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a carefully specified stochastic fluid or compatibility-kernel model could make long-run fairness and loss computational questions. I cannot honestly rule out that stronger version; the negative case is strongest as a warning that the paper itself does not provide a qualifying computational anchor or a ready-made finite-type model.
