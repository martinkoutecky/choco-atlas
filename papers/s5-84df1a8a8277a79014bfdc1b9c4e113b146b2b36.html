What the mirror covers

No paper result is mirrored. The empirical comparisons in Tables 2–4 and the estimated voter-type proportions are left outside a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational result: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its findings are empirical comparisons in Tables 2–4, and its EM procedure estimates voter-type proportions; neither supplies the required anchor. So there is no honest anchor-based continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The best positive lead is an unanchored population model: let a type record a voter’s utilities for the five parties and the information they use about past vote shares; let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of voters of type \(t\). For each district magnitude, ask how the masses of sincere, strategic, and frontrunner voters—and the resulting vote shares—change under the paper’s behavioral assumptions. Large electorates with many voters sharing similar utility and information profiles make that population description plausible.

But this is a continuous behavioral or estimation question, not a computational-complexity question established by the paper. The main weakness is that a nonatomic population removes individual pivotality, which underlies the paper’s account of strategic voting. I would treat the model as a possible extension, not claim this paper provides a strong ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. It makes empirical claims about voting behavior and estimates latent behavior-type proportions with an EM procedure. Neither is a theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent’s unanchored lead—compute how the masses of sincere, strategic, and frontrunner voters change with district magnitude—is therefore an extension, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

That extension is easy to state: let a type include a voter’s utility vector, beliefs about other voters, and behavioral parameters, then ask how the vote-share distribution changes with district magnitude. But making it a faithful model requires more than assigning those types masses. In the experiment, voters act in groups of 25, observe aggregate results from earlier rounds, and use those results to form beliefs about whether their vote could matter. A deterministic continuum makes one voter’s influence zero: the aggregate shares fix the outcome, so the paper’s strategic-voting incentive disappears. Preserving finite-electorate pivotality would require an additional model of vote-count uncertainty or finite-size effects. That may be a reasonable new modeling choice, but it is doing essential work that the paper neither formulates nor studies.

There is another problem with treating the estimates as a population of stable types. The paper’s estimated shares of sincere, strategic, and frontrunner behavior vary with district magnitude. A fixed mass of behavioral types would miss that finding; letting each mass change with the treatment merely restates the empirical comparison. Modeling how the masses change endogenously would require a new behavioral or learning model, not a continuous computational problem extracted from the paper.

A large real electorate with recurring voter profiles is a plausible setting for such a future model. That makes the behavioral question potentially worthwhile in its own right, but it does not supply this paper with a ChoCo-style mirror: there is no computational task or complexity result here for continuization to reclassify. The case against is strongest for the paper as written; the broader claim that no rebuilt scenario could ever be worthwhile is necessarily less secure.
