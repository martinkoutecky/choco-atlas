The continuous mirror question

Given finite worker-type and task-type supports \(R\) and \(Q\), rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), population totals \(N_W\) and \(N_T\), and a fixed nondecreasing price table \(P\) with exactly two distinct values, find a feasible rational assignment of mass \(x_{r,q}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{r,q} P(s,\ell)x_{r,q}\), where \(q=(s,\ell,c)\). Enforce worker and task mass capacities \(N_W\mu_r\) and \(N_T\nu_q\), qualification \(r_s\ge\ell\), and affordability \(P(s,\ell)\le c\). For every eligible pair \((r,q)\), require that the mass of type-\(r\) workers unmatched or assigned below price \(P(s,\ell)\) and the mass of type-\(q\) tasks unmatched or assigned to workers with skill level below \(r_s\) cannot both be positive; determine the complexity in the support sizes and binary input length.

The model it lives in

A two-sided high-multiplicity matching market with worker skill-vector types \(r\), task types \(q=(s,\ell,c)\), rational masses, fixed prices, and assignment variables \(x_{r,q}\). Maximize revenue subject to capacity, qualification, affordability, and positive-mass cohort stability.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s two-price fixed-pricing allocation problem and, as a uniform-price subcase, Theorem 3. It leaves the pricing choice, truthfulness guarantees, and online arrival results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the two-price problem remain NP-hard when agents are represented by rational masses over worker and task types?
  • What is the complexity as a function of the type-support sizes and input bit length, and does bounded support admit an efficient algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s offline allocation problem with prices fixed. It keeps the paper’s workers, tasks, qualifications, prices, revenue objective, and stability requirement; it replaces lists of individual agents with distributions over repeated types. That is a high-multiplicity computational question, not a mean-field equilibrium question.

A plausible regime is a large crowdsourcing platform in which workers have publicly verified skill profiles and task owners repeatedly post from a small set of templates. A worker type \(r\) is their complete skill vector. A task type \(q=(s,\ell,c)\) specifies the required skill, minimum level, and maximum price it will accept. There may be millions of workers and tasks but far fewer distinct profiles and templates: write the populations as totals \(N_W,N_T\) and type distributions \(\mu,\nu\), with support sizes \(\tau_W,\tau_T\ll N_W,N_T\). The type definitions preserve everything the allocation problem uses; names and individual identities do not affect eligibility, preferences, or payment.

Lead mirror: Two-Price Stable Mass Allocation. Take the paper’s fixed, nondecreasing price table \(P(s,\ell)\), restricted to exactly two distinct prices. Let \(x_{r,q}\) be the mass of worker type \(r\) matched to task type \(q\). Require \(x_{r,q}\ge 0\), \(x_{r,q}=0\) unless the worker is qualified and the task can afford its price, and
\[ \sum_q x_{r,q}\le N_W\mu_r,\qquad \sum_r x_{r,q}\le N_T\nu_q. \]
A unit of mass represents an individual worker or task, still matched at most once; \(x\) can be fractional because a cohort can split across assignments.

Stability should rule out a positive mass of mutually blocking workers and tasks. For an eligible pair \(r,q=(s,\ell,c)\), let \(b^W_{r,q}\) be the unmatched mass of worker type \(r\), plus its mass assigned to tasks priced below \(P(s,\ell)\). Let \(b^T_{r,q}\) be the unmatched mass of task type \(q\), plus its mass assigned to worker types whose level in skill \(s\) is below \(r_s\). Require \(b^W_{r,q}b^T_{r,q}=0\) for every eligible pair. Thus, the instance asks for a rational mass allocation satisfying feasibility and stability that maximizes \(\sum_{r,q}P(s,\ell)x_{r,q}\). The computational question is whether this optimum can be found in time polynomial in the represented type supports and input bit length, or whether the problem remains hard in that compressed regime.

This mirrors Theorem 9: given workers, tasks, and a fixed pricing, the paper’s Allocation Problem is NP-hard even when the pricing uses only two distinct values. The authors say they prove this result; the proceedings paper defers its proof to the technical report. [Theorem 9 and the paper’s proof note](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/13299/13299-64-16816-1-2-20201228.pdf)

I would provisionally expect the mass version to be tractable in the high-multiplicity regime: divisible matching mass may remove some of the indivisible allocation choices behind the discrete hardness. That is a conjecture, not a claimed LP formulation. Stability introduces support choices—either one side of each pair has no mass preferring the other—which may retain hard combinatorics. A useful boundary question is whether hardness persists when population sizes grow while the number of types stays small. If it does, that would be evidence of continuum-specific hardness; if complexity tracks the number of represented types, the discrete hardness may dissolve in the regime this mirror targets. This mirror also raises questions about parameterization by \(\tau_W+\tau_T\), and about the gap between stable mass allocations and finite-market stable matchings.

A second, cleaner anchor is the uniform-price slice of the same model. Set \(P(s,\ell)=p\) for every task type and ask for a maximum-revenue feasible stable mass allocation \(x\). Theorem 3, proved in the paper, says that with a given uniform price, the discrete allocation problem is solvable in time \(O(S^2W+\min\{W,T\}(SW+T))\), using max flow and a stability adjustment. [Theorem 3](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/13299/13299-64-16816-1-2-20201228.pdf) For the mass mirror, I would expect a polynomial algorithm using rational capacities and a type-level flow construction; the point to prove is that the resulting allocation remains stable under the positive-mass definition. This gives a concrete tractable subcase alongside the harder nonuniform question.

The authors should recognize these as mirrors of their allocation problem: the matching, task affordability, worker and task preferences, stability, and revenue all stay in place. The scope here is deliberately the allocation problem with prices fixed. It does not continuize the paper’s pricing choice, truthfulness analysis, or online arrival model.

The soft spot is stability. In a continuum, a single worker–task pair has zero mass, so literal individual-pair blocking can become vacuous. Positive-mass blocking is the population-scale version: it rules out a cohort of workers and a cohort of tasks who can mutually improve by matching. That choice fits markets where the meaningful concern is a non-negligible group left with worse matches, but authors who insist that even one individual deviation must count may ask for a model retaining atoms. The mirror is strongest when that difference is made explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s stability guarantee is about individual worker–task pairs, while the proposed mirror guarantees only that no *positive mass* on both sides can block. If a single worker and task are left in a blocking position in a market of size \(N\), their masses are \(1/N\); they disappear in the atomless limit. Preserving the paper’s guarantee for such rare individuals would require retaining agent-level atoms, which gives up the continuous population model. This is a real change in what “stable” protects, especially since the paper motivates stability as preventing unfair treatment of a worker or task.

That objection does not defeat either anchor across all plausible scenarios. If fairness is meant to protect substantial cohorts, the proposed condition is a coherent stability notion. With finitely many complete types, everyone of a type has the same qualifications and preferences, so positive mass on both sides of a blocking type pair captures a genuine group that can improve by matching. The authors could reasonably recognize that as their allocation problem in a large market.

Theorem 9 remains a strong mirror. Divisibility may remove some of the discrete choices, but stability still imposes type-level disjunctions, so the mass problem need not reduce to an ordinary flow or LP. Whether hardness survives with a small type support is a worthwhile question under the programme’s own trichotomy; either answer would be informative.

Theorem 3 also survives. Its max-flow formulation appears to admit a direct version with rational capacities for worker and task types, followed by an aggregate stability adjustment. That may make the mirror straightforward to solve, but an easy computational answer is not itself a reason to reject the question.

So the individual-fairness objection gives a meaningful reason to narrow the regime: these mirrors are most convincing when the market cares about cohort-level stability and negligible populations may be ignored. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The negative case is weak against both anchors.
