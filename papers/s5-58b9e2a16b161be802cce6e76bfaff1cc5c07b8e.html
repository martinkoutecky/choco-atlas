The continuous mirror question

Given item classes \(J\), rational per-capita supplies \(b\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{J}\), and a common additive valuation vector \(u\in\mathbb{Q}^{J}\) that is all nonnegative (goods) or all nonpositive (chores), a unit-mass population receives integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\). Choose a finite-support distribution \(p_z\ge 0\) with \(\sum_z p_z=1\) and \(\sum_z p_z z=b\); compute the maximum \(q^*\) such that every bundle with \(p_z>0\) has utility \(u\cdot z\ge q^*\), and return a distribution attaining it.

The model it lives in

A society of mass \(1\) with one complete agent type and common additive valuation \(u\); item classes have per-capita supply \(b\). Variables \(p_z\) assign mass to integral bundle vectors \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\), subject to the supply constraints, and the objective is to maximize the minimum utility \(u\cdot z\) among bundles receiving positive mass.

The objection that survived

The proposed bundles \(S\subseteq J\) forbid multiple copies of a class in one recipient's bundle, although the paper permits this; integer bundle vectors \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\) are needed.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4.6's homogeneous-valuation hardness setting for goods and chores; it leaves the heterogeneous-type nonexistence results, round-robin approximation, and fixed-agent-count PTAS results outside this one-type model.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational \(b,u,q\), can one decide efficiently whether \(b\in\operatorname{conv}\{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}:u\cdot z\ge q\}\), or is this continuous allocation problem hard for its own pricing reasons?
  • How do the continuous optimum and the finite-population MmS values compare as the population grows with per-capita supplies fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is one-type continuous MmS decomposition. It mirrors the paper’s strongest relevant computational result, including the part of its hardness proof where every agent has the same valuation.

The anchor is Proposition 4.6. It states that computing an MmS allocation, when one exists, is strongly NP-hard for both goods and chores, and is weakly NP-hard even with two agents. The paper’s Appendix C gives the 3-Partition and Partition reductions; the chores result is established there using the goods–chores relationship in Proposition 4.2. The paper credits the goods hardness as already proved by Bouveret and Lemaître (2014). In the strong-hardness reduction, the agents share one additive utility function. That is exactly the homogeneous population regime I would mirror.

Here is the continuous problem. Let \(J\) be a finite set of indivisible item classes, let \(b_j\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) be the per-capita supply of class \(j\), and let \(u\in\mathbb{Q}^{J}\) be the common additive valuation vector. All agents have this one complete type, of mass \(1\); \(u_j\geq0\) for goods or \(u_j\leq0\) for chores. A bundle is a subset \(S\subseteq J\), so each recipient gets at most one unit of each class. The decision variables are masses \(p_S\geq0\) assigned to each bundle, satisfying
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq J}p_S=1,\qquad \sum_{S\ni j}p_S=b_j\quad\text{for every }j\in J. \]
The problem is to compute
\[ q^*=\max_{p}\ \min\{u(S):p_S>0\}, \qquad u(S)=\sum_{j\in S}u_j, \]
and return a finite-support allocation \(p\) attaining \(q^*\). For chores, maximizing the least utility means making the worst burden as small as possible. The bundle given to each positive-mass slice remains integral; only the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

This is the high-multiplicity relaxation of the identical-agent instance: with \(n\) agents and \(n b_j\) copies of each class, a discrete allocation gives bundle masses in multiples of \(1/n\). The continuum allows arbitrary real masses. A plausible regime is a large cooperative or service organization assigning standardized goods or chore slots to a large cohort with common, contractually fixed valuations. There, \(n\) can be large while the number of agent types is \(\tau=1\). For the paper’s 3-Partition construction, each original item can be its own class with per-capita supply \(1/n\).

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous form: it retains their max-min share objective and indivisible bundles, while replacing named recipients with mass. It does not try to mirror the whole paper. In particular, its fixed-agent-count PTAS results are not my anchors; a regime with fixed \(N\) is not the high-multiplicity question.

I expect the unrestricted exact mirror to be Class C, though that is a research hypothesis, not a hardness theorem. Its implicit configuration LP has up to \(2^{|J|}\) bundle columns. A dual pricing problem must optimize over bundles subject to a utility threshold, which has a knapsack-like shape and could itself be hard. But the discrete strong-hardness reduction does not simply transfer: on its 3-Partition instances, the average utility is \(W\), so any allocation achieving \(W\) must use only bundles of utility exactly \(W\). Those are triples, and the continuous feasibility question becomes a fractional exact-cover LP with only polynomially many candidate triples. Fractional mass may therefore remove the reduction’s integral partition obstruction. The interesting question is whether the general mirror remains hard for its own pricing reasons.

Further questions include whether that pricing problem has a polynomial separation algorithm, how the continuum optimum compares with the finite-\(n\) MmS values as \(n\) grows, and whether a multi-type version with type-specific MmS thresholds is tractable.

The weak point is that mass mixtures can change the answer: the original allocation assigns whole bundles to named agents, while this mirror permits arbitrary proportions of a bundle pattern. That is precisely the relaxation, but it means the paper’s NP-hardness is motivation for the question, not evidence that its hardness survives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed formulation, not to Proposition 4.6 as an anchor. Proposition 4.6 is a named computational result, and identical valuations give a natural one-type population regime. The paper’s definition also allows a recipient to receive several items of the same kind, which the proposed bundles \(S\subseteq J\) rule out.

For example, consider chores with two copies of class \(A\), each costing \(10\), and one copy of class \(B\), costing \(100\), split between two identical agents. The best allocation gives both \(A\) chores to one agent and \(B\) to the other, so the largest burden is \(100\). In the proposed model, \(b_A=1\) and \(b_B=\tfrac12\), but every bundle can contain at most one \(A\). Half the population must then receive \(A\) with \(B\), giving burden \(110\). As written, that model can therefore change the MMS value.

That defect has a natural repair: use integral bundle vectors \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\), and choose masses \(p_z\) satisfying \(\sum_z p_z=1\) and \(\sum_z p_z z=b\). Each person still receives an indivisible bundle; only the mass receiving each bundle varies continuously. A large cohort with common valuations and standardized item classes is a plausible setting for this model, and it is one the authors could reasonably recognize as theirs.

The paper’s strong-hardness reduction does not establish that the repaired mirror is hard: it uses \(3N\) distinct item classes, each with per-capita supply \(1/N\), and the fractional version may lose the partition obstruction. But that only weakens the case for carrying over the hardness result. Whether the continuous problem becomes tractable is exactly a worthwhile question under the programme’s terms. Nor do the paper’s fixed-agent-count scheduling PTAS results answer it: they concern finitely many machines, not a continuous population receiving integral bundles.

So the negative case is weak. The proponent’s subset-bundle model needs repair, and the paper’s reduction does not transfer directly; neither point defeats the better continuous mirror. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
