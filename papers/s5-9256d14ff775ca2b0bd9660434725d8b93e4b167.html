What the mirror covers

No computational result is mirrored. Theorems 1, 2, and 5 are axiomatic characterizations; Theorems 3 and 4 compare disagreement sets, and Theorem 6 characterizes rationalizations. The paper’s distributions over posterior beliefs describe predictions, not a continuous population.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a plausible population setting, not an anchored continuous computational problem. A large government employment-support programme could serve many people who share the same tastes and differ only in their predictions about future labor-market information. A type would specify those shared characteristics, and \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of participants of that type. The offered menus could be the paper’s finite sets of state-contingent acts.

That scenario fits the paper’s motivation, but its named results do not supply the required anchors. Theorem 1 characterizes BML preferences by the basic axioms, completeness for lotteries, transitivity, preference for flexibility, and dominance; it also establishes uniqueness of \(u\) up to positive affine transformation and of \(\Pi\). Theorem 2 gives the corresponding JML characterization using completeness, unambiguous transitivity, and favorable mixing monotonicity. Theorem 5 characterizes HML preferences using the basic axioms, completeness for lotteries, unambiguous transitivity, and reflexivity. These are axiomatic representation results, not results that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The rationalization results likewise provide representations, not algorithms for finite encoded inputs.

There is also a substantive obstacle to turning the employment scenario into a faithful mass-based mirror: BML, JML, and HML aggregate through unanimity, existence, and extrema over information structures or groups. If two populations have the same positive-mass types but very different proportions, these criteria can give the same comparison. Making proportions affect the collective judgment would require a weighted aggregation rule, which changes the paper’s central model.

So I would not claim a qualifying anchor or hand a per-anchor problem to a prover. The employment-program setting makes high multiplicity conceivable, but the paper contains no named computational result to mirror, and its existing criteria largely ignore population shares. Its continuum of information structures is continuity in predictions, not a continuized population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes BML preferences axiomatically; Theorem 2 does the same for JML; Theorem 5 characterizes HML. Their uniqueness claims do not change that. Theorems 3 and 4 compare disagreement sets, and Theorem 6 characterizes rationalizations. None poses an encoded computational problem or establishes its complexity. In particular, the results take sets of information structures as abstract objects; they do not specify an input representation, an algorithmic task, or parameters such as input length and support size. So there is no result here whose computational complexity a continuous population could mirror.

The employment-program scenario is plausible, and a type could sensibly include a member’s tastes and predictions. But to preserve the paper’s criteria, the population distribution would feed into the set of information structures and groups. BML, JML, and HML then judge menus by unanimity, existence, or extrema over those structures—not by how much population mass holds each type. Reweighting positive-mass types would leave the criterion unchanged. That is not by itself a reason the model is invalid; it does mean the paper supplies no computational question about those shares. Making shares affect the judgment would require a weighted aggregation rule, which changes the central behavioral model.

One could devise a better computational project: encode finitely supported forecasts and ask whether two menus are ranked, whether observed choices admit a BML or JML representation, or which menu a constrained program should offer. But each choice adds an input encoding and computational objective the paper does not study. It may be worthwhile work; it is not a continuization of Theorems 1, 2, or 5. The paper’s probability distributions over posterior beliefs are already continuous objects, but they describe predictions, not a continuously modelled population—and their presence is not a computational treatment of the proposed tasks.

So I would reject all three anchors as mirrors for ChoCo’s complexity programme. The negative case is strong on that boundary, but weaker as the universal claim that no useful extension could ever be designed: the paper leaves room for new computational questions, just does not provide a named computational result for one to mirror.
