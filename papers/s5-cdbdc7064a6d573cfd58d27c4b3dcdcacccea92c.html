What the mirror covers

The proposed limit question draws on Theorem 2, Theorem 4, and Corollary 5 to ask whether liquid price-of-stability bounds persist as the population and item supplies grow; it does not cover the other auction bounds, and none of these results is computational.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a sequence of repeated-item auctions with a fixed finite set of advertiser types attain the liquid price-of-stability bound \(2\) in the large-market limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is an asymptotic one. In a large platform auction, advertisers may fall into a small number of recurring classes with identical valuations and budgets. Let type \(t\) mean the complete pair \((v_t,c_t)\), and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s share of advertisers. Each advertiser bids on items; the social objective is liquid welfare, averaged over the population. If the number of item copies grows with the number of advertisers, the shares receiving each integral bundle can converge to fractional type-level averages. This continuizes the population, while keeping the paper’s strategic choices and welfare benchmark recognizable.

A precise candidate is the high-multiplicity liquid price of stability problem. Take a sequence of instances \(G_N\) from the paper’s auction class, with \(n_N\to\infty\) advertisers, a fixed finite set of types, \(n_{t,N}/n_N\to\mu_t\), and item supplies scaled with population size. Each advertiser of type \(t\) has the same subadditive valuation and budget. For each instance, let
\[ R_N=\frac{\max_Y LW_N(Y)}{\max_{b\in EQ(G_N)}LW_N(X(b))}. \]
Ask for the worst possible \(\limsup_{N\to\infty}R_N\) among such sequences, and whether a matching sequence attains the bound. A platform selling repeated impressions to many advertisers with a small number of shared valuation and budget profiles is a plausible regime. The actions remain bids; the objective remains the paper’s liquid-welfare ratio.

There is, however, no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in this paper. It proves no named result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally hard or tractable. Its named results concern equilibrium welfare. The closest anchor is Corollary 5, derived from the paper’s Theorems 2 and 4: for convex auctions in \(S\) with subadditive valuations, the pure liquid price of stability is at most \(2\), and this bound is tight. Theorem 2, proved here, gives the upper bound of \(2\) on pure liquid price of anarchy for every auction in \(S\) with subadditive valuations. Theorem 4, also proved here, gives a lower bound of \(2-\varepsilon\), for every \(\varepsilon>0\), on pure liquid price of stability for convex auctions with additive valuations. These are strong anchors for an equilibrium-welfare limit question, but they do not meet the requested computational-anchor requirement.

For the candidate limit question, the uniform upper bound of \(2\) follows from Theorem 2 on every finite instance, hence also bounds any limit. Whether the tight lower bound survives a genuine high-multiplicity regime is an open part of the proposed question; Theorem 4’s construction does not by itself establish that. The A/B/C complexity trichotomy does not fit this candidate, because it asks for an asymptotic welfare guarantee rather than computational complexity.

The weak point is substantial: with fixed item supply, an atomless population can leave each item allocated to zero mass. Scaling supplies and specifying how ties, rationing, and budget-constrained bundle outcomes behave is necessary to get a meaningful limit, and those choices can change the strategic game. So there is a plausible population-continuity research question here, but it is an equilibrium-analysis mirror, not a ChoCo computational-complexity mirror anchored by this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed high-multiplicity price-of-stability question has a plausible population model: advertisers can share a complete type \((v,c)\), and repeated impressions could scale with the population. I would not reject it on the grounds that agents lack multiplicity. The problem is that the anchors it uses—Theorem 2, Theorem 4, and Corollary 5—are all equilibrium-welfare bounds, not computational results. They bound ratios over games and pure equilibria; they give no algorithmic problem, running time, hardness result, or tractability boundary to continuize.

For Theorem 2 and Corollary 5, the proposed limit asks whether welfare guarantees persist for sequences of games. For Theorem 4, it asks whether a lower-bound construction persists in that regime. Those may be worthwhile equilibrium-analysis questions, but \(R_N\) and its \(\limsup\) describe welfare, not computation. Recasting them as a continuum of agents with per-capita impression supply may produce a sensible game-theoretic model; it does not turn the paper’s results into computational anchors.

One could instead define a new task, such as deciding whether a pure equilibrium exists or computing one above a liquid-welfare threshold. That would require choices about how subadditive valuations are represented, as well as the bid and equilibrium model. It could lead to valuable research, but it would be a new computational problem rather than a mirror of these theorems.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope: this paper offers no named computational result for a continuous-population complexity landscape to chart. It is weaker as a universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous study. A broader equilibrium-analysis project may be sensible; the anchors raised here do not make it a computational-continuization project.
