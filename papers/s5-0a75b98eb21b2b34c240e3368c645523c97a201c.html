Its numbered results are representation and existence theorems: for example, Theorem 4.3 proves the existence of an individually rational Pareto optimal partition under saturation, and Theorems 4.4–4.6 prove existence of core, Walrasian equilibrium, and Pareto optimal envy-free partitions under additional assumptions. The optimization problem in the proof of Theorem 4.3 establishes existence; the paper does not analyze its computational complexity.

The tempting mirror would be a large exchange economy whose types specify complete preferences and endowments, with rational masses and per-capita allocations. One could ask for an individually rational Pareto optimal allocation. But that would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

There is also a scope distinction: the paper makes the commodity a measurable, divisible object, while its society consists of a finite set of \(n\) individuals. Its saturation and purification results concern divisions of the commodity, not a continuous population. Those results may inspire a population-level model, but they do not supply the required anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case does not actually offer a positive mirror anchor: it names Theorem 4.3 and Theorems 4.4–4.6, then argues that none is computational. That is the strongest case against a mirror here. The paper’s numbered results establish representation and existence. Theorem 4.3’s maximization problem is used to prove that an individually rational Pareto optimal partition exists; it is not analyzed as an algorithm. The core and equilibrium results likewise rely on existence theorems. The purification result, Theorem 4.1, says fractional allocations have partition counterparts under saturation, but gives no computational problem or complexity claim.

The strongest candidate mirror would be a high-multiplicity exchange economy: finitely many complete consumer types, each specifying preferences and endowment, with rational masses; ask for a type-level allocation satisfying individual rationality and Pareto optimality, or core, equilibrium, or envy-freeness. That is a coherent scenario, and the resulting type-level averages are a legitimate co-modification of the population model. I would not reject it on the ground that fair division has no plausible large-population regime.

The problem is that this candidate is a new computational model, not a computational mirror of the paper’s results. The paper allows arbitrary measurable commodity spaces, Banach-valued measures, and continuous utility transformations. It gives no finite encoding for those objects, no representation of an output partition, and no oracle model. Its existence proof uses compactness and extreme points, not a procedure with a running time. To ask a complexity question, one must supply those computational choices from outside the paper. Restricting to finite goods or finitely encoded utilities may make a worthwhile problem, but it changes the inputs and can leave behind the saturation and purification structure on which these theorems depend.

So the negative case is strong for the claim that this paper supplies a ChoCo computational mirror: it supplies no computational result to mirror. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile population-continuous computational fair-division question could be built from the subject.
