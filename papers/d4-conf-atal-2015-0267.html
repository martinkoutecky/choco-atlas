The continuous mirror question

Given finite contexts \(\Omega\), action sets \(A_\omega\), optimal subsets \(Good_\omega\), and agent types \(t\) with rational vote probabilities \(q_{t,\omega}(a)\), decide whether a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over types exists such that, at every context, all actions in \(Good_\omega\) have equal vote mass and strictly exceed every action outside \(Good_\omega\), where \(v_{\mu,\omega}(a)=\sum_t\mu_tq_{t,\omega}(a)\). With uniform tie-breaking, this makes each optimal action equally likely at each context and each combination of optimal actions equally likely across independent contexts.

The model it lives in

A continuous team is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete agent-configuration types. Each type carries a per-context vote profile \(q_{t,\omega}\), and action \(a\) receives mass \(v_{\mu,\omega}(a)=\sum_t\mu_tq_{t,\omega}(a)\). Choose \(\mu\) to maximize a common positive gap \(\epsilon\), subject to equal vote mass for all optimal actions at each context and at least \(\epsilon\) more mass than every non-optimal action.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 samples teams from a fixed distribution \(F\), while the proposed feasibility problem chooses the type proportions \(\mu\); that changes the theorem’s mechanism, though team selection is central to the paper.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s diverse-team goal of selecting every optimal action with equal probability. It does not capture finite-team divisibility effects, the uniform-team results, or the synthetic and architectural experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under the paper’s sampling assumptions, when does the finite-team plurality outcome distribution converge to uniform tie-breaking over the optimal actions in the mass model?
  • When can a feasible balanced mass distribution be rounded to finite teams while preserving near-uniform coverage of optimal solutions?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named complexity result in this paper: it proves no theorem that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class, and gives no running-time analysis. Its formal results concern probabilities and team composition. So under your anchor rule, I cannot make a positive case grounded in an eligible anchor.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here. Under the paper’s random model for the numbers of optimal actions and for drawing agents from \(F\), let \(D\) contain one factor from each \(|Good_\omega|\). For arbitrary team size \(n\), the probability of drawing an optimal team tends to \(0\) as the number of world states \(|\Omega|\) grows. But if \(n=c\prod_{d\in D}d\), that probability tends to \(1\) as \(c\) grows. The theorem explains when a diverse finite team can select every optimal solution with equal probability. It does not establish a complexity result.

If that mathematical result is accepted as a broader anchor, my lead mirror would be Continuous Balanced-Team Feasibility. Consider a large design-search service running many copies of a small library of agent configurations. A type \(t\) is a complete agent behavior profile: for each decision context \(\omega\), it specifies the probability \(q_{t,\omega}(a)\) of voting for each action \(a\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). This regime has many agents but few distinct configurations. The mass-weighted vote for action \(a\) is
\[ v_{\mu,\omega}(a)=\sum_t\mu_tq_{t,\omega}(a). \]

The input consists of finite contexts and action sets \(A_\omega\), designated optimal actions \(Good_\omega\subseteq A_\omega\), and rational type profiles \(q_{t,\omega}(a)\). The question is whether there is a rational population mix \(\mu\) for which, at every context, all optimal actions tie for the plurality and every non-optimal action has strictly less vote mass. A solution is such a \(\mu\), with a positive gap certifying the strict inequalities. In the continuum limit, ties are broken uniformly, so each optimal action is selected with probability \(1/|Good_\omega|\); across independent contexts, every optimal combination is equally likely. For correlated design parameters, the paper’s Proposition 8 suggests treating a correlated block as one context whose actions are complete feasible bundles.

This question is tractable: the tie conditions are linear equalities in \(\mu\), and the strict gaps can be handled by maximizing a slack variable in a linear program. That is a plausible continuous version of Theorem 1’s goal of making all optimal solutions equally likely. It replaces the theorem’s integer team-size divisibility condition with fractional type masses.

The scenario is credible for replicated design-agent services, but the mirror changes the theorem’s central mechanism: it studies exact population shares instead of drawing a finite team whose size must satisfy divisibility constraints. That is my weakest point, and it means this is a plausible extension rather than a demonstrated continuous counterpart. The paper’s actual architectural experiments use four configurations, not a high-multiplicity population. Further questions would include how to round a feasible \(\mu\) into a finite team, how sensitive the balance is to estimated vote probabilities, and whether maximizing the number of balanced optimal actions remains tractable when full balance is impossible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor the proponent raises, and it is not a computational result: it gives an asymptotic probability statement about finite team sizes and divisibility. The paper proves no complexity classification, algorithm, or running-time bound for a computational problem. So the proposed mirror has no qualifying result in this paper to mirror.

The strongest version of their idea still changes the question. Theorem 1 takes an agent distribution \(F\), samples a team of integer size \(n\), and studies the chance that the sampled team balances its votes. Continuous Balanced-Team Feasibility instead chooses a mass vector \(\mu\) that makes expected vote shares balance. That is an intervention on the population mix, not the theorem’s analysis of sampling from a given distribution. If \(\mu\) is fixed to \(F\), a continuum of weighted votes captures expected shares, but not the finite-sample variation that determines which action wins when expected shares tie. Retaining that variation means retaining the finite sampling problem; replacing it with mass shares changes the object being studied.

One could make the new intervention more like a ChoCo problem by assigning costs to changing the mix of agent types and asking for the cheapest changes that equalize optimal actions. But neither those costs nor the choice over type proportions appears in the paper. That may be a sensible extension for a large replicated-agent service, but it does not rescue Theorem 1 as an anchor. The paper’s architectural experiments use four GA configurations, and the proposed service scenario is hypothetical.

That is the strongest negative case I can support: the paper offers no named computational result whose complexity a continuous-population mirror would chart, and the proponent’s LP is a new team-design question. The universal claim remains weak at its edges: a large service with many replicas could make type proportions a natural object, and its authors might recognize the proposed intervention as related work. But that possibility does not turn this paper’s theorem into a computational result.
