The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a continuous mirror is also sharply limited: this paper has no qualifying named computational result to anchor one to. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The phase-transition findings are simulation results. Appendix D, Eq. (24), poses an optimization problem and calls it “generally intractable,” but does not prove a complexity result. Its equations and heuristic solver do not supply the required anchor.

The closest genuine lead is that Appendix D inverse problem, though it is not an admissible anchor. A plausible population version would model millions of platform users grouped into a moderate number \(\tau\) of cohorts that share the features and interaction patterns relevant to propagation. Cohort \(i\) has mass \(\mu_i\), feature vector \(x_i\), and interaction weights \(W_{ij}\) with other cohorts. Given initial active fractions \(a_i^0\) and the paper’s parameters \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\), choose a unit propagation vector \(C\) to maximize the eventual active mass.

One precise mean-field formulation sets
\[ \ell_i(a)=\frac{\sum_j\mu_jW_{ij}(2a_j-1)}{\sum_j\mu_jW_{ij}}, \qquad p_i(a,C)=\gamma\left[ \alpha\frac{1+C^\top x_i}{2} +\beta\frac{1+\ell_i(a)}{2} +(1-\alpha-\beta)\sum_j\mu_j a_j \right], \]
and evolves each cohort’s active fraction by
\[ a_i^{t+1}=a_i^t+(1-a_i^t)p_i(a^t,C). \]
The underlying question is: given these rational type masses, features, interaction weights, parameters, and a target \(q\), does there exist a unit vector \(C\) for which the limiting active mass \(\sum_i\mu_i a_i^\infty\) is at least \(q\)? A solution is such a vector, or a certificate that none exists.

This is a plausible mirror of Appendix D’s goal of choosing a propagation vector for maximal expected spread, and the population regime is credible for a large platform with many users per behavioral and network-role cohort. But it is a mean-field formulation: it replaces the paper’s stochastic cascades on a sparse preferential-attachment graph with deterministic type-level fractions. The paper gives no result showing that this approximation preserves its cascade behavior, and its assertion of intractability does not establish that the resulting decision problem is hard. I would not assign it a tractability or hardness class from the paper.

So the positive case is only that a recognizable, concrete population optimization question can be constructed from the paper’s inverse problem. Its weakest point is decisive under the requested standard: the paper supplies no named computational result for that question—or for any other problem—to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would vote against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror. It reports simulations and proposes a stochastic cascade model, but it has no named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Appendix D defines an inverse optimization problem and calls it “generally intractable,” without establishing that claim or giving an algorithmic guarantee. A new population problem inspired by that appendix could be worth studying, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The proposed Appendix D mirror also has a problem in its stated objective. Under the paper’s experimental parameters, the global influence weight is positive: \(1-\alpha-\beta=1/3\). Once the seed is active, every inactive node has activation probability at least \(\gamma |S|/(3n)>0\) at each step. With repeated updates, every node therefore eventually activates with probability one, regardless of \(C\). The type-level recurrence has the same limiting behavior: once active mass is positive, each cohort’s active fraction tends to one. Maximizing limiting spread is consequently trivial in this regime.

The paper’s simulations avoid that limit by stopping after \(\epsilon\) steps with no changes. A nontrivial inverse problem could instead maximize expected spread by a specified horizon \(H\), or the probability of reaching a viral threshold before the paper’s stopping rule fires. A platform with many users in behavioral and network-role cohorts could make that a plausible population question, and the authors would likely recognize the goal of choosing a propagation vector for spread. But it requires selecting and studying a finite-time objective that the paper does not analyze as a computational problem.

There is a further modeling cost. The paper’s cascade dynamics depend on sparse network paths, core–periphery position, and node-specific spectral features. Replacing nodes by a small set of cohorts with type-to-type interaction weights assumes a form of exchangeability that can erase those pathways. A richer model could preserve them, but may need many structural types or a separate sparse-network limit. That is a serious obstacle to this particular mean-field formulation; it does not rule out every credible population model.

So the case against is decisive for a *mirror of a result*: the paper supplies no computational-complexity result to mirror, and its closest optimization objective is trivial in the stated infinite-time regime. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any scenario is weaker. A finite-horizon, high-multiplicity cascade-control problem is coherent and might be worthwhile as a new extension. I would not present it as a mirror established by this paper.
