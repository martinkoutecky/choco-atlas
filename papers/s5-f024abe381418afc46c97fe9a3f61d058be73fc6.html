The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(B\), a finite supported set of student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), school capacities \(\kappa_b\), each type’s school preferences, and each type’s priority class at each school, define feasible aggregate allocations \(x_{tb}\) with \(\sum_b x_{tb}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tb}\leq\kappa_b\). Each unit of mass receives one school or the outside option. Use positive-mass blocking pairs and the paper’s internal- and external-stability conditions to define the set of legal allocations. Determine whether that set has an allocation that is first-order-stochastic-dominance greatest for students, and compute one if it exists.

The model it lives in

A large school district modeled by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete student types, where types include school preferences and priority classes. The decision variable \(x_{tb}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(b\); capacities scale with population, and mass splitting represents aggregate assignments of individuals who each receive an integral assignment. Legal allocations use positive-mass blocking, and optimality compares each type’s mass at or above every preference cutoff.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers optimal legal allocations and, through Theorem 6.2, their positive-mass type-school support. It leaves the consent-specific EADAM result and the Latin-instance results about the number of legal matchings outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuum of feasible allocations have a unique legal set, and does it contain a student-FOSD-greatest allocation?
  • Can a legalized type-school support characterization or an efficient algorithm be obtained for this model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is narrow: the paper’s legal-assignment results have a plausible high-multiplicity mirror in a large school district. I would lead with computing the best legal allocation, and use computing the legal type-school pairs as a second, structurally useful question. The paper contains named polynomial-time results, but no named hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor to prefer.

Take a finite set \(B\) of schools and a finite supported set \(T\) of student types. A type records a student’s complete school preferences and priority attributes at every school; for EADAM it would also record consent, though consent is not part of the two questions I anchor on. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu_t\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). School \(b\) has capacity \(\kappa_b\), measured as a fraction of the population. Each type has a strict preference order over schools and an outside option. Schools rank types by priority; priority ties can be treated as classes whose mass may be split at a capacity cutoff.

A decision is a mass-allocation matrix \(x\): \(x_{tb}\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\) assigned to school \(b\). It satisfies \(\sum_b x_{tb}\leq \mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tb}\leq\kappa_b\); the unassigned mass of each type is assigned to the outside option. These are aggregate counts of people receiving whole school assignments, not fractional bundles given to each person.

For an allocation \(x\), type \(t\) has positive demand for \(b\) if some positive mass of \(t\) is assigned below \(b\) in its preference order or is unmatched. School \(b\) can accommodate that mass if it has a vacancy or assigns positive mass to types with lower priority than \(t\). Call \((t,b)\) a blocking pair when both conditions hold. An allocation \(y\) blocks \(x\) if \(y_{tb}>0\) for some pair that blocks \(x\). Define a *legal set* of allocations using the paper’s internal and external conditions: allocations in the set do not block one another, and every feasible allocation outside it is blocked by some allocation in it.

The student-side objective has a precise aggregate form. A legal allocation \(x^\star\) is student-optimal if, for every type \(t\), every preference cutoff, and every legal allocation \(x\), \(x^\star\) assigns at least as much mass of \(t\) to schools at or above that cutoff as \(x\) does. This is first-order stochastic dominance over each type’s school assignments. The school-side version compares, for each school, the mass assigned from above each priority cutoff, treating an unfilled seat as worse than an acceptable student. There is no scalar welfare objective in the paper; the mirror preserves its optimality order.

The lead anchor is Theorem 5.5, proved in this paper. It says that the rotate-remove algorithm can be implemented in \(O(|E|)\) time and finds the \(Y\)-optimal legal assignment: choosing students as \(Y\) gives the student-optimal legal assignment, and choosing schools as \(Y\) gives the school-optimal one. The underlying problem in the mirror is therefore:

Continuous Optimal-Legal-Allocation. Given \(T,B,\mu,\kappa\), the preference orders and school priorities, compute a legal allocation that is FOSD-greatest on a designated side. If none exists, report that fact.

I expect this to be Class A, tractable, with running time polynomial in the number of supported type-school pairs, the bit length of the rational masses and capacities, and the other explicit input parameters. The paper’s key structural result, Theorem 3.1, identifies legal assignments with stable assignments in a subinstance. A mass-flow counterpart to that result could make the problem approachable through stable-allocation algorithms and continuous optimization. Whether that identity and the requisite optimality order survive aggregation is part of the question, not an assumption built into the answer.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 6.2, also proved here. It says that the subgraph \(G_L\), whose edges are exactly those appearing in at least one legal assignment, can be found in \(O(|E|)\) time. Its mirror is:

Continuous Legalized-Support. Given the same mass instance and its legal set \(\mathcal L\), compute \(\widehat E_L=\{(t,b): x_{tb}>0\text{ for some }x\in\mathcal L\}\).

I also expect this to be Class A if the legal-set structure extends: the output has at most \(|T||B|\) pairs, and the continuous analogue of Theorem 3.1 could expose them through stable-flow or separation routines. The further questions are whether the continuum legal set is unique, whether it equals the stable allocations of a legalized type-school graph, and whether it retains a lattice with FOSD extrema.

The regime is large centralized school choice with repeated cohorts: for example, a district with tens of thousands of applicants, hundreds of programs, and many students sharing preference templates and priority profiles. The paper itself describes a New York City scale of about 90,000 applicants and 700 programs. In the proposed regime, the number of people \(N\) is much greater than the number of supported types \(\tau\), and capacities scale as \(\kappa_b=q_b/N\). A discrete instance with \(n_t/N=\mu_t\) aggregates directly to these masses; rational flows can conversely be scaled to integer counts. The intended complexity is polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of schools, and the bit length \(L\), rather than polynomial in the population count encoded through all individual edges.

This scenario is plausible but conditional. If each student’s exact lottery rank is part of the input and nearly every rank is unique, then the number of types can approach \(N\), leaving little high-multiplicity gain. The mirror needs shared priority classes or other repeated priority profiles; treating a cutoff as splitting class mass is a natural co-modification for an atomless population, but the authors could insist that strict individual priorities are essential to their problem.

That is the weakest point overall: the paper’s legality notion quantifies over a set of discrete assignments, and its unique legal set and lattice need not automatically persist for fractional mass allocations. The proposed questions make that issue testable rather than hiding it. If the legalized-subinstance characterization survives, the paper’s own structure points toward tractability; if it fails, the failure would locate a genuine boundary for continuization. The paper’s input is still a finite list of named students, so its large-market experiments do not themselves collide with these mass-based questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both named algorithmic results depend on the exact identities the proposed types erase. In the paper’s actual input, every school has a strict ranking of students. If a type includes the student’s position in those rankings, as the programme’s definition of a complete type requires, then each student has a distinct type: \(\tau=N\). Shared preference templates do not fix that. The paper’s own NYC-scale experiments generate random preference orders and priorities, so they do not demonstrate a small support of complete types.

That leaves a choice. Keep the exact priority ranks, and the mass model offers no high-multiplicity compression. Omit them, and the model merges students who can block different assignments. For example, two students with identical school preferences but different priority at \(b\) are not interchangeable in the blocking test. The proposed priority-class version avoids this by introducing ties and splitting mass at a cutoff. That may be a sensible school-choice model, but it replaces the paper’s strict, lottery-resolved priorities with a divisible tie rule. Its legal assignments and rotations need not be the projections of those in any particular tie-breaking realization. Calling it the mirror therefore requires more than a natural aggregate interpretation: it needs a canonical extension of the paper’s blocking and legality relations.

This is a problem for Theorem 5.5. Its \(O(|E|)\) algorithm returns an individual assignment through rotations among identified students and schools. If types retain the priority information that determines those rotations, their number grows with the population. If types collapse priority ties, the proposed FOSD-optimal flow is a new objective on a new feasible set; the theorem does not identify it as the continuous counterpart of the student-optimal legal assignment. One can ask whether that new problem is tractable, but the paper gives little reason to expect its legal set or its optimality order to carry over. The finite uniqueness argument for legal assignments also does not settle this: the paper’s iteration over sets of assignments terminates in a finite assignment universe, while the mass model has infinitely many feasible flows and blocking by any positive mass is sensitive to arbitrarily small changes.

Theorem 6.2 has a related mismatch. It computes whether an individual edge appears in *some* legal assignment. After quotienting students into types, the direct analogue might be that some member of a type can use a school, or that positive mass can. Those differ: an edge used by one exceptional student can survive the first projection but disappear in the atomless limit. Requiring positive mass avoids that issue, but changes the theorem’s output from possible individual edges to type-school pairs with positive-flow realizations. A further result would be needed to show that those pairs form a legalized subinstance supporting the aggregate legal set.

So the case against is not that the proposed flow questions are meaningless; they are coherent candidates for a new model. It is that the paper’s headline complexity results attach to a feature that school choice makes unusually hard to aggregate faithfully: named students’ strict priority positions. The proponent’s priority-tier scenario is the best escape, but it weakens the connection to these theorems. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile project on legal assignments with mass priority classes; that is where this negative case is weakest.
