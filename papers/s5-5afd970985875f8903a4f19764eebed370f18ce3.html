The continuous mirror question

Given a finite vocabulary \(V\), background knowledge \(B\), and a finite support of complete rule-package types \(t\) with definite Horn expressions \(R_t\) and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), suppose \(F_\mu=\bigcup_{\mu_t>0}R_t\) is acyclic, non-redundant, and not in conflict. Compute a finite Horn expression \(G\) that is a common ground for the positive-mass types and \(B\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete rule packages \(R_t\), and the society is the rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types. The decision variable is the output Horn expression \(G\); it must satisfy the paper's six common-ground postulates for the supported types and \(B\). There is no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers common-ground construction for acyclic, non-redundant, conflict-free unions of supported rule packages. It does not cover inputs outside those conditions or add a prevalence-weighted selection among common grounds.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a prevalence-sensitive common-ground semantics reflect mass magnitudes while retaining the paper's six postulates?
  • How do existence and construction behave outside the acyclic, non-redundant, conflict-free regime?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case rests on one algorithmic anchor: Theorem 15, proved in this paper. Given definite Horn rule sets \(F_1,\ldots,F_n\) and shared background knowledge \(B\), if their union is acyclic, non-redundant, and not in conflict, Algorithm 1 returns a common ground in \(O((|\bigcup_i F_i|+|B|)^4)\) time. The theorem’s output is a common ground in the paper’s full sense: it is coherent; equals the union when that union is coherent; does not entail any stakeholder rule unchanged in the prohibited way; contains only rules traceable to an input rule; retains a non-trivial consequence of every input rule; and does not introduce an unrelated qualifier to resolve an incoherence. This is a polynomial-time construction result, not a hardness result.

A concrete mirror is Support-Faithful Continuous Common-Ground Construction. An instance consists of a finite vocabulary \(V\), shared background knowledge \(B\), and a finite set \(T\) of complete stakeholder types. Each type \(t\) specifies a definite Horn rule set \(R_t\); two people have the same type exactly when their rule sets and all other details relevant to this problem agree. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and support size \(\tau=|\{t:\mu_t>0\}|\). Promise that \(F_\mu=\bigcup_{\mu_t>0}R_t\) is acyclic, non-redundant, and not in conflict with \(B\). The task is to output a finite Horn expression \(G\) satisfying the six common-ground postulates for the positive-mass stakeholder types and \(B\). Such a \(G\) is the solution.

The regime could be a large public consultation on rules for an autonomous public-service system: millions of participants choose among a bounded menu of complete, standardized rule packages, so \(N\) is enormous while \(\tau\) is modest. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants with package \(R_t\); \(B\) records shared constraints such as mutually exclusive conditions or actions. This makes the population the continuous object. The rules and the resulting common ground remain ordinary Horn expressions. The authors would have a strong reason to recognize the underlying question: it is still how to construct their common ground from stakeholders’ possibly incoherent conduct rules.

The expected direction is tractable: instantiate Theorem 15 with one \(F_i=R_t\) for each supported type. The running time is polynomial in the rule and background input sizes, plus the time to read the mass encoding \(L\); the algorithm need not expand the \(N\) participants. This covers the paper’s common-ground construction under its stated structural conditions, not its broader discussion of resolving normative conflicts.

The soft spot is that the theorem’s equal-stake postulates make the *values* of the masses irrelevant once they are positive: changing \(\mu\) from \((0.9,0.1)\) to \((0.1,0.9)\) leaves the common-ground question unchanged, while removing a type from the support can change it. That is defensible for a process where every represented stakeholder type must retain standing, however small its constituency. It is a narrow mirror if the intended process should respond to how prevalent each view is. A mass-weighted common ground would be an additional problem, not a result Theorem 15 establishes.

Natural follow-up questions are whether a mass-sensitive version remains tractable; how common-ground existence and construction behave outside the theorem’s promised class; and how to select among multiple common grounds using a rule-edit cost.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 15 is a genuine algorithmic anchor: it gives a polynomial time construction under explicit conditions. I can’t honestly dismiss it on the ground that the paper lacks a computational result. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror gives the population a distribution \(\mu\), while the paper’s common-ground postulates give that distribution no role.

For any support of rule-package types, the relevant input is just \(F_\mu=\bigcup_{\mu_t>0}R_t\) and \(B\). The six postulates ask whether rules are supported by stakeholders and whether the resulting expression is coherent; they do not use how many stakeholders support a rule or what fraction they represent. Two societies with the same positive-mass types therefore present the same common-ground problem, whatever their masses. Theorem 15 already takes the resulting finite rule input. Encoding a large consultation as a distribution over standardized packages would compress duplicate packages, but would not make prevalence part of the problem.

That is a poor fit for the most persuasive population scenario. In a public consultation, a package supported by \(49\%\) of respondents and one supported by a tiny minority would have the same standing under this model, provided both have positive mass. That may be a deliberate equal-standing norm, but it is not a model of how public opinion is distributed. And if the intended stakeholders are institutions rather than individual respondents, the number of people behind each institution does not establish a large population of stakeholders.

The stronger mirror would make common-ground construction respond to mass: perhaps require that at least a chosen fraction of stakeholders retain some rule, or minimize a weighted measure of rules discarded or changed. Those are possible research questions, but the paper supplies neither the weighting semantics nor a distance or trade-off between rule changes. Its postulates instead give each stakeholder equal standing, and the paper identifies preferences over rules as future work. A mass-sensitive formulation would need that normative design work before Theorem 15 could anchor it.

So the negative case is limited. The support-based mirror is formally coherent, and the consultation scenario cannot be ruled out; the authors could recognize it as applying their construction to a population of rule packages. My case is that it adds little of the continuous-population content the programme is seeking: the model is sensitive to which types occur, but not to their masses. That makes it a weak priority, not a decisive demonstration that no worthwhile mirror exists.
