The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is that the paper’s subject could be lifted to a high-multiplicity population: many people share a small set of complete rankings over an infinite agenda, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction holding ranking type \(t\). Weighted pairwise majority then gives an aggregate relation. That is a plausible continuous-population scenario.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror. Theorem 1 proves that three voters with orders of type \(\mathbb Z\) can have a majority aggregate of type \(\mathbb N\); Theorem 2 proves the corresponding possibility from three orders of type \(\mathbb Q\). Theorem 3 proves that three orders of type \(\mathbb N\) cannot aggregate to type \(\mathbb Q\), using Lemma 3.1’s finite-distance preservation result. These are existence and impossibility results about order types, not results about complexity or algorithms.

An unanchored follow-on question might be: given finitely represented dense-order types and rational masses, does their weighted-majority relation have order type \(\mathbb N\)? But the paper supplies neither a finite representation for those infinite rankings nor a computational result about this question. Presenting it as a mirror of one of the paper’s anchors would overstate the case. So, under the requested anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors and no per-anchor continuous problems to put to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 give existence constructions for majority aggregates of specified order types; Theorem 3 proves an impossibility from finite-distance preservation. None studies an algorithm, a complexity class, or a finite computational problem. Under ChoCo’s scope, these are not eligible anchors.

The strongest population version is easy to state: take a finite set of complete rankings over the countably infinite candidates, assign each ranking a rational mass, and aggregate each pair by weighted majority. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2’s witnesses survive unchanged with three types of mass \(1/3\). They would establish the same order-theoretic possibilities for a continuous society. Theorem 3 also has a natural finite-support weighted version: if both comparisons place \(x\) above \(z\) and \(z\) above \(y\) by strict majorities, those two groups overlap in positive mass. With finitely many types, the candidates between \(x\) and \(y\) in the aggregate therefore lie in the union of finitely many finite intervals. The aggregate remains finite-distant. These are sensible mathematical questions, and high multiplicity is plausible here; neither point supplies the missing computational problem.

A proposed algorithmic version would need to take rankings over infinitely many candidates as input. A finite list of rational masses does not encode those rankings. The paper gives no finite representation or access model for arbitrary orders of type \(\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Q\), or \(\mathbb N\). Choosing programs, automata, or another succinct representation could produce computational questions, but the answers would depend on that added representation and would concern a new model. The paper’s theorems do not identify such a model or an algorithmic task within it.

So the proponent has a legitimate continuous-population restatement of all three order-theoretic anchors, but no computational mirror for any of them. The honest limit of this negative case is that one could independently devise a compelling representation-sensitive complexity problem. I cannot rule that out universally; it would be a new research direction, rather than a continuization of a computational result in this paper.
