The continuous mirror question

Given fixed spatial clusters and finite-support distributions over requester and quality-qualified executor types, with each type specified by its role, task capabilities, capacity, and gross-substitutes value or cost schedule, compute clearing prices and a feasible allocation maximizing total gain from trade. The allocation assigns mass \(x_{abY}\) of requester type \(a\) and executor type \(b\) to trade bundle \(Y\), respects type-mass and capacity limits, and gives each unit of mass an integral bundle; type-level allocated mass may be fractional.

The model it lives in

A finite-support, high-multiplicity spatial double-auction market: types carry rational masses and complete task, capacity, qualification, and valuation information; the decision variables are bundle-specific trade masses and clearing prices; the objective is maximum gain from trade subject to feasibility and willingness to trade.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has one clear computational anchor: Lemma 1, proved here, states that TRUST-SC is computationally efficient. Its proof claims total running time \(O(Nn^2k+m^2+L(n+m))\): clustering takes \(O(Nn^2k)\), quality selection \(O(m^2)\), and price finding \(O(L(n+m))\), with the remaining allocation steps linear. The claimed polynomial bound depends on \(N\), \(k\), and \(L\) being polynomially bounded. The paper gives no named hardness result, so this algorithmic result is the right anchor.

My lead mirror is the Qualified-Mass TRUST-SC Allocation-and-Price Problem. Its instance has a fixed set of spatial clusters and, within each, finite-support distributions over requester types and quality-qualified executor types. A type includes the agent’s role, cluster, task capability and capacity, qualification status, and complete gross-substitutes value or cost schedule. Each type has a binary-rational mass. The task types \(\chi\) and per-agent capacity \(\beta\) are fixed, as in the paper’s model.

The question is to compute a market price vector \(p\), a feasible type-level trade plan \(x_{abY}\), and the associated payments. Here \(x_{abY}\) is the mass of requester type \(a\) matched to executor type \(b\) for bundle \(Y\in\{0,\ldots,\beta\}^{\chi}\). Each type’s allocated mass must not exceed its input mass; requesters must be willing to buy their assigned bundles at \(p\), and executors must be willing to supply them. Among feasible trades at the clearing prices, select one maximizing total gain from trade, \(\sum_{a,b,Y}x_{abY}(v_a(Y)-c_b(Y))\), where \(v_a\) and \(c_b\) are the type’s value and cost schedules. Payments are \(p\cdot Y\) per unit of traded mass. A person still receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate amount of people receiving each bundle can be fractional.

The mass regime is a citywide service that repeatedly commissions road audits, air-quality readings, or similar tasks within standard spatial zones. Large numbers of workers share the same zone, sensing capabilities, capacity, reliability qualification, and cost schedule; recurring requesters share task bundles and value schedules. The population can be very large while the number of complete types remains moderate. This fits the paper’s focus on large markets, spatial clustering, bounded task types, and gross-substitutes valuations. The paper’s use of existing multi-unit double-auction work is supporting evidence that this market formulation is already meaningful to its authors.

The continuous version of random market splitting divides each type’s mass equally between the two sides. Both halves then have the same type measures, so price computation uses the same aggregate market on either side. With fixed \(\chi\) and \(\beta\), there are only \(O(|T_R||T_E|(\beta+1)^\chi)\) type-pair/bundle variables; the resulting rational allocation problem is an LP. I expect this scoped problem to be Class A: tractable, with running time polynomial in the support size and input bit length.

This mirrors the allocation-and-pricing computation in TRUST-SC’s third tier, not the entire Lemma 1 result. In particular, the cluster assignments and quality-qualified population are inputs to this problem. It does not claim to continuize the clustering or peer-grading procedures, or to transfer the paper’s truthfulness result.

My weakest point is that the paper does not fully specify a clean optimization problem behind its third-tier algorithm: it mixes vector prices in the model with a scalar price sweep in the pseudocode, and its loop requiring exact demand-supply equality may fail to terminate. The mass LP therefore makes the allocation question precise in a way that may not reproduce every detail of the stated algorithm. Also, splitting a continuum into two identical halves removes the finite-sample variation that motivates random splitting, so the paper’s truthfulness argument needs a separate mass-deviation analysis. Those are real limits, but the underlying question—how to allocate heterogeneous task bundles and compute prices for a large spatial crowd—is plainly the paper’s own.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named computational anchor is Lemma 1, and the strongest case against starts by questioning whether it establishes a computational result at all. Its polynomial bound assumes without proof that the clustering iterations \(N\) and price increments \(L\) are polynomially bounded. The price loop can fail to find exact demand–supply equality on its \(\epsilon\)-grid, and the paper gives no input model or complexity bound for computing demand under its GS valuations. So Lemma 1 does not provide a sound complexity theorem to mirror as written.

That objection does not dispose of the underlying question. The proposed Qualified-Mass mirror is recognizable as a high-multiplicity market: recurring requesters and workers share task bundles, locations or zones, capacities, quality status, and value or cost schedules. A type-level allocation \(x_{abY}\) can describe what mass of requester type \(a\) trades bundle \(Y\) with executor type \(b\). People still receive integral bundles; the aggregate mass receiving each bundle can be fractional. That fits the programme’s model, and the paper’s reliance on multi-unit double-auction work supports the plausibility of the regime.

The mirror’s main weakness is fidelity. The paper’s APM uses prices found by a split-market procedure and queue-based selection. Maximizing aggregate gain from trade at clearing prices is a cleaner and more standard problem, but it is not exactly the procedure Lemma 1 claims to analyze. In a continuous population, splitting every type’s mass equally also makes the two halves identical, removing the finite-sample variation behind random splitting. The peer-voting quality stage has a separate identity-linked structure that a type-mass model would need to reformulate.

Those points narrow the mirror; they do not defeat it. A better version can ask for exact allocation and prices in a finite-support market of qualified types, treating qualification and clusters as inputs, as the paper’s third tier does. The programme permits such co-modifications, and computational tractability would still be a relevant result. Existing high-multiplicity auction work is evidence for this setting, not a novelty collision.

So the honest negative case is weak. I can argue that Lemma 1 is too underspecified to support its claimed complexity bound, and that the proposed LP does not reproduce the paper’s mechanism. I cannot show that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives: a qualified type-mass auction is a plausible mirror of the paper’s central allocation-and-pricing computation.
