The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), finite action sets \(A_t\), security levels \(\ell_t\), and rational affine payoffs \(u_{ta}(q)\) in the population action shares \(q\), define \(\mathcal F\) by \(q_{ta}\ge 0\), \(\sum_{a\in A_t}q_{ta}=\mu_t\), and \(q_{ta}>0\Rightarrow u_{ta}(q)\ge\ell_t\). Let \(M_t\) be the greatest security-feasible payoff available to a type-\(t\) agent over actions and \(q\in\mathcal F\). Compute a feasible \(q\) maximizing \(G(q)=-\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in A_t}q_{ta}\log(1+M_t-u_{ta}(q))\), or, given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), return a rational feasible \(q\) within \(\varepsilon\) of the optimum.

The model it lives in

A type records all payoff-relevant characteristics; \(\mu_t\) is its population mass and \(q_{ta}\) is the mass assigned pure action \(a\). The payoffs depend affinely on aggregate action shares, security feasibility is imposed on every action receiving positive mass, and the objective is the population-normalized log of the paper’s GSE product.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the computation of GSEs and their scalar selection criterion; it leaves the CSE and SSE results, Pareto claims, bargaining axioms, and comparisons with other equilibria aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact and additive-approximation complexity of cGSE in the number of types, actions, and input bit length?
  • Which payoff structures make the feasible region or scalar objective efficiently optimizable, and which yield hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see one credible mirror, for the paper’s greedy scalar equilibrium (GSE). Its anchor is Result 3.8, proved in this paper. The result says that, for an \(n\)-player game with action-set sizes \(m_1,\ldots,m_n\), all GSEs can be found in worst-case time \(O(N)\), where \(N=n\prod_{i=1}^n m_i\) is the number of payoff entries in the full strategic-form table. The stated RAM model counts arithmetic operations, comparisons, and replacements as unit-cost steps. The GSE being computed maximizes \(\prod_i(1+M_i-u_i(s))^{-1}\) over profiles meeting the players’ security levels, where \(M_i\) is player \(i\)’s best acceptable payoff. I would use this single computational anchor; the paper has no named hardness result that I would rely on.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous Greedy Scalar Equilibrium (cGSE). An instance has a finite set \(T\) of player types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), finite pure-action sets \(A_t\), security levels \(\ell_t\), and rational affine payoff functions \(u_{ta}(q)\). Here \(u_{ta}(q)\) is the per-capita payoff to a type-\(t\) agent who takes action \(a\), given the population’s action shares \(q\). The decision variable is \(q_{ta}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned action \(a\). It must satisfy
\[ q_{ta}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{a\in A_t}q_{ta}=\mu_t, \]
and the security condition \(q_{ta}>0\Rightarrow u_{ta}(q)\ge\ell_t\). Call this feasible set \(\mathcal F\), and define \(M_t\) as the greatest security-feasible payoff available to a type-\(t\) agent over actions and \(q\in\mathcal F\). The problem is to find a feasible \(q\) maximizing
\[ G(q)=-\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in A_t}q_{ta} \log\!\bigl(1+M_t-u_{ta}(q)\bigr). \]
For an algorithmic version, given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), return a rational feasible \(q\) with \(G(q)\ge \max_{q'\in\mathcal F}G(q')-\varepsilon\).

This extends the paper’s GSE scalarization in the natural population way: each individual still takes one pure action, while the aggregate assignment can split a type’s mass across actions. In a finite population of size \(N\), with \(q_{ta}=x_{ta}/N\), the GSE product over individuals is \(\prod_{t,a}(1+M_t-u_{ta}(q))^{-x_{ta}}\). Its logarithm divided by \(N\) is exactly \(G(q)\). Replacing those grid-valued shares by real masses is the high-multiplicity relaxation.

A plausible regime is a large trade association choosing a binding operating arrangement for many firms. A type records the firm’s action menu, security level, and payoff response to the shares choosing each operating mode. Firms with the same payoff-relevant characteristics share a type; a population of many thousands of firms over tens or hundreds of types makes the fractions \(q_{ta}\) more natural than named-firm counts. This retains the paper’s arbitrated or negotiated assignment of actions, its security constraint, and its greedy criterion. It does assume aggregate-dependent, type-symmetric payoffs. That is a substantive but recognizable high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every normal-form game has a compact population representation.

For the general affine-payoff version, my prior is Class C: the feasible region can be a union of polytopes, and the objective has no evident concavity. That is a conjectured direction for the new problem, not a hardness claim from this paper. Convex or otherwise structured subclasses may be Class A. The mirror also raises questions about approximation as \(N\) grows, the dependence on the number of types and actions, and which payoff structures admit efficient optimization.

The weak point is that Result 3.8’s \(O(N)\) bound is linear in the *fully expanded* payoff table, not in a compact description of a large population game; its unit-cost model also suppresses payoff bit lengths. So it does not predict that cGSE is easy. The case for the mirror is narrower: the paper studies choosing a GSE over security-feasible pure action profiles, and cGSE asks that same normative selection question when many indistinguishable agents can be described by type-level masses. It covers the GSE computation only, not the paper’s other equilibrium criteria or its broader claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Result 3.8 is a genuine computational anchor, so I would not dismiss it as an axiomatic result. The strongest case against the mirror is that its \(O(N)\) bound is a scan of the explicitly supplied payoff table, where \(N=n\prod_i m_i\). The result gives no compact representation of a large game and no optimization method whose bottleneck is the number of players. Keeping that input model in a population limit leaves the payoff table enormous. To make the population version compact, one must add a structure such as anonymous, aggregate-dependent payoffs.

That is the main gap in the proposed cGSE mirror. Its mass assignment and log objective do recover the finite GSE objective for a symmetric population game: \(q_{ta}\) can represent the fraction of type \(t\) taking pure action \(a\), and the objective is the normalized sum of the individual log factors. But affine payoff functions, type-level security levels, and aggregate dependence specify a narrower game class that the paper’s arbitrary strategic-form payoff table does not supply. Deriving security levels directly from the paper’s definition would improve the formulation, but would not remove the need for this new structure. Any algorithmic result for that compact model would depend on the chosen payoff representation and its geometry, not on Result 3.8’s table-scanning algorithm.

The stronger version of the mirror survives that objection: study type-symmetric games with compact payoff functions of population action shares, and extend GSE to a continuum of pure-action assignments. A large association of similar firms choosing operating modes is a plausible instance. The population objective need not vanish, and types can preserve every payoff-relevant distinction. This looks like a sensible high-multiplicity problem, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as an extension of their selection rule.

So the negative case is weak at its universal claim. I can argue that Result 3.8 offers little guidance for the compact continuous problem, and that the mirror would introduce substantial modeling choices. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists: the type-symmetric population version is coherent, and nothing in the paper rules it out.
