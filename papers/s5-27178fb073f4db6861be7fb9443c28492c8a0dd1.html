The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest computational anchor in this paper. Its closest results are Proposition 1 (proved here): under plurality, no trade is the only equilibrium; and Proposition 2 (proved here): under power sharing, for any intensity profile there is a unique full-trade equilibrium in which only the strongest supporter of each party buys votes, bidding
\[ \bar b_L=(n-2)\frac{(\bar w_L)^2\bar w_R}{n(\bar w_L+\bar w_R)^2}, \qquad \bar b_R=(n-2)\frac{\bar w_L(\bar w_R)^2}{n(\bar w_L+\bar w_R)^2}, \]
while all other voters sell. These are equilibrium characterizations, not results that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor.

The nearest possible continuous analogue would take types to be party and preference intensity, \((L,w)\) or \((R,w)\), with \(\mu\) giving each type’s population share. Voters choose whether to sell, abstain from trading, or bid; aggregate bids and offers determine the price and the parties’ vote shares. One could ask to characterize equilibria under power sharing and whether the full-trade outcome retains the proportional-justice property. But that is an analytic equilibrium question, not a computational one. And in a continuum, individual actions may have no effect on market aggregates, changing the strategic market game; the “strongest supporter” may also be a zero-mass supremum rather than a voter type. That makes this a weak candidate for a mirror of the paper’s actual problem, even before the missing computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the threshold: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 says no trade is the only equilibrium under plurality; Proposition 2 characterizes the full-trade equilibrium under power sharing; Proposition 3 describes partial-trade equilibria. These are equilibrium results, not complexity or algorithmic results. Lemma 3 is an asymptotic welfare statement. The proponent’s proposed question about whether proportional justice persists is therefore an analytic question, outside ChoCo’s computational focus.

The strongest computational rewrite would give a rational distribution over party and preference intensity as input, then ask for all equilibria. That still runs into a problem with preserving the game. Its full-trade result relies on two individual strongest supporters buying \(O(n)\) votes each, while the price per vote is \(O(1/n)\). In an atomless population, no individual can move the aggregates or play that strategic role; a highest-intensity type may have positive mass, but it is a group of individuals, not a single buyer. Making that group choose bids as one player would add coalition coordination and change the strategic unit. Treating individuals as price takers would change the game’s strategic-market mechanism.

A finite, high-multiplicity version could retain individual agency, but it would remain a finite strategic game; its equilibrium characterization would not supply a continuous computational result. I don’t see a paper-grounded computational anchor that survives those alternatives.
