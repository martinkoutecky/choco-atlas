Theorem 3

Every anchor argued

The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(m\), let \(r\) be an anonymous voting rule that reduces to majority with two candidates and has a fractional winner extension. Given only independent samples from a society \(\mu\) over complete rankings, promised to have positive total-variation winner margin \(\rho_r(\mu)\), output \(r(\mu)\) with error at most \(\epsilon\). What expected sample count and memory are necessary in the worst case as functions of \(\rho_r(\mu)\) and \(\epsilon\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings of the fixed candidate set, with mass \(\mu_t\). The society is accessible only through independent samples; the output is its winner, and the computational measures are expected sample count and memory, with error at most \(\epsilon\) under a positive total-variation margin promise. Theorem 3 yields a worst-case lower bound of \(\Omega(1/\rho^2)\) samples for constant error on this class.

The objection that survived

The proposed mirror requires sample or coherent-oracle access: with explicit rational masses, the paper's named winners can be computed directly, and the quantum guarantee does not include the cost of preparing its oracle.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's sample-only lower bound for rules that reduce to two-candidate majority, and Theorem 2's quantum margin-dependent guarantee under coherent access. It leaves the explicit-profile runtime claims, experiments, and proposed heuristics outside the continuous query model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the \(\Omega(1/\rho^2)\) sample lower bound tight for every fixed-\(m\) rule in this class, including its dependence on \(\epsilon\)?
  • What coherent-query bound holds for the same distributional winner problem, and what cost should be charged for preparing the state oracle?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a query-complexity version of winner determination. The paper’s central object is already an anonymous histogram of rankings. Replacing counts by masses is natural when the many agents are a large cohort whose ballots come from a relatively small set of recurring types—for example, users ranking a fixed slate in a high-volume platform consultation. A type is a complete ranking, and its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. The relevant regime has \(N\) agents with \(\tau\ll N\) distinct rankings; the continuous instance keeps the distribution \(\mu\), not individual identities.

To make the computational question nontrivial, the population must be accessed through an oracle. For the quantum version, use coherent access to a distribution state \(U_\mu|0\rangle=\sum_t\sqrt{\mu_t}|t\rangle\) and its inverse. For the classical version, allow only independent samples from \(\mu\). This access model is a substantive part of the mirror: it suits a large electorate whose records are not read one by one, but it is not the paper’s explicit finite-list input model.

Let \(r(\mu)\) be the winner under the rule applied to the fractional type histogram, with a fixed tie-breaking rule. Define the continuous margin
\[ \rho_r(\mu)=\inf_{\nu:\,r(\nu)\ne r(\mu)}\operatorname{TV}(\mu,\nu), \]
the least mass that must be reassigned among rankings to change the winner. Require \(\rho_r(\mu)>0\). This is the continuous counterpart of the paper’s margin of victory: it measures how much of society must change its vote, rather than how many named voters must do so.

My lead is Quantum Continuous Winner Estimation: given fixed \(m\), a rule \(r\) with a well-defined fractional extension, the mass oracle \(U_\mu\), and an error target \(\epsilon\), output \(r(\mu)\) with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\). The solution is the winning candidate; the resource measured is coherent oracle calls, with qubits also recorded.

Its anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For fixed \(m\), under the paper’s anonymity and canceling-out assumptions, Algorithm 1 with \(K=\lfloor24\ln(1/\epsilon)\rfloor+1\) and \(s=\sigma(1/4)=\Theta(\log(n/\mathrm{MoV}))\) outputs the correct winner with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\), in runtime \(\Theta(n\log(1/\epsilon)/\mathrm{MoV})\) and space \(\Theta(\log(n\log(1/\epsilon)/\mathrm{MoV}))\). Translating to normalized mass margin \(\rho=\mathrm{MoV}/n\), the proposed oracle analogue asks whether the winner can be found using \(O(\log(1/\epsilon)/\rho)\) coherent queries, with \(O(\log(\log(1/\epsilon)/\rho))\) qubits, for fixed \(m\). The paper estimates each ranking’s frequency, applies the rule to the estimated histogram, and repeats before aggregating the tentative winners. Since the paper fixes \(m\), its enumeration of all \(m!\) rankings is a constant in this bound.

A second useful anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. For fixed \(m\ge2\), error parameter \(\epsilon\in(0,0.5]\), and a rule that reduces to two-candidate majority when there are two candidates, any fast algorithm whose only information about the profile comes from sampling with replacement needs expected runtime \(\Omega(n^2(1/2-\epsilon)^2/\mathrm{MoV}^2)\) and expected space \(\Omega(\log(n^2(1/2-\epsilon)^2/\mathrm{MoV}^2))\) to achieve correctness at least \(1-\epsilon\) in the worst case.

That anchors Classical Sample-Only Continuous Winner Estimation: given \(\mu\) only through independent ranking samples, output \(r(\mu)\) with error at most \(\epsilon\); measure expected samples and memory. The question is precise for the same fractional winner and positive-margin promise as above. The theorem’s finite empirical societies are instances of this continuous problem, with normalized margin \(\rho=\mathrm{MoV}/n\). Thus it supplies a worst-case lower bound of \(\Omega((1/2-\epsilon)^2/\rho^2)\) samples and \(\Omega(\log((1/2-\epsilon)^2/\rho^2))\) space on that subclass. For constant error, this is \(\Omega(1/\rho^2)\). In this restricted access model, the lower bound transfers from finite profiles; it is not a lower bound against algorithms given the exact mass vector.

The two problems make a coherent, limited case: Quantum Continuous Winner Estimation is expected to be tractable in the coherent-query model, while the sample-only version retains the paper’s hardness-style lower bound through its finite-profile instances. This mirrors the paper’s margin-sensitive winner computation for anonymous rules. It does not continuize outcomes, time, or axioms, and it does not claim a classical polynomial-time result from continuous optimization. Further questions include the cost of implementing \(U_\mu\) from a support of size \(\tau\) and binary masses of bit length \(L\), the dependence on variable \(m\), and the rounding gap between \(\rho_r(\mu)\) and \(\mathrm{MoV}/n\) in finite populations.

The weak point is the access model. In the programme’s canonical explicit-input model, where the support and rational masses are supplied, plurality and positional scores can be computed by summing over the support; the paper’s sampling lower bound does not apply to an algorithm that reads those masses. Its quantum result assumes access through quantum RAM and fixes the number of candidates. So this is not a strong mirror of explicit-input continuous complexity. The case survives as a mirror of winner determination when society is genuinely represented by mass and is accessible only through samples or coherent queries—a plausible regime for a very large, high-multiplicity electorate, and a computational question the paper’s authors would likely recognize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two theorems are real computational results, and normalizing the margin is mathematically natural. If \(\mathrm{MoV}/n\) tends to a positive limit, the paper’s scale \(n/\mathrm{MoV}\) becomes \(1/\rho\), where \(\rho\) is the least total variation change needed to alter the winner. That makes these unusually strong anchors for a continuous mirror. The problem is that the proposed mirror needs an access model the paper’s result depends on, while ChoCo’s society is supplied as a distribution over types.

For Theorem 2, the explicit-input mirror gives the rational masses \(\mu_t\). For plurality, scoring rules, Copeland, and STV, the winner can then be computed directly from those masses in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). There is no unknown histogram to estimate. The paper’s speedup comes from a different input: an oracle that lets the algorithm query an indexed voter, with the vote data available through quantum RAM. Replacing that with a coherent state-preparation oracle \(U_\mu\) preserves a nontrivial query problem, but charges access to an unknown distribution rather than computation on the given society. If state preparation is built from the stated support and rational masses, its cost must also be counted. The proposed \(O(1/\rho)\) query bound alone does not describe the cost of the continuous computation.

A stronger version would ask for query complexity directly over arbitrary mass distributions, with coherent access to \(U_\mu\). That is well-posed, and the finite theorem strongly suggests its answer. But its substance is still the paper’s histogram-estimation argument in the limit: estimate enough of the distribution to stay within the winner’s margin. It does not open the kind of explicit-input complexity landscape ChoCo is charting. The paper already uses \(\mathrm{MoV}\) as a promise in its analysis; it does not compute that margin. Turning the margin into an optimization objective could produce a separate continuous robustness problem, but that would be a new question rather than a mirror of Theorem 2.

Theorem 3 has the same access-model problem. Its lower bound is explicitly for algorithms whose information comes only from samples with replacement. Under a continuous sample-only formulation, the two-candidate construction is a binary hypothesis-testing problem: distinguish two distributions whose winning masses differ by about \(\rho\). The \(\Omega(1/\rho^2)\) bound is meaningful for that statistical task, but it says nothing about algorithms given the exact rational masses. Calling the latter a continuous mirror would turn “compute the winner of the supplied society” into “infer the winner of a hidden society.” That changes the task at its input, and the theorem itself gives no lower bound for the stated-mass problem.

The best negative case is therefore a dilemma for both anchors: provide the continuous society explicitly and the winner is directly computable for the paper’s named rules; hide it behind sampling or coherent queries and the result concerns oracle-based estimation, not computation over the given continuous society. The quantum version also inherits the paper’s special oracle assumption, while the classical lower bound is confined to a sample-only model.

This case is not airtight. A large platform or electorate whose ballot distribution is available only through a sampling interface is a plausible regime, and the authors could recognize winner estimation from that distribution as their problem. The paper’s fractional margin argument makes that connection especially natural. I would still argue against spending ChoCo’s effort on these anchors: the clean continuous formulation either removes their computational bottleneck or retains it by changing the input model.
