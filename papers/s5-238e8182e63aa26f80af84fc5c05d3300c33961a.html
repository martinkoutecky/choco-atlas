The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of employer and worker types with rational masses \(\mu_v>0\), summing to one, per-person quotas \(q_v\), weak orders over compatible counterpart types, aggregate channel capacities \(B_e\), and per-unit costs \(c_e\), find a stable flow \(x_e\) minimizing \(\sum_e c_e x_e\). Require \(0\le x_e\le B_e\), \(\sum_{e\in E_v}x_e\le Q_v:=\mu_v q_v\), and mixed-type stability: no unsaturated channel is in the choice-rule tail at both endpoints, using aggregate quota \(Q_v\).

The model it lives in

Types are the vertices, each carrying mass \(\mu_v\); aggregate quotas are \(Q_v=\mu_v q_v\), and channels carry contract mass \(x_e\) up to capacity \(B_e\). Minimize \(\sum_e c_e x_e\) over stable flows under the paper’s mixed choice rule with aggregate quotas.

The objection that survived

Theorem 7.1 already accepts real quotas and capacities, so its algorithm applies to the aggregated type graph without new complexity analysis; this limits the mirror’s novelty, but the proponent’s population-level change leaves that question meaningful.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the minimum-cost stable assignment result in Theorem 7.1. It does not separately assess the rotation-poset, affine-representation, or separation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite markets with exchangeable agents of each type, when does an optimal type-level flow lift to individual contracts, and what additive-cost guarantee can rounding provide?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a large, recurring matching market with relatively few kinds of participants: for example, employer and applicant cohorts defined by occupation, location, and qualifications. Members of each type share the same weak ranking of counterpart types, per-person quota, and relevant costs. The number of people can be very large while the number \(\tau\) of types stays modest. This is a regime property, not a claim about matching markets in general.

Call the mirror Continuous Min-Cost Mixed-Type Stable Assignment. Its input is a finite bipartite graph on employer types \(F\) and worker types \(W\), with rational type masses \(\mu_v>0\), summing to one across the population. Each type \(v\) has a per-person quota \(q_v\), so its aggregate quota is \(Q_v=\mu_v q_v\), and a weak order on its compatible counterpart types. Each type-pair channel \(e\) has a rational aggregate capacity \(B_e\), scaled with the cohort sizes, and a cost \(c_e\) per unit of contract mass.

Choose a nonnegative mass \(x_e\) for each channel, subject to \(x_e\le B_e\) and \(\sum_{e\in E_v}x_e\le Q_v\) for every type \(v\). Apply the paper’s mixed choice rule using \(Q_v\) as the type’s quota: it retains better-ranked channels first and truncates the critical tie at a common level. Call a feasible \(x\) stable if there is no channel \(e=fw\) with \(x_e<B_e\) that belongs to the choice-rule tail at both endpoints. The task is to return a stable \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_e c_ex_e\). Here \(x_e\) is contract mass: an individual can still receive an integral bundle, while the cohort average is fractional.

The named anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper: given a bipartite graph with edge capacities, vertex quotas, mixed weak preferences, and edge costs, finding a stable assignment of minimum total cost is solvable in strongly polynomial time. The theorem is proved using the paper’s rotation representation: it reduces the objective to minimum-weight closure over the rotation poset, then to a minimum cut. In the mirror, the graph’s vertices are types and the quotas and capacities are the corresponding aggregate quantities. The expected classification is tractable, Class A; the algorithm should run in strongly polynomial time in the size of the type graph, hence polynomial in \(\tau\) and the number of type-pair channels, rather than in the number of people. This anchors the case in the underlying stable-assignment problem, not in the paper’s encoding.

The paper contains no named hardness result to anchor. Theorem 7.1 is therefore the lead; I would not pad the case with a second, weaker algorithmic anchor. Its scope is specifically the minimum-cost stable assignment result. This does not claim to mirror every result about rotations, facets, or stable-assignment geometry.

This seems like a mirror the authors would recognize: the type graph retains their bipartite agents, weak preferences, quotas, capacities, blocking condition, and cost objective. The programme’s focus is the population change; scaling quotas and channel capacities with type mass is the accompanying model change. The paper’s real-valued assignment quantities are outcome-side continuity, which by itself would not qualify. High-multiplicity matching work would support the plausibility of the regime; it would not make this a novelty collision.

The weakest point is the interpretation of stability after aggregation. The mirror is strongest when counterpart-type channels accurately describe contracts and all members of a cohort are interchangeable in the relevant respects. It does not claim to preserve every market whose named individuals have distinct preferences, prices, or constraints; those distinctions would need to become types, and \(\tau\) could then grow until aggregation offers little benefit. The additional questions are whether one can give a precise lifting and rounding theorem between this type-level problem and finite markets, and whether a discrete stable assignment can be recovered with a useful additive cost guarantee when the number of types is moderate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7.1 is the problem in the proposed mirror, under a change of interpretation. Take one vertex for each employer or worker type, set its quota to \(Q_v=\mu_v q_v\), use \(B_e\) as the edge capacity, and keep the type rankings and edge costs. Then the mirror’s variables \(x_e\), feasibility constraints, blocking condition, and objective are exactly those of the paper. The theorem already allows real quotas and capacities, so these rational aggregate values are within its stated model. Its strongly polynomial algorithm therefore already runs in time polynomial in the type graph’s size.

That is stronger than saying the paper happens to use fractional assignments. The proposed population masses do not create a new computational object: they supply values for the quotas and capacities of a graph the paper already handles. The distribution over types adds no algorithmic input beyond those aggregate quantities. Calling the vertices “cohorts” makes the model a population interpretation, but the lead result needs no continuization or new complexity analysis.

The best alternative is a nonatomic market with exchangeable agents within each type and stability defined through type-pair flows. Under those assumptions, it again reduces to the same type graph, so Theorem 7.1 applies. If instead the mirror must preserve individual contract neighborhoods, capacities, or costs, those details must be included in the type description; the proposed aggregate stability condition would then need a separate argument showing that it preserves individual blocking and lifts back to the market. That could be a useful bridge theorem, but it is not the min-cost stable assignment result the proponent anchored on.

This is a strong collision with the anchor, though it does not establish that no separate continuum project could be worthwhile. A substantive lifting or rounding theorem for individual markets might be valuable; the case against is that this paper’s headline computational result already solves the proposed type-level mirror.
