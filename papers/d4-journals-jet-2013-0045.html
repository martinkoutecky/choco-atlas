What the mirror covers

The candidate reuses the expected-revenue selling setup in Section 4.1 with finite-support type masses. It does not mirror a computational result; Theorems 1 and 2 and the other applications remain analytical.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive direction I can see is a high-volume market based on the loss-averse buyer model in Section 4.1. Suppose many buyers have the same loss-aversion parameters \(\gamma,\lambda\) and reference-quantity rule, and each buyer’s type is \(\theta_i\). Let \(\mu_i\) be the population share of type \(\theta_i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A seller chooses quantities \(q_i\in[0,\bar x]\) and payments \(p_i\). The problem is to maximize \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i\), subject to individual rationality and incentive compatibility:
\[ v(q_i,\theta_i)-p_i\ge v(q_j,\theta_i)-p_j \quad\text{for all }i,j, \qquad v(q_i,\theta_i)-p_i\ge v(0,\theta_i). \]
Here a type completely specifies the buyer’s preferences, and mass is the share of buyers in that cohort. A plausible regime is a large customer base with \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of distinct types. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s selling problem. I would treat its complexity as an open question; the paper does not establish that this finite-support problem is tractable.

But this is an unanchored research direction, not a case meeting your anchor requirement. The paper contains no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or giving a computational formulation that it uses as such. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes incentive compatibility through subderivative selections, closed-path integrability, integral monotonicity, and the Mirrlees representation. Theorem 2, also proved here, bounds the difference in indirect utilities for incentive-compatible mechanisms sharing an allocation rule and a normalized lowest-type utility. Both are analytical results, not computational classifications. The optimal selling mechanism in Section 4.1 is not stated as a numbered theorem; Example 2 gives a particular allocation and payment rule.

So I can offer a plausible population mirror of the selling application, but there are zero eligible anchors and no honest per-anchor trichotomy to report. The weakest point is that the paper’s distribution is over one buyer’s private type, not over a society of buyers; the cohort model changes the population interpretation but may reduce to the same screening problem with an empirical type distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has supplied no eligible anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are numbered results, but they characterize incentive compatibility and bound utility differences; they do not state a complexity result for an encoded computational problem. The selling application derives an optimal mechanism, including an explicit rule in Example 2, but gives no running-time claim. More generally, the paper allows arbitrary measurable allocation sets and valuation functions, and an arbitrary type distribution \(F\); it supplies no finite input representation from which a computational mirror could be specified. That is the strongest objection here: there is no computational result in the paper to mirror.

The proposed high-volume selling model does not supply one. Section 4.1 already puts a distribution \(F\) over the buyer’s types into the seller’s expected-revenue objective. With independent customers and no shared constraint, a menu is incentive compatible for each customer exactly as in the paper, and \(N\) customers simply yield \(N\) times expected revenue. For finite support, masses weight the revenue objective while the type-by-type incentive constraints remain the same. This is a reasonable market interpretation, but it adds a population story to the paper’s prior distribution; it does not turn one of the paper’s results into a computational problem. A shared supply constraint or other coupling could make population mass consequential, but that would require a new model and new results.

The public-good application offers a more plausible population-coupled extension, but it has a different obstacle. In an atomless resident population, one resident’s report has zero effect on the aggregate location. The paper’s two-agent incentive problem therefore does not carry over directly: preserving finite strategic influence requires atoms or group-level reporting, which changes the agents and incentive constraints. Such a model may be worth studying, but the paper does not provide a computational result that anchors it. The single-agent principal–agent application likewise gains no new structure merely by replicating independent agents.

So the negative case is strong for this paper’s proposed mirror: it has no computational anchor, and the proponent’s best scenario either repeats the existing distribution over buyer types or requires a materially different, coupled mechanism-design model. It is not an airtight claim that no worthwhile continuous-population project can be built from these ideas. A carefully specified multi-agent model might be valuable; its value would be as a new project, rather than as the continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
