The case FOR (proponent)

The admissible anchor set is empty. This paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Definitions 1 and 2 formalize demographic stability, but they do not classify a problem as tractable or hard. The closest computational findings are unnumbered empirical results: the paper reports that its 42 maximum-stability allocations also satisfied degressive proportionality using 2025 populations. Those findings cannot serve as the named anchors you asked for.

The strongest positive research lead is still a direct mirror of the paper’s stability-maximization problem. Consider a large federation with many jurisdictions receiving seats under degressive proportionality. A type is a jurisdiction’s complete allocation-relevant description, including its population entitlement; \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of jurisdictions of type \(i\). Assume the types have distinct populations \(p_1>\cdots>p_\tau\). Each jurisdiction of type \(i\) receives the same integer number \(k_i\) of seats, while \(\sum_i\mu_i k_i=h\), where \(h\) is the average seat supply per jurisdiction. The instance also gives allowable integer seat bounds for each type.

The question is to choose the \(k_i\) that maximize the paper’s stability coefficient, subject to degressive proportionality:
\[ k_i\ge k_{i+1},\qquad \frac{k_i}{p_i}\le\frac{k_{i+1}}{p_{i+1}}. \]
For each adjacent pair, set \(r_i=1\) if \(k_i=k_{i+1}\), and otherwise set
\[ r_i=1-\frac{p_{i+1}k_i}{p_i k_{i+1}}. \]
The objective is to maximize \(\min_i r_i\). A solution is the integer seat count for each type and the resulting maximum coefficient. Mass matters through the average seat constraint; each jurisdiction still receives an integral number of seats, while the aggregate seats assigned to a type can be fractional in this normalized model.

A plausible regime is a federation with many local jurisdictions and repeated population entitlements, so the number \(N\) of jurisdictions is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct types. The allocation question stays close to the paper’s: it uses the same degressive constraints and the same stability objective, extended to a high-multiplicity setting. Its weakest point is the regime: the paper’s actual subjects are 27 distinct EU Member States, and many real jurisdiction populations may be unique. This is therefore a plausible extension of the authors’ problem, but the paper does not establish that they would regard it as their intended regime.

I would leave the extension’s A/B/C classification open. The paper gives no complexity result to mirror, and its empirical enumeration does not establish one. Further questions include how the complexity depends on \(\tau\), the bit length of the rational masses, and the seat bounds; whether demographic trajectories can replace the baseline stability coefficient; and whether a finite apportionment problem embeds in this type-mass version. This is the most concrete positive lead I can offer, but it is not an anchor-compliant case based on a named result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The required anchor set is empty. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity of a computational problem. Definitions 1 and 2 formalize demographic stability; the allocation search and the 2025 comparison are unnumbered computational and empirical findings. So the proponent’s extension is not anchored to a named computational result of this paper.

Their extension also changes what is being counted. In the paper, \(p_i\) is the population of a Member State, but the allocation unit is the state: seats go to states, not to citizens. In the proposed model, \(\mu_i\) counts jurisdictions of a given type, and \(\sum_i \mu_i k_i=h\) constrains average seats per jurisdiction. That can describe a federation with many repeated districts, but it is a distribution over allocation recipients, not over the citizens whose populations supply the paper’s entitlements. Treating citizens as the mass instead would require a new model connecting citizen types to state-level seat allocations.

There is a further gap between the paper’s stated notion and the proposed objective. Demographic stability is defined over a time interval and changing population vectors \(p^t\). The coefficient \(STD_s\) used in the allocation search is a static index evaluated at one population vector. The article checks the chosen allocations against 2025 data, but gives no computational result about stability over population trajectories or uncertainty sets. A trajectory-based mirror could be designed, perhaps with growth profiles included in each type, but that would be a new robust-apportionment problem rather than a mirror of a named result here.

That is the strongest negative case: no eligible result to anchor, and the proposed mirror shifts both the counted population and the stability question. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A federation with many repeated districts is plausible, and maximizing the paper’s coefficient over their allocations is a well-posed computational question. I can reject it as an anchor-compliant case for this paper; I cannot honestly rule out that adjacent project as worthwhile.
