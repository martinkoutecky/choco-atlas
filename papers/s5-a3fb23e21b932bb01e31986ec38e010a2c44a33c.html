The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s notion of dimension: the minimum number of weighted quota rules whose intersection implements a voting rule. It gives a precise continuous question, though it is a narrow case. The paper proves representational results about dimension; it gives no named result classifying an algorithmic problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federated membership body with many voting units drawn from a small number of constitutional types. Units of a type share their formal vote weight and the population they represent; each unit still casts an integral yes-or-no vote. With \(M\) units of each of 25 types, the body has \(25M\) members but only \(\tau=25\) types. The relevant decision variable is \(x_i\), the fraction of type \(i\)’s mass supporting a proposal. This describes a different institution from the EU Council itself, while preserving its three-part voting rule.

My anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It establishes that the post-November 1, 2004 game \(u_3=v_1\cap v_3\cap v_4\) has dimension exactly 3: it requires the intersection of three weighted majority games and cannot be represented by one or two. The three conditions are a majority of the 25 members, a vote-weight quota of 232 using the weights \(29(4),27(2),13(1),12(5),10(2),7(5),4(5),3(1)\), and a population quota of 620 using the population weights printed for \(v_4\). The paper proves the lower bound through coalition trades; its argument invokes the trade-robustness characterization cited as Theorem 2.4.

The continuous mirror is the Continuous Quota-Dimension problem. An instance consists of rational type masses \(\mu_i\) summing to 1, nonnegative per-unit vote weights \(w_i\) and population weights \(p_i\), and thresholds \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\). A supporting coalition is a vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_i\le\mu_i\). The rule accepts it when

\[ \sum_i x_i\ge\alpha,\qquad \sum_i w_i x_i\ge\beta\sum_i\mu_iw_i,\qquad \sum_i p_i x_i\ge\gamma\sum_i\mu_ip_i. \]

The question is to find the least \(k\) for which this accepted region, over the whole box \(0\le x\le\mu\), is exactly representable as the intersection of \(k\) weighted quota conditions \(a^j\cdot x\ge q_j\), with nonnegative rational \(a^j\) and positive \(q_j\). A solution gives the quotas and certifies that fewer cannot suffice.

For the paper’s data, take 25 types with \(\mu_i=1/25\), use its printed vote and population weights, and set \(\alpha=13/25\), \(\beta=232/321\), and \(\gamma=620/999\). I expect the continuous dimension to be 3 as well, but Theorem 4.2 does not establish that: it reasons about indivisible coalitions, while the mirror ranges over every fractional mass vector. The continuous problem for this three-quota family should be tractable: linear optimization can test whether each quota constraint is redundant, and an irredundant set of facet inequalities certifies the minimum. The exact continuous dimension for the printed instance is a question for that prover.

This mirror covers the dimension results in Section 4, especially Theorem 4.2. It leaves the paper’s Shapley–Shubik power and decisiveness calculations outside the proposed mirror. Natural further questions are whether dimension changes as the type distribution or thresholds change, how the continuous result relates to finite replicated voting bodies, and what complexity arises for more general monotone rules.

The weak point is that the paper’s examples are councils of a few indivisible sovereign states, and the proposed regime replaces those states with many voting units. The theorem also concerns a measure of representation size rather than the running time for solving an input problem. The case rests on a close match between the paper’s central object—intersections of weighted quota games—and a well-defined continuous extension of that same object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.2 is not a computational result. It establishes that one particular 25-country voting game has dimension exactly 3. The upper bound is its stated three-game representation; the lower bound uses coalition trades to rule out smaller representations. The paper does not define a problem of computing dimension from input, give an algorithm for it, or classify its complexity. Its other results are also analyses of particular games and power measures. So the proposed mirror may be a sensible new computational question, but it does not continue a computational result this paper made.

The proposed high-multiplicity scenario is not obviously unreasonable: a large federation could have many interchangeable units of each constitutional type. I would not reject it just because the actual Council has only 25 sovereign states. The difficulty is that the mirror changes what the theorem establishes. The paper’s dimension compares representations on the finite set of coalitions; the proposed continuous dimension compares regions over every fractional mass vector. The paper’s trade witnesses do not establish that the fractional region needs three quotas.

One can make the mirror closer by studying replicated versions of the rule and asking whether their type-level quota dimension agrees with the paper’s dimension. That is a coherent question, but it is a new result about a family of games. In the narrow formulation offered, the continuous input is already given as three quota inequalities, so computing its dimension becomes a question about which inequalities define facets of the resulting polytope. To get a broader complexity problem, one must choose a more general input language for voting rules; Theorem 4.2 does not supply one.

This is a real weakness in the case for treating the paper as a ChoCo contribution, but it does not establish that the mirror is unworthy. The continuous quota-dimension question is mathematically coherent, and the alternative federation story could make its population model sensible. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: the anchor is a poor fit to the programme’s computational gap, while the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is not supported by this paper.
