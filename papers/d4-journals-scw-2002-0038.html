No computational result is mirrored; Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollaries 1 and 2 concern equilibrium behavior and limiting payoffs.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is the population regime: a large electorate can have just three payoff-relevant voter types, with their state-dependent proportions \(r(t\mid\omega)\) fixed as the expected electorate size \(n\) grows. That is a credible high-multiplicity scenario, and the paper’s Poisson model already works with those type proportions.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which establishes plurality equilibria with \(h=0\) and \(h=2\), plus at least one intermediate equilibrium for sufficiently large \(n\), with a sequence of intermediate equilibria converging to \(h=1\); and Theorem 2, also proved here, which, when \(C\) is a serious contender, establishes a runoff equilibrium with \(0<\tilde h<2\) for sufficiently large \(n\), with every sequence of equilibria converging to \(\tilde h=1\). Corollaries 1 and 2 give limiting expected payoffs. These are equilibrium and asymptotic results, not classifications such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. They therefore cannot serve as anchors under your instructions.

A plausible follow-on question would be: given rational priors, type proportions, utilities, and a voting rule, compute the set of limiting type-contingent vote strategies and corresponding majority payoffs obtained from Bayes–Nash equilibria of Poisson games with intensities \(n r(t\mid\omega)\) as \(n\to\infty\). Its solution would be that limit set and its payoffs. This is close to the paper’s subject, but it is not an eligible anchored mirror, and the paper already analyzes that limit for its restricted model.

The main weakness is also structural: the paper’s strategic incentives turn on the relative probabilities of near ties, which vanish as \(n\) grows but still determine best responses. In a nonatomic model where each person controls zero mass, a voter cannot affect the outcome, so those pivotal incentives disappear. Preserving them requires retaining the Poisson finite-game deviations. That makes the proposed follow-on question resemble the paper’s asymptotic analysis more than a new continuous computational problem. Without a qualifying anchor, I can’t honestly assign it to the programme’s tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness classes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium strategies under plurality; Theorem 2 does so for runoff under a stated condition; the corollaries give limiting payoffs. The lemmas analyze probabilities of near ties. None asks for an algorithmic intervention or establishes a complexity bound. That leaves no named result for ChoCo’s complexity programme to take up.

The best candidate mirror is to input rational type proportions and utilities, then compute limiting equilibrium strategies and majority payoffs. But for the paper’s three-candidate, three-type model, that is already the analysis the paper performs. Making it a computational-complexity problem requires enlarging the candidate or type space. That could be worthwhile game-theoretic research, but it would be a new problem, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

There is also a substantive mismatch at the limit. The paper’s strategic predictions depend on comparing probabilities of pivotal events that vanish as \(n\) grows; Lemmas 2 and 5 use their exponential rates to determine best responses. In a nonatomic society, an individual has zero influence on the outcome, so those pivotal incentives disappear. Keeping them requires retaining the finite-\(n\) Poisson game and its rare-event asymptotics. That preserves the paper’s analysis, but does not yield the sort of continuous computational problem ChoCo is designed to classify.

This is not a claim that high multiplicity is implausible: the paper’s large-electorate regime is credible. Nor does an existing high-multiplicity model count against a mirror. The negative case is narrower: the paper already studies that regime analytically, and no computational question is central to its results. A further model could introduce continuous mass interventions or algorithmic equilibrium computation, but the honest case against is that neither is anchored here. The universal claim remains weak: plausible adjacent computational questions can be invented, though they would need to establish their connection to the authors’ problem rather than inherit it from these theorems.
