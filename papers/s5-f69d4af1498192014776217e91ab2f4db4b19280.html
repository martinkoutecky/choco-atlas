The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest substantive opening is large district admissions in which students fall into a modest number of preference and priority classes. A type would need to include each student’s full ranking and all priority information the mechanism uses; mass would be the fraction of the district in that type, and school capacities would scale with population. That makes a continuum version of the paper’s blocking-student comparisons imaginable.

But there is no qualifying named computational result to anchor it to. The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: when there are at least two schools and \(|S|>k>\ell\ge 1\), constrained Gale–Shapley with list limit \(k\) has weakly fewer blocking students than with limit \(\ell\) at every problem, and strictly fewer at some problem. That is a comparative fairness result, not a complexity or algorithmic result. The other named results likewise concern fairness, stability, or manipulation. The deferred-acceptance algorithm is described as background, but the paper gives no named result classifying the complexity of a problem it computes.

So I can’t honestly state a continuous *computational* problem per anchor under your criteria. A continuum analogue of Theorem 1 could be a follow-on question about blocking mass, but it would be a new comparative fairness result, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The priority structure also makes a finite-type regime difficult to defend when individual strict priorities matter: encoding each student’s exact priority position may make the number of types grow with the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not argue that school admissions has no sensible high-multiplicity regime. A large district could have many students in the same preference and priority classes, with school capacities scaled to population. The paper’s common-priority example makes that scenario especially credible.

But the proponent’s only anchor, Theorem 1, does not give ChoCo a computational result to mirror. It proves a comparison: for every finite instance, \(GS^k\) has no more blocking students than \(GS^\ell\), with a strict improvement on some instance. A natural continuous analogue would compare the *mass* of blocking students under the two mechanisms. One could make that model carefully, including priority tiers and fractional assignments at capacity cutoffs. That would be a sensible continuous fairness question, but it would still be a comparative theorem, not a complexity or algorithmic result in the paper.

The stronger computational reframe—given a distribution over preference and priority types, compute each mechanism’s outcome and blocking mass, or decide which has fewer blockers—would ask for work the paper does not do. The paper’s new results compare mechanisms across instances; they do not study the complexity of computing an outcome, counting blockers, or finding a manipulation. Deferred acceptance appears as background, not as a complexity result. Turning the comparison into an input-and-algorithm problem would therefore add a computational question rather than continuize one of the paper’s computational results. Theorem 2 has the same problem: its relationship between blocking and manipulating students is also a mathematical comparison, not a complexity claim.

So the negative case is strongest on scope, not on population modeling. A continuous blocking-mass analysis could be worthwhile school-choice research, and the high-multiplicity story is plausible. But none of the paper’s named results supplies the computational anchor this programme is meant to mirror.
