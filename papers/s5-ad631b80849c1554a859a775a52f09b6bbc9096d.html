The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) identical machines, a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), processing times \(p_t\), and a per-machine polymatroid \(P_\rho\) induced by a repeated-job matroid, choose \(z_{it}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_i z_{it}=\mu_t\) and \((z_{it})_{t\in T}\in P_\rho\) for each machine; minimize \(\max_i\sum_t p_tz_{it}\).

The model it lives in

Use job types carrying mass \(\mu_t\), processing time \(p_t\), and their complete role in the matroid constraints. Each machine receives a type-mass vector in the normalized polymatroid \(P_\rho\), and the objective is the maximum machine load. For feasible instances, convexity and machine symmetry give the optimum \(\sum_t p_t\mu_t/m\).

The objection that survived

Identical-machine symmetry makes the continuous optimum immediate, so the mirror yields little continuous complexity landscape; the proponent’s LP discussion does not address how simple the answer is.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 6’s makespan result in exchangeable repeated-type regimes; it does not cover the paper’s fair-division, discrepancy, prefix-basis, or unsplittable-flow results.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which exchangeable replicated-job matroids does the normalized type-count feasibility region converge to \(P_\rho\), and what rate of convergence does this give for the normalized finite makespan optimum?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It says: for a matroid \(M=(J,\mathcal I)\) whose jobs can be partitioned into \(m\) independent sets, and nonnegative processing times \(p\), there is a polynomial-time algorithm that finds a feasible schedule \(J=I_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_m\) with
\[ \max_{i\in[m]}p(I_i)\leq \mathrm{OPT}+\left(1-\frac1m\right)p_{\max}. \]
The paper proves this by adding zero-processing-time dummy jobs and applying its weighted equitability result. The anchor is the matroid-constrained makespan problem itself, not that proof technique.

A natural high-multiplicity regime has many jobs but relatively few complete job types. A type records processing time and the job’s full role in the matroid constraints; jobs are interchangeable only when those features agree. For example, a large service system might assign millions of cases from a few recurring categories to identical centers, with rules limiting which category combinations each center may handle. The number of cases \(N\) is large compared with the number of types \(\tau\). In a replicated instance, the matroid is invariant under swapping copies of a type, and its normalized type-count constraints converge to a polymatroid with rank function \(\rho\).

This gives a precise mirror, Mass Matroid Scheduling. The input is \(m\) identical machines, a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), processing times \(p_t\geq 0\), and a rational type-level polymatroid rank function \(\rho\) induced by the repeated-job matroid. Choose masses \(z_{it}\geq 0\), where \(z_{it}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to machine \(i\), subject to
\[ \sum_{i=1}^m z_{it}=\mu_t \quad (t\in T), \qquad \sum_{t\in S}z_{it}\leq \rho(S) \quad (i\in[m],\,S\subseteq T). \]
Minimize the maximum machine load \(\max_i\sum_{t\in T}p_tz_{it}\). A solution is the mass allocation \(z\), or that allocation together with its maximum load. Every unit of population mass is assigned to one machine; the \(z_{it}\) describe the resulting allocation of each cohort.

This is a sensible continuous form of the authors’ problem because it keeps the identical-machine objective and the matroid-based feasibility rule, while the repeated jobs become type masses. The per-machine polymatroid is the co-modification needed to express the limiting matroid constraints on type counts. The mirror covers Theorem 6’s makespan result in this repeated-type regime; it does not claim to cover the paper’s EF1, discrepancy, or prefix-constrained results.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: it is a linear program with \(m\tau\) variables. With a separation oracle for \(\rho\), separation reduces to minimizing the submodular function \(\rho(S)-z_i(S)\); thus an oracle-model algorithm should be polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) measures the rational input size. The associated bridge question is whether, for the stated exchangeable replications, the normalized finite optimum converges to the mass optimum and at what rate. Theorem 6 gives a useful scale check: dividing its additive guarantee by \(N\) makes its \(\left(1-\frac1m\right)p_{\max}\) term vanish as \(N\) grows. That bound is relative to the finite optimum, so it does not by itself establish convergence to the continuous optimum.

My weakest point is that Theorem 6 does not study repeated types or prove this limiting correspondence. The proposed mirror therefore depends on a restricted, symmetric matroid regime and on a type-level rank description that the paper does not develop. A referee might see the LP as a familiar relaxation unless the finite-to-continuous dictionary is made explicit. I still think the case is worth making: the object being aggregated is the large population of jobs, and its matroid constraints remain part of the allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is a real computational result, so the “nothing to mirror” objection does not apply. Nor is repeated job types an implausible regime. The better version of the proposed mirror still runs into a more basic problem: with identical machines and divisible type masses, makespan optimization disappears.

Let \(P_\rho\) be the per-machine polymatroid of feasible type masses. For any feasible allocation \(z\), average the machine allocations:
\[ \bar z_{it}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{h=1}^{m}z_{ht}=\frac{\mu_t}{m}. \]
Since \(P_\rho\) is convex and every machine has the same constraints, this averaged allocation is feasible on every machine. It gives each machine load \(\sum_t p_t\mu_t/m\), which is also a lower bound on the maximum load of any allocation. Thus the continuous optimum is exactly average total load. The same argument applies to any sensible normalized base-polytope model that preserves the paper’s identical-machine symmetry.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is also unusually immediate. With \(N\) jobs, Theorem 6 and the average-load lower bound put the finite optimum within \((1-1/m)p_{\max}\) of \(Np^\top\mu/m\); after normalization, the gap is \(O(1/N)\). That is a useful limit observation, but it leaves no continuous makespan complexity landscape to chart. Making the mirror computationally richer would require features such as machine-specific constraints or ordered assignments, which change the identical-machine problem Theorem 6 addresses.

This is the strongest objection to the anchor, but it is not an airtight case against every possible mirror of the paper. The job population has a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation, and the normalization result may interest the authors. The negative case is that this particular computational result yields a continuous mirror whose optimization is solved by symmetry, not that no worthwhile mirror can exist anywhere in the paper.
