The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a strong positive case that satisfies the anchor requirement: the paper has no named computational result. Its named results are mathematical characterizations and duality statements, not claims of polynomial time, hardness, or parameterized complexity; the paper gives no computational problem encoding, algorithm, or running time.

The closest results are Theorem 3, proved here, which says that for a setting with vN–M utilities, \(\epsilon\)-Semistrong Pareto is equivalent to \(u_0\) being within \(\epsilon/2\) in supremum norm of an aggregator with nonnegative weights; and Theorem 5, also proved here, which gives the corresponding equivalence between Sequential \(\epsilon\)-Strong Pareto and approximation by an aggregator with strictly positive weights. Both are analytic representation results, so neither is a valid complexity anchor.

A tempting extension would take types to be complete vN–M utility functions, let \(\mu\) describe their population shares, and seek a nonnegative weight measure \(\lambda\) and constant \(b\) minimizing
\[ \sup_{x\in X}\left|u_0(x)-\int u_t(x)\,d\lambda(t)-b\right|. \]
That is a recognizable continuous-population analogue of the paper’s aggregation question. But the paper studies finitely many utilities, says its methods do not appear to extend to infinitely many individuals, and asks an axiomatic representation question rather than a computational one. So this candidate does not supply a defensible ChoCo mirror anchored in a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own admission is decisive under ChoCo’s criteria: Theorems 3 and 5 are not computational results. Theorem 3 characterizes \(\epsilon\)-Semistrong Pareto by proximity to an aggregator with nonnegative weights; Theorem 5 gives the corresponding characterization for Sequential \(\epsilon\)-Strong Pareto and strictly positive weights. Neither states a complexity result for an encoded problem. There is no decision or search problem, input representation, algorithm, or running time to mirror.

The best version of the proposed mirror would take complete utility functions as types, a population distribution \(\mu\) over them, and ask for the closest aggregator
\[ \inf_{\lambda,b}\sup_{x\in X} \left|u_0(x)-\int u_t(x)\,d\lambda(t)-b\right|. \]
That is a coherent continuous-agent representation question, but it does not make the paper’s question computational. If the types have finite support, then any positive mass on a type can be absorbed into its free Pareto weight: the theorem imposes no relationship between population shares and aggregation weights. Replicating people or changing their proportions therefore adds no population-complexity problem. Requiring weights to follow population shares would make the model depend on mass, but it adds a new aggregation axiom the paper does not study.

For an atomless population, an integral gives each individual zero weight. The natural alternatives are to require the Pareto condition almost everywhere and positive influence on positive-mass sets, or to impose a full-support weighting measure. Those could be sensible extensions, especially for Theorem 5, but they change the representation question and remain analytic unless a finite input model and computational task are supplied. With explicitly listed finite alternatives and utility types, finding the nearest aggregator is ordinarily a linear program. If instead one chooses a succinctly represented continuum of types or an infinite alternative space where optimization is difficult, the complexity comes from that added representation and its separation problem, not from a computational result in this paper.

So neither named theorem survives as a ChoCo anchor, and the proposed integral formulation does not repair the gap. The negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: it cannot prove that no useful algorithmic problem could ever be built around continuous populations and utility aggregation. It can say that such a problem would be a new research question, rather than a worthwhile computational mirror of the results this paper establishes.
