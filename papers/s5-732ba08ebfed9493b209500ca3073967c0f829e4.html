The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that the paper has no result to mirror. It is a position paper: it presents empirical analyses and recommendations, but no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the computational complexity of a problem. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is cited from elsewhere and concerns manipulability, not a computational classification. The cited work on dynamic heart allocation is also prior work, not a result established here. Under ChoCo’s named-anchor gate, that is decisive: a newly formulated mass optimization problem may be worthwhile, but it cannot become a mirror of a result this paper does not contain.

Section 6 is the closest candidate, as the proponent notes. One could model a large community as masses of stakeholder preference types and study how to elicit or aggregate those preferences into an allocation policy. That could be a sensible computational social-choice problem, but the paper specifies no elicitation rule, input-access model, policy space, objective, or computational task. Supplying the full preference distribution as input would assume away the elicitation problem the section discusses; adding a query model would introduce a new one. The paper’s “continuous distribution” in Section 2 means a real-valued priority score for each patient, and its “continuous monitoring” in Section 4 concerns time. Neither makes the population continuous.

A stronger extension could model patients as cohorts with shared clinical features and manipulation costs, then optimize an incentive-aware policy over those cohorts. The authors would recognize that as part of their research agenda. But to make it a computational problem, one must specify the policy class, clinical and strategic objectives, donor supply, and how changing patient states and compatibility are handled. Those choices could produce good research; they are not a continuization of a named result here. The same gap applies to proposals based on OPO discretion, center monitoring, or multi-listing: the paper identifies these as problems, but does not give a computational result whose population dependence can be carried into a mirror.

So the proponent raises no surviving anchor, and their Section 6 possibility does not supply one. The defensible negative verdict is that this text does not support a paper-anchored ChoCo mirror. The broader claim that no useful continuous model could ever be built from its agenda is weaker: a fluid, cohort-based allocation model is plausible, but it would be a new formalization rather than a mirror of this paper.
