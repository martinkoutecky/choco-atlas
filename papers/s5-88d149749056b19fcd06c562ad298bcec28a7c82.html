The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime here, but the paper has no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under the stated standard. Its numbered results concern incentive guarantees, not whether a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified:

  • Theorem 1, cited from Brandl et al. (2021), says no strategyproof distribution rule satisfies both efficiency and positive share.
  • Theorem 2, proved here, says the incentive ratios of MP and FUT are \(\Theta(n)\), while EGAL’s is \(\Theta(m)\).
  • Theorem 3, proved here, says NASH’s incentive ratio is exactly \(2\), even when voters can report arbitrary nonzero concave utility functions.
  • Theorem 4, proved here, gives lower bounds of \(2\) for rules satisfying AFS, strictly concave welfare maximizers, and regular efficient rules satisfying GFS.

So the closest named result is Theorem 3, but it is not a valid complexity anchor. I would not present this paper as having one.

The strongest plausible mirror is in participatory budgeting: a large city has many residents but a relatively small set of distinct approval ballots over projects. A type is an approval set \(A\subseteq C\); its mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of residents with that ballot. The resource allocation is still a distribution \(p\in\Delta(C)\), and type \(A\)’s utility is \(u_A(p)=\sum_{c\in A}p(c)\). The corresponding mass-NASH rule chooses \(p\) to maximize
\[ \sum_{A\in T}\mu_A\log u_A(p). \]
This is a population mirror: the divisible budget in the paper is not the reason it qualifies.

A precise new, unanchored computational question would be: given rational masses \(\mu\), a type \(A\), a bloc size \(0<\delta\leq\mu_A\), and a threshold \(r\), is there a nonempty approval set \(B\) such that moving mass \(\delta\) from type \(A\) to report \(B\) raises that bloc’s true utility by a factor of at least \(r\)? Formally, with \(\mu'=\mu-\delta e_A+\delta e_B\), ask whether
\[ u_A(p(\mu'))\geq r\,u_A(p(\mu)), \]
where \(p(\cdot)\) is the mass-NASH outcome. The output is a witnessing ballot \(B\), or “no.” Its input parameters would include \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the bit length of the masses and threshold.

Theorem 3 is relevant support, though not an anchor for this search problem. Its unnumbered Remark 1 extends the \(2\)-factor bound to group manipulations; for a bloc with one shared approval type, that bounds the bloc’s gain. Theorem 4(1) supplies the matching lower-bound phenomenon for AFS rules in finite societies. Rational mass profiles reproduce finite profiles after clearing denominators, so the global factor-\(2\) bound carries over to this mass-NASH formulation. The paper does not, however, classify the cost of finding the best report \(B\). For a fixed \(B\), computing the outcome is a concave optimization problem and looks tractable to numerical precision. Searching over all ballots has combinatorics in \(m\); I would tentatively expect hardness to persist from a discrete version if one can establish that version’s hardness, but this paper does not do so.

The weak point is the strategic change: an individual has zero mass in an atomless population, so the meaningful action is a positive-mass bloc’s deviation. The paper’s group-manipulation remark makes that extension plausible, but it remains a different question from its single-voter incentive-ratio theorem. Thus this is the best positive research direction I see, not an anchor-based case for this paper under ChoCo’s computational-complexity test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result; Theorems 2–4 bound incentive ratios and characterize classes of rules. None classifies an algorithmic problem by running time, hardness, or parameterization. The experiments compute manipulation statistics for sampled finite profiles, but do not make a complexity claim. So there is no computational result here for ChoCo to mirror.

The strongest candidate is NASH. In an atomless society, a single voter has measure zero, so changing that voter’s report leaves the outcome unchanged. The direct analogue of Theorem 3’s unilateral incentive ratio therefore degenerates. Giving the deviator positive mass avoids that problem, but changes the question to coalition manipulation. The paper itself already considers group manipulations in Remark 1, with a factor-\(2\) guarantee; for rational masses, the weighted model corresponds to a finite replicated profile. That is useful support for the model, but it does not turn the theorem into a computational-complexity result.

The proposed search over reports \(B\) for a fixed bloc and profile is coherent, and a stronger version could let the bloc split its mass across multiple reports. But either version adds a computational search problem that the paper does not state or analyze. It could be a worthwhile project in its own right; it is not an anchor in this paper. The same applies to computing the mass-NASH allocation: it is a natural algorithmic question about the rule, but no named result here asks or answers it.

The negative case is strongest on the paper’s fit for the programme, not on the universal claim that no useful question can be built from it. Citywide approval budgets provide a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the bloc-search question deserves consideration independently. I would reject this paper as a strong ChoCo anchor, while conceding that the proposed extension may be worthwhile on its own.
