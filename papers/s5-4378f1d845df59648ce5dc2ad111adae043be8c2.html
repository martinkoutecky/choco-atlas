The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with binary cost vectors \(a_{t,e}\in\{0,1\}\), type masses \(\mu_t\), equal entitlements, and per-capita chore supplies \(q_e\), compute a finite-support lottery over measurable allocations of integral bundle vectors \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{E}\) that assigns all chore mass, makes every realization ex-post EFX and fPO, and satisfies ex-ante EF and fPO for every positive-mass ordered pair of agents.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity binary-chore model with type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_e\), integral bundle patterns \(b\), mass-transport variables \(x_{t,b}\), and lottery weights over integral population assignments; the guarantees are ex-ante EF, ex-post EFX, and fPO.

The objection that survived

The finite Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition does not by itself prove that a type-level transportation solution has a finite-support measurable lottery whose positive-mass cells satisfy ex-post EFX and fPO; the compressed problem may also involve exponentially many integral bundle patterns.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorems 3.5 and 4.8. Theorem 4.9 could receive an analogous cancelable-cost treatment, while the non-existence examples, boundary counterexamples, and auxiliary structural lemmas remain outside the proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-size type-level transportation or configuration LP construct the lottery from binary-encoded \(\mu_t\) and \(q_e\)?
  • Can every feasible mass coupling be realized by a finite-support measurable lottery with support-wise EFX and fPO?
  • What rounding theorem transfers a continuum solution to a finite population with controlled WEFX or EF error?
  • Does the deterministic WEFX potential admit a mass-level termination or pricing argument?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper does admit a genuine continuous-population mirror, with Theorem 3.5 as the lead anchor and Theorem 4.8 as a particularly natural second one. The paper has no named NP-hardness or parameterized-hardness theorem: Examples 3.6, B.3, and B.4 are non-existence results, not complexity results. Thus the positive case must be built from its polynomial algorithms.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper. It states that, for binary additive chores allocated to agents with positive weights summing to \(1\), Algorithm 1 runs in polynomial time and computes an allocation that is simultaneously WEFX and fPO. Concretely, if \(X_i\) is agent \(i\)'s bundle, the output satisfies

\[ \frac{\hat c_i(X_i)}{w_i}\leq \frac{c_i(X_j)}{w_j} \]

for every pair \(i,j\), and no fractional allocation Pareto dominates it. The algorithm achieves this by assigning every zero-available chore to an agent who incurs cost \(0\), distributing universal chores, and then performing local transfers guided by the rank potential in Lemma 3.4.

I would mirror this as Population-WEFX-fPO\(_\infty\).

There is a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types. A type \(t\) specifies a binary cost vector

\[ a_{t,e}\in\{0,1\} \]

over a finite set \(E\) of chore classes, together with an entitlement parameter \(\omega_t>0\). The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\omega_t=1. \]

Chore class \(e\) has per-capita supply \(q_e\). Thus a finite realization with \(N\) agents has \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_e\) copies of chore class \(e\), whenever these quantities are integral. Each individual still receives an integral bundle; only the population is continuous.

A bundle is an integral count vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}^{E}\). Its cost to type \(t\) is

\[ c_t(b)=\sum_{e\in E}a_{t,e}b_e, \]

and its EFX-reduced cost is

\[ \hat c_t(b)= \max_{e:b_e>0}\bigl(c_t(b)-a_{t,e}\bigr), \]

with \(\hat c_t(0)=0\).

The decision variable is a finite-support mass table \(x_{t,b}\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving integral bundle \(b\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t \]

for every type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,b}b_e x_{t,b}=q_e \]

for every chore class \(e\).

The continuous problem asks for such an \(x\) satisfying:

\[ x_{t,b}>0,\ x_{u,b'}>0,\ b\neq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\hat c_t(b)}{\omega_t} \leq \frac{c_t(b')}{\omega_u}, \]

together with fPO. Equivalently, no fractional population allocation may weakly reduce every agent’s cost and strictly reduce the cost of a positive-mass set of agents. A convenient sufficient condition, exactly paralleling the paper, is minimum aggregate cost:

\[ \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}c_t(b) = \sum_{e:\,a_{t,e}=1\ \forall t}q_e. \]

The mass table is only a compressed description. In every realization, each agent receives an integral bundle. The fractional object appears only in the fPO comparison, just as in the paper.

This is not merely fractional fair division in disguise. The continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\), while bundle integrality, WEFX, and the fractional Pareto comparison remain the paper’s own. The natural regime is a large municipal or platform workforce assigning standardized unpleasant tasks: agents repeat a small number of profiles determined by certification, location, shift, or responsibility, while the number of agents and task copies is large. A type is genuinely complete: agents of one type have the same binary costs and entitlement.

I expect this problem to be Class A in the natural high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s \(M^+\)/\(M^0\) distinction becomes a type-level distinction between universally costly chore mass and mass that some type can perform at zero cost. The local transfers in Algorithm 1 become transfers of rational population mass between type-bundle cells. The main technical question is whether the rank-potential argument can be compressed into a configuration LP or a polynomial pricing procedure whose running time depends on \(\lvert T\rvert\), \(\lvert E\rvert\), and the encoding length of the \(q_e\), rather than on the expanded population size \(N\).

The further questions are substantive: can the discrete-to-continuous dictionary be made exact for rational masses; can finite solutions be rounded back with controlled WEFX error; and does the bundle-support or pricing problem become hard when the number of chore classes is unbounded? Those would test whether any hardness is caused by population multiplicity or by the underlying type/chore geometry.

The second anchor is Theorem 4.8, also proved in this paper. It states that for binary additive chores and unweighted agents, the paper’s algorithm computes in polynomial time a lottery satisfying ex-ante EF and fPO, while every realization is ex-post EFX and fPO. The proof constructs a feasible configuration, represents admissible reallocations by a bipartite matching graph, solves a polynomial-size LP, and applies Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition. Thus the expected fractional allocation is fPO, and every integral allocation in the lottery’s support is both EFX and fPO.

The corresponding mirror is Population-BoBW-EFX\(_\infty\). It uses the same \((T,\mu,E,q)\) model, now with equal entitlements. A solution is a finite-support lottery

\[ \mathcal L=\{(\lambda_r,X^r)\}_{r=1}^R, \qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1, \]

where every \(X^r\) is a measurable population allocation assigning integral bundles to individual agents and using exactly the prescribed chore mass.

Every realization must satisfy, for almost every pair of agents,

\[ \hat c_{\theta(a)}(B^r(a)) \leq c_{\theta(a)}(B^r(b)), \]

and must be fPO. The ex-ante requirement is

\[ \mathbb E_{\mathcal L} \left[c_{\theta(a)}(B^r(a))\right] \leq \mathbb E_{\mathcal L} \left[c_{\theta(a)}(B^r(b))\right] \]

for almost every ordered pair \(a,b\). The lottery is represented at the type-and-mass level, but each realization remains an integral assignment; randomization is not being used to fractionalize an individual’s chore bundle.

This mirror is especially plausible because the paper’s decomposition

\[ |M^+|=sn+r \]

becomes, in the continuum,

\[ q^+=s+\rho, \qquad \rho\in[0,1), \]

where exactly mass \(\rho\) receives \(s+1\) universal chores and the remaining mass receives \(s\). The discrete remainder \(r\) becomes an exact population fraction. The feasible-configuration condition becomes a mass-transport condition: positive mass of high type-bundle cells must see every positive-mass low bundle as costing at least one. The matching LP becomes a transportation LP over type masses and bundle slots. Birkhoff decomposition then produces a lottery over integral population allocations, not over fractional individual outcomes.

I would also expect this mirror to be Class A, and more confidently than the deterministic one. The matching and charging arguments already have the correct aggregate form, and the continuum removes rather than creates rounding complications. The main open issues are the size of a compressed lottery, whether every feasible type-level coupling admits a measurable ex-post-EFX realization, and how the exact unweighted result changes under unequal entitlements.

My weakest point is that Theorem 3.5 is polynomial in the explicitly listed agents and chores, not in a compressed high-multiplicity encoding. Its rank potential counts individual agents and universal chores, so a genuine continuization requires a new mass-level termination or pricing argument. Likewise, “almost every agent” is weaker than a guarantee for every named individual, although that weakening is intrinsic to treating sublinear exceptional populations as negligible.

That weakness does not undermine the mirror itself. The proposed regime has many genuine clones, a finite type description, per-capita chore supply, and integral bundles in every outcome. It is therefore a continuous population version of the paper’s problem, not a replacement by outcome-space continuity. The paper’s two polynomial results provide a credible Class-A starting point; the unresolved question is whether their algorithms compress cleanly, not whether the mirror is a sensible object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both anchors are finite-allocation results whose apparent continuum requires changing more than the population.

For Theorem 3.5, keeping the chore set fixed while the population grows makes the mirror degenerate. Almost every agent receives the empty bundle, while the finitely many chores become negligible population events. WEFX then either becomes vacuous or forces bundles to contain at most one costly chore; fPO is likewise trivial because every non-universal chore can be assigned to some zero-cost agent. The theorem’s local-search potential, which counts individual agents and universal chores, has no meaningful atomless limit.

The proponent’s repair is to scale chore supplies per capita. That does restore a nontrivial problem, but it is no longer merely continuizing the population: it introduces a second high-multiplicity resource model and replaces the paper’s finite allocation by a distribution over integral bundle patterns. The proposed \(x_{t,b}\) is therefore a new configuration problem, not a continuous version supplied by Theorem 3.5. In particular, the paper’s polynomial rank argument says nothing about computation from binary-encoded masses. The number of possible integral bundles may be exponential in the chore-class supplies, and WEFX is a support-wise, nonconvex condition rather than a linear mass constraint. There is no evident polynomial pricing or separation theorem.

That is the best technical objection to the first anchor, but it does not defeat the strongest mirror. Binary additive costs make grouping chores into classes lossless, and scaling supplies is exactly the co-modification forced by preserving nonzero per-agent bundles. A type can include the complete cost vector and entitlement, so no identity information has been discarded. The resulting high-multiplicity configuration problem is well posed even if the paper’s algorithm does not solve it. Its unknown complexity is a reason to study it, not a reason to reject it.

Theorem 4.8 has a similar apparent problem. With a fixed finite chore set, the remainder \(r/n\) tends to zero, so the high-agent part of the lottery disappears and the ex-ante/ex-post distinction loses its force. But again, scaling chore supplies yields a nontrivial limit: \(q^+=s+\rho\), with a positive mass \(\rho\) receiving \(s+1\) universal chores. The matching construction can then be replaced by a transportation problem over bundle mass, and ex-ante envy remains linear because costs are additive. The finite Birkhoff decomposition does not automatically provide the required atomless lottery, but that is a technical gap rather than a modelling impossibility.

One could object that “almost every agent” is weaker than the paper’s guarantee for every named agent. Yet the mirror can require an essential, support-wise EFX condition, or equivalently require every positive-mass type–bundle cell to satisfy the inequalities against every positive-mass cell. The continuous formulation then retains the substantive guarantee and does not rely on measure-zero exceptions.

Nor is this an “already continuous” result. The paper’s fractional allocations and lotteries continuize outcomes, while the proposed model continuizes the population and preserves integral bundles for each agent. Existing high-multiplicity allocation work would support, rather than collide with, this formulation.

So the negative case is ultimately weak. The fixed-resource limit degenerates, and neither finite polynomial algorithm transfers automatically to a polynomial algorithm in a compressed mass encoding. Those are real cautions about the proposed Class-A story. They do not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: both Theorem 3.5 and Theorem 4.8 admit a coherent high-multiplicity population interpretation, with precisely the new pricing, transport, rounding, and complexity questions the continuization programme is meant to expose.
