The case FOR (proponent)

There are no compliant anchors in this paper. It states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about a problem being in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Sections 4.2–4.6 report allocations from the five rules for a two-agent case study; the conclusions recommend PRO and modified CEA for that case. Those are computational results in the everyday sense, but they do not meet the anchor requirement. So there is no anchor-backed question to write down.

The strongest positive candidate is an extension of the paper’s PRO rule. Imagine a river compact with many water-demand units—such as irrigation districts or municipal systems—whose per-capita claims fall into a small number of shared types. A type \(t\) is specified by its per-capita claim \(c_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of represented demand units of that type. With available water \(E\) per unit of normalized mass, where \(0<E<C=\sum_t\mu_t c_t\), the problem is: compute the exact PRO allocation \(y_t\) per unit of mass for each type. The solution is \(y_t=E c_t/C\), giving total allocation \(\mu_t y_t\) to type \(t\). The allocation vector is the decision output; PRO supplies the rule, and the paper specifies no welfare objective to optimize. This is computable in polynomial time in the support size and rational input bit length, directly from the formula.

A plausible regime would have many districts or accounts but few recurring claim schedules, so the number of represented units is much larger than the number of types. The authors could recognize this as applying their general bankruptcy rules to a high-multiplicity claimant population: the paper defines rules for \(n\) agents, while its Missouri application groups them into upstream and downstream agents. That recognition is an inference, and this candidate mirrors only the PRO allocation calculation. It does not mirror the paper’s fairness comparison or its conclusions about the two Missouri regions.

The weak point is the identity of the agents. The paper’s claimants are politically distinct regions with claims and contributions tied to geography; a population of water-demand units may not be the unit of agreement in a river compact. The proposed mirror also extends an explicit formula rather than resolving a hard computational problem. Further questions would include whether a continuous modified-CEA rule behaves consistently when identical claimants are split into types, and how to extend sequential sharing to a continuum of ordered river locations. Neither question is anchored by a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper states no computational result to mirror. It defines five bankruptcy allocation rules and applies them to a two-agent Missouri River case study. Its recommendation of PRO and modified CEA is an empirical and normative conclusion about that case, not a complexity result or an algorithmic classification. Equations (6)–(14) specify the rules; they do not establish the complexity of a computational problem. So none of the paper’s results supplies a compliant anchor.

The proposed PRO extension is mathematically coherent. With claimant types indexed by per-capita claim \(c_t\), masses \(\mu_t\), mean claim \(C=\sum_t\mu_t c_t\), and water supply \(E\), it gives each claimant of type \(t\) an allocation \(y_t=E c_t/C\), for total allocation \(\mu_t y_t\). A large population of recurring claimant types could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Fractional mass and the simple formula are not objections. But this is a mass version of evaluating a rule the paper already defines, not a continuous counterpart to a computational result the paper proves. It also leaves out the paper’s central comparison of geographically distinct upstream and downstream agents, whose claims and contributions have different roles in the case study. Keeping those distinctions in a fuller type description may make for a plausible new water-allocation study, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The negative case is strongest on that narrow point. I cannot honestly claim the paper’s subject could never support a worthwhile continuous-population study: the PRO example shows a plausible adjacent question. What the paper does not support is treating that question as a mirror of one of its computational results.
