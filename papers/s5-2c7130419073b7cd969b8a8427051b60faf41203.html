The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of weight types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive integer weights \(w_t\), a per-capita quota \(q\), and accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate value of \(\Psi_\mu(q)\) and an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal profile \(x\), where \(\Psi_\mu(q)=\max\{\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t h(x_t/\mu_t): 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\ \sum_{t\in T}w_tx_t=q\}\) and \(h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous society has type masses \(\mu_t\), with each type carrying weight \(w_t\). The decision variable \(x_t\) is the society mass of type \(t\) in a coalition; maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t h(x_t/\mu_t)\) subject to the per-capita quota constraint. This gives the exponential rate of coalition counts in finite replications.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's coalition-weight coefficient computation through its exponential rate. It does not cover the paper's full all-player Banzhaf algorithm or recover the finer counts needed for exact pivotality.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit complexity in \(\tau\), input length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\) suffices to compute the value and an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal profile?
  • What convergence rates hold for finite coalition counts, including cases with lattice effects?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest numbered anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that for \(G(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x^{w_i})\), if \(C\) is the number of nonzero coefficients, then all coalition-weight coefficients \(a_k\) can be computed in \(O(nC)\) time and \(O(C)\) memory. These coefficients count coalitions of each total weight. The paper’s stronger-sounding statement that exact Banzhaf-index computation is \(\#P\)-complete appears unnumbered in Section 3 and is attributed to [14], so I use it as context rather than as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large federation or association whose members vote in a weighted yes/no game. There may be millions of members but only \(\tau\) voting-weight classes: everyone in class \(t\) has the same weight \(w_t\). The equal-weight-class assumption is exactly the high-multiplicity grouping, and the quota scales with the size of the membership. Banzhaf’s equiprobable-coalition convention remains the mathematical measure of power; it need not describe how members actually vote.

The mirror of Theorem 3 is the Continuous Coalition-Sum Entropy Profile problem. Its input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), positive integer weights \(w_t\), a per-capita quota \(q\in[0,\sum_t\mu_t w_t]\), and an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\). The question is to compute, to additive error \(\varepsilon\), the maximum entropy of a coalition mass profile at that quota:

\[ \Psi_\mu(q)= \max_{\substack{0\le x_t\le\mu_t\\ \sum_{t\in T}w_t x_t=q}} \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,h\!\left(\frac{x_t}{\mu_t}\right), \qquad h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p). \]

A solution is an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate value and an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal vector \(x\); \(x_t\) is the fraction of the whole society from type \(t\) in the coalition. In finite replications with \(N\mu_t\) members of each type, the number of coalitions with total weight near \(Nq\) grows exponentially at rate \(\Psi_\mu(q)\). Thus the coefficient profile in Theorem 3 becomes an entropy profile over type-level masses. A narrow weight window avoids lattice cases where an exact total weight is unattainable.

I expect this mirror to be Class A: it is a concave optimization problem with one linear constraint. Its dual reduces evaluation to one-dimensional convex optimization,

\[ \Psi_\mu(q)=\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb R} \left[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log(1+e^{\lambda w_t})-\lambda q \right]. \]

That gives a concrete route to a precision algorithm, though its bit complexity in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\) would need to be established. Further questions include convergence rates from finite coalition counts to this profile, the effects of lattice structure, and whether subexponential corrections recover limiting type-level Banzhaf shares.

This is a focused mirror of the integer-weight coefficient computation in Theorem 3, not of the paper’s complete all-player Banzhaf algorithm. That boundary is also the case’s weakest point: Banzhaf swings depend on counts in a quota window only a constant width, while the entropy profile retains only exponential growth rates. It loses the finer factors that may determine the indices. I think the authors could recognize this as the continuous, large-population version of their coalition-sum counting subproblem; whether they would accept it as a mirror of their main power-index question is less certain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3: it gives an algorithm and complexity bound for computing coalition-weight coefficients. A large federation with a fixed set of voting-weight classes is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Grouping equal-weight members into types preserves the information relevant to their individual Banzhaf indices, so identity and multiplicity do not defeat the mirror.

The best version of the proposed mirror would ask for the full coalition-sum rate profile. In finite replications with \(N\mu_t\) members of each type, the coefficient at weight near \(Nq\), on a logarithmic scale divided by \(N\), leads to the entropy optimization \(\Psi_\mu(q)\). That is a coherent continuous question, and its authors could reasonably recognize it as the large-population counterpart of their coefficient computation.

The strongest objection is that this rate profile is not an intrinsic coalition-counting object of a nonatomic society. Under the finite Banzhaf convention, a uniformly random coalition selects each member independently with probability \(1/2\); at the level of type masses, the limiting coalition is simply \(x_t=\mu_t/2\). The profile \(\Psi_\mu\) recovers rare quota events by taking an exponential rate along a chosen sequence of finite replications. It discards lower-order information, which can matter for exact coefficients and for pivotality. A direct continuous pivotality measure also gives an infinitesimal member zero mass. These are real reasons to question whether the entropy profile mirrors the paper’s exact power-index computation.

They do not defeat the anchor. The paper’s theorem concerns coalition coefficients, and their exponential growth rate is a natural continuous target. The entropy profile is itself a computational problem over a continuous population, and its tractability would be a relevant result under ChoCo’s stated aims. The negative case is therefore weak: the best objection challenges whether this is the right continuous object, but it cannot honestly rule out that the authors would recognize it as a worthwhile mirror.
