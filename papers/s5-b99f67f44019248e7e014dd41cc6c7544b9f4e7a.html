The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under your anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 2, proved here, says that with congestion costs continuous and strictly increasing in type, no allocation rule on the unrestricted range can satisfy both strategy-proofness and cost efficiency. Theorem 3, also proved here, rules out strategy-proofness, Pareto optimality, and individual rationality together; the corollary extends the impossibilities to equal cost sharing. Theorem 4 gives the contrasting possibility for dichotomous costs: an equal-cost-sharing rule satisfying strategy-proofness, cost efficiency, individual rationality, Pareto optimality, and nondictatorship. These are results about which axioms allocation rules can satisfy, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified.

There is a tempting mirror in a large municipality whose residents share a small number of congestion types: type mass could describe the population, and club configurations could describe how that mass is grouped. But the paper’s central question is truth-telling by individual agents. In an atomless population, a unilateral report changes no type mass, so that strategic question does not carry over without additional modeling choices. Also, the paper’s continuous domain is the private type parameter; the society remains finite. Those features make the possible mirror worth investigating, but they do not supply a named computational anchor or a grounded continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the proposed anchors: none is a computational result. Theorem 2 rules out strategy-proofness together with cost efficiency; Theorem 3 and its corollary give related axiom incompatibilities; Theorem 4 establishes that a particular combination of axioms is possible for dichotomous costs. Those are results about mechanisms’ properties, not algorithms or computational complexity. The paper gives no named complexity result for a continuous-population mirror to inherit.

The municipality scenario does not carry those results over directly. If a mechanism depends only on a type-mass distribution \(\mu\), one person’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so individual strategy-proofness loses its usual force. To preserve the paper’s incentives, a model would need to represent how an individual joins a finite club and how that changes the club’s membership and cost shares. A distribution over types alone does not specify those assignments. Theorem 4’s serial choice among efficient partitions also uses an individual priority order, which a type-mass description does not provide without further structure.

There is a stronger possible repair: represent the population by a distribution over finite club compositions, then ask for a cost-efficient allocation of type mass across those compositions. That could produce a configuration LP and a genuine high-multiplicity computational question. It would preserve indivisible facilities through club patterns, with an appropriate per-capita cost scaling. I cannot rule it out as worthwhile; it is motivated by the paper’s cost-efficiency question, even though the paper proves no complexity result about it.

So the negative case is strong against treating the paper’s theorems as computational anchors. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weak: the cost-efficient club-pattern problem is a plausible mirror, and dismissing it would go beyond what this paper supports.
