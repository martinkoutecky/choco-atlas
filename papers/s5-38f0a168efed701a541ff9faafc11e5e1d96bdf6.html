The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its numbered theorems are about whether social choice functions can be implemented by mechanisms, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. It gives no algorithmic problem with an input encoding or complexity classification to continuize.

The nearest result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: if \(|N|\le |X|\), then any \(k\)-implementable social choice function is not minimally democratic; equivalently, no minimally democratic social choice function is \(k\)-implementable in that regime. That is a mechanism-existence impossibility, not a complexity result. A continuous-population question about implementing a rule for a distribution of preference types is conceivable, but it would ask an equilibrium implementability question, and Theorem 1 does not establish its continuous analogue. It would not supply the computational anchor this programme calls for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The attached case raises no surviving positive anchor. Its sole named candidate, Theorem 1, is an impossibility theorem about game forms and equilibria, not a complexity result. The paper gives no input encoding, algorithmic task, or complexity classification for a computational problem. Its other numbered theorems are also about implementation, and its fixed-agenda examples establish axiomatic properties of voting rules.

The strongest continuous version of Theorem 1 would replace a profile of individual rankings with a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, then ask whether a democratic rule can be implemented for every such \(\mu\). That is a recognizable game-theoretic question, but the theorem quantifies over preference profiles and unrestricted mechanisms; the paper supplies no finite representation of those mechanisms or computational task for an algorithm to solve. Theorem 1’s stated regime, \(n\le |X|\), also does not describe the usual high-multiplicity limit with a fixed set of alternatives.

There is a further obstacle to carrying over the paper’s strategic concepts. In an atomless population, a single voter has zero mass and cannot change an aggregate outcome by deviating. The paper’s iterative elimination of individually dominated strategies therefore loses its force. One could define deviations by positive-mass coalitions, or use finite blocs, but that adds a strategic model and a computational question absent from the paper. Replacing the one-voter near-consensus condition with an overwhelming-majority condition handles its mass-scale issue; it does not supply the missing computational result or restore individual pivotality.

The fixed-agenda examples offer the most plausible alternative: compute a sophisticated outcome from a distribution of rankings. That could be a sensible new question. It would be an algorithmic problem built from the paper’s setting, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper proves. The continuous outcome spaces in Theorems 2–4 do not change this: they concern alternatives, while the electorate remains finite.

So the case against prioritizing this paper for ChoCo is strong: the paper has no computational anchor, and its central equilibrium notion behaves awkwardly under an atomless population. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror could be devised is weaker. A mass-coalition implementation project could make sense on its own; it would need its own model and computational results rather than serving as a mirror of this paper.
