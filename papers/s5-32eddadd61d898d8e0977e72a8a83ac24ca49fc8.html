The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many schools with rational per-capita capacities and an atomless population described by rational piecewise-constant densities over student preferences, between-group priority classes, and within-group rank vectors, does there exist, and can one compute in polynomial time, a finite-description measurable assignment that is individually rational and has no positive-mass blocking coalition? For within-group blocks, require each school’s mass by between-group class to remain unchanged and define the interrupter-slack condition by integrating, over coalition mass assigned to that school, the mass of higher-ranked same-class interrupters outside the coalition, bounded by the school’s per-capita capacity slack.

The model it lives in

An atomless school-choice population has a rationally specified density over preference rankings, finite between-group priority classes, and school-specific within-group rank coordinates. The decision variable is a measurable assignment of population mass to schools, with each individual assigned to one school and type-level averages allowed to be fractional. Capacities scale with population; blocking follows the paper’s between-group rule and an explicitly normalized mass version of its within-group interrupter rule.

The objection that survived

The paper sums each coalition member’s number of within-group interrupters and compares that sum with capacity slack; no established scaling shows that a mass analogue preserves those blocks.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a mass version of unified-core selection for large school districts. The relevant regime is a district assigning a very large recurring cohort, where students fall into relatively few profiles: each profile records school preferences, between-group eligibility or priority at each school, and its within-group priority tier. Let \(\mu_t\) be the population share of profile \(t\), and let \(\kappa_s\) be school \(s\)’s seats per unit of population. A mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\) says what share of profile \(t\) attends school \(s\), with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\leq\kappa_s\). Each student still attends one school; the fractional quantities describe how a cohort’s mass is divided.

My sole anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: “\(\mu_2\) is in the unified core.” Its constructive content is that, for every finite school-choice instance in the paper’s model, deferred acceptance first produces a match with no between-group block; a restricted top-trading-cycles stage then produces a match with neither kind of block. The paper says this DA-TTC algorithm runs in polynomial time, although the theorem’s printed statement itself asserts core membership rather than a complexity class. The matching problem being mirrored is to find a mass assignment in the continuous unified core. There is no welfare objective to optimize: as in the paper, the goal is to return any assignment satisfying the two blocking requirements.

Precisely, take a finite set of schools and a finite set of profiles \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\), capacities \(\kappa_s\), strict school preferences, and school-specific between-group and within-group priority tiers recorded in each profile. A candidate solution is a feasible mass assignment \(x\). It counts as a solution if there is no positive-mass coalition that can all weakly improve, with positive mass strictly improving, by either (i) moving into vacancies or displacing lower between-group-priority mass, or (ii) making a within-group-enforceable move: keeping each school’s mass by between-group class unchanged and satisfying the mass analogue of the paper’s within-group-interrupter and capacity-slack condition. Coalitions can select any submass of a profile, since otherwise identical students can be partitioned across assignments. The resulting question is concrete enough to give a prover: does every such instance have a continuous unified-core assignment, and can one be found in time polynomial in the number of schools, profiles, and input bit length?

This is a plausible school-choice question rather than continuity of the outcome alone. A district can sensibly ask what fraction of its cohort receives each school, and capacity naturally scales with cohort size. The authors’ distinction between inviolable priority groups and tradable within-group priority remains the organizing principle of the mirror. The paper also cites Leshno and Lo’s continuum model of top trading cycles. That is supporting precedent for a continuum population in this domain, not a collision with this particular result: Theorem 1 combines between-group and within-group blocking in the unified core.

I expect the finite-profile version to be tractable, perhaps through mass versions of the DA and TTC stages. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1: the paper’s finite cycle argument does not establish that a mass algorithm terminates in polynomially many steps or that its output avoids every mass block. Further questions include whether the continuous core is always nonempty, whether it admits an efficient algorithm, and whether the answer changes when lottery priorities are represented by a genuinely continuous rank rather than finitely many tiers.

The weakest point is the priority representation. The paper gives every individual a strict within-group priority, whereas repeated members of one complete profile are indistinguishable. Finite priority tiers make the high-multiplicity regime clean, but unique lottery ranks require a continuous rank coordinate and may undermine the finite-profile algorithmic argument. The authors might regard that as a substantive change; the case for the mirror rests on the large-cohort, tiered-priority regime being a sensible instance of their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against Theorem 1 is that the proposed finite-profile mirror does not preserve the paper’s strict within-group priorities. Those priorities order individual students, and DA–TTC uses that order in both stages. A complete type must therefore include each student’s position in the relevant priority orders. With strict priorities, repeated students who share preferences and between-group status still have different types once their within-group ranks are included. Omitting those ranks merges students the algorithm distinguishes; retaining them makes the support grow with the cohort rather than stay small. “Finite priority tiers” avoids that problem only by changing the model to allow ties and then supplying a separate tie-breaking rule.

The stronger version gives lottery priority a continuous rank coordinate, so the population is a distribution over preferences and rank vectors. That is a credible school-choice regime, but it changes the computational question: a finite list of rational masses does not specify an atomless distribution over ranks. The authors would need to choose an input representation—perhaps a restricted class of densities or an oracle—and show that the unified-core blocking conditions have a well-defined limit. There is also a scaling issue in Definition 2: it compares the sum of interrupter counts across coalition members with capacity slack. Under population growth, that sum can scale quadratically while capacity scales linearly. A mass version would need a deliberate normalization, which could change what counts as a block.

Leshno and Lo’s continuum TTC paper confirms that continuum school-choice models with distributed preference and priority ranks are sensible, and derives TTC cutoffs using differential equations. It does not analyze the unified core or the DA–TTC combination, so it is supporting precedent, not a collision. [Leshno and Lo, *The Simple Structure of Top Trading Cycles in School Choice: A Continuum Model*](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/leshnolo_-_simple_ttc.pdf)

That leaves a real objection to the proponent’s *finite-profile formulation*: it has not yet shown that the paper’s strict-priority model compresses to a small set of repeated types or that its blocking rule has a canonical mass limit. But I cannot honestly extend that objection to every plausible scenario. A continuous-rank version for a large district cohort remains a worthwhile, distinct question about whether the unified core exists and can be computed. Theorem 1 is a constructive anchor: the paper gives a DA–TTC algorithm, states that it runs in polynomial time, and proves its output belongs to the unified core. The negative case is therefore weak on its universal claim; this anchor survives.
