The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous-computational mirror: it gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem with an input encoding.

The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which says that on a rich preference domain, individual strategyproofness is equivalent to coalitional strategyproofness; and Theorem 3, also proved here, which says that with three public goods, two individuals, strictly convex preferences, and an onto mechanism, strategyproofness implies dictatorship. Both are incentive and characterization results, not claims about tractability or hardness. Theorem 2 is a cited result, also about mechanism characterization.

One could propose a separate population extension: let types be preferences \(R\in D\), let \(\mu\) distribute society across those types, and ask whether a positive-mass coalition can change reports so that every member prefers the new outcome. But that is not an anchored computational question from this paper. If outcomes depend only on \(\mu\), a single nonatomic agent has no effect, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous; replacing it with positive-mass coalition manipulation changes the paper’s question. The paper’s two-person public-goods theorem also supplies no high-multiplicity result. I therefore see no defensible lead mirror or expected complexity class under the programme’s standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational problem to continuize. Theorem 1 is a theorem about arbitrary social choice mechanisms \(C:D^n\to X\): on a rich domain, individual strategyproofness and coalitional strategyproofness coincide. It gives no finite input, representation of \(C\), algorithm, or complexity question. Theorem 2 is cited background, and Theorem 3 is an axiomatic characterization of mechanisms in a public-goods setting. These are substantial results, but they do not define the algorithmic object ChoCo is meant to study.

Theorem 1 is the closest candidate, and a large society with many voters sharing preference types is entirely plausible. But the direct continuum version has a specific problem: if the outcome depends on the preference distribution \(\mu\), a single nonatomic voter cannot change it, so individual strategyproofness is vacuous. Replacing it with resistance to positive-mass coalitions preserves the paper’s interest in group manipulation, but no longer mirrors the theorem’s comparison between individual and coalitional incentives. Keeping voters individually consequential by assigning each mass \(1/n\) instead leaves a finite-population problem. A minimum-mass manipulation problem could be worthwhile for a chosen, explicitly represented rule; the paper supplies neither that rule nor the encoding that would make its complexity a determinate question.

Theorem 3 does not close the gap. Its two-person public-goods model makes a large population scenario easy to imagine, but the theorem characterizes mechanisms rather than computing anything about them. A mass-manipulation problem for a specified allocation rule would require choosing how preferences are represented, how the rule acts on distributions, and what manipulation is measured. Those choices would define the computational problem; they do not follow from the theorem.

So the case is not that high multiplicity is implausible, or that a continuous extension could not be interesting. It is that the plausible extensions are new computational problems adjacent to this paper’s subject, rather than continuous mirrors of its results. The universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile project is hard to establish; the more defensible conclusion is that this paper offers no anchored computational mirror for the programme.
