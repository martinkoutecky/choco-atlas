The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), groups \(G_1,G_2\), committee size \(k\), quota \(\ell\), and a rational distribution \(\widehat{\mu}\) over observed rankings with support size \(\tau\), find an integral committee \(S\) using only \(\widehat{\mu}\), with \(|S|=k\) and \(|S\cap G_2|\geq\ell\). The guarantee is relative to a hidden joint law \(P\) over latent and observed rankings whose observed marginal is \(\widehat{\mu}\), whose latent marginal is \(\mu\), and which satisfies the paper’s \((\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\)-smoothness promise and \(\ell=|M\cap G_2|\): require \(F_\mu(S)\geq(\beta-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\), where \(F_\mu(S)=\mathbb{E}_{\succ\sim\mu}[f(S,\succ)]\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\max_{|S'|=k}F_\mu(S')\).

The model it lives in

The underlying continuous society is a rational distribution over paired types \((\succ,\widehat{\succ})\). The selector sees only the observed marginal \(\widehat{\mu}\); the latent marginal remains hidden and evaluates the integral committee. Exact expected observed scores drive the constrained greedy choice, with support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\) governing input representation.

What the mirror covers

This mirrors Theorem 4.2’s constrained selection guarantee and its scoring-rule applications; it leaves the finite-sample lower bound in Theorem 6.3 and the empirical simulations outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the latent-derived quota \(\ell\) be supplied or inferred under useful application assumptions?
  • What running-time bounds follow for explicit rational support as a function of \(m\), \(k\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for an exact-population version of the paper’s representational-constraint selection problem. The paper has no named NP-hardness or other conventional complexity-class result; its strongest computational anchor is its algorithmic theorem.

Lead anchor — Theorem 4.2, proved here. For a multiwinner score function \(F\) and latent and biased preference models \(\mu,\widehat{\mu}\), suppose \(F\) is \((\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\)-smooth with respect to those models. For any \(0<\varepsilon,\delta<1\), if
\(n \geq \frac{16k}{(\alpha\min\{\varepsilon,1-\gamma\})^2}\log\frac{m}{\delta}\),
then an algorithm given the candidate groups, \(k\), the quota \(\ell=|M\cap G_2|\), and a value oracle for the empirical biased score outputs a size-\(k\) committee \(S\in\mathcal K(\ell)\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\) such that \(F(S)\geq(\beta-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\). Here \(M\) is the set of \(k\) candidates with highest expected latent singleton scores. The algorithm makes \(O(mk)\) oracle calls and \(O(m\log m)\) arithmetic operations. Its underlying procedure greedily selects \(\ell\) candidates from \(G_2\), then \(k-\ell\) from \(G_1\), using observed biased marginal scores.

A plausible regime is a large ranking panel for a recommendation slate or shortlist: many evaluators share recurring preference and reporting patterns, and the selected items include a group subject to systematic ranking bias. For fixed \(m\), voter types are pairs \((\succ,\widehat{\succ})\) of latent and observed rankings. The mass of a type is its fraction of the evaluator population. A finite-support distribution over such pairs captures the whole population; the regime of interest has \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of evaluators and \(\tau\) is the number of pair types with positive mass. This is most convincing for stable preference archetypes, not for settings where nearly every evaluator has a unique relevant type.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Exact-Population Representational Selection. An instance has candidates \(C\), groups \(G_1,G_2\), committee size \(k\), a quota \(\ell\), a multiwinner score contribution \(f\), and an explicitly represented rational distribution over observed rankings. For the guarantee, there is an underlying distribution \(P\) over ranking pairs \((\succ,\widehat{\succ})\); the solver sees only its observed marginal \(\widehat{\mu}\), while its latent marginal \(\mu\) defines the evaluation objective. The promise is that the pair \((\mu,\widehat{\mu})\) satisfies the paper’s smoothness conditions with parameters \((\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\), and that \(\ell=|M\cap G_2|\). Define \(F_\mu(S)=\mathbb E_{\succ\sim\mu}[f(S,\succ)]\), \(\widehat F(S)=\mathbb E_{\widehat{\succ}\sim\widehat{\mu}}[f(S,\widehat{\succ})]\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\max_{|S|=k}F_\mu(S)\). The task is to return a size-\(k\) committee with \(|S\cap G_2|\geq\ell\) and \(F_\mu(S)\geq(\beta-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\), using only the observed distribution to choose it.

I expect this promised problem to be tractable: run the same constrained greedy algorithm with exact population scores instead of empirical scores. For explicit support of size \(\tau\), each score-oracle call is a sum over at most \(\tau\) types, so the paper’s \(O(mk)\) oracle bound becomes \(O(mk\tau)\) score evaluations, with bit complexity polynomial in the rational-mass encoding length \(L\) for standard scoring rules. In the exact-population limit, I expect the guarantee to reach \(\beta\,\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\), or equivalently \((\beta-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\); the finite-sample theorem’s concentration error is what necessitates the \(n\) threshold.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ problem because it retains their central decision: choose an integral committee from biased rankings, enforce representation, and judge quality against latent rankings. Only the population representation changes—from \(n\) sampled ballots to exact masses over ranking types. The output remains a size-\(k\) committee; the continuum is in the society, not the outcome.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 4.2 and its consequences for score functions such as SNTV and Borda. I would not use Theorem 6.3 as a second anchor. Its impossibility statement is about how many finite samples are needed when disadvantaged candidates are rarely observed; exact population masses remove that sampling bottleneck, so it has no direct counterpart in this mirror. Further questions include whether the quota \(\ell\) can be found without latent information, and what guarantees remain without the smoothness promise.

The weakest point is that \(\mu\) and \(\widehat{\mu}\) already appear in the paper as generative distributions, and its main contribution is precisely a finite-sample guarantee about estimating from them. An exact-population formulation may therefore look like a limit of the paper’s existing analysis, rather than a new computational question; it also strips away much of the sample-complexity content. The case survives as a sensible high-multiplicity mirror because the algorithmic task still has a distinct input regime—an exact distribution over types, with latent quality hidden from the selector—and the paper’s constrained selection problem remains intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.2 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives an algorithm, an oracle-call bound, and an approximation guarantee. The negative case has to be about whether its continuous mirror is worthwhile, not whether there is a result to mirror.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is an exact distribution over observed rankings, with rational masses and support size \(\tau\). A hidden latent model supplies the smoothness promise, and the correct quota \(\ell=|M\cap G_2|\) is given. The selector runs the paper’s constrained greedy algorithm using exact expected biased scores.

The difficulty is that the explicit population does not include the information needed to define its quality measure. A complete type for this problem includes the latent ranking as well as the observed ranking. Giving the solver masses over those paired types reveals the latent preferences and changes the hidden-truth problem. Giving only observed-ranking masses leaves \(F_\mu\), \(M\), and the correct quota unspecified. The proposed mirror fills that gap with a hidden latent-model promise and a latent-derived quota as input. That makes it a conditional version of the paper’s own setup, rather than an explicit-mass instance of the continuous-society model.

Even granting that promise formulation, the computation is almost entirely inherited from Theorem 4.2. Its \(O(mk)\) oracle calls already do not grow with the number of voters; evaluating each call from explicit support costs \(O(\tau)\). The same greedy algorithm and guarantee carry over, while the concentration argument and the sample-size question disappear. The paper already frames its results through generative distributions and expected scores, so this is a thin exact-population specialization, with no new optimization structure to investigate.

A large evaluator panel with recurring ranking patterns is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not reject it on that ground. My negative case is weaker: the mirror is formally sensible, but its computational content appears too derivative to justify a ChoCo investigation. I cannot honestly claim the anchor is defeated universally. A reader who counts this exact-population specialization as worthwhile should let it survive.
