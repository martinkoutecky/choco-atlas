What the mirror covers

The proposed population extension concerns Theorem 2’s axiomatic characterization; the other characterizations and the maximal-domain result are also axiomatic, and the construction procedure gives no named computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires as an anchor. The closest is Theorem 2 (proved here): a rule on single-peaked economies satisfies own-peak-onliness, efficiency, the equal division guarantee, and NOM if and only if it is a simple rule. That is a characterization of admissible rules, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The other numbered results are likewise axiomatic claims; the appendix’s construction procedure gives no complexity classification. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and no honest way to state one continuous computational problem per anchor.

There is a plausible *axiomatic* population extension. Imagine a large irrigation district with many farms, where farms with the same complete single-peaked preference over water allotments form a type. Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population share, let \(\omega\) be water supply per farm, and let \(x_t\) be the allotment to type \(t\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=\omega\). One could ask whether the continuous-population analog of Theorem 2 characterizes rules satisfying efficiency, the per-capita equal division guarantee, own-peak-onliness, and NOM. Its candidate “simple” allocations would fully satiate below-average-peak types under excess demand and assign the remaining types allotments between \(\omega\) and their peaks, with the symmetric rule under excess supply.

That scenario makes high multiplicity credible: many farms, relatively few preference types, and supply naturally expressed per farm. But the proposed question is a population-continuous axiomatic characterization, not a computational question. Also, in a nonatomic model an individual has zero mass, so the authors’ manipulation notion would need careful definition to ensure NOM remains substantive. I would not classify this candidate as A, B, or C without first resolving that issue.

The strongest point for a mirror is that the population regime is natural for the allotment problem. The decisive weakness is that the paper supplies no computational anchor: even a convincing continuum extension of Theorem 2 would not establish the computational-complexity gap this programme targets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The farm scenario is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Many farms can share the same complete preference type, supply can be expressed per farm, and a type-level allocation \(x_t\) can satisfy \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=\omega\). I would not rest the negative case on the claim that a nonatomic agent cannot manipulate: a mechanism could still make an agent’s own allotment depend on their report while their report has zero effect on aggregate mass.

That gives a sensible continuous axiomatic version of Theorem 2. But it does not give the paper a computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes rules by properties; Corollary 1 and Propositions 3 and 4 give related characterizations, and Theorem 3 characterizes a maximal preference domain. None defines a finite computational input and output problem or establishes its complexity. The appendix’s construction procedure does not change that: it describes how to choose feasible adjustments, while leaving the choice among them open.

The strongest computational reframing would be: given type masses, peaks, and supply, compute a simple allocation. But “compute one” only operationalizes the definition; to ask which allocation to choose requires an objective or a particular rule. The paper deliberately permits a broad family of claims rules, with no common finite encoding or selection criterion. Choosing CEA, CEL, or proportional allocation could yield a specific computational task, but that would add a rule-selection question the paper does not pose. It would not be a computational mirror of one of its results.

So there are no qualifying anchors for the other analyst’s case to defend. A continuous population may make this allocation model natural, and an axiomatic extension may be worth studying. That does not supply the missing computational problem. This is a strong negative verdict for ChoCo’s present purpose, though not proof that no future computational problem could be built from allotment rules.
