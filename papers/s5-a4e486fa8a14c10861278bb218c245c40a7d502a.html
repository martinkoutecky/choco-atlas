The continuous mirror question

Given destinations \(V\), colors \(C\), a finite support \(T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), where each type \(t\) has a color \(c_t\) and eligible set \(S_t\subseteq V\), decide whether there exist nonnegative flows \(x_{t,v}\) for \(v\in S_t\) such that \(\sum_{v\in S_t}x_{t,v}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and, writing \(q_{v,c}=\sum_{t:c_t=c}x_{t,v}\), \(\max_{c\in C}q_{v,c}-\min_{c\in C}q_{v,c}\le\epsilon\) for every \(v\in V\), given rational \(\epsilon\ge0\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with types \(t=(c_t,S_t)\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\); assignment variables \(x_{t,v}\) send each type’s mass to eligible destinations. The objective is feasibility, with per-destination Max-Min gap at most \(\epsilon\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Max-Min branch of Theorem 17, including Proposition 18’s three-color hardness result. It leaves the MoV results, the degree dichotomies, and the paper’s parameterized algorithms unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For an LP-feasible flow induced by \(n\) discrete agents, what is the smallest additive increase \(\delta\) in the Max-Min threshold that always permits rounding to an integral assignment, as a function of the support size and number of destinations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is continuous Max-Min Fair Matching, anchored on Theorem 17, proved in this paper. As printed, it says that Max-Min and MoV Fair Matching are polynomial-time solvable for \(|C|=2\) and NP-hard for \(|C|\ge 3\); the polynomial result holds even with arbitrary size lower bounds. For the Max-Min hardness arm I rely on the paper’s own reduction: Proposition 18 proves NP-hardness with three colors, even when \(\Delta_U\le 3\) and \(\Delta_V\le 3\). Theorem 17 also covers MoV: its hardness result there draws on Stoica et al. [31]. My mirror below covers the Max-Min problem, not the MoV arm.

A plausible regime is assigning a large cohort of students to schools, or residents to service destinations. Each person has a demographic or qualification color and the same set of destinations they are eligible for as everyone in their type. With many people but comparatively few combinations of color and eligibility, the population can be represented by a modest support of types. That is a natural high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s many-to-one assignment model.

Define Continuous Max-Min Fair Matching as follows. An instance has a set \(V\) of destinations, a color set \(C\), a finite support \(T\), and a rational threshold \(\epsilon\ge 0\). Each type \(t\in T\) has a color \(c_t\), an eligible set \(S_t\subseteq V\), and rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Types are complete for this problem: agents of type \(t\) share both their color and their eligibility set. Ask whether the population can be assigned so every unit of mass goes to one eligible destination and every destination is Max-Min fair within \(\epsilon\).

A solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_{t,v}\), defined for \(v\in S_t\), such that
\[ \sum_{v\in S_t}x_{t,v}=\mu_t\qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
Let \(q_{v,c}=\sum_{t:c_t=c}x_{t,v}\) be the mass of color \(c\) assigned to \(v\). The fairness condition is
\[ \max_{c\in C}q_{v,c}-\min_{c\in C}q_{v,c}\le\epsilon \qquad\text{for every }v\in V. \]
A feasible flow is the solution. Each individual in this model still goes to one destination; because the population is nonatomic, a type’s mass can be partitioned among eligible destinations.

This is a linear feasibility problem: the fairness condition is equivalent to \(q_{v,c}-q_{v,c'}\le\epsilon\) for every ordered color pair and destination. The LP has \(O(\tau |V|)\) assignment variables and polynomially many constraints, so the mirror is Class A, tractable, in \(|V|,\tau,|C|\), and the input bit length. It preserves the paper’s eligibility graph, its colors, its requirement to assign all left-side mass, and its per-destination Max-Min criterion. The continuous change is at the population level: agents of one type contribute divisible mass rather than indivisible vertices.

The normalization has a direct discrete interpretation. If a discrete instance has \(n\) agents and permits a Max-Min gap of \(\ell\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(\epsilon=\ell/n\). Any feasible discrete matching gives a feasible continuous flow. The reverse need not hold: a fractional flow may not round to an integral assignment while preserving the fairness bounds. That gap is part of the question the mirror raises, not a reason to change the model. The paper’s NP-hardness can dissolve in the nonatomic version while remaining intact for indivisible agents.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem continued in the population dimension. The paper explicitly motivates fair assignment of students to schools and voters to constituencies, and a type records precisely the features its matching model uses for assignment and fairness. This is not outcome-space fractionalization or a lottery over matchings: it is a distribution of people across destinations.

The soft spot is that the hardness reduction’s indivisible assignment choices are exactly what the LP relaxes. In a scenario where nearly every person has a distinct eligibility profile, the support may grow with the population, and the high-multiplicity story becomes less convincing. The case is strongest when many agents genuinely share a color and eligibility set. The continuous problem remains well-defined even when \(\tau\) is large, but its value as a mirror depends on whether that regime is useful for the application.

This mirror naturally raises questions about when a feasible flow can be rounded to a discrete fair matching, and what additive fairness loss rounding requires as a function of the number of types or destinations. It also suggests the separate question of whether the MoV criterion has a tractable continuous analogue: its top-two-color condition is not the same pairwise linear constraint as Max-Min, so Theorem 17’s MoV hardness arm should be mirrored and assessed separately.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper’s difficulty comes from the incidence graph and indivisible assignments, while the proposed mirror replaces those choices with divisible flow. A type must include both a person’s color and their entire eligible-destination set. In applications with personalized eligibility, that can leave almost as many types as people, so the low-support population story may be a poor fit. The paper’s result does not guarantee that real instances have few such types.

That objection narrows the useful regime, but it does not defeat the mirror. For example, students can be grouped by demographic category and school catchment: everyone in a type shares a color and the same eligible schools. The authors could recognize that as their assignment problem continued over population mass. The Max-Min condition remains meaningful on that mass, and with \(\epsilon=\ell/n\), the discrete threshold has a direct proportional interpretation. The resulting constraints are linear.

The named anchor survives: Theorem 17 gives a computational result to mirror, and Proposition 18 supplies a three-color hardness result. The fact that the flow version may be easier is not a valid objection under the programme’s criteria; it is the kind of Class A outcome the programme seeks. Nor does the model track named individuals, and the paper’s discussion of discrete high-multiplicity work supports the regime rather than colliding with it. I do not see an honest way to defeat this anchor. The negative case is limited to arguing that the mirror applies to a narrower set of assignment regimes than the paper’s broad application list might suggest.
