The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case for a population model, but I can’t supply a compliant result anchor. No numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the paper classifies an underlying computational problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise.

The closest result is Claim 7, proved in Appendix A.5: if the costs satisfy \(c_i\ge 2c_{i-1}\), every Nash-stable coalition is downward-closed, and all Nash-stable coalitions can be found in \(O(n)\) time by checking the \(n-1\) prefixes. That is a computational claim, but it is labelled a Claim, outside the anchor forms you specified. Its regime is also a poor high-multiplicity fit: repeated agents with the same cost violate the separation condition, and a fixed bounded cost interval cannot contain arbitrarily many successively doubling costs. The paper’s observation that general search may require \(2^n\) enumeration is motivation, not a hardness result.

The most credible population scenario is a large data-sharing consortium where many holders have the same data distribution and privacy cost. A type is the cost \(c_j\); \(\mu_j\) is that type’s population share; and \(N\) is the population scale. This gives small support \(\tau\) relative to \(N\). A concrete follow-on question for the paper’s centralized baseline is: given rational \((N,\sigma^2,\alpha)\) and rational type masses \(\mu_j\), choose participating shares \(y_j\in[0,\mu_j]\) and a privacy level \(\epsilon_j>0\) for each participating type to minimize per-capita social cost. With \(q=\sum_j y_j>0\) and \(K=Nq\), the estimator variance and objective are

\[ V(y,\epsilon)=\frac{\sigma^2}{K}+\frac{2}{K^2}\sum_j\frac{Ny_j}{\epsilon_j^2}, \qquad F(y,\epsilon)=\sum_j y_j\bigl(V(y,\epsilon)+c_jK^\alpha\epsilon_j\bigr)+(1-q)\sigma^2, \]

with \(F=\sigma^2\) when \(q=0\). The requested solution is a global minimizer, including the no-sharing option. This is a sensible mass version of the centralized optimization studied in the paper; sorting types by cost and optimizing coalition mass suggests tractability for fixed \(\alpha\). That expectation is a conjecture about this new problem, not a result established by the paper. It most directly mirrors the centralized results, including Theorem 1, and does not preserve the paper’s decentralized stability analysis.

The weakest point is also the decisive one for this task: the paper’s named results characterize welfare, variance, and price of stability as \(n\) grows; they do not give the required computational-complexity anchor. The proposed mass optimization may be a reasonable extension, but its likely tractability and its exact relationship to the authors’ results would need to be established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a scope mismatch: the proponent has not identified a computational-complexity result in the required sense. Theorem 1 gives asymptotic welfare and variance for the centralized model; it does not classify a computational problem. Claim 7 is the closest algorithmic result, and its assumptions exclude a scalable high-multiplicity regime under the paper’s fixed cost bounds. If \(c_i\ge 2c_{i-1}\), then \(c_n\ge 2^{n-1}c_{\min}\), while \(c_n\le c_{\max}\). Thus \(n\le 1+\log_2(c_{\max}/c_{\min})\). Repeated cost types are impossible there, and the number of players cannot grow without bound. A continuum of cost types would also abandon the geometric-separation premise that makes Claim 7 work.

That does not defeat the Theorem 1 mirror, though. For the paper’s social-cost and variance questions, agents with the same privacy cost are indistinguishable: their data are drawn from the same distribution, and their modeled burdens depend on \(c_i\), not on the realized data value. A population distribution over costs is therefore a sensible type model. With a fixed participating mass, the centralized solution still favors the lowest-cost types; their privacy levels can be optimized by type, leaving an optimization over the participating mass and the no-sharing option. The paper’s decentralized stability analysis need not carry over, because this mirror targets its centralized result.

So the negative case can make a narrower point: the paper has no named complexity theorem to anchor the programme’s intended complexity landscape, and its one explicit algorithmic claim does not extend to large populations under the stated assumptions. But the proposed population version of Theorem 1 is well-posed and plausibly recognizable to the authors. Calling it unworthy because the resulting optimization may be simple would rely on how easy the answer is, which is not a valid objection here. I can’t honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
