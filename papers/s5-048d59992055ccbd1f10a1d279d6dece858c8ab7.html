The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result in the sense required for an anchor. Its sole numbered result, Proposition 1, says that every agenda network has a unique, nonempty largest farsightedly consistent set, and that this set is externally stable under farsighted dominance. That is an existence result, not a complexity or algorithmic result; the paper says its proof is identical to a result in Page, Wooders, and Kamat (2005).

The paper does report computed stable sets for three examples, but those results are unnumbered, and the algorithm is attributed to Kamat and Page’s 2001 typescript. I therefore can’t honestly give the requested anchor-based continuous problem or predict its complexity without inventing an anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor, so there is none to defeat. That is the strongest case against a continuous mirror here: Proposition 1 establishes existence and external stability for an already given agenda network. It gives no algorithm, running time, or complexity result, and its proof is imported from earlier work. The three “Computational Result” passages report worked examples using an existing algorithm; they do not establish a computational theorem of this paper.

The strongest possible rescue is a large assembly represented by a distribution over complete preference and move types. For a quota \(q\), one could ask whether enough mass both has the right to move and unanimously prefers the destination, then compute the largest farsightedly consistent set of agendas. That is a coherent question, and such an assembly could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

But it is a follow-on question, not a continuous mirror of a result this paper proves. Once the coalition conditions have been turned into arcs, the distribution has done its work: the remaining problem is the graph computation already illustrated in the paper. If the aim is a new complexity study of compiling agenda networks from mass distributions, that may be worthwhile on its own, but it does not supply the missing result anchor.

So the negative case is strong under the paper’s result-anchored test. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise from the model: the large-assembly formulation is plausible, though its value would have to come from a new computational contribution rather than a mirror of this paper’s findings.
