The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(U,V\), rational masses \(\mu_u,\nu_v\) summing to \(1\) on each side, rankings over acceptable counterpart types, and deviator types \(D\subseteq U\), does there exist a perfect mass matching \(x\geq 0\) with row sums \(\mu\), column sums \(\nu\), and \(x_{uv}=0\) on unacceptable pairs, such that for every acceptable pair \((u,v)\) with \(u\in D\), \(\left(\sum_{v':\,v\succ_u v'}x_{uv'}=0\right)\lor\left(\sum_{u':\,u\succ_v u'}x_{u'v}=0\right)\)?

The model it lives in

Applicants and positions are cohorts of interchangeable agents, with each type recording its side, acceptable counterpart types, ranking, and deviator status. The decision variable \(x_{uv}\) is the mass of type \(u\) matched to type \(v\); row and column sums exhaust the two populations, and the disjunction rules out blocking mass. For any fixed choice of which side has zero mass for each type pair, the remaining constraints form a transportation problem. Scaling rational masses gives integral capacities, supporting hardness transfer; replicating each gadget-role type gives arbitrarily large populations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1's NP-completeness result for perfect bipartite matching with no blocking pair involving a deviator. It leaves aside the general Stable Roommates results, optimization objectives, parameterized algorithms, and short-list algorithms.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of this problem as a function of support size \(\tau\), and does hardness persist under role replication?
  • Does the mass problem become polynomial-time solvable when preference lists have length at most two, as in the paper's discrete results?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 3.1: existence of a perfect matching with no blocking pair involving a deviator. Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, says that 0-Deviator-Perfect-smi-Dec is NP-complete even when every preference list has length at most three and all deviators are on the same side of the bipartition. In full, its input is a Stable Marriage with Incomplete Lists instance and a designated set \(D\) of agents; it asks whether there is a perfect matching in which no pair containing an agent in \(D\) blocks. The paper proves hardness by reduction from the restricted SAT problem \((2,2)\)-e3-sat.

A continuous counterpart is 0-Deviator-Perfect-SMI\(_\infty\). Its input consists of two finite sets of types \(U,V\), rational masses \(\mu_u,\nu_v\geq 0\) summing to one on each side, strict rankings by each type over acceptable types on the other side, and a set \(D\subseteq U\) of deviator types. A type includes the side, its full preference ranking and acceptability, and whether its members are deviators or conformists. The question is whether there is a perfect mass matching. A solution is a matrix \(x_{uv}\geq 0\), where \(x_{uv}\) is the mass of type \(u\) matched to type \(v\), with \(\sum_v x_{uv}=\mu_u\) and \(\sum_u x_{uv}=\nu_v\); unacceptable pairs must have \(x_{uv}=0\). Each individual receives one partner. The matrix allows a type’s members to be distributed among partner types.

For each acceptable pair \((u,v)\) with \(u\in D\), require
\[ \left(\sum_{v':\,v\succ_u v'}x_{uv'}=0\right) \;\lor\; \left(\sum_{u':\,u\succ_v u'}x_{u'v}=0\right). \]
The first sum is the mass of type \(u\) assigned below \(v\) in its ranking; the second is the mass of type \(v\) assigned below \(u\). If both are positive, positive mass on both sides would prefer each other to their assignments, so the pair of types offers a blocking opportunity to deviators. This makes the stability requirement population-level: it rules out blocking mass, rather than trying to detect a measure-zero event involving just one person.

A plausible regime is a large residency or fellowship match with many applicants and unit positions. Applicants share complete rankings over program types, eligibility, and propensity to initiate deviations; positions share rankings over applicant types. For example, a market might have hundreds of thousands of participants but only hundreds or thousands of recurring preference profiles. Positions remain individual units in the matching, while their members’ assignments are represented in aggregate. This preserves the paper’s central question—whether a perfect matching can avoid deviations involving the designated agents—while making population proportions the natural input.

I expect this mirror to fall in Class B, with the paper’s hardness transferring. Give each agent in a Theorem 3.1 instance its own type, assign each type mass \(1/N\) on its side, and copy its preference list and deviator status. The type rankings then reproduce the original instance. There is also a reason fractional assignment need not dissolve this particular hardness: for any feasible mass matching, each blocking-pair condition has at least one zero side. Choose that side for every pair. Those zero-sum constraints simply forbid certain matching edges. Scale the resulting transportation problem by \(N\); each type has unit supply or demand, and the bipartite flow polytope with those edges removed is integral. Thus a feasible fractional matching has an integral perfect-matching witness for the same zero conditions. The discrete yes/no answer is therefore preserved. The reduction’s types can also be read as cohorts in a large market sharing those rankings. The expected computational question is consequently hardness as a function of the number of supported types \(\tau\), alongside the bit length \(L\), even when the underlying population is large.

This case covers Theorem 3.1’s perfect bipartite, zero-deviator-blocking existence result. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s general Stable Roommates results, its minimization objectives, or its parameterized algorithms; each would need its own mass formulation and analysis. Further questions include whether this problem becomes tractable with preference lists of length at most two, and what its complexity is when parameterized by the number of deviator types or by \(\tau\).

The soft spot is that the theorem’s reduction uses a number of distinct preference profiles that grows with the SAT instance. It establishes hardness in the type structure, not hardness with a fixed small number of types. Also, the mirror treats agents as ranking counterpart *types* and asks whether positive mass can block. An author who considers even one named individual’s blocking opportunity essential might see that as a changed stability notion. The case survives if they accept cohorts of interchangeable agents and mass as the right unit of deviation—the premise that makes this a continuous-population problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that its stability constraints are disjunctive. For each deviator type \(u\) and counterpart type \(v\), the model requires either zero mass of \(u\) below \(v\), or zero mass of \(v\) below \(u\). Choosing which side is zero for every such pair gives a family of ordinary transportation problems. Once rational masses are scaled to integer capacities, each feasible transportation polytope has an integral solution. Thus the fractional matching itself may add no freedom: the continuous problem can amount to a compact encoding of a finite typed matching problem, with the combinatorics still in the choices of which assignments to forbid.

There is also a modeling choice to make explicit. In the paper, preferences are strict rankings of named agents. Repeated agents with identical rankings over counterpart *types* are indifferent among members of a type. That is a natural typed-market model, but it changes the preference domain and needs a stated blocking convention.

Those points expose limits of the mirror, but they do not defeat it. Stable-matching research already treats agents grouped by shared preference types as a sensible market regime, including in applications such as residency matching; that work supports the population model, though it does not settle this deviator-stability question ([Meeks and Rastegari](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109)). And the reduction can be strengthened beyond the proponent’s one-agent-per-type embedding: give every gadget-role type \(q\) interchangeable copies. After selecting a zero side for each type-pair condition, an integral \(q\)-capacity flow can be decomposed into perfect matchings of the role types. A layer therefore recovers a witness for the original instance while every role type has multiplicity \(q\).

So the negative case identifies a possible lack of fractional algorithmic gain and a preference-modeling issue. The programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity matching as in scope, and neither point makes the question unrecognizable to the authors. I cannot honestly defeat the anchor: the continuous mirror survives, and the case against it is weak.
