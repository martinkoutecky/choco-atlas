The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that when utilities are increasing, continuous, and quasi-concave, and each agent’s possible endowments lie in \((0,\bar\omega_i)\), the Constrained Walrasian social choice rule is implementable in Nash equilibrium using generalized severe punishment. That is a natural candidate for continuization: large exchange economies can have many households but relatively few combinations of preferences and endowments.

A precise exploratory mirror would take a finite type set \(T\), where each type \(t\) specifies a utility function \(u_t\) and endowment \(\omega_t\), and a population distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). An allocation assigns a net trade \(x(i)\) to each agent, with \(x(i)+\omega_t\geq 0\) for an agent of type \(t\), and aggregate trades summing to zero. Infeasible assignments revert to a generalized punishment outcome. The question is whether one state-independent mechanism can Nash-implement the continuum Constrained Walrasian correspondence for every \(\mu\) in the stated domain: that is, whether there is a game form whose equilibrium assignments after reversion are exactly the constrained Walrasian assignments at each population distribution. A solution would be such a game form, or an impossibility proof.

This is plausible as a large-economy version of Proposition 3, but it is not an anchor that meets your computational-results requirement. Proposition 3 establishes implementation, not a complexity bound or algorithm. I cannot honestly assign this existence question to Class A, B, or C without turning it into a different, algorithmically specified problem. The paper has no named result asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized complexity, or another computational classification.

The weakest point is also central: with an atomless population, an individual’s deviation may have no effect on aggregate feasibility or the mechanism’s outcome. That can change the strategic content of Nash implementation, even if the economic setting is a convincing high-multiplicity regime. So the paper supports a plausible continuous-population implementation-theory question, but it does not furnish a qualifying computational anchor for the ChoCo programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is a credible continuous-population *implementation* question. In an exchange economy, grouping agents by preference and endowment is natural, and replacing individual assignments with mass assignments does not by itself invalidate the model. The atomless-deviation concern is a complication to formulate, not a convincing reason to reject the mirror.

But the proposition establishes an existence result: under its assumptions, the Constrained Walrasian rule is implementable in Nash equilibrium by generalized severe punishment. It gives no computational problem to mirror. The mechanism is an abstract game form, the utilities may be arbitrary continuous quasi-concave functions, and the result specifies neither a finite encoding nor an algorithmic output. Asking whether a continuum mechanism implements the rule faithfully restates an implementation question; it does not ask for the complexity of bribery, control, campaigning, robustness, or another computational task.

The strongest repair would be to specify finitely many types and rational masses, encode preferences and endowments, and ask for an algorithm that constructs a mechanism or computes a constrained Walrasian allocation. That could be a worthwhile computational project, but it adds the representation, algorithm, and output that Proposition 3 does not supply. Computing allocations also changes the target: the paper asks whether a rule can be implemented, not how to find its outcomes. Propositions 1, 2, 4, and 5 and Remark 2 do not provide another computational anchor; they give implementation conditions or applications.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated boundary, though it does not show that continuous implementation theory is uninteresting. The proponent’s proposed mirror may be valuable economics, but it does not establish a computational mirror of this paper’s results.
