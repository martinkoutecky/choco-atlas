The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor requirement is the main obstacle: this paper has no numbered complexity result saying that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 3.1 gives a fixed-point characterization, and Theorem 4.8 gives an equilibrium characterization; neither analyzes finite input encodings or running time. So there is no fully compliant computational anchor here.

The strongest positive candidate is a finite-support computation built around Theorem 4.8 (proved in this paper). Under Conditions C-(i)–C-(iii), for the put payoff and the paper’s one-barrier setting, there is a threshold \(a^*\) such that \([0,a]\) is an equilibrium exactly when \(a\geq a^*\), and \([0,a^*]\) is optimal: its value dominates those of the other equilibria. The paper’s geometric Brownian motion specialization gives an explicit threshold formula.

Call the candidate problem Finite-support optimal-equilibrium threshold computation. An instance consists of positive rational \(\mu,\sigma,K\), and a finite list of nonnegative rational discount rates \(r_i\) with positive rational masses \(\lambda_i\) summing to \(1\). The common project state follows \(dX_t=\mu X_t\,dt+\sigma X_t\,dW_t\), the payoff is \(g(x)=(K-x)^+\), and the attitude function is \(\phi(z)=z\). Type \(i\) means a member with discount rate \(r_i\); mass \(\lambda_i\) is that type’s share of the population. The question is to return the exact smallest optimal-equilibrium threshold \(a^*\). A solution specifies the policy \([0,a^*]\), which is an equilibrium and pointwise optimal among the eligible equilibria.

For this instance, the paper’s formula is
\[ q=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\lambda_i\left( \sqrt{\left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}-\frac12\right)^2+\frac{2r_i}{\sigma^2}} +\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}-\frac12 \right), \qquad a^*=\frac{q}{1+q}K. \]
Thus the proposed computation is tractable in a natural exact-expression model: the formula uses \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic and square-root operations. That is a complexity observation about this restricted input problem, not a runtime theorem established by the paper.

A plausible regime is a large jurisdiction deciding whether to stop an infrastructure or R&D project: many residents share the same project exposure and payoff, and fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of discount-rate cohorts. Millions of people against dozens of types is a credible high-multiplicity instance. The policy choice is the stopping region; the mass distribution enters the group’s aggregate preference. The authors should recognize this as their stopping-equilibrium problem, though the finite rational support and exact computation question are new restrictions.

The scope is deliberately narrow: one result, geometric Brownian motion, linear aggregation, and threshold equilibria. I would expect this restricted computation to be tractable. Further questions include computing all equilibria under \(\phi(z)=\min(z,\alpha)\), and deciding whether a pointwise optimal equilibrium exists in the paper’s examples where it may fail to exist.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already models the group through a distribution \(F_\rho\), so the population continuization itself is already present. The potential new contribution is the computational formulation, and in this narrow case the paper’s explicit formula largely supplies the answer. Also, Theorem 4.8 is an analytic structural result, not the kind of named computational result the prompt requires. So this is the best positive lead I can defend, but it is not a qualifying complexity-theoretic anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.8 is the only plausible anchor, and it is an analytic characterization: it identifies which one-barrier regions are equilibria and when the smallest is optimal. Theorem 3.1 similarly characterizes equilibria as fixed points. Neither gives a computational problem with an input encoding, running time, hardness result, or algorithm. The proposed finite-support threshold task supplies those computational ingredients itself; it is not a complexity result from the paper to mirror.

The deeper obstacle is that the paper already models the group as a distribution over discount-rate types. In the group interpretation, a type can be a discount rate \(r\), and \(F_\rho\) is the population’s distribution over those types. For finitely many cohorts, \(F_\rho=\sum_i\lambda_i\delta_{r_i}\), and the paper’s objective is exactly \(\sum_i\lambda_i\phi\!\left(\mathbb{E}[e^{-r_i\tau}g(X_\tau)]\right)\). The government and intergenerational examples make this a credible high-multiplicity regime. So the objection is not that mass is meaningless; it is that this paper has already made population heterogeneity a measure-valued object. Recasting its finite-support case as a “continuous mirror” leaves the modeled society and stopping problem unchanged.

A stronger computational follow-up could ask for the complexity of finding all equilibria, or deciding whether an optimal equilibrium exists, with finite rational support and a specified diffusion and attitude function. That could be worthwhile algorithmic research. It would still analyze a model that is already continuous in its population description, rather than study what changes when a discrete society is continuized. Broader versions would also need finite encodings for the diffusion and attitude function before complexity claims could be stated.

So the negative case is strongest on scope and novelty: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and its group model already has the continuous-population form. I cannot honestly claim that every nearby computational project lacks value; the narrower conclusion is that such a project would be complexity analysis of this paper’s existing continuous model, not a new population continuization mirror.
