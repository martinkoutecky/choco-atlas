The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and for each type a group label, a strict school ranking, and a priority tier at each school, take school priority tiers to order types, with ties only among identical types. Given rational capacities \(q_c\) and reserves \(r^m_c\), with \(0\le r^m_c\le q_c\) and \(\sum_c q_c\ge 1\), and a mass allocation \(x^Q\) stable under majority quotas \(q^M_c=q_c-r^m_c\), compute an allocation \(x^R\) stable under minority reserves. Require that the old and new assignments admit a coupling, type by type, in which every unit of mass weakly prefers its new school and a positive total mass strictly prefers it, unless \(x^Q\) is already stable under reserves. Stability uses the paper’s reserve rule on mass: a positive mass of a type blocks a school if it prefers that school and the rule would admit that mass by filling vacancies or displacing lower-priority mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\) schools. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Each type has a minority or majority label and a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(C\cup\{\varnothing\}\), where \(\varnothing\) is the outside option. Each school strictly orders distinct types; units of the same type have equal priority.
Capacities and reserves are binary rationals satisfying \(0\le r_c^m\le q_c\) and \(\sum_c q_c\ge1\); put \(q_c^M=q_c-r_c^m\). An allocation has divisible masses \(x_{ta}\ge0\), \(\sum_{a\in C\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{ta}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tc}\le q_c\), and no mass assigned below its outside option. Quota feasibility additionally requires \(\sum_{t\text{ majority}}x_{tc}\le q_c^M\).
For offered mass vector \(y\), the reserve choice \(\operatorname{Ch}_c^R(y)\) first takes minority mass in priority order up to \(r_c^m\), then takes remaining mass in priority order until capacity or offers are exhausted. The quota choice \(\operatorname{Ch}_c^Q(y)\) takes mass in priority order subject to capacity and the majority cap. Both may split a type's mass.
Under policy \(P\in\{Q,R\}\), a feasible allocation is stable if there are no \(t,c,a\) and \(0<\varepsilon\le x_{ta}\) with \(c\succ_t a\) and \([\operatorname{Ch}_c^P(x_{\cdot c}+\varepsilon e_t)]_t>x_{tc}\), where \(e_t\) is the unit vector for type \(t\).
Given a binary-rational quota-stable allocation \(x^Q\), seek a reserve-stable allocation \(x^R\) admitting nonnegative couplings \(\gamma_{tab}\) with \(\sum_b\gamma_{tab}=x^Q_{ta}\), \(\sum_a\gamma_{tab}=x^R_{tb}\), and \(\gamma_{tab}=0\) unless \(b\succeq_t a\). Require \(\sum_{t,a,b:\,b\succ_t a}\gamma_{tab}>0\) unless \(x^Q\) is already reserve-stable, in which case returning \(x^Q\) is permitted.
Does such an allocation always exist? Is finding one, or deciding that none exists, polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, where \(L\) is the total input bit length?
For finite students with strict priorities, every quota-stable matching is either reserve-stable or strictly Pareto dominated by a reserve-stable matching ([Hafalir, Yenmez, and Yildirim](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/117443/1/Hafalir_et_al-2013-Theoretical_Economics.pdf)).
A continuum study establishes reserve comparative statics for a model with two school tiers and continuously distributed priority scores ([Escobar and Huerta](https://www.dii.uchile.cl/~jescobar/school-choice-segregation-2024-july.pdf)); it does not settle this finite-support computational question. Result

Such an allocation always exists, and it can be found in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The algorithm also constructs the required couplings. Thus, on inputs satisfying the stated promise, the “none exists” case never occurs.

The proof uses finite cloning to establish existence, but the algorithm itself works directly with supported types. Its computational ingredient is the polynomial-time algorithm for ordinary stable allocation.

1. Choice thresholds and rational cloning

Fix a school, abbreviating its capacity and reserve by \(q,r\), and put \(q^M=q-r\). For an offered vector \(y\) and a type \(t\), define:

  • \(H^m\): minority mass of types ranked above \(t\);
  • \(H^M\): majority mass of types ranked above \(t\);
  • \(L^m\): minority mass of types ranked below \(t\).

Write \(z_+=\max\{z,0\}\).

Both choice rules have the form
\( [\operatorname{Ch}^P(y)]_t=\min\{y_t,K_t^P(y_{-t})\} \),
where the threshold is independent of \(y_t\). Explicitly:

| Policy | Label of \(t\) | Threshold \(K_t^P\) |
|---|---|---|
| Quota | Minority | \( (q-H^m-\min\{H^M,q^M\})_+ \) |
| Quota | Majority | \( \min\{(q-H^m-H^M)_+,(q^M-H^M)_+\} \) |
| Reserve | Minority | \( \max\{(r-H^m)_+,(q-H^m-H^M)_+\} \) |
| Reserve | Majority | \( (q-H^m-H^M-\min\{(r-H^m)_+,L^m\})_+ \) |

Here and below, quantities such as \(H^m\) are computed separately at each school.

Verification of the formulas

For a minority type under quotas, higher-priority majority types consume at most \(\min\{H^M,q^M\}\), while higher-priority minorities consume \(H^m\), unless capacity has already been exhausted. This gives the first formula.

For a majority type, admission requires both remaining total capacity and remaining majority capacity. If neither has already been exhausted, all higher-priority offers have been admitted. This gives the second formula.

For a minority type under reserves, let \(A=(r-H^m)_+\). If \(y_t\le A\), all its mass is selected in the reserve stage. If \(y_t>A\), the reserve is full, higher-priority minorities occupy \(\min\{r,H^m\}\) reserved mass, and the remaining amount available to \(t\) is
\(B=(q-r-H^m-H^M+\min\{r,H^m\})_+\).
Its total threshold is therefore \(A+B\). Directly distinguishing \(H^m\le r\) from \(H^m\ge r\) gives
\(A+B=\max\{(r-H^m)_+,(q-H^m-H^M)_+\}\).

For a majority type, the reserved minority mass ranked below it is precisely
\(\min\{(r-H^m)_+,L^m\}\).
This mass and the higher-priority mass must precede \(t\) in the overall selection, giving the fourth formula.

Consequently, for a feasible allocation \(x\), a type with positive mass at an assignment worse than \(c\) blocks \(c\) exactly when
\(K_t^P(x_{-t,c})>x_{tc}\).
This follows because adding \(\varepsilon\) changes its selected amount to
\(\min\{x_{tc}+\varepsilon,K_t^P(x_{-t,c})\}\).

Cloning lemma

Let \(D\) be a common denominator of all masses, capacities, reserves, and entries of a rational allocation. Replace type \(t\) by \(D\mu_t\) distinct students with identical preferences and labels, and multiply school capacities and reserves by \(D\).

Refine each school’s type priority into a strict student priority while preserving the order between distinct types. Under either policy, the number selected from a type depends only on offered type counts. Moreover, among offered students of that type, choice selects a prefix of their refined priority order.

Two consequences will be used.

Lifting stability. A rational policy-stable allocation can be lifted to a finite policy-stable matching. Assign its clones according to the allocation, and, within each type at each school, rank incumbent clones above nonincumbent clones.

Indeed, if an entering clone were selected, the prefix property would imply that all incumbent clones of its type were selected as well. Its type’s selected count would increase, contradicting stability of the original allocation with an offer of mass \(1/D\).

Projecting stability. Every finite reserve-stable matching respecting the type order projects to a reserve-stable divisible allocation.

To prove this, suppose the projection has a blocker. Its reserve threshold exceeds its current assigned mass. Both quantities are multiples of \(1/D\), by the displayed formulas. Thus offering \(1/D\) increases the selected type mass by \(1/D\). The corresponding entering clone must be selected, producing a finite blocking pair. This is a contradiction.

The same projection argument applies to ordinary priority choice, whose threshold is
\((q-\sum_{s\text{ above }t}y_s)_+\).

2. Existence of a rational improving allocation

Lift \(x^Q\) to a finite quota-stable matching using the cloning lemma.

The finite comparison theorem supplied in the question states that, with strict student preferences and strict school priorities, a quota-stable matching either is reserve-stable or is Pareto dominated by a reserve-stable matching when reserves and majority quotas sum to capacity. [Hafalir, Yenmez, and Yildirim, Theorem 1](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/117443/1/Hafalir_et_al-2013-Theoretical_Economics.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here: cloning gives finite students, integer capacities and reserves, strict refined priorities, and \(Dq_c^M+Dr_c^m=Dq_c\). Individual rationality and quota feasibility are preserved.

If \(x^Q\) is not reserve-stable, its finite lift cannot be reserve-stable, by the projection part of the cloning lemma. The theorem therefore supplies a strictly improving finite reserve-stable matching.

Project that matching to type masses, obtaining a rational reserve-stable allocation \(w\). Tracking each clone’s old and new assignment gives a coupling from \(x^Q\) to \(w\), supported on weak improvements and containing positive mass of strict improvements.

This proves existence. It does not yet give a polynomial-time algorithm: the number of clones can be exponential.

3. Reduction of reserve stability to ordinary stable allocation

For each school \(c\), introduce two copies:

  • a reserve copy \(c^S\), of capacity \(r_c^m\), ranking every minority type above every majority type and preserving \(c\)’s order within each label;
  • an open copy \(c^O\), of capacity \(q_c-r_c^m\), with \(c\)’s original type priority.

Every type replaces \(c\) in its preference list by \(c^S\), followed immediately by \(c^O\). Remove zero-capacity copies and omit schools below the outside option.

Both copies use ordinary priority choice.

Equality of the school choices

For any offered vector, first filling \(c^S\) and then filling \(c^O\) from the remaining offers gives exactly the original reserve choice.

If minority offers total at least the reserve, the first copy selects the highest-priority reserved minority mass; the second performs the open stage.

If minority offers total less than the reserve, the first copy selects all minorities and then the highest-priority majority mass up to its capacity. The second continues selecting majorities in the same order. Their aggregate is again the original reserve choice.

Canonical splitting

For a school’s incumbent vector \(z\), let \(S\) be its ordinary choice up to capacity \(r\) under the minority-first order, and put \(O=z-S\). This is feasible at the two copies: if total incumbent mass exceeds \(r\), then \(S\) has mass \(r\); otherwise \(O=0\).

Let \(h\) denote the minority-first order. For a type \(t\), define
\(A_t=(r-\sum_{s\text{ above }t\text{ in }h}z_s)_+\)
and
\(B_t=(q-r-\sum_{s\text{ above }t\text{ in the original order}}O_s)_+\).

Canonical splitting gives \(S_t=\min\{z_t,A_t\}\). The reserve copy accepts additional type-\(t\) mass exactly when \(S_t<A_t\), and the open copy does so exactly when \(O_t<B_t\).

An offer of additional type-\(t\) mass increases original reserve choice exactly when at least one of these inequalities holds:

  • If \(S_t<A_t\), then \(z_t<A_t\) and \(O_t=0\); a sufficiently small offer increases reserved type-\(t\) mass.
  • Otherwise \(z_t\ge A_t\). Adding type-\(t\) mass leaves the entire first-copy selection unchanged, and increases total type-\(t\) selection exactly when the open copy accepts it.

These observations establish both directions of the stability correspondence.

A reserve-stable allocation has a stable canonical lift. A block coming from a worse original school or the outside option would produce an original reserve block. An internal move from \(c^O\) to \(c^S\) cannot block: \(O_t>0\) implies \(S_t=A_t\).

Every stable allocation at the copies collapses to a reserve-stable allocation. First, its splitting must be canonical. If the reserve copy has slack and some mass is at the open copy, that mass blocks the reserve copy. If a higher-ranked type in the minority-first order has open mass while a lower-ranked type has reserved mass, the higher-ranked type blocks the reserve copy. These conditions force the unique greedy splitting, allowing a split of the boundary type.

An original reserve block would then admit additional mass at at least one copy, as shown above. Both copies are preferred to the source assignment, so it would be a virtual blocking pair.

Thus reserve-stable allocations are exactly the collapses of stable ordinary allocations in this construction.

4. The ordinary optimum dominates entire type distributions

The following distinction matters: the required coupling order is stronger than merely comparing rows lexicographically.

For a type’s ordered assignments \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), define the cumulative mass
\(F_x(j)=\sum_{i=1}^j x_{ta_i}\).
We say that \(x\) cumulatively dominates \(v\) for this type if
\(F_x(j)\ge F_v(j)\) for every \(j\), including the outside option.

Known algorithmic theorem

Ordinary bipartite stable allocation, with strict preferences, rational vertex capacities, and rational edge capacities, admits a unique stable allocation that simultaneously maximizes every proposing-side row lexicographically. This allocation can be computed in polynomial time; a strongly polynomial arithmetic algorithm is available. [Dean and Munshi, Lemma 1 and Section 3](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf). The underlying stable-allocation results also appear in the [corrected Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

Use only redundant edge bounds \(\min\{\mu_t,\text{copy capacity}\}\). They impose no additional restriction on feasible allocations or blocking moves.

For completeness, a formulation requiring full row and column sums can be obtained by adding a dummy school of capacity \(1\) and a dummy type of mass \(Q=\sum_c q_c\). Real types rank the dummy school last, every school ranks the dummy type last, and the dummy type ranks the dummy school last.

For any allocation \(z\) at the real copies, set:

  • dummy-type mass at copy \(j\) to \(k_j-\sum_t z_{tj}\);
  • real-type mass at the dummy school to \(\mu_t-\sum_j z_{tj}\);
  • dummy-type mass at the dummy school to \(\sum_{t,j}z_{tj}\).

All margins are then full. Dummy assignments introduce no additional blockers: the dummy type is lowest at every school, and the dummy school is last for every type. Stability at real copies is unchanged.

Let \(B\) be the computed proposing-side lexicographic optimum.

Lemma: \(B\) cumulatively dominates every rational stable allocation

Proof. Fix another rational stable allocation \(V\) in the completed ordinary instance, and choose a common denominator for the instance, \(B\), and \(V\).

Clone every type into unit students. Within each type, clones have identical preferences. Schools strictly order types, with an arbitrary strict refinement within each type.

The aggregate outcome of student-proposing deferred acceptance is independent of these refinements. To see this, run synchronous rounds. At every school, accepted and rejected counts of each type depend only on offered type counts. Every rejected clone of a given type at a given school next proposes to the same successor school. Induction on rounds therefore determines all proposal and holding counts independently of clone identities.

We also use the finite deferred-acceptance property that its outcome weakly improves every student relative to any stable matching. Its usual rejection proof applies: consider the first rejection of a student from her school in a proposed stable matching. That school holds a full capacity of higher-priority students. At least one is assigned elsewhere in the proposed matching and prefers this school to that assignment; otherwise that student would already have been rejected from her proposed assignment. This gives a blocking pair. Hence no such first rejection exists.

Now refine priorities to favor the incumbents of the clone lift of \(B\). The lifting lemma makes that matching stable. Deferred acceptance weakly improves every clone, so its aggregate output \(W\) cumulatively dominates \(B\).

The projection \(W\) is a stable ordinary allocation. By lexicographic optimality, each row of \(B\) is lexicographically at least its row in \(W\). Cumulative dominance implies the opposite lexicographic inequality: at a first differing coordinate, the cumulative inequality makes \(W\)’s coordinate larger. Therefore every row is equal, and \(W=B\).

Instead refine priorities to favor the incumbents of the lift of \(V\). That lift is stable, so deferred acceptance cumulatively dominates \(V\). Its aggregate output is still \(W=B\), by refinement invariance. Thus \(B\) cumulatively dominates \(V\). \(\square\)

This lemma applies to all real types simultaneously.

5. The polynomial-time construction

The algorithm is:

  • Test whether \(x^Q\) is reserve-stable, using the thresholds in Section 1. If it is, return \(x^Q\) with the identity coupling.
  • Construct the two-copy ordinary stable-allocation instance.
  • Compute its proposing-side lexicographic optimum \(B\).
  • Return its collapse \(x^R\), where \(x^R_{tc}=B_{t,c^S}+B_{t,c^O}\), with unmatched mass assigned to the outside option.
  • Construct the couplings as described below.

The collapse is reserve-stable by Section 3.

To prove dominance, use the rational allocation \(w\) obtained in Section 2. Its canonical lift \(V\) is stable in the copy instance. Section 4 implies that \(B\) cumulatively dominates \(V\).

The copies of each original school are consecutive in every type’s preference list. Hence inequalities at the ends of copy pairs imply that \(x^R\) cumulatively dominates \(w\) in the original preferences. Since \(w\) cumulatively dominates \(x^Q\), transitivity gives cumulative dominance of \(x^R\) over \(x^Q\).

Constructing the couplings

For each type, list all original assignments from best to worst as \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), and write
\(F_Q(i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^i x^Q_{ta_\ell}\),
\(F_R(i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^i x^R_{ta_\ell}\),
with \(F_Q(0)=F_R(0)=0\).

Define
\(\gamma_{t,a_i,a_j}=\max\{0,\min\{F_Q(i),F_R(j)\}-\max\{F_Q(i-1),F_R(j-1)\}\}\).

These are intersection lengths of the old and new assignment intervals in \((0,\mu_t]\). They have the required row and column sums.

Moreover, \(F_R(i)\ge F_Q(i)\) for every \(i\). At every quantile, the new assignment therefore has rank no worse than the old assignment. Consequently \(\gamma_{t,a_i,a_j}>0\) only if \(j\le i\), exactly the required weak-improvement condition.

If \(x^Q\) was not reserve-stable, then \(x^R\ne x^Q\), since \(x^R\) is reserve-stable. If the coupling had zero strictly improving mass, all its mass would be diagonal, forcing equality of the allocations. Thus its strictly improving mass is positive.

6. Bit complexity and conclusion

The ordinary instance has at most \(\tau+1\) types, \(2m+1\) schools, and \(O(m\tau+m+\tau)\) edges, including the dummies. Its preferences are strict, and all capacities and redundant edge bounds have polynomial encoding length. These verify the stable-allocation algorithm’s hypotheses.

For exact arithmetic, take the product of input denominators as a common denominator \(D\). Its bit length is \(O(L)\). The cited augmentation algorithm uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of residual quantities. Starting from input rationals, its allocation values remain on the \(1/D\) grid. Their magnitudes are bounded by total supply or capacity, whose encoding length is polynomial in the input length.

Thus the polynomial arithmetic bound gives polynomial bit complexity. The cloning arguments above require no explicit clones in the algorithm. Stability checking, collapsing the copies, and computing the interval-intersection couplings also take polynomial time and produce polynomial-size rational output.

Therefore existence and construction are both settled for the stated finite-support model.
