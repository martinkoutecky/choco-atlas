The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given request types \(r\) with rational masses \(q_r\), vehicle types \(v\) with rational masses \(p_v\), feasible pairs \(E\), scores \(s(v,r)\), and historical group rates \(z_g\), compute a maximum-score mass assignment for a specified \(\beta\), including null actions and the passenger incentive \(\beta(\bar z-z_{g(r)})\). With post-batch rates \(z'_g\) computed from historical service mass and current request mass, determine whether a mass-level analogue of Theorem 1 holds: under a suitable positive-mass version of passenger-min-unfairness, does some \(\beta>0\) yield \(z'_{g_{\min}}(\beta)>z'_{g_{\min}}(0)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(\tau=|R|+|V|\), with request types \(R\), vehicle types \(V\), positive masses \(q_r,p_v\), and explicitly listed feasible pairs \(E\subseteq V\times R\). Masses use a common unit and need not sum to one.
There are \(m\ge2\) passenger groups and a map \(g:R\to\{1,\ldots,m\}\). Historical request and served masses are binary rationals \(H_g>0\) and \(0\le S_g\le H_g\); define \(z_g=S_g/H_g\), \(\bar z=m^{-1}\sum_g z_g\), and assume a unique historical minimum group \(g_{\min}\).
Each unit of vehicle mass accepts at most one request unit or the null action \(\bot\). Scores \(s(v,r)\) and \(s(v,\bot)\) are arbitrary binary rationals. A feasible assignment has nonnegative real coordinates satisfying \(\sum_{r:(v,r)\in E}x_{vr}+x_{v\bot}=p_v\) and \(y_r:=\sum_{v:(v,r)\in E}x_{vr}\le q_r\).
For \(\beta\ge0\), let \(x(\beta)\) maximize \(\sum_{(v,r)\in E}x_{vr}[s(v,r)+\beta(\bar z-z_{g(r)})]+\sum_vx_{v\bot}s(v,\bot)\); break ties by taking the lexicographically smallest coordinate vector in an input-specified order.
Write \(Q_g=\sum_{r:g(r)=g}q_r\) and \(z'_g(\beta)=[S_g+\sum_{r:g(r)=g}y_r(\beta)]/(H_g+Q_g)\). Define positive-mass passenger-min-unfairness of \(x(0)\) by the existence of \((v,r_f)\in E\) with \(g(r_f)=g_{\min}\), \(q_{r_f}-y_{r_f}(0)>0\), and either \(x_{v\bot}(0)>0\) or \(x_{vr}(0)>0\) for some \(r\) outside \(g_{\min}\).
State running times in \(m,\tau,|E|\) and total binary input length \(L\), including any specified rational \(\beta\).

Is deciding whether some \(\beta>0\) satisfies \(z'_{g_{\min}}(\beta)>z'_{g_{\min}}(0)\) in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and what is the complexity of computing \(x(\beta)\) for a specified rational \(\beta\)?
Does positive-mass passenger-min-unfairness guarantee such an improving \(\beta\), or can that implication fail?

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\), and computing the specified lexicographically selected \(x(\beta)\) is polynomial-time solvable. Positive-mass passenger-min-unfairness guarantees an improving \(\beta>0\).

Both running times are \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|E|,L)\) bit operations, with the encoding of a specified rational \(\beta\) included in \(L\).

We prove a stronger characterization: improvement exists exactly when the baseline serves less than the maximum feasible mass of the historical minimum group.

1. Reduction to served mass

Write \(g_*=g_{\min}\), \(R_*=\{r\in R:g(r)=g_*\}\), and define
\[ \]
\(A(x)=\sum_{r\in R_*}y_r\), and \(M=\max\{A(x):x\text{ is feasible}\}\).

The feasible set is nonempty, since every vehicle can choose the null action. It is compact: all coordinates are nonnegative and each vehicle’s coordinates sum to its finite mass. Thus \(M\) is attained.

Because \(H_{g_*}+Q_{g_*}>0\) is independent of the assignment,
\[ \]
\(z'_{g_*}(\beta)>z'_{g_*}(0)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A(x(\beta))>A(x(0)).\)

The value \(M\) is computable by maximum flow. Construct a network with source \(u\), sink \(t\), all vehicles, and the requests in \(R_*\), with arcs:

  • \(u\to v\), capacity \(p_v\);
  • \(v\to r\), capacity \(p_v\), for feasible pairs with \(r\in R_*\);
  • \(r\to t\), capacity \(q_r\).

Restricting any feasible assignment to its \(R_*\)-coordinates gives a flow of value \(A(x)\). Conversely, any flow in this network extends to a feasible assignment by sending unused vehicle mass to the null action. Hence its maximum-flow value is exactly \(M\).

2. Computing the specified assignment

Let \(d=|E|+|V|\), the number of assignment coordinates. For a specified rational \(\beta\), the primary optimization is a rational linear program with \(d\) variables and \(O(d+\tau)\) constraints.

Compute its optimal value and restrict to its optimal face. Then, in the input-specified coordinate order, minimize each coordinate and fix it to its minimum before proceeding to the next coordinate. After all \(d\) coordinates have been fixed, the remaining assignment is precisely the lexicographically smallest primary optimizer.

This uses at most \(d+1\) exact rational LP optimizations. Every successive feasible set is a nonempty face of the original compact polytope. Each fixed coordinate value is therefore attained at an original vertex and has polynomial encoding length by the standard rational-vertex bounds. The primary optimal value also has polynomial encoding length. Consequently, the successive LP encodings remain polynomial in size.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for binary-encoded rational linear programming therefore gives a running time of \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|E|,L)\) bit operations, including exact tie-breaking.

3. A polynomially encoded incentive threshold

Define \(a_g=\bar z-z_g\), and assign incentive \(a_\bot=0\) to the null action. The unique historical minimum implies
\[ \]
\(a_{g_*}>0,\qquad a_{g_*}-a_g=z_g-z_{g_*}>0\quad(g\ne g_*).\)

Thus the rational number
\[ \]
\(\delta=\min\bigl(\{a_{g_*}\}\cup\{a_{g_*}-a_g:g\ne g_*\}\bigr)\)
is strictly positive.

Let
\[ \]
\(C=\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{|s(v,r)|:(v,r)\in E\}\cup\{|s(v,\bot)|:v\in V\}\bigr)\)
and set
\[ \]
\(B=1+\frac{(\tau+1)C}{\delta}.\)

All these rationals can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Their encoding lengths are polynomial in the input length: forming ratios, summing the explicitly listed historical rates, and carrying out the displayed rational operations produces numerators and denominators of polynomial bit length.

Claim. For every real \(\beta\ge B\), every primary optimizer satisfies \(A(x)=M\).

We prove this using residual cycles.

Flow representation

Construct a directed network on \(V\cup R\cup\{t\}\). Vehicle \(v\) has supply \(p_v\); every request has zero net supply; and \(t\) has demand \(\sum_v p_v\). Use the following arcs:

| Arc | Capacity | Objective coefficient |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(v\to r\), for \((v,r)\in E\) | \(p_v\) | \(s(v,r)\) |
| \(r\to t\) | \(q_r\) | \(\beta a_{g(r)}\) |
| \(v\to t\) | \(p_v\) | \(s(v,\bot)\) |

Feasible assignments correspond exactly to feasible flows: put \(x_{vr}\) on \(v\to r\), \(y_r\) on \(r\to t\), and \(x_{v\bot}\) on \(v\to t\). Flow conservation at each request verifies that this representation preserves the stated objective.

Existence of an increasing residual cycle

Let \(f\) be a feasible flow with \(A(f)<M\), and choose a feasible flow \(h\) with \(A(h)=M\).

Represent \(h-f\) in the residual graph of \(f\): a positive difference uses an original arc forward, and a negative difference uses its reverse. Feasibility of \(f\) and \(h\) ensures that every used residual arc has sufficient positive residual capacity. Since the two flows have identical supplies and demands, this difference is a nonnegative circulation.

Every finite nonnegative circulation decomposes into positive multiples of simple directed cycles. To see this, follow positive-flow arcs until a vertex repeats, subtract the minimum flow on the resulting cycle, and repeat; each subtraction removes at least one positive-flow arc.

The total change in \(A\) is positive, so at least one cycle in this decomposition has positive change in \(A\).

A simple cycle avoiding \(t\) does not change \(A\). A simple cycle containing \(t\) enters and leaves it exactly once. Every original arc incident to \(t\) points into \(t\), so the cycle enters on a forward original arc and leaves on a reverse original arc.

For its change in \(A\) to be positive, it must enter \(t\) through \(r_f\to t\), with \(r_f\in R_*\), and leave through the reverse of either:

  • \(r\to t\), where \(g(r)\ne g_*\); or
  • a null arc \(v\to t\).

Its change in \(A\), per unit circulated, is therefore exactly \(1\). Its incentive gain is respectively \(a_{g_*}-a_{g(r)}\) or \(a_{g_*}\), hence at least \(\delta\).

Objective gain on that cycle

The network has \(\tau+1\) vertices, so a simple cycle has at most \(\tau+1\) arcs. Every residual arc’s base-score contribution has absolute value at most \(C\). Therefore its base-score gain is at least \(-(\tau+1)C\).

For \(\beta\ge B\), its total objective gain per unit is at least
\[ \]
\(-(\tau+1)C+\beta\delta\ \ge\ -(\tau+1)C+B\delta=\delta>0.\)

Augmenting by any sufficiently small positive amount around this residual cycle preserves feasibility and strictly increases the objective. Consequently, a flow with \(A(f)<M\) cannot be optimal. This proves the claim.

4. Exact decision criterion and running time

The preceding claim establishes
\[ \]
\(\exists\beta>0:\ z'_{g_*}(\beta)>z'_{g_*}(0)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M>A(x(0)).\)

Indeed:

  • If \(M=A(x(0))\), no feasible assignment can improve the group’s served mass.
  • If \(M>A(x(0))\), then \(B>0\) and every optimizer at \(B\), including the specified lexicographic optimizer, serves mass \(M\).

The decision algorithm therefore computes \(x(0)\), computes \(M\) by maximum flow, and compares the two rational served masses exactly.

It uses at most \(d+1\) rational LP optimizations with \(d\) variables and \(O(d+\tau)\) constraints, followed by one maximum-flow computation on \(O(\tau)\) vertices and \(O(|E|+\tau)\) arcs. Rational capacities can be scaled to integers using a product of their input denominators, whose bit length is polynomial in \(L\). Standard polynomial-time maximum-flow algorithms then apply.

Thus the decision running time is \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|E|,L)\) bit operations. Neither algorithm expands binary-encoded masses into individual units.

5. Passenger-min-unfairness guarantees improvement

Suppose the baseline satisfies positive-mass passenger-min-unfairness. Choose its witnessing feasible pair \((v,r_f)\), where \(r_f\in R_*\) and \(q_{r_f}-y_{r_f}(0)>0\).

There is also a positive donor coordinate: either \(x_{v\bot}(0)>0\), or \(x_{vr}(0)>0\) for a request outside \(R_*\). Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) no larger than both the donor coordinate and the unmet mass of \(r_f\).

Increase \(x_{vr_f}\) by \(\varepsilon\) and decrease the donor coordinate by \(\varepsilon\). This preserves vehicle \(v\)’s total assigned mass, keeps every coordinate nonnegative, and respects all request capacities. Its served mass for \(g_*\) increases by exactly \(\varepsilon\). Hence
\[ \]
\(M\ge A(x(0))+\varepsilon>A(x(0)).\)

The decision criterion now implies strict improvement; specifically, \(\beta=B\) is a valid witness.

Therefore the implication cannot fail under the stated assumptions. This independently proves the rational-mass analogue of the discrete existence guarantee stated in Theorem 1 of the [published paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICAPS/article/download/27199/26972/31268).
