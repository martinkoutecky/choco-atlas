Corollary 1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given iid samples from an unknown two-candidate society \(\mu\) over ballot types, estimate the mass-transfer margin \(\rho_r(\mu)\), the infimum mass whose ballot types must change for the winner set under \(r\) to change, within additive error \(c\rho_r(\mu)+\epsilon\) with failure probability at most \(\delta\). Determine the minimum sample complexity for rules \(r\) that reduce to plurality on two candidates.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C=\{a,b\}\), with ballot types the two strict rankings. A society is represented by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational, summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The support representation is hidden: an estimator accesses the unknown society \(\mu\) only through independent samples revealing complete ballots. The rule \(r\) agrees with plurality on every two-candidate mass profile and returns all tied winners.
Arbitrary fractions of existing mass may be reassigned to either ballot type, including an initially absent type, at unit cost per unit mass. Define \(\rho_r(\mu)=\inf_{\nu:r(\nu)\ne r(\mu)}\frac12\sum_t|\mu(t)-\nu(t)|\), where \(\nu\) ranges over probability distributions on these types.
For rational \(0\le c<1\), \(0<\epsilon<1\), and \(0<\delta<1/2\), let \(N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta)\) be the least integer sample budget permitting a randomized estimator satisfying \(\Pr[|\widehat\rho-\rho_r(\mu)|\le c\rho_r(\mu)+\epsilon]\ge1-\delta\) for every such \(\mu\), with no restriction on its encoding length. Probability includes sampling and estimator randomness.
State any running times in \(m=2\), \(\tau\), total binary encoding length \(L\), sample budget, and accuracy parameters.

What is the minimum worst-case sample complexity \(N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta)\) for estimating this mass-transfer margin?

For finite elections, a verified sampling lower bound is \(\Omega((1-c)^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) in the small-error regime, while plurality admits an additive-error estimator using \(O(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) samples. [Source](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/162.pdf)

Partial resolution

The exact minimum can be determined by a finite procedure throughout the regime
$$ \epsilon\ge \frac{(1-c)^2}{8}. $$
Below this threshold, the exact minimum remains unresolved here.

In addition, the arguments below completely characterize when the minimum is zero, two, or three samples. They improve the exact regimes in the supplied notes.

1. Statements of the proved results

Set
$$ \alpha=1-c,\qquad \beta=1+c,\qquad h=\frac{2\epsilon}{\alpha}. $$

Allowing a budget of zero samples,
$$ N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \epsilon\ge\frac{\alpha}{4}. $$

Suppose henceforth that
$$ 0<\epsilon<\frac{\alpha}{4}, $$
so that $0<h<1/2$. Define
$$ d_2(h)=\frac{1}{2+4h^2} $$
and
$$ d_3(h)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{1-4h^2}{2-4h^2},&h^2\le\dfrac16,\\[6pt] \dfrac{1}{2+12h^2},&h^2\ge\dfrac16. \end{cases} $$
The two expressions defining $d_3$ agree at $h^2=1/6$.

The following are exact:
$$ N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta)=2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \epsilon\ge\frac{\alpha^2}{8} \ \text{and}\ \delta\ge d_2(h), $$
and
$$ N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta)=3 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \epsilon\ge\frac{\alpha^2}{8} \ \text{and}\ d_3(h)\le\delta<d_2(h). $$
One sample never suffices in the regime under consideration.

In particular,
$$ 0<\epsilon<\frac{\alpha^2}{8} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta)\ge4. $$

For example, when $c=0$ and $\epsilon=1/8$,
$$ N^*(0,1/8,\delta)= \begin{cases} 2,&4/9\le\delta<1/2,\\ 3,&3/7\le\delta<4/9, \end{cases} $$
and smaller confidence parameters require at least four samples.

Section 4 gives an exact finite decision procedure for every sample budget when
$$ \frac{\alpha^2}{8}\le\epsilon<\frac{\alpha}{4}. $$
Thus that entire parameter regime is settled algorithmically, including budgets larger than three.

2. Reduction to a statistical estimation problem

Let $p$ denote the mass of the ballot $a\succ b$. The mass of the other ballot is $1-p$. For a second society with parameter $q$, the mass-transfer distance is
$$ \frac12\bigl(|p-q|+|(1-p)-(1-q)|\bigr)=|p-q|. $$

When $p>1/2$, changing the winning set requires reaching $q\le1/2$, and the nearest such profile is the tied profile. The argument for $p<1/2$ is symmetric. At $p=1/2$, arbitrarily small nonzero perturbations change the winning set. Consequently,
$$ \rho_r(\mu)=\left|p-\frac12\right|. $$

Write
$$ t=\left|p-\frac12\right|\in[0,1/2]. $$
An output $y$ succeeds exactly when
$$ y\in A(t):=[\alpha t-\epsilon,\ \beta t+\epsilon]. $$

Zero samples and the exclusion of one sample

If $\epsilon\ge\alpha/4$, the constant output $\epsilon$ belongs to every $A(t)$, since
$$ \alpha t-\epsilon\le\frac{\alpha}{2}-\epsilon\le\epsilon \le\beta t+\epsilon. $$

If $\epsilon<\alpha/4$, the intervals
$$ A(0)=[-\epsilon,\epsilon] \quad\text{and}\quad A(1/2)= \left[\frac{\alpha}{2}-\epsilon,\frac{\beta}{2}+\epsilon\right] $$
are disjoint. A common output distribution cannot assign probability at least $1-\delta>1/2$ to both. This proves the zero-sample characterization.

More generally, suppose an estimator uses at most $N\ge1$ samples. It may be padded to use exactly $N$ samples. Under each unanimous society, successful estimation requires output greater than $\epsilon$. Therefore, conditional on either constant sample sequence, the probability of output greater than $\epsilon$ is at least $1-\delta$.

Under the tied society, these two sequences each have probability $2^{-N}$, and output greater than $\epsilon$ is a failure. Hence
$$ \delta\ge2^{1-N}(1-\delta), $$
so
$$ N\ge1+\log_2\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}. $$
Since $\delta<1/2$, this excludes one sample.

Restricting the output range

In the nonzero-sample regime, define
$$ y_{\mathrm{low}}=\epsilon,\qquad y_{\mathrm{high}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\epsilon. $$
Clipping any output to $[y_{\mathrm{low}},y_{\mathrm{high}}]$ cannot turn a success into a failure:

  • Raising an output below $\epsilon$ to $\epsilon$ preserves success because every acceptable interval has upper endpoint at least $\epsilon$.
  • Lowering an output above $y_{\mathrm{high}}$ to $y_{\mathrm{high}}$ preserves success because every acceptable interval has lower endpoint at most $y_{\mathrm{high}}$.

Thus outputs may be written as
$$ y=\epsilon+\beta X,\qquad X\in[0,g], $$
where
$$ R=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}, \qquad g=\frac{\alpha/2-2\epsilon}{\beta}. $$
The defining relations give
$$ Rg+h=\frac12. $$
The success condition becomes
$$ X\le t\le RX+h. $$

The intervals overlap in the useful sense $g\le h$ exactly when
$$ \epsilon\ge\frac{\alpha^2}{8}. $$
In that regime:

  • For $0\le t<g$, success means $X\le t$.
  • For $g\le t\le h$, every permitted output succeeds.
  • For $h<t\le1/2$, success means $X\ge(t-h)/R$.

This decomposition is the basis of the exact decision procedure.

3. Symmetry and the available statistical experiment

The hidden support representation does not affect the sample distribution: each sample is Bernoulli with parameter $p$.

An estimator can be symmetrized by randomly permuting its samples and randomly interchanging $a$ and $b$. This preserves its guarantee because the target $t$ is unchanged and the guarantee applies to both parameters $p$ and $1-p$.

For a symmetrized estimator using $N$ samples, the conditional output distribution depends only on
$$ Z=\left|2K-N\right|, $$
where $K$ is the number of samples ranking $a$ first.

Let the possible values be
$$ z_0<z_1<\cdots<z_d. $$
For $p=1/2+t$, their probabilities are the rational polynomials
$$ w_i(t)= \sum_{\substack{0\le k\le N\\|2k-N|=z_i}} \binom Nk \left(\frac12+t\right)^k \left(\frac12-t\right)^{N-k}. $$
Define
$$ F_j(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{j}w_i(t), \qquad F_{-1}(t)=0. $$

For every $t<1/2$, all the $w_i(t)$ are positive.

Monotone likelihood ratios

For $t<1/2$, introduce $\theta\ge0$ by
$$ \frac12+t=\frac{e^\theta}{2\cosh\theta}. $$
For suitable positive constants $b_i$,
$$ w_i(t)= b_i\frac{\cosh(z_i\theta)}{(2\cosh\theta)^N}. $$

If $\theta_2>\theta_1$, the likelihood ratio is increasing in $z_i$. Indeed,
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log\cosh(z\theta) =z\tanh(z\theta), $$
and this is increasing in $z\ge0$. Integrating between $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ proves the assertion.

Consequently, $Z$ is stochastically increasing in $t$. Also, for $u>x$, among all tests $\phi_i\in[0,1]$ satisfying
$$ \sum_i w_i(x)\phi_i\ge\gamma, $$
the minimum of
$$ \sum_i w_i(u)\phi_i $$
is attained by filling the smallest $z_i$ first, with at most one fractional value. This follows directly by exchanging test mass from a larger likelihood ratio to a smaller one until the constraint is tight.

Here and below,
$$ \gamma=1-\delta. $$

4. An exact finite decision procedure in the overlap regime

Assume
$$ \frac{\alpha^2}{8}\le\epsilon<\frac{\alpha}{4}. $$

For $x\in[0,g]$, let $\phi_x$ be the randomized lower-tail test having probability exactly $\gamma$ under parameter $x$. Explicitly,
$$ \phi_x(i)= \min\left\{ 1,\, \max\left\{ 0,\, \frac{\gamma-F_{i-1}(x)}{w_i(x)} \right\} \right\}. $$
Define
$$ T_N(x)=\sum_i w_i(Rx+h)\phi_x(i). $$

Exact feasibility criterion. A budget of $N$ samples is feasible if and only if
$$ T_N(x)\le\delta \qquad\text{for every }x\in[0,g]. $$

Necessity

First, a guarantee for every admissible binary-rational society also gives the guarantee for every real parameter.

To see this, fix an estimator and a sample budget. Conditional on each of the finitely many sample sequences, its output has a fixed probability distribution. Since $A(t)$ is closed and its endpoints vary continuously, its success probability is upper semicontinuous in $p$. Approaching any real $p$ through binary rationals therefore preserves the lower bound $\gamma$ on success probability.

After symmetrization and clipping, let $G_i(x)$ be the conditional distribution function of $X$ given $Z=z_i$.

For $x<g$, success at parameter $t=x$ implies
$$ \sum_i w_i(x)G_i(x)\ge\gamma. $$
For any $v>Rx+h$, output with $X\le x$ fails. Taking $v$ down to $Rx+h$, continuity of the sample probabilities gives
$$ \sum_i w_i(Rx+h)G_i(x)\le\delta. $$

The likelihood-ratio argument in Section 3 shows that replacing the test $G_i(x)$ by $\phi_x(i)$ can only decrease the latter probability. Thus $T_N(x)\le\delta$ for $x<g$. The functions $\phi_x(i)$ are continuous in $x$, so the inequality also holds at $x=g$.

Sufficiency

For each observation $z_i$, prescribe the conditional distribution function of $X$ by
$$ \Pr[X\le x\mid Z=z_i]=\phi_x(i), \qquad 0\le x<g, $$
and put the remaining probability at $X=g$.

These are valid distribution functions. To verify monotonicity, note that randomized lower-tail tests are totally ordered pointwise. Stochastic monotonicity implies that a test of probability $\gamma$ at a larger parameter must be at least as large pointwise as the corresponding test at a smaller parameter. Thus $\phi_x(i)$ is nondecreasing in $x$. Its displayed formula also proves continuity.

At $0\le t<g$, success probability is
$$ \sum_i w_i(t)\phi_t(i)=\gamma. $$
At $g\le t\le h$, success probability is one.

For $h<t<1/2$, put $x=(t-h)/R$. Continuity of the conditional distribution functions gives success probability
$$ 1-\sum_i w_i(t)\phi_x(i) =1-T_N(x)\ge\gamma. $$
At $t=1/2$, the atom at $g$ gives the same formula with $x=g$. This proves sufficiency.

Reduction to finitely many polynomial inequalities

If the quantile cutoff is $j$, then
$$ F_{j-1}(x)\le\gamma\le F_j(x) $$
and
$$ T_N(x)= F_{j-1}(Rx+h) + \frac{\gamma-F_{j-1}(x)}{w_j(x)} w_j(Rx+h). $$
Since $w_j(x)>0$ on $[0,g]$, the exact criterion is equivalent to requiring
$$ \begin{aligned} &[\delta-F_{j-1}(Rx+h)]w_j(x)\\ &\qquad- [\gamma-F_{j-1}(x)]w_j(Rx+h) \ge0 \end{aligned} $$
for every $x\in[0,g]$ satisfying the cutoff inequalities, for each $j$.

These are finitely many univariate polynomial sign conditions with rational coefficients. They can be decided exactly by isolating their real roots, partitioning $[0,g]$ at those roots, and checking the signs on every resulting interval and boundary point.

Section 7 supplies a finite upper bound on the budget. Testing budgets up to that bound therefore determines the exact integer $N^*$ throughout the overlap regime. This is a finite algebraic procedure, rather than an optimization over unspecified estimators.

5. Exact two- and three-sample minima

For two samples, the symmetrized experiment has two outcomes: agreement and disagreement. Agreement has probability
$$ q(t)=\frac12+2t^2. $$

For three samples, the two outcomes are unanimity and non-unanimity. Unanimity has probability
$$ q(t)=\frac14+3t^2. $$

Both experiments have the form
$$ q(t)=a+bt^2,\qquad b=4(1-a), $$
with $a=1/2$ or $a=1/4$. Call the outcome of probability $q(t)$ the high observation.

A necessary testing bound

Let $u$ and $v$ be the probabilities of output greater than $\epsilon$ conditional on the high and low observations, respectively.

At the tie,
$$ au+(1-a)v\le\delta. $$
At every parameter $t>h$, successful output must be greater than $\epsilon$. Taking $t$ down to $h$ gives
$$ q(h)u+[1-q(h)]v\ge1-\delta. $$

Write $q=q(h)$. For fixed $\delta$, the largest possible left side of the second inequality, subject to the first, is
$$ \begin{cases} q\delta/a,&\delta\le a,\\[4pt] q+(1-q)(\delta-a)/(1-a),&\delta\ge a. \end{cases} $$
Indeed, spending the allowed probability on $u$ first is optimal because
$$ \frac qa>\frac{1-q}{1-a}. $$

Solving for the smallest feasible $\delta$ gives
$$ d(a,q)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{a}{a+q},&a+q\ge1,\\[6pt] \dfrac{1-q}{2-a-q},&a+q\le1. \end{cases} $$
Substituting $a=1/2$ gives $d_2(h)$; substituting $a=1/4$ gives $d_3(h)$.

A ratio inequality

Suppose $g\le h$ and $Rg+h=1/2$. For either of these experiments,
$$ \frac{q(Rx+h)}{q(x)} \ge\frac{q(h)}a \qquad(0\le x\le g). $$

Here is a proof. Differentiation shows that the sign of the derivative of the ratio is the sign of
$$ aRh+\bigl[a(R^2-1)-bh^2\bigr]x-bRhx^2. $$
This quadratic has positive constant term and negative leading coefficient, and therefore exactly one positive root. The ratio first increases and then decreases; its minimum on $[0,g]$ is at an endpoint.

At $x=0$, its value is $q(h)/a$. At $x=g$, its value is $1/q(g)$. It remains to show
$$ q(h)q(g)\le a. $$

Since $R\ge1$,
$$ h+g\le\frac12. $$
Put $H=1/2-g\ge h$ and $z=gH\le1/16$. Then
$$ q(h)q(g)\le q(H)q(g) =a+bz(bz-2a)\le a. $$
The last inequality follows from
$$ bz\le\frac b{16}=\frac{1-a}{4}\le2a, $$
which holds for both $a=1/2$ and $a=1/4$.

Attaining the testing bound

Let $d=d(a,q(h))$ and $\gamma=1-d$.

If $a+q(h)\ge1$, set
$$ w=1,\qquad A=d. $$
If $a+q(h)\le1$, set
$$ w=\frac{\gamma}{1-a}, \qquad A=aw. $$
In both cases,
$$ 0<w\le1,\qquad w-A=\gamma,\qquad 1-w+A\frac{q(h)}a=\gamma. $$

Conditional on the low observation, output $X=0$ with probability $w$ and $X=g$ otherwise.

Conditional on the high observation, use the distribution function
$$ L(x)=w-\frac{A}{q(x)} \qquad(0\le x<g), $$
with the remaining probability placed at $g$. This is valid: $L(0)\ge0$, it is nondecreasing, and $L(g)<1$.

For $0\le t<g$, its success probability is
$$ q(t)L(t)+[1-q(t)]w=w-A=\gamma. $$
For $g\le t\le h$, every output succeeds.

For $h<t\le1/2$, write $x=(t-h)/R$. The success probability is
$$ 1-w+A\frac{q(t)}{q(x)} \ge 1-w+A\frac{q(h)}a =\gamma. $$
Thus the necessary testing bound is attained whenever $g\le h$.

Why the overlap condition is necessary

Suppose $h<g$. Choose a dyadic $t_1$ with
$$ h<t_1<g. $$
Then $A(t_1)$ is disjoint from both $A(0)$ and $A(1/2)$.

For either the two- or three-sample experiment, its distribution at $t_1$ is a convex combination of its distributions at $0$ and $1/2$, because the experiment has just two outcomes and
$$ a<q(t_1)<1. $$
At each endpoint, the probability of output in $A(t_1)$ is at most $\delta$. The same is therefore true at $t_1$, contradicting required success probability $1-\delta>\delta$.

Consequently, neither two nor three samples can suffice when $h<g$.

Combining necessity, attainability, and the exclusion of smaller budgets proves the exact characterizations in Section 1. Finally, direct algebra gives
$$ d_3(h)<d_2(h) \qquad(0<h<1/2), $$
so the stated three-sample regime is nonempty.

6. Finite-coin implementation of the two- and three-sample optima

The preceding distributions can also be replaced by distributions on countably many rational outputs, sampled with finitely many fair random bits almost surely. Thus their exact attainability does not depend on physically producing an arbitrary real number.

Retain $a,b,w,A,L$ from Section 5 and define
$$ U(x)=w-\frac{Aq(h)}{a\,q(Rx+h)}. $$
It suffices to replace the high-observation distribution function by any distribution function $D$ satisfying
$$ L(x)\le D(x)\le U(x) \qquad(0<x<g), $$
with $D(0)=L(0)$ and $D(g-)=L(g)$.

The ratio inequality proves $U(x)\ge L(x)$. More precisely, set
$$ J=\min\left\{ 2aRh,\, 2aRh+\bigl[a(R^2-1)-bh^2\bigr]g \right\}>0. $$
Positivity follows from the strict endpoint inequality in the ratio proof. A direct calculation gives
$$ U(x)-L(x)\ge\frac{AbJ}{a}\,x. $$
Also,
$$ L(s)-L(r)\le\frac{Ab}{a^2}(s^2-r^2) \qquad(0\le r\le s\le g). $$

Choose the rational number
$$ \eta=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac{aJ}{6g}\right\}, \qquad r_j=g(1+\eta)^{-j}. $$
For $x\in[r_{j+1},r_j)$, define
$$ D(x)=L(r_j). $$
Set $D(0)=L(0)$ and $D(g)=1$.

The preceding bounds imply
$$ L(r_j)-L(r_{j+1}) \le U(r_{j+1})-L(r_{j+1}). $$
Since $U$ is increasing, this proves $L\le D\le U$ on every such interval.

The resulting rational atom probabilities are:

  • $L(0)$ at $0$;
  • $L(r_{j-1})-L(r_j)$ at $r_j$ for $j\ge1$;
  • $1-L(g)$ at $g$.

They sum to one. The conditional tail probability of the index $j$ is at most
$$ \frac1a(1+\eta)^{-2(j-1)}. $$
Consequently, sequential sampling of these rational atoms terminates almost surely and has finite moments of every fixed order.

For completeness, an exact rational coin with probability $s/t$ is sampled by drawing a uniform integer from $0$ through $2^\ell-1$, where $\ell=\lceil\log_2 t\rceil$, rejecting integers at least $t$, and accepting the event that the remaining integer is less than $s$. Each batch is accepted with probability greater than $1/2$.

7. General bounds and computational costs

Let
$$ s=\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha}, \qquad H(\delta)=(1-2\delta)\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}, $$
and
$$ D(x)= \left(\frac12+x\right)\log(1+2x) + \left(\frac12-x\right)\log(1-2x). $$

Throughout the nonzero-sample regime,
$$ \max\left\{ 1+\log_2\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}, \frac{H(\delta)}{D(2s)} \right\} \le N^* \le \left\lceil \frac{1}{2s^2}\log\frac2\delta \right\rceil. $$

Upper bound

With $N$ samples, let $\widehat p$ be their empirical proportion ranking $a$ first and output
$$ \widehat\rho=\alpha\left|\widehat p-\frac12\right|. $$
The reverse triangle inequality gives
$$ |\widehat\rho-t| \le\alpha|\widehat p-p|+ct. $$
Thus $|\widehat p-p|\le s$ implies success.

The indicators are independent Bernoulli variables, so Hoeffding’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr[|\widehat p-p|>s]\le2e^{-2Ns^2}. $$
This proves the upper bound.

Relative-entropy lower bound

Choose dyadic $t>2s$ with $t<1/2$. The event that output exceeds $\epsilon$ has probability at most $\delta$ at the tie and at least $1-\delta$ at parameter $1/2+t$.

The relative entropy between their $N$-sample distributions is $ND(t)$. Passing to that event by the log-sum inequality yields
$$ ND(t)\ge H(\delta). $$
Indeed, binary relative entropy increases in its first probability and decreases in its second when the first is larger, so its minimum over these probability constraints occurs at $1-\delta$ and $\delta$.

Dyadic alternatives can approach $2s$ from above. Continuity therefore gives
$$ N\ge\frac{H(\delta)}{D(2s)}. $$
This use of arbitrarily close alternatives is valid because society encoding lengths are unrestricted.

The elementary relative-entropy bound by chi-squared divergence gives $D(x)\le4x^2$. For $\delta\le1/4$,
$$ H(\delta)\ge\frac14\log\frac1\delta. $$
Hence, uniformly in that confidence range,
$$ N^*(c,\epsilon,\delta) = \Theta\left( \frac{(1-c)^2}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta \right) $$
whenever $\epsilon<(1-c)/4$.

This order does not extend uniformly to $\delta<1/2$. For example, take
$$ \alpha=2^{-j},\qquad c=1-\alpha,\qquad \epsilon=\alpha^2,\qquad \delta=\frac12-\alpha^2, \qquad j\ge3. $$
Here $h=2\alpha$ and $\delta\ge d_2(h)$, so the exact minimum is two samples, while
$$ \frac{\alpha^2}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta =\alpha^{-2}\log\frac1\delta\longrightarrow\infty. $$

Running times and representation dependence

Let $B$ be the total binary encoding length of $c,\epsilon,\delta$.

A sufficient budget can be selected using rational arithmetic: compute
$$ M=\min\{j:2^j\ge2/\delta\}, \qquad \overline N=\left\lceil\frac{M}{2s^2}\right\rceil. $$
Since $M\ge\log(2/\delta)$, this budget satisfies the upper bound’s requirement. Its computation takes time polynomial in $B$.

For a supplied budget $N$, the empirical estimator takes
$$ O\!\left( N\log(N+1)+\operatorname{poly}(B+\log(N+1)) \right) $$
bit operations and $O(B+\log(N+1))$ working bits.

The rational implementations in Section 6 use exactly two or three ballots and expected time polynomial in $B$ and $\eta^{-1}$.

For the exact offline feasibility procedure, each budget produces $O(N)$ rational polynomials of degree at most $2N$, with coefficient encoding lengths
$$ O\bigl(N(B+\log(N+1))\bigr). $$
Exact root isolation gives a terminating decision procedure; no sharper bit complexity bound is asserted here.

All sampling and estimation costs exclude the oracle’s sample-generation cost. Here $m=2$ and $\tau\le2$. The estimators never read the hidden society representation, so their sample requirements and computation have no dependence on its encoding length $L$.

The continuous-model bounds above are proved directly; the related [finite-election sampling results](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/162.pdf) provide context rather than an exact solution to this oracle problem.

8. Remaining open region

The exact integer minimum is not determined here when
$$ 0<\epsilon<\frac{(1-c)^2}{8}. $$
In that region, the proof establishes $N^*\ge4$ and the general quantitative bounds, but the overlap-based feasibility criterion does not apply: intermediate parameters require simultaneous control of both tails of the output distribution.
