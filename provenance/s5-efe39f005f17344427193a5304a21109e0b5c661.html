Theorem 6.1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite bipartite graph of worker and firm types with rational type masses \(\mu_v\), rational contract capacities \(b_e\), and finite descriptions of continuous substitutable, size-monotone choice functions, can one construct in time polynomial in the support size, description size, and bit length a standard stable-allocation instance with at most \(2|E|\) vertices? Require aggregate output capacities to equal \(b_e\), output quota totals to equal the common stable contract mass at each type, and every stable mass allocation \(x\) to map to a stable output allocation whose contract masses sum to \(x_e\), with the map preserving lattice order and respecting an order-saturating decomposition.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) worker and firm types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_v\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses need not sum to one.
Let \(G=(W\sqcup F,E)\) be a finite simple bipartite graph without isolated vertices, with \(m=|E|\) and nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(b_e\). Write \(E_v\) for the incident edges and \(B_v=\prod_{e\in E_v}[0,b_e]\).
Each continuous choice function \(C_v:B_v\to B_v\) is given by a rational piecewise-affine circuit using affine, minimum, and maximum gates. Let \(D\) be the total circuit size and \(L\) the total binary input length. The input promises \(0\le C_v(z)\le z\), \(\sum_e C_v(z)_e\le\mu_v\), and, whenever \(z\ge z'\), substitutability \(C_v(z)\wedge z'\le C_v(z')\) and size monotonicity \(\sum_e C_v(z)_e\ge\sum_e C_v(z')_e\); vector comparisons, minimum, and maximum are coordinatewise.
A real allocation \(x\in\prod_{e\in E}[0,b_e]\) is acceptable if \(C_v(x_v)=x_v\) for every type. An edge \(e\in E_v\) is interesting to \(v\) if some \(t\in(x_e,b_e]\) satisfies \(C_v(x_v+(t-x_e)\mathbf1_e)_e>x_e\). It blocks if interesting to both endpoints; an acceptable allocation is stable if no edge blocks.
For stable allocations define \(x\preceq_F y\) by \(C_f(x_f\vee y_f)=y_f\) for every \(f\in F\), and let \(\theta_v=\sum_{e\in E_v}x_e\), which is independent of the stable allocation chosen.
A required splitting is a finite bipartite multigraph \(\widehat G\) with at most \(2m\) vertices, a surjective graph homomorphism \(h:\widehat G\to G\) preserving worker and firm sides, nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(\widehat b\) and quotas \(\widehat q\), and strict preference orders at its vertices. Require \(\sum_{a\in h^{-1}(e)}\widehat b_a=b_e\) and \(\sum_{\nu\in h^{-1}(v)}\widehat q_\nu=\theta_v\), allowing zero quotas.
Also supply a strict linear order \(\triangleleft_e\) on each edge fiber. Define \(\kappa_x\) uniquely by \(0\le\kappa_x\le\widehat b\), \(\sum_{a\in h^{-1}(e)}\kappa_x(a)=x_e\), and order saturation: \(a\triangleleft_e a'\) and \(\kappa_x(a')>0\) imply \(\kappa_x(a)=\widehat b_a\). Every stable real \(x\) must map to a standard Baiou–Balinski stable allocation, and \(x\preceq_F y\) must imply \(\kappa_x\preceq_{\widehat F}\kappa_y\), using the standard quota-and-strict-order choice functions for the output order.

Does every input admit the required splitting? Is constructing such a splitting solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,D,L\), or is this construction problem NP-hard?

For integer capacities and integer choice functions, an order-preserving, order-saturating stable-allocation splitting with at most \(2m\) vertices exists; this result does not establish polynomial time in binary input length. [Karzanov](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24581v1)
For continuous substitutable, size-monotone choices, stable allocations exist, form a distributive lattice, and have the same total allocation at each type. These structural results do not settle the specified splitting construction. [Alkan–Gale](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/id/eprint/266/1/3011800000835.pdf)

  • Made existence of the continuous splitting an explicit question because the discrete existence theorem does not establish continuous existence.

Answer

Not every input admits the required splitting. The counterexample below has \(m=6\) edges and \(\tau=5\) types. In fact, it admits no finite splitting satisfying the stability and quota requirements, even if the bound of \(2m\) vertices is removed.

Consequently, an algorithm that constructs the required splitting on every stated input cannot exist. The proposed polynomial-time-versus-NP-hard dichotomy presupposes existence of an output, which fails here.

1. A necessary property of standard stable allocations

Consider a finite quota-and-strict-preference allocation instance. Write \(Q_\nu\) for the quota at vertex \(\nu\), and suppose an allocation \(k\) fills every quota:

\( \sum_{a\ni\nu}k(a)=Q_\nu. \)

Call an edge \(a\) fractional if \(0<k(a)<\widehat b_a\).

Lemma. If \(k\) is stable, every fractional edge is the least preferred positive-allocation edge at at least one endpoint. Consequently, for every collection \(A\) of fractional edges,

\( |A|\leq |V(A)|, \)

where \(V(A)\) is the set of their endpoints.

Proof. Fix a fractional edge \(a\) incident to \(\nu\). If a less preferred edge \(c\) has \(k(c)>0\), choose

\( 0<\varepsilon<\min\{\widehat b_a-k(a),\,k(c)\}. \)

Offering an additional \(\varepsilon\) on \(a\) makes the standard choice function retain an additional \(\varepsilon\) on \(a\), displacing allocation from less preferred edges. Thus \(a\) is interesting to \(\nu\).

Conversely, suppose no less preferred incident edge has positive allocation. Since the quota is full, the total allocation on edges preferred to \(a\) is \(Q_\nu-k(a)\). Increasing the offer on \(a\) therefore leaves its chosen amount equal to \(k(a)\). Hence \(a\) is not interesting to \(\nu\).

Stability requires a fractional edge to be uninteresting at some endpoint. At that endpoint it must therefore be the least preferred positive-allocation edge. Strict preferences give each vertex at most one such edge. Assigning each edge of \(A\) to an endpoint where it is uninteresting gives an injection into \(V(A)\), proving the inequality. \(\square\)

2. The input instance

Let \(G=K_{2,3}\), with workers \(w_1,w_2\), firms \(f_1,f_2,f_3\), and edges \(e_{ij}=w_if_j\).

Set the capacities to

\( b_{11}=b_{12}=b_{21}=b_{22}=1,\qquad b_{13}=b_{23}=2. \)

Set the masses to

\( \mu_{w_1}=\mu_{w_2}=2,\qquad \mu_{f_1}=\mu_{f_2}=1,\qquad \mu_{f_3}=2. \)

All masses are positive integers, and the graph is simple and has no isolated vertices.

All vertices except \(w_1\) use standard quota-and-strict-order choice functions, with quota equal to their mass. Their preferences, listed from best to worst, are:

| Vertex | Preference order |
|---|---|
| \(w_2\) | \(e_{21},e_{22},e_{23}\) |
| \(f_1\) | \(e_{11},e_{21}\) |
| \(f_2\) | \(e_{12},e_{22}\) |
| \(f_3\) | \(e_{23},e_{13}\) |

For \(w_1\), write its offer vector as \(z=(z_1,z_2,z_3)\), corresponding to \(e_{11},e_{12},e_{13}\). Define

\( H=\min\{z_3,2\},\qquad R=2-H, \)

and set

\( C_{w_1}(z)_3=H, \)

\( C_{w_1}(z)_1=\min\{z_1,\max\{R-z_2,R/2\}\}, \)

\( C_{w_1}(z)_2=\min\{z_2,\max\{R-z_1,R/2\}\}. \)

Thus \(w_1\) first takes the third edge and then divides its remaining quota between the first two edges as equally as their offers permit.

These formulas are constant-size rational piecewise-affine circuits.

3. Verification of the choice axioms

The standard choices satisfy the promised axioms. For completeness, under a strict order their coordinate formulas are

\( C(z)_i=\min\{z_i,\max\{0,q-\sum_{j\text{ preferred to }i}z_j\}\}. \)

Increasing one offer leaves more preferred choices unchanged and can only decrease less preferred choices. The choice on the increased coordinate has the form \(\min\{z_i,K\}\), with \(K\) independent of that coordinate. This proves substitutability for a single-coordinate increase, and hence for arbitrary coordinatewise increases by iteration. Their total choice is \(\min\{q,\sum_i z_i\}\), proving size monotonicity.

We now verify the nonstandard choice at \(w_1\).

Bounds, continuity, and size monotonicity

Since \(R\geq0\), every coordinate lies between zero and its offer. The circuit is continuous.

Let \(c_1,c_2\) denote its first two chosen amounts. We claim

\( c_1+c_2=\min\{R,z_1+z_2\}. \)

If both offers are at least \(R/2\), both choices equal \(R/2\). Otherwise, suppose \(z_2<R/2\). Then

\( c_2=z_2,\qquad c_1=\min\{z_1,R-z_2\}, \)

which proves the claim in this case. The remaining case follows by exchanging the coordinates.

Consequently,

\( \sum_i C_{w_1}(z)_i =H+\min\{2-H,z_1+z_2\} =\min\{2,z_1+z_2+z_3\}. \)

The last equality follows separately from \(z_3\leq2\), when \(H=z_3\), and \(z_3\geq2\), when \(H=2\). Thus the mass bound and size monotonicity hold.

Substitutability

Consider increasing just \(z_1\). The first choice is \(\min\{z_1,K\}\), with \(K=\max\{R-z_2,R/2\}\) fixed. The second choice can only decrease, and the third is unchanged. Therefore, if \(z'\) is the increased offer,

\( C_{w_1}(z')\wedge z\leq C_{w_1}(z). \)

The argument for increasing \(z_2\) is symmetric.

When \(z_3\) increases, \(H\) has the form \(\min\{z_3,2\}\), while \(R\) decreases. Each of the first two choices is nondecreasing as a function of \(R\), so those choices can only decrease. The same substitutability inequality follows.

For arbitrary \(z'\geq z\), increase the three coordinates successively. Taking coordinatewise minima with the original \(z\) at each step propagates the single-coordinate inequalities and gives

\( C_{w_1}(z')\wedge z\leq C_{w_1}(z). \)

Thus the instance satisfies every input promise.

4. A continuous chain of stable allocations

For \(s\in[0,1]\), define \(x(s)\) by

| Worker | \(f_1\) | \(f_2\) | \(f_3\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(w_1\) | \(s\) | \(s\) | \(2-2s\) |
| \(w_2\) | \(1-s\) | \(1-s\) | \(2s\) |

The capacity bounds hold. Both workers receive total allocation \(2\), and the firms receive totals \(1,1,2\).

At \(w_1\), the formulas give \(H=2-2s\) and \(R=2s\), so the chosen amounts on the first two edges are both \(s\). At every other vertex, the offer fills its quota exactly and is therefore accepted. Hence \(x(s)\) is acceptable.

Every edge is uninteresting to at least one endpoint:

  • At \(w_1\), increasing either \(e_{11}\) or \(e_{12}\), with the other coordinates fixed, leaves its chosen amount equal to \(s\).
  • At \(f_1\) and \(f_2\), respectively, \(e_{21}\) and \(e_{22}\) are the less preferred edges of a full quota.
  • At \(f_3\), \(e_{13}\) is the less preferred edge of a full quota.
  • At \(w_2\), \(e_{23}\) is the least preferred edge of a full quota.

Thus every \(x(s)\) is stable.

Moreover, if \(s\leq t\), then \(x(s)\preceq_F x(t)\). At each firm, the amount on its preferred edge increases, and the amount on its other edge decreases by the same amount. Applying its choice function to the coordinatewise maximum therefore selects exactly \(x(t)_f\).

The required totals are consequently

\( \theta_{w_1}=\theta_{w_2}=2,\qquad \theta_{f_1}=\theta_{f_2}=1,\qquad \theta_{f_3}=2. \)

Here we use the unisizeness property stipulated in the question, established in [Alkan–Gale](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/id/eprint/266/1/3011800000835.pdf).

5. No finite order-saturating splitting can represent this family

Suppose, for contradiction, that a finite required splitting exists. Write

\( k(s)=\kappa_{x(s)}. \)

By the required property, every \(k(s)\) is a standard stable allocation.

Every split vertex has a full quota

For an original vertex \(v\), allocation feasibility gives

\( \sum_{a\ni\nu}k(s)(a)\leq\widehat q_\nu \quad\text{for every }\nu\in h^{-1}(v). \)

Summing over its copies yields

\( \sum_{\nu\in h^{-1}(v)}\sum_{a\ni\nu}k(s)(a) =\sum_{e\in E_v}x(s)_e =\theta_v =\sum_{\nu\in h^{-1}(v)}\widehat q_\nu. \)

All quota slacks are nonnegative and sum to zero. Therefore every split vertex fills its quota, for every \(s\).

On a suitable interval, each original edge has exactly one fractional copy

List an edge fiber in its saturation order. Its cumulative capacities give finitely many thresholds. Whenever \(x_e\) lies strictly between consecutive distinct thresholds, its lift has exactly one fractional edge; earlier edges are saturated and later edges have zero allocation.

Every coordinate \(x(s)_e\) has nonzero slope. Hence it equals a fiber threshold at only finitely many values of \(s\). Choose a nonempty open interval \(I\subset(0,1)\) avoiding all such values for all six fibers.

Throughout \(I\), each original edge \(e_{ij}\) has a fixed unique fractional copy \(a_{ij}\). Its lifted allocation has the same slope as \(x(s)_{ij}\). All other lifted allocations are constant. The six slopes are

\( d_{11}=d_{12}=1,\quad d_{13}=-2,\qquad d_{21}=d_{22}=-1,\quad d_{23}=2. \)

The six fractional edges have exactly five endpoints

Because every split quota is full throughout \(I\), the slopes of the allocations incident to each split vertex sum to zero.

At copies of \(w_1\), the only nonzero incident slopes are \(1,1,-2\). No nonempty proper subset of these three slopes sums to zero. Therefore all three edges \(a_{11},a_{12},a_{13}\) must meet the same copy of \(w_1\).

The same reasoning with \(-1,-1,2\) forces \(a_{21},a_{22},a_{23}\) to meet the same copy of \(w_2\).

At copies of \(f_1\), the nonzero slopes are \(1,-1\), so \(a_{11},a_{21}\) must meet the same copy of \(f_1\). Likewise, \(a_{12},a_{22}\) meet one copy of \(f_2\), and \(a_{13},a_{23}\) meet one copy of \(f_3\).

These five copies are distinct because they map to five distinct original vertices. Thus the six fractional edges have exactly five endpoints.

For any \(s\in I\), the lemma from Section 1 now gives the contradiction

\( 6\leq5. \)

Therefore no finite required splitting exists. The proof did not use the vertex bound, rationality of the output parameters, or order preservation: stability, quota sums, finiteness, and order saturation already contradict one another.

6. Consequences and relation to the integer theorem

This example does not contradict [Karzanov’s integer splitting theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24581v1). Its nonstandard choice is not integer-valued on integer offers: at \(z=(1,1,1)\), it returns \((1/2,1/2,1)\).

The universal construction requested in the question is therefore impossible. This does not classify modified problems such as deciding whether a particular input admits a splitting, or constructing one under an additional existence promise; those are separate computational questions.
