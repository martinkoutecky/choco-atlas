Theorem 14 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(q\in(0,1]\) and happy mass \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), consider finite-support societies with type masses \(\mu_t\), a happy-or-sad label for each type, and symmetric interaction intensities \(w_{tt'}\in[0,1]\), where every type has total interaction mass \(\sum_{t'}\mu_{t'}w_{tt'}=q\). Determine whether all, none, or both of the regular interaction structures with happy mass \(\alpha\) have more than half the population in types whose interaction mass with happy types exceeds \(q/2\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\), and a happy-or-sad label for each type. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also contains binary rationals \(q\in(0,1]\) and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), with \(H\subseteq T\) the happy types and \(\sum_{t\in H}\mu_t=\alpha\). The support, masses, and labels remain fixed.
An admissible interaction structure is a symmetric real matrix \(W=(w_{tt'})\) with \(0\leq w_{tt'}\leq1\) and \(\sum_{t'\in T}\mu_{t'}w_{tt'}=q\) for every \(t\). Diagonal entries describe interaction within a type and are unrestricted within these bounds; individual self-interaction has zero mass.
Type \(t\) is a proponent exactly when \(\sum_{t'\in H}\mu_{t'}w_{tt'}>q/2\). A structure approves exactly when the total mass of proponents exceeds \(1/2\); ties at either stage count against approval.
Classify the admissible structures as uniformly approving, uniformly disapproving, or mixed, according as all approve, none approve, or both approving and disapproving structures exist.
Is this classification problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(\tau\) and the total input bit length \(L\); the number of alternatives is fixed at \(m=2\).
For finite societies with odd population size \(n\), odd degree \(d\leq n\), and \(h\) happy agents, an explicit arithmetic characterization classifies all undirected regular graphs with one loop per vertex, counted once, in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.03410v4)
No result settling this exact continuous classification problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to 1 and required alpha to be a binary rational equal to the sum of happy type masses.
  • Resolved the ambiguous quantifiers by fixing the supplied support, masses, and labels and varying the interaction matrix; the matrix is not supplied as input.
  • Allowed real interaction intensities, since only the input quantities were required to be rational.
  • Interpreted types as nonatomic population classes: diagonal entries measure within-type interaction rather than individual loops, whose mass is zero.
  • Retained strict majorities at both stages and assigned ties against approval.
  • Used two alternatives, the proposal and the status quo, so m is fixed at 2.

Result

The classification problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(q=1/4\), there is exactly one happy type, and every mass is dyadic.

More precisely, deciding whether an approving structure exists is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, deciding whether the classification is mixed is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and deciding whether it is uniformly disapproving is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete.

An exact classification algorithm takes \(2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) bit operations. Thus the problem is polynomial-time solvable for fixed \(\tau\), but a polynomial-time algorithm for unrestricted \(\tau\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). Throughout, \(m=2\).

1. Conservation of happy interaction mass

For an admissible structure, define the happy interaction mass received by type \(t\) as

\(\displaystyle a_t=\sum_{h\in H}\mu_h w_{th}.\)

Symmetry and the row constraints imply

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t =\sum_{h\in H}\mu_h\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t w_{ht} =\sum_{h\in H}\mu_h q =\alpha q.\)

Every \(a_t\) is nonnegative, and type \(t\) is a proponent precisely when \(a_t>q/2\).

Suppose now that there is exactly one happy type, denoted \(u\), with mass \(\alpha\). Let \(b\) be the total mass of sad proponents. Two necessary conditions follow.

  • If \(u\) is a proponent, conservation gives

\(\alpha q>(\alpha+b)q/2\), because every proponent receives strictly more than \(q/2\) happy interaction mass. Hence \(b<\alpha\). Approval additionally requires \(\alpha+b>1/2\), so
\(\displaystyle \frac12-\alpha<b<\alpha.\)

  • If \(u\) is not a proponent and the structure approves, then \(b>1/2\). Conservation gives \(\alpha q>bq/2\), since the sad proponents have positive total mass. Hence

\(\displaystyle \frac12<b<2\alpha.\)

These restrictions hold for every admissible real matrix, without any assumption that its entries are rational or constant on groups.

2. Polynomial reduction from Partition

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Partition problem: given positive binary integers \(x_1,\ldots,x_r\), determine whether some subset sums to half their total. Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is a classical result of [Karp](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Making the total a power of two

Set \(S=\sum_i x_i\). Choose the smallest power of two \(M\) satisfying \(M>6S\), and put

\(\displaystyle B=\frac M2-S.\)

Consider the padded integer list

\(\displaystyle y_1,\ldots,y_{r+2}=2x_1,\ldots,2x_r,B,B.\)

Its total is \(M\). A subset sums to \(M/2\) exactly when the original Partition instance is positive:

  • A subset containing neither copy of \(B\) has sum at most \(2S<M/2\).
  • A subset containing both copies has sum at least \(2B=M-2S>M/2\).
  • A subset containing exactly one copy sums to \(M/2\) precisely when its selected original entries satisfy \(\sum_{i\in I}2x_i=S\), equivalently \(\sum_{i\in I}x_i=S/2\).

Also, \(M\leq12S\), so this preprocessing has polynomial bit complexity.

Constructing the society

Define

\(\displaystyle g=\frac1{2M},\qquad \delta=\frac1{16M}=\frac g8.\)

Create the following distinct types:

  • one happy type \(u\), of mass \(\alpha=1/4+\delta\);
  • \(r+2\) sad item types, of masses \(\mu_i=y_i/(2M)=y_i g\);
  • one additional sad type \(v\), of mass \(\gamma=1/4-\delta\).

Finally, set \(q=1/4\).

The item types have total mass \(1/2\), while \(\alpha+\gamma=1/2\). Consequently all masses sum to \(1\). They are positive dyadic rationals: \(M\geq8\), and thus \(\delta<1/4\).

The support size is \(\tau=r+4\), and the output length is \(L=O((r+4)\log M)\). The construction is therefore polynomial in the original input length.

An item subset has mass \(1/4\) exactly when its integer entries sum to \(M/2\), and hence exactly when the original Partition instance is positive.

3. Approval exists exactly when Partition is positive

Necessity

Suppose an admissible structure approves, and let \(b\) again be the mass of its sad proponents.

First suppose the happy type is a proponent. Section 1 gives

\(\displaystyle \frac14-\delta<b<\frac14+\delta.\)

If the additional sad type \(v\) is not a proponent, then \(b\) is an item-subset mass and therefore belongs to \(g\mathbb Z\). Since \(1/4=(M/2)g\) also belongs to \(g\mathbb Z\), and \(\delta<g\), the only possible value in this interval is \(b=1/4\).

If \(v\) is a proponent, write \(b=\gamma+s\), where \(s\) is an item-subset mass. The interval above becomes

\(\displaystyle 0<s<2\delta.\)

This is impossible: \(s\) is a multiple of \(g\), whereas \(2\delta=g/4<g\).

Now suppose the happy type is not a proponent. Section 1 gives

\(\displaystyle \frac12<b<\frac12+2\delta.\)

Without \(v\), sad proponents have mass at most the total item mass \(1/2\), which is insufficient. With \(v\), write \(b=\gamma+s\). Then

\(\displaystyle \frac14+\delta<s<\frac14+3\delta.\)

Again this is impossible. The number \(s\) is a multiple of \(g\), and the displayed interval lies strictly between the consecutive grid points \(1/4\) and \(1/4+g\), because \(3\delta=3g/8<g\).

Thus every approving structure supplies an item subset of mass exactly \(1/4\). The original Partition instance must be positive.

Sufficiency

Conversely, suppose an item subset \(I\) has mass \(1/4\). Let \(C\) consist of the happy type together with the selected item types. Its mass and its complement’s mass are

\(\displaystyle p=\mu(C)=\frac12+\delta,\qquad c=1-p=\frac12-\delta.\)

Define the symmetric matrix by

\(\displaystyle w_{tt'}= \begin{cases} q/p,&t,t'\in C,\\ q/c,&t,t'\notin C,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\)

Both \(p\) and \(c\) exceed \(q=1/4\), so all entries lie in \([0,1]\). A row belonging to \(C\) has weighted sum \(p(q/p)=q\); a row outside \(C\) has weighted sum \(c(q/c)=q\). Thus the matrix is admissible, including its diagonal entries.

Every type in \(C\) receives happy interaction mass \(\alpha q/p\). Since

\(\displaystyle 2\alpha-p=\delta>0,\)

we have \(\alpha q/p>q/2\). Every type outside \(C\) receives zero happy interaction mass. Hence the proponents are exactly \(C\), whose mass \(p=1/2+\delta\) strictly exceeds \(1/2\). The structure approves.

This proves the required equivalence, with strict inequalities at both voting stages.

The classification distinguishes the instances

Every constructed instance also has a disapproving structure: take \(w_{tt'}=q\) for every pair of types. Each row has weighted sum \(q\), and every type receives happy interaction mass \(q\alpha<q/2\), because \(\alpha<1/2\). There are no proponents.

Consequently:

  • positive Partition instances produce mixed societies;
  • negative Partition instances produce uniformly disapproving societies.

This polynomial reduction proves hardness of the requested classification.

4. Exact algorithm and complexity memberships

Write \(\mu(U)=\sum_{t\in U}\mu_t\) for any subset \(U\subseteq T\). Rational linear programming is solvable in time polynomial in the dimensions and binary encoding length; this is the theorem of [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

Testing existence of approval

Enumerate subsets \(P\subseteq T\) with \(\mu(P)>1/2\). For each, maximize a variable \(\varepsilon\), subject to:

  • symmetry, bounds, and all admissibility row equations;
  • \(0\leq\varepsilon\leq1\);
  • \(a_t\geq q/2+\varepsilon\) for every \(t\in P\).

An approving structure exists exactly when some program has optimum strictly positive.

Indeed, a positive optimum makes every member of \(P\) a proponent. Conversely, in an approving structure, take \(P\) to be its actual proponents. Since this set is finite and each margin \(a_t-q/2\) is positive, their minimum is positive, giving a feasible positive \(\varepsilon\).

Testing existence of disapproval

Enumerate subsets \(D\subseteq T\) with \(\mu(D)\geq1/2\). For each, test feasibility of the admissibility constraints together with

\(\displaystyle a_t\leq q/2\quad\text{for every }t\in D.\)

Feasibility guarantees nonproponent mass at least \(1/2\), hence disapproval. Conversely, any disapproving structure supplies such a set by taking all its nonproponents. The non-strict inequalities correctly include local ties.

Each program has \(O(\tau^2)\) variables and constraints, with encoding length polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). There are at most \(2^{\tau+1}\) programs altogether, giving the claimed running time

\(\displaystyle 2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L).\)

The admissible family is always nonempty, because the constant matrix \(w_{tt'}=q\) is admissible. Thus the two existence answers determine exactly one of the three classifications.

Guessing \(P\) or \(D\) and running its polynomial-time linear-programming test shows that both existence questions belong to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Mixed classification belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\) by guessing both sets. Together with the reduction, this proves the completeness statements in the opening section.

5. Relation to the finite characterization

The [cited finite result](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.03410v4#S3.SS3) classifies configurations by aggregate integer parameters. Here, proponent status applies to an entire listed type, so selected proponent masses are subset sums of fixed input masses. The reduction exploits exactly this restriction. Expanding a dyadic mass into individually behaving agents would change that restriction and can require exponentially many agents relative to \(L\).
