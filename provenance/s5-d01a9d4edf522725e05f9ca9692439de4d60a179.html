The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \A=[m], \E=2^{\A}, an unknown symmetric threshold function \tau:\M_k\times 2^{\E}\to\{0,\dots,k+1\}, and oracle access to \X_\tau^\infty(\mu)=\{W\in\M_k:\mu(G)<\tau(W,G)/k\ \forall G\subseteq\E\} for societies \mu\in\Delta(\E), determine the minimum number of queries needed to output a hypothesis with error at most \varepsilon and confidence at least \1-\delta, and characterize that query complexity via \operatorname{Ndim}.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\geq1\), \(1\leq k\leq m\), \(\mathcal A=[m]\), \(\mathcal E=2^{\mathcal A}\), and \(\mathcal M_k=\{W\subseteq\mathcal A:|W|=k\}\).
A society \(\mu\) is a probability distribution on \(\mathcal E\), given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be its encoding length and \(\mathcal S=\Delta(\mathcal E)\cap\mathbb Q^{\mathcal E}\).
A symmetric threshold function \(\theta:\mathcal M_k\times2^{\mathcal E}\to\{0,\ldots,k+1\}\) satisfies \(\theta(\sigma W,\sigma G)=\theta(W,G)\) for every candidate permutation \(\sigma\), where \(\sigma G=\{\sigma A:A\in G\}\).
Define \(\mu(G)=\sum_{A\in G}\mu(A)\) and \(X_\theta^\infty(\mu)=\{W\in\mathcal M_k:\mu(G)<\theta(W,G)/k\text{ for every }G\subseteq\mathcal E\}\); empty outputs are allowed. Let \(\mathcal F_{m,k}^\infty\) contain all these mappings.
Use realizable, distribution-free, proper PAC learning: an unknown distribution \(\Pi\) on \(\mathcal S\) supplies independent societies, and each oracle call returns the complete set \(X_\theta^\infty(\mu)\). Queries are these random examples; the hypothesis \(h\) belongs to \(\mathcal F_{m,k}^\infty\).

Any running times are stated in \(m,k,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta)\), with \(\tau,L\) the maximum example sizes.

Uniform matching dimension bounds and PAC query complexity

1. Proven results and scope

Write
$$ \mathcal F=\mathcal F_{m,k}^{\infty},\qquad d_{m,k}=\operatorname{Ndim}(\mathcal F), $$
and define
$$ N=2^m,\qquad H=S_k\times S_{m-k},\qquad h=|H|=k!(m-k)!, $$
$$ B_N=\binom{N}{N/2},\qquad D_{m,k}=\frac{2^{2^m}}{2^{m/2}k!(m-k)!}. $$

The following bounds hold with absolute positive constants $c,C$, uniformly over $m\geq1$ and $1\leq k\leq m$:
$$ cD_{m,k}\leq d_{m,k}\leq CD_{m,k}. $$
This closes the factor-$k$ gap in the supplied notes. More precisely, uniformly over $k$ as $m\to\infty$,
$$ d_{m,k}\leq(1+o(1))\frac{B_N}{h}, $$
while, for every $k\geq2$,
$$ d_{m,k}\geq\frac{B_N}{2h}. $$
A separate construction gives a constant-factor matching lower bound for $k=1$.

For proper, realizable PAC learning, throughout $0<\varepsilon,\delta<1$,
$$ q_{m,k}(\varepsilon,\delta) \leq \left\lceil \frac{4\bigl(d_{m,k}+\ln(1/\delta)\bigr)}{\varepsilon} \right\rceil. $$
For $0<\varepsilon\leq1/16$ and $0<\delta\leq1/8$, matching lower bounds give
$$ q_{m,k}(\varepsilon,\delta) = \Theta\!\left( \frac{d_{m,k}+\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon} \right) = \Theta\!\left( \frac{D_{m,k}+\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon} \right), $$
with absolute constants uniform in $m,k$.

The same expression does not characterize the minimum throughout the entire parameter square. For example, when $k=m$ and $\varepsilon>1/2$, a proper learner uses at most
$$ \left\lceil \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2(\varepsilon-1/2)^2} \right\rceil $$
examples, independently of $m$. Thus the unrestricted parameter question requires additional analysis. This document proves the uniform dimension bounds and the matching PAC rate in the stated small-error, high-confidence regime; it does not determine the minimum for every $\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)$.

2. Definitions and normalization

A set of distinct societies $\{\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_t\}$ is Natarajan-shattered if there are label sets
$$ A_i,B_i\subseteq\mathcal M_k,\qquad A_i\neq B_i, $$
such that every independent choice between $A_i$ and $B_i$ is realized by some member of $\mathcal F$. The Natarajan dimension is the largest possible $t$.

The class is finite: a threshold function has a finite domain and finitely many possible values. In particular, $d_{m,k}$ is finite.

The rational-society class has exactly the same dimension as the normalized finite-profile class with these thresholds. The cited paper uses the corresponding finite-profile inequalities and symmetry condition in [Definitions 4 and 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03082v1).

Here is the normalization argument explicitly. For a nonempty profile $P$, define
$$ \mu_P(A)=\frac{\operatorname{Hist}(P)_A}{|P|}. $$
Its finite-profile inequality
$$ \operatorname{Hist}(P)(G)<\frac{\theta(W,G)|P|}{k} $$
is equivalent to
$$ \mu_P(G)<\frac{\theta(W,G)}k. $$
Conversely, clearing the denominators of the masses of any rational society produces integer multiplicities defining a nonempty finite profile.

Therefore a shattered set of societies lifts to a shattered set of profiles. A shattered set of profiles also projects to a shattered set of societies: two profiles in such a set cannot have the same normalization, because every hypothesis would then give them identical outputs, contradicting independent choices between their distinct witness labels. Hence the two dimensions agree.

This equivalence is information-theoretic. Clearing denominators may produce a profile exponentially larger than its binary encoding.

We will also use
$$ d_{m,k}\geq2. $$
Indeed, consider the pure societies concentrated on $\varnothing$ and on $\mathcal A$. Independently set the thresholds for the two groups $\{\varnothing\}$ and $\{\mathcal A\}$ to either $k$ or $k+1$, identically for all committees; set all remaining thresholds to $k+1$. These assignments are symmetric. Each pure society independently receives either $\varnothing$ or $\mathcal M_k$.

3. A proper closure learner

3.1. Intersection closure

For symmetric thresholds $\theta,\theta'$, their coordinatewise minimum is symmetric and satisfies
$$ X_{\min(\theta,\theta')}^\infty(\mu) = X_\theta^\infty(\mu)\cap X_{\theta'}^\infty(\mu). $$
Indeed, for each committee and group, satisfying both strict inequalities is equivalent to satisfying the inequality with the smaller threshold.

The class therefore is closed under finite pointwise intersections. It contains the constant full-output mapping, obtained by setting every threshold to $k+1$.

Given a realizable sample
$$ S=((\mu_1,Y_1),\ldots,(\mu_n,Y_n)), $$
define
$$ h_S= \bigcap \left\{ f\in\mathcal F: Y_i\subseteq f(\mu_i)\text{ for every }i \right\}. $$
The intersected family is nonempty because it contains the constant full-output mapping. Finiteness and intersection closure imply $h_S\in\mathcal F$.

If $f^\star$ is the target, it belongs to this family. Consequently,
$$ h_S(\mu)\subseteq f^\star(\mu) $$
at every society. At each training society,
$$ Y_i\subseteq h_S(\mu_i)\subseteq f^\star(\mu_i)=Y_i. $$
Thus $h_S$ is proper and consistent.

For a candidate-permutation orbit $p$ of pairs $(W,G)$, its threshold representation is
$$ \widehat\theta_S(p) = \max\left( \{0\}\cup \left\{ \lfloor k\mu_i(G)\rfloor+1: W\in Y_i,\ (W,G)\in p \right\} \right). $$
To justify the formula, an integer threshold admits $W$ at $\mu_i$ precisely when, for every $G$,
$$ \theta(W,G)\geq\lfloor k\mu_i(G)\rfloor+1. $$
Symmetry requires the same threshold throughout each orbit. Hence the displayed maximum is the coordinatewise smallest admissible threshold, and
$$ h_S=X_{\widehat\theta_S}^{\infty}. $$

Adding examples increases the closure hypothesis pointwise:
$$ T\subseteq S\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_T\subseteq h_S. $$
This follows because adding constraints reduces the family being intersected.

3.2. A defining subsample has size at most the dimension

Choose an inclusion-minimal subsample $C\subseteq S$ such that $h_C=h_S$. Repeated societies have identical labels in a realizable sample, so minimality removes repetitions.

For every $i\in C$,
$$ h_{C\setminus\{i\}}\neq h_C, \qquad h_{C\setminus\{i\}}\subseteq h_C. $$
Moreover,
$$ B_i:=h_{C\setminus\{i\}}(\mu_i)\subsetneq Y_i. $$
Otherwise $h_{C\setminus\{i\}}$ would satisfy every positive constraint defining $h_C$. The definition of $h_C$ would then give the reverse containment, contradicting minimality.

At every other example in $C$, the leave-one-out hypothesis equals its training label. Therefore, for any $I\subseteq C$, the proper hypothesis
$$ h_C\cap\bigcap_{i\in I}h_{C\setminus\{i\}} $$
has label $B_i$ when $i\in I$ and label $Y_i$ otherwise. This Natarajan-shatters $C$, proving
$$ |C|\leq d_{m,k}. $$

3.3. The PAC upper bound

Put $d=d_{m,k}$ and suppose $n\geq4d$. Independently of the sample, choose a uniformly random set $I$ of $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ sample indices.

Conditional on $S$, choose a defining subsample $C$ as above. Since $|C|\leq d$,
$$ \Pr(C\subseteq S_I\mid S) = \prod_{j=0}^{|C|-1} \frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-j}{n-j} \geq4^{-d}. $$
If $C\subseteq S_I$, monotonicity gives
$$ h_C\subseteq h_{S_I}\subseteq h_S=h_C, $$
so $h_{S_I}=h_S$.

Define
$$ \operatorname{err}(h) = \Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[h(\mu)\neq f^\star(\mu)]. $$
If $\operatorname{err}(h_S)>\varepsilon$ and $C\subseteq S_I$, then $h_{S_I}$ has error exceeding $\varepsilon$ but is correct on every held-out example.

Conditional on the training half, the held-out examples remain independent with distribution $\Pi$. Thus
$$ 4^{-d}\Pr(\operatorname{err}(h_S)>\varepsilon) \leq (1-\varepsilon)^{n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor} \leq e^{-n\varepsilon/2}. $$
It follows that
$$ \Pr(\operatorname{err}(h_S)>\varepsilon) \leq4^d e^{-n\varepsilon/2}. $$
The sample size
$$ n= \left\lceil \frac{4(d+\ln(1/\delta))}{\varepsilon} \right\rceil $$
satisfies both $n\geq4d$ and the desired failure bound.

No label-alphabet factor or $\ln(1/\varepsilon)$ factor is needed.

3.4. Matching PAC lower bounds

These lower bounds allow randomized learners and do not require properness.

Take $d$ shattered societies. Give one anchor society probability $1-8\varepsilon$ and each remaining society probability
$$ \frac{8\varepsilon}{d-1}. $$
Fix the anchor’s label, and choose the other witness labels independently and uniformly. Shattering ensures a realizable target for each choice.

If
$$ n\leq\frac{d-1}{32\varepsilon}, $$
the expected number of non-anchor draws is at most $(d-1)/4$. Markov’s inequality implies that, with probability at least $1/2$, at least $(d-1)/2$ non-anchor societies remain unseen.

Conditional on the observations and the learner’s randomness, every unseen witness label remains an independent fair choice. If $U$ societies remain unseen and $Z$ are predicted incorrectly, then
$$ \mathbb E[Z]\geq U/2,\qquad 0\leq Z\leq U. $$
Writing $p=\Pr(Z>U/4)$, we have
$$ U/2\leq\mathbb E[Z]\leq U/4+(3U/4)p, $$
and therefore $p\geq1/3$. On this event,
$$ \operatorname{err}(h) \geq\frac{8\varepsilon Z}{d-1} > \frac{2\varepsilon U}{d-1} \geq\varepsilon. $$
The failure probability, averaged over the random target, is at least $1/6$. Hence there is one fixed realizable target whose unconditional failure probability is at least $1/6$. This averaging argument also includes randomized learners. It proves the dimension lower bound when $\delta\leq1/8$.

For the confidence lower bound, use two shattered societies and two targets agreeing at the anchor and differing at a society of probability $2\varepsilon$. If that society is never sampled, the learner’s observations have the same distribution under the two targets. Its prediction cannot be correct for both differing labels. Averaging over the two targets gives failure probability at least
$$ \frac12(1-2\varepsilon)^n. $$
Thus uniform success requires
$$ n\geq \frac{\ln(1/(2\delta))}{-\ln(1-2\varepsilon)} = \Omega\!\left(\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right) $$
for $\varepsilon\leq1/16$ and $\delta\leq1/8$. Again, averaging supplies a fixed target attaining the lower bound.

Combining these inequalities with $d\geq2$ proves the claimed matching rate.

4. Shattering yields a stronger combinatorial restriction

Fix $W_0=\{1,\ldots,k\}$. Its candidate stabilizer is $H$. Candidate-permutation orbits of pairs $(W,G)$ correspond bijectively to $H$-orbits of groups $G\subseteq\mathcal E$: transport $W$ to $W_0$, after which the remaining ambiguity is exactly $H$.

Suppose $t\geq2$ societies are Natarajan-shattered. Orient their witness labels so that
$$ A_i\setminus B_i\neq\varnothing. $$
This is always possible because the labels differ.

The sample assigning every $\mu_i$ the label $A_i$ is realizable. Moreover, deleting any example changes its closure hypothesis. To see this, intersect a hypothesis realizing all $A_i$ with one realizing $B_i$ at $\mu_i$ and $A_j$ elsewhere. Their intersection admits every other training label but loses a committee from $A_i$ at $\mu_i$. The closure of the sample without $i$ is contained in this intersection.

For every pair orbit $p$, define
$$ z_i(p) = \max\left\{ \lfloor k\mu_i(G)\rfloor: W\in A_i,\ (W,G)\in p \right\}. $$
The maximum exists because $A_i$ is nonempty and each pair orbit has representatives with every committee.

The full-sample and leave-one-out thresholds are respectively
$$ 1+\max_jz_j(p), \qquad 1+\max_{j\neq i}z_j(p). $$
Since deletion changes the closure, some orbit $p_i$ satisfies
$$ z_i(p_i)>\max_{j\neq i}z_j(p_i). $$
Set
$$ r_i=z_i(p_i)\in\{1,\ldots,k\}. $$
The selected orbits $p_i$ are distinct.

Let $\mathscr B$ be the union of their corresponding $H$-orbits of groups.

Order pair orbits by inclusion with a common committee. If $p\preceq q$, then
$$ z_j(p)\leq z_j(q) $$
for every $j$: transport a witnessing inclusion to any representative contributing to the first maximum.

Now take $G\in\mathscr B$ from orbit $p_i$. A permutation transports a representative attaining $z_i(p_i)$ to $(W_0,G)$. Transporting $\mu_i$ by the same permutation produces a probability distribution $\nu_G$ satisfying
$$ \nu_G(G)\geq\frac{r_i}{k}. $$
For every $F\in\mathscr B$ with $F\subsetneq G$, let its selected orbit be $p_j$. Its smaller cardinality ensures $j\neq i$. Monotonicity and unique maximality give
$$ r_j=z_j(p_j)\leq z_j(p_i)<r_i. $$
Also, since $W_0$ belongs to the transported positive label,
$$ \lfloor k\nu_G(F)\rfloor\leq z_i(p_j)<r_j. $$
Consequently,
$$ \nu_G(F)<\frac{r_j}{k}\leq\frac{r_i-1}{k}. $$

This has an important consequence:

For each $G\in\mathscr B$, the family of nonempty differences
$$ > \{G\setminus F:F\in\mathscr B,\ F\subsetneq G\} > $$
contains at most $k$ pairwise disjoint members.

Indeed, suppose it contained $r_i+1$ pairwise disjoint differences $D_a=G\setminus F_a$. Since these differences are disjoint subsets of $G$,
$$ \sum_{a=1}^{r_i+1}\nu_G(F_a) = (r_i+1)\nu_G(G)-\sum_{a=1}^{r_i+1}\nu_G(D_a) \geq r_i\nu_G(G) \geq\frac{r_i^2}{k}. $$
But the preceding upper bounds imply
$$ \sum_{a=1}^{r_i+1}\nu_G(F_a) < \frac{(r_i+1)(r_i-1)}k = \frac{r_i^2-1}{k}, $$
a contradiction. The maximum disjoint collection therefore has size at most $r_i\leq k$.

Because $d_{m,k}\geq2$, bounding shattered sets with $t\geq2$ suffices; no undefined singleton leave-one-out maximum is used.

5. A counting lemma closing the factor-$k$ gap

Lemma. Let $\mathscr B\subseteq2^{[N]}$. Suppose that, for every $G\in\mathscr B$, the differences
$$ \{G\setminus F:F\in\mathscr B,\ F\subsetneq G\} $$
contain at most $k$ pairwise disjoint members. If $k\leq\log_2N$, then
$$ |\mathscr B|\leq(1+o(1))B_N $$
as even $N\to\infty$, uniformly over such $k$.

Proof. Put
$$ w=\left\lceil\sqrt{N\ln N}\right\rceil,\qquad \ell=2w, $$
and restrict initially to
$$ \mathscr B_0= \left\{ G\in\mathscr B: \bigl||G|-N/2\bigr|\leq w \right\}. $$
For sufficiently large $N$, all these groups have size exceeding $\ell$.

Fix $G\in\mathscr B_0$, of size $r$. Call an $\ell$-element subset $T\subseteq G$ good if it contains $G\setminus F$ for some $F\in\mathscr B_0$ with $F\subsetneq G$.

There cannot be $k+1$ pairwise disjoint good subsets: choosing a contained difference from each would violate the hypothesis. Partition a uniformly random ordering of $G$ into
$$ b=\left\lfloor\frac r\ell\right\rfloor $$
disjoint blocks of size $\ell$, ignoring leftover elements. Each block is a uniform $\ell$-element subset of $G$, and at most $k$ blocks can be good. Taking expectations gives
$$ \Pr(T\text{ is good})\leq\frac{k}{b}. $$

Choose a uniform maximal chain in $2^{[N]}$. Conditional on its containing $G$, the first $\ell$ elements deleted below $G$ form a uniform $\ell$-element subset of $G$. If the chain contains an earlier member $F$ of $\mathscr B_0$, then
$$ |G|-|F|\leq2w=\ell. $$
The deleted subset therefore contains $G\setminus F$ and is good. Uniformly over $G\in\mathscr B_0$, the conditional probability of an earlier selected member is at most
$$ \eta_N= \frac{k}{ \left\lfloor (N/2-w)/(2w)\right\rfloor } = O\!\left(k\sqrt{\frac{\ln N}{N}}\right) =o(1). $$

Let $X$ count the members of $\mathscr B_0$ on the random chain. Exactly $(X-1)_+$ of those members have an earlier selected member. Summing the conditional bounds yields
$$ \mathbb E[(X-1)_+]\leq\eta_N\mathbb E[X]. $$
Since $X=\mathbf1_{\{X\geq1\}}+(X-1)_+$,
$$ \mathbb E[X]\leq1+\eta_N\mathbb E[X], \qquad \mathbb E[X]\leq\frac1{1-\eta_N}. $$
A fixed $r$-element group lies on a uniform maximal chain with probability $1/\binom Nr$. Thus
$$ \mathbb E[X] = \sum_{G\in\mathscr B_0}\frac1{\binom N{|G|}} \geq\frac{|\mathscr B_0|}{B_N}, $$
and
$$ |\mathscr B_0|\leq\frac{B_N}{1-\eta_N}. $$

Finally, the number of subsets outside this central interval is at most
$$ 2^{N+1}e^{-2w^2/N}. $$
This is the binomial Chernoff bound: for independent fair Bernoulli variables, the centered exponential moment is bounded by $e^{Nt^2/8}$, using $\cosh(t/2)\leq e^{t^2/8}$; Markov’s inequality optimized at $t=4w/N$ bounds each tail.

Stirling’s formula gives $B_N\asymp2^N/\sqrt N$, so the omitted subsets number
$$ O(N^{-3/2})B_N. $$
Combining the central and tail estimates proves the lemma. $\square$

6. Applying the lemma with candidate symmetry

The family $\mathscr B$ from Section 4 satisfies the lemma’s hypothesis. Since $N=2^m$ and $k\leq m=\log_2N$,
$$ |\mathscr B|\leq(1+o(1))B_N $$
uniformly over $k$.

We must account for groups whose $H$-orbits have size smaller than $h$.

A nonidentity candidate permutation has at most $m-1$ candidate cycles. An approval type is fixed exactly when it is a union of candidate cycles, so at most
$$ 2^{m-1}=N/2 $$
approval types are fixed. Every other cycle on approval types has length at least two. Its total number of cycles on $\mathcal E$ is consequently at most
$$ N/2+\frac{N-N/2}{2}=3N/4. $$
A group $G\subseteq\mathcal E$ fixed by this permutation must be a union of these cycles. There are at most $2^{3N/4}$ such groups.

Taking a union bound over nonidentity elements of $H$, the number of groups with nontrivial $H$-stabilizer is at most
$$ (h-1)2^{3N/4}. $$
Every selected orbit without such a stabilizer contributes exactly $h$ groups to $\mathscr B$. Therefore the number $t$ of selected orbits satisfies
$$ t\leq\frac{|\mathscr B|}{h}+(h-1)2^{3N/4}. $$
The second term is negligible compared with $B_N/h$, uniformly over $k$, because
$$ \frac{h(h-1)2^{3N/4}}{B_N} \leq C(m!)^2\sqrt N\,2^{-N/4}\longrightarrow0. $$
Here $\log_2(m!)=O(m\log m)$, whereas $N=2^m$.

It follows that
$$ d_{m,k}\leq(1+o(1))\frac{B_N}{k!(m-k)!} $$
uniformly over $k$.

This replaces the previous $kB_N/h$ upper bound.

7. Lower bound for $k\geq2$

Consider the family
$$ \mathscr G= \{G\subseteq\mathcal E:|G|=N/2,\ W_0\in G\}, $$
where $W_0$ is also regarded as an approval type. It is $H$-invariant and has cardinality
$$ |\mathscr G| = \binom{N-1}{N/2-1} = B_N/2. $$
Choose one representative $G_i$ from each $H$-orbit. Their number is at least $B_N/(2h)$.

At $\mu_i$, assign mass
$$ 1-\frac1{2k} $$
to approval type $W_0$, and distribute the remaining mass positively and rationally over $G_i\setminus\{W_0\}$. Its support is exactly $G_i$.

Define a symmetric baseline threshold by
$$ \theta_0(W,\mathcal E\setminus\{W\})=1 $$
for every committee $W$, and set all other thresholds to $k+1$. Its acceptance condition is
$$ W\in X_{\theta_0}^{\infty}(\mu) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu(W)>1-\frac1k. $$
At each constructed society, $W_0$ meets this condition. Every other committee type has mass at most $1/(2k)$, which is at most $1-1/k$ because $k\geq2$. Thus the baseline output is exactly $\{W_0\}$.

For each selected orbit of $(W_0,G_i)$, independently either retain threshold $k+1$ or lower it to $k$ throughout that orbit. These coordinates do not coincide with baseline coordinates: their group sizes are $N/2$, whereas baseline group sizes are $N-1$, and $N\geq4$.

At society $\mu_j$, an activated constraint with committee $W_0$ excludes it exactly when
$$ \mu_j(G)=1. $$
Positive mass on every support type makes this equivalent to $G_j\subseteq G$. Both groups have size $N/2$, so it is equivalent to $G_j=G$. Distinct selected $H$-orbits therefore prevent interference.

The optional constraints independently switch each output between $\{W_0\}$ and $\varnothing$. Hence
$$ d_{m,k}\geq\frac{B_N}{2k!(m-k)!} \qquad(k\geq2). $$

8. Lower bound for $k=1$

For $k=1$, a threshold $1$ rejects a candidate exactly when the society’s support is contained in its constraint group. Thresholds $0$ and $2$ respectively reject unconditionally and impose no restriction. Thus every mapping depends only on support.

For candidate $a$, define
$$ r_a(G)=|G|+|\{A\in G:a\in A\}|. $$
Every added approval type increases this rank by either $1$ or $2$, so it strictly increases under proper inclusion.

For $m\geq2$, put $s=3N/4$. Consider supports $G$ for which exactly one candidate $a$ satisfies $r_a(G)=s$, and select one representative from each candidate-permutation orbit of these supports.

Define the symmetric baseline
$$ \theta_0(\{a\},J)= \begin{cases} 1,&r_a(J)<s,\\ 2,&r_a(J)\geq s. \end{cases} $$
For a society with support $G$, its output is
$$ Y_G=\{\{a\}:r_a(G)\geq s\}. $$
Indeed, if $r_a(G)<s$, the constraint $J=G$ rejects $a$. If $r_a(G)\geq s$, every containing group also has rank at least $s$, so no baseline constraint rejects it.

For each representative $G_i$, with its unique marked candidate $a_i$, independently lower the threshold on the orbit of $(\{a_i\},G_i)$ from $2$ to $1$.

An optional constraint can change an accepted candidate at a test support $G$ only when $G$ equals its constraint group. If $G\subsetneq J$ and $r_a(J)=s$, strict rank increase gives $r_a(G)<s$, so the baseline already rejected that candidate. At equality, uniqueness of the marked candidate ensures that only $a_i$ is removed. Distinct selected support orbits prevent interference. Hence these supports are shattered with label pairs
$$ Y_{G_i}, \qquad Y_{G_i}\setminus\{\{a_i\}\}. $$

It remains to count them.

Choose $G$ uniformly from $2^{\mathcal E}$. For a fixed candidate,
$$ r_a(G)=U+2V, $$
where $U,V$ are independent $\operatorname{Bin}(N/2,1/2)$ variables. There is an absolute $c>0$ such that
$$ \Pr(r_a(G)=s)\geq\frac c{\sqrt N} $$
for sufficiently large $N$.

To verify this, write $n=N/2$ and sum over integers $v$ with
$$ |v-n/2|\leq\sqrt n/8. $$
The required value $u=s-2v$ satisfies $|u-n/2|\leq\sqrt n/4$. Each relevant binomial point probability is at least $c_0/\sqrt n$, and there are at least $c_1\sqrt n$ choices. The point-probability estimates follow from Stirling’s formula at the center and
$$ \frac{\binom n{n/2+j}}{\binom n{n/2}} = \prod_{b=1}^{j} \frac{n/2-b+1}{n/2+b}. $$
For $j=O(\sqrt n)$ this ratio is bounded below by an absolute positive constant: taking logarithms and using $\ln(1-x)\geq-2x$ for $0\leq x\leq1/2$ bounds its logarithm below by $-O(j^2/n)$.

For distinct candidates $a,b$, partition approval types according to membership in them. The four group counts
$$ C_{00},C_{10},C_{01},C_{11} $$
are independent $\operatorname{Bin}(N/4,1/2)$ variables. Conditional on $C_{00},C_{11}$, the simultaneous equations $r_a(G)=r_b(G)=s$ determine $C_{10},C_{01}$ uniquely:
$$ 2C_{10}+C_{01}=s-C_{00}-2C_{11}, $$
$$ C_{10}+2C_{01}=s-C_{00}-2C_{11}. $$
Each remaining binomial point probability is at most $C_0/\sqrt N$, by the central binomial estimate. Therefore
$$ \Pr(r_a(G)=s,\ r_b(G)=s)\leq\frac C N. $$

Let $u(G)$ count the marked candidates. The total number of marked pairs $(a,G)$ is at least
$$ c\,m\,\frac{2^N}{\sqrt N}. $$
Removing supports with multiple marks discards at most
$$ 2\sum_G\binom{u(G)}2 \leq C\,m^2\,\frac{2^N}{N} $$
marked pairs, using $u\leq2\binom u2$ for $u\geq2$. Since $m/\sqrt N\to0$, at least
$$ c'\,m\,\frac{2^N}{\sqrt N} $$
uniquely marked supports remain.

Every candidate-permutation orbit has size at most $m!$. Thus
$$ d_{m,1} \geq c'\frac{2^N}{\sqrt N\,(m-1)!}. $$
This matches the upper bound within an absolute constant.

Finally, Stirling’s formula gives
$$ B_N=(1+o(1))\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac{2^N}{\sqrt N}. $$
Combining all bounds, and adjusting constants for the finitely many smaller $m$, proves
$$ d_{m,k}=\Theta(D_{m,k}) $$
uniformly over every admissible $m,k$. In particular,
$$ \log_2 d_{m,k} = 2^m-\frac m2-\log_2\!\bigl(k!(m-k)!\bigr)+O(1), $$
with a uniform remainder.

9. Why the full parameter square is different

When $k=m$, there is one committee. Every output is therefore either $\varnothing$ or $\{\mathcal A\}$. Both constant mappings belong to $\mathcal F$.

Let
$$ p=\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[f^\star(\mu)=\{\mathcal A\}]. $$
The constant empty and full mappings have errors $p$ and $1-p$, respectively.

For $\varepsilon>1/2$, estimate $p$ from the labels and output the full mapping when the empirical positive fraction is at least $1/2$, otherwise the empty mapping.

If
$$ 1-\varepsilon\leq p\leq\varepsilon, $$
both outputs meet the error requirement. Outside this interval, selecting the wrong constant requires the empirical fraction to deviate from $p$ by more than $\varepsilon-1/2$. The Bernoulli concentration bound gives failure probability at most
$$ e^{-2n(\varepsilon-1/2)^2}. $$
Thus the dimension-independent query bound stated in Section 1 follows.

For completeness, this concentration inequality follows by applying Markov’s inequality to independent centered Bernoulli variables. Their log moment-generating function has value and derivative zero at the origin, and second derivative at most $1/4$, yielding the bound $t^2/8$; optimizing the resulting exponential bound gives $e^{-2na^2}$ for a deviation of size $a$.

Under the usual convention permitting internal learner randomization, an even simpler boundary phenomenon occurs:
$$ q_{m,m}(\varepsilon,\delta)=0 \qquad \text{if }\varepsilon\geq1/2,\ \delta\geq1/2. $$
Choose the two constant mappings with equal probability. At least one has error at most $1/2$, so success probability is at least $1/2$.

Consequently, the unrestricted assertion
$$ q_{m,k}(\varepsilon,\delta) = \Theta\!\left( \frac{d_{m,k}+\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon} \right) $$
is false with constants uniform over the entire parameter square. Even with fixed $\varepsilon>1/2$ and fixed small $\delta$, the majority learner gives bounded query complexity for $k=m$, while $d_{m,m}\to\infty$.
