The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) goods, \(\tau\) known consumer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and an unknown linear utility vector \(v_t\) for each type, training observations are drawn by sampling \(t\sim\mu\), then \((p,B)\sim D\), and observing that type’s utility-maximizing bundle. Can an efficient learner return hypotheses \(\hat v_t\) such that, with high probability over training, a random type and future market example satisfy \(x^*(v_t,p,B)=x^*(\hat v_t,p,B)\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\geq1\) divisible goods, with bundles \(x\in[0,1]^m\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) type labels with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has an unknown fixed vector \(v_t\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^m\), giving utility \(v_t\cdot x\). Define \(x^*(v,p,B)\) to maximize this utility subject to \(p\cdot x\leq B\), choosing the cheapest maximizer and then the lexicographically smallest among remaining ties, in a fixed known coordinate order.
The learner receives independent observations \((t,p,B,x^*(v_t,p,B))\), where the type label is observed, \(t\sim\mu\), and independently \((p,B)\sim D\). The unknown distribution \(D\) is arbitrary over binary-rational \(p\in[0,1]^m\) and \(B\geq0\). Let \(L\) bound the total encoding length of the supplied support and of each market example.
For every such society, utility family, distribution, and rational \(0<\delta<1\), the learner must return rational vectors \(\hat v_t\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}^m\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over training and learner randomness, \(\Pr_{t\sim\mu,(p,B)\sim D}[x^*(v_t,p,B)=x^*(\hat v_t,p,B)]\geq1-\delta\).

Does this learning problem admit sample complexity and training and prediction time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\delta\), or is it NP-hard?

For one consumer, [Zadimoghaddam–Roth](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.4150) establish efficient learning with \(O(m^2\log(m^2/\delta)/\delta)\) observations; their formal guarantee is equality of achieved true utility.
For one consumer with almost surely unique optimal bundles, [Balcan et al.](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ninamf/papers/revealed-pref-optimal.pdf) establish efficient learning with \(O((m\log(1/\delta)+\log(1/\delta))/\delta)\) observations when error and confidence parameters both equal \(\delta\).
No result settling exactly the supported society problem with observed type labels and the stated tie rule was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Renamed the number of goods from n to m so running times use the required society convention.
  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one, as required by sampling a type from them.
  • Made the sampled type label observable in training and available at prediction; 'known consumer types' did not explicitly specify label visibility.
  • Retained the bounded divisible-bundle domain, nonnegative linear valuations, prices in [0,1], and nonnegative budgets from the supplied definitions.
  • Specified a fixed known selection rule: cheapest utility maximizer, then lexicographically smallest. The anchor leaves ties unresolved; a later primary learning paper uses cheapest maximizers followed by a fixed tie rule.
  • Restricted market examples to binary rationals with encoding length bounded by L, and required rational output hypotheses, to make bit complexity well-defined while leaving unknown valuations real-valued.
  • Set training confidence to 1−δ, matching the anchor's confidence convention, and required the graded question's exact bundle equality.

1. Result

The problem admits polynomial sample complexity and polynomial training and prediction time. Exact ties, zero prices, zero utilities, and arbitrarily small type masses are all allowed.

One sufficient sample bound is
\(N=O\!\left((\tau m^2(L+\log(m+1))+\log(1/\delta))/\delta\right)\).

As usual, an arbitrarily long encoding of the accuracy parameter contributes its input-reading cost separately; the algorithm only needs a dyadic lower approximation to \(\delta\) within a factor of two.

The proof establishes equality of selected bundles directly. This matters because the formal utility-equality criterion in [Zadimoghaddam–Roth, Definition 2.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.4150) does not by itself determine choices among tied bundles.

2. The canonical demand rule

Relabel coordinates so that the given lexicographic order is \(1,\ldots,m\).

For any nonnegative utility vector \(w\), its canonical demand can be computed as follows:

  • For every free good, set \(x_i=1\) if \(w_i>0\), and \(x_i=0\) otherwise.
  • Set every positive-price, zero-value good to zero.
  • Sort the remaining goods by decreasing \(w_i/p_i\), breaking equal-ratio ties by decreasing coordinate index.
  • Buy goods in that order, filling each before proceeding to the next, until the budget is exhausted or all such goods are full.

We first justify this rule, including its tie-breaking direction.

The feasible set is nonempty and compact. Maximizing utility, then minimizing cost, then successively minimizing coordinates therefore defines a unique bundle.

A free positive-value good must be full: increasing it otherwise improves utility. A zero-value good must have quantity zero: decreasing its quantity either lowers cost, if its price is positive, or improves the lexicographic objective, if its price is zero.

Let \(P=\{i:p_i>0,\ w_i>0\}\), and let \(S=\sum_{i\in P}p_i\). The selected bundle spends exactly \(\min\{B,S\}\). Indeed, if its expenditure were smaller, some good in \(P\) would be unfilled and could be increased while preserving feasibility and improving utility.

Now suppose \(i,j\in P\), \(i\ne j\), \(x_i>0\), and \(x_j<1\). For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\), the exchange
\(x_i\leftarrow x_i-\varepsilon/p_i\), \(x_j\leftarrow x_j+\varepsilon/p_j\)
is feasible and preserves cost. Its utility change is
\(\varepsilon(w_j/p_j-w_i/p_i)\).

Consequently, \(w_i/p_i\geq w_j/p_j\). If these ratios are equal and \(i<j\), the exchange lowers the first changed coordinate, contradicting lexicographic minimality. Hence equal ratios require \(i>j\).

Thus every purchased good precedes every distinct unfilled good in the stated total order. This forces a full prefix, followed by at most one fractional good, followed by zeros. The prescribed expenditure uniquely determines that prefix bundle, proving the rule.

In particular, rational prices and budgets produce rational selected bundles, even when the utility vector is irrational.

3. Exact consistency is a system of linear constraints

Consider one genuine observation \((p,B,x)\). We characterize precisely those nonnegative vectors \(w\) satisfying \(x^*(w,p,B)=x\).

Impose the following constraints:

  • For every \(i\) with \(x_i>0\), require \(w_i>0\).
  • For every free good with \(x_i=0\), require \(w_i=0\).
  • If \(p\cdot x<B\), require \(w_i=0\) for every \(i\) with \(x_i<1\).
  • If \(p\cdot x=B\), then for every distinct \(i,j\) with \(p_i,p_j>0\), \(x_i>0\), and \(x_j<1\), require

\(p_jw_i-p_iw_j>0\) when \(i<j\), and
\(p_jw_i-p_iw_j\geq0\) when \(i>j\).

These are strict linear inequalities, weak linear inequalities, and linear equalities.

Necessity. If a purchased good had zero value, decreasing its quantity would improve either cost or the lexicographic objective. If an unpurchased free good had positive value, increasing it would improve utility. With budget slack, increasing any unfilled positive-value good would improve utility. Finally, the exchange argument above gives exactly the pairwise inequalities, including strictness when \(i<j\).

Sufficiency with budget slack. Every positive coordinate of \(x\) requires positive value, while every coordinate below one requires zero value. Therefore \(x\) consists precisely of all positive-value goods at quantity one and all zero-value goods at quantity zero. It is affordable and is the canonical demand.

Sufficiency with a binding budget. The free coordinates agree with the canonical rule, since a genuine observation has each free coordinate in \(\{0,1\}\). Every positive-price good with \(w_i=0\) has \(x_i=0\).

Order the positive-price, positive-value goods as in Section 2. Whenever \(x_i>0\) and \(x_j<1\), the pairwise constraints say that \(i\) precedes \(j\): for \(i<j\), the ratio comparison is strict; for \(i>j\), equality also favors \(i\).

Hence \(x\) has the required prefix form. Its expenditure is \(B\), so it is exactly the greedy prefix bundle. This also covers \(B=0\), when every positive-price coordinate is zero.

We have therefore proved the exact equivalence:
\(x^*(w,p,B)=x\) if and only if \(w\) satisfies the displayed constraints.

Each observation contributes only \(O(m^2)\) constraints.

4. Polynomial-time fitting with uniformly bounded rational output

Group the observations by their observed type labels. For each type, combine all its consistency constraints.

Introduce variables \(w_1,\ldots,w_m,\eta\), and impose
\(w_i\geq0\), \(\sum_iw_i\leq1\), and \(0\leq\eta\leq1\).
Keep every equality and weak inequality. Replace every strict inequality \(a\cdot w>0\) by \(a\cdot w\geq\eta\). Maximize \(\eta\).

The optimum is positive. To prove this, first suppose the true type vector is nonzero and normalize it to have coordinate sum one. Positive rescaling preserves every consistency constraint. There are finitely many strict constraints, and every strict left-hand side is positive at this normalized vector. Their minimum, capped at one, supplies a feasible positive \(\eta\). If there are no strict constraints, \(\eta=1\) is feasible. The zero true vector can occur only in the latter case.

Thus an optimal solution satisfies all original strict constraints and reproduces every training bundle exactly.

We use the standard rational linear programming theorem: an optimal vertex of a nonempty bounded rational polytope can be found in time polynomial in its inequality encoding. Its hypotheses hold here: the constraints are rational, feasible, and bounded by the normalization and bounds on \(\eta\).

Crucially, the output vertex has a bit bound independent of the number of observations. Here is an explicit bound.

Set \(d=m+1\). Every constraint row contains at most two price coefficients; all other coefficients and right-hand sides belong to \(\{0,1,-1\}\). Under the stated encoding bound, each price numerator and denominator is at most \(2^L\). Clearing denominators separately in each row gives integer coefficients and right-hand sides of absolute value at most \(M=2^{2L}\).

At a vertex, choose \(d\) linearly independent tight rows. Cramer’s rule expresses every coordinate as a ratio of integer determinants. The determinant expansion bounds both numerator and denominator in absolute value by
\(d!M^d\leq d^d2^{2Ld}\).

Therefore, with
\(K=2Ld+d\lceil\log_2d\rceil+1\),
every returned utility coordinate has a representation \(a/b\) with
\(0\leq a\leq2^K\) and \(1\leq b\leq2^K\).

This bound depends only on \(m,L\). It requires no lower bound on positive utility coordinates or on utility-ratio gaps. For an unobserved type, return the zero vector.

5. Generalization across the supported society

Let \(\mathcal H\) be the finite class of all families of \(\tau\) utility vectors whose coordinates lie in \([0,1]\) and have numerator and denominator bounded as above. The learner’s output always belongs to \(\mathcal H\).

There are at most \(2^{2K+2}\) possible rational coordinates, so
\(|\mathcal H|\leq2^C\), where \(C=(2K+2)m\tau\).

For a fixed family \(h\in\mathcal H\), define its error by
\(\operatorname{err}(h)=\Pr_{t\sim\mu,\,(p,B)\sim D}[x^*(v_t,p,B)\ne x^*(h_t,p,B)]\).

If \(\operatorname{err}(h)>\delta\), independence of the training observations gives
\(\Pr[h\text{ agrees with all }N\text{ observations}]=(1-\operatorname{err}(h))^N\leq e^{-\delta N}\).

Taking a union bound over the fixed finite class yields
\(\Pr[\exists h\in\mathcal H:\operatorname{err}(h)>\delta\text{ and }h\text{ is sample-consistent}]\leq2^Ce^{-\delta N}\).

For example, set \(r=\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\rceil\) and take
\(N=\lceil(C+r)/\delta\rceil\).
Then the preceding probability is at most
\(e^{C\ln2-C-r}\leq e^{-r}\leq\delta\).

The fitting procedure always returns a sample-consistent member of \(\mathcal H\). Hence, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), its population error is at most \(\delta\), exactly as required.

Since \(K=O(m(L+\log(m+1)))\), this gives the sample bound stated in Section 1.

No minimum type mass is needed. The argument bounds error under the joint population distribution directly, including errors on types absent from the training sample.

6. Training and prediction complexity

The learner constructs \(O(Nm^2)\) observation constraints and solves at most \(\tau\) rational linear programs, each having \(m+1\) variables. Their coefficient lengths, and the returned utility lengths, are polynomially bounded as established above. Training therefore takes polynomial bit time in the stated parameters.

For prediction, retrieve the utility vector of the observed type and apply Section 2. Ratio comparisons use rational cross-products, and the final fractional quantity uses rational arithmetic on the query prices and budget. The utility encoding bound makes prediction time polynomial as well.

Thus the supported society problem with observed labels and the prescribed tie rule is efficiently learnable.
