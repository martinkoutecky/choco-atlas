The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 12 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and symmetric compatibility with each type ranking at most three compatible partner types, decide whether there is a stable mass assignment \(q\). Require \(q_{tu}=q_{ut}\geq 0\), \(\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\text{ compatible}}q_{tu}\leq\mu_t\), and, for every compatible pair, \(B_t(u)=0\) or \(B_u(t)=0\), where \(B_t(u)=\mu_t-\sum_{v\succeq_t u}q_{tv}\). Here \(q_{tt}\) counts the mass of type-\(t\) agents used in within-type pairs, and \(B_t(u)\) is the mass unmatched or assigned below \(u\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Compatibility is symmetric and may include within-type pairing. Let \(m\) count compatible unordered type pairs, including diagonal pairs. Each type weakly orders its compatible partner types, allowing ties, and lists at most three types, counting itself once if compatible. Every compatible partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched.
A mass assignment has real entries \(q_{tu}=q_{ut}\geq0\), zero on incompatible pairs, with \(\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\text{ compatible}}q_{tu}\leq\mu_t\). For distinct types, \(q_{tu}\) is the mass from each endpoint paired together; \(q_{tt}\) is the total mass of type \(t\) used in pairs of distinct agents of that type and is counted once in its capacity constraint.
Write \(v\succeq_t u\) when \(t\) weakly prefers \(v\) to \(u\), and define \(B_t(u)=\mu_t-\sum_{v:\{t,v\}\text{ compatible},\ v\succeq_t u}q_{tv}\). An assignment is stable when every compatible pair satisfies \(B_t(u)=0\) or \(B_u(t)=0\), including diagonal pairs.

Is deciding whether a stable mass assignment exists solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents, stable roommates existence with ties and lists of length at most three is NP-complete. [Cseh–Irving–Manlove](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.04609v3)
For unit-capacity vertices without diagonal pairs, ordinally stable fractional roommate matchings always exist, even with ties, and can be found in polynomial time. [Chen–Roy–Sorge](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)
Existence of fractional stable allocations is also known for general capacitated NTU-games, a different formulation from the support-based decision problem above. [Biró–Fleiner](https://real.mtak.hu/26264/7/BF16do_last.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Preferences were unspecified; use weak orders allowing ties, as in the discrete anchor, with every compatible partner preferred to being unmatched.
  • Interpret the list bound as three distinct compatible partner types, rather than three indifference classes.
  • Remove zero-mass entries so the input list is exactly the positive support.
  • Allow within-type compatibility when explicitly listed, count it once toward the list bound, and apply stability to diagonal pairs.
  • Take assignment entries to be nonnegative real masses; impose no integrality or parity restriction.
  • Define m as the number of compatible unordered type pairs and L as total input bit length.

Result

Deciding existence is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, every valid instance admits a stable mass assignment, including instances with unequal masses, ties, and diagonal pairs.

We prove this by applying Scarf’s lemma directly to the capacity constraints.

1. The version of Scarf’s lemma used

Let \(A,C\) be matrices with \(n\) rows and \(n+p\) columns, and let \(b\) have strictly positive coordinates. Suppose:

  • The first \(n\) columns of \(A\) form the identity matrix.
  • The set \(\{x\geq 0:Ax=b\}\) is bounded.
  • For every row \(i\), every column \(k>n\), and every \(j\leq n\) with \(j\neq i\),

\[ C_{i,i}<C_{i,k}<C_{i,j}. \]

Then there exists \(x\geq 0\) with \(Ax=b\) such that, for every column \(k\), some row \(i\) satisfies
\[ C_{i,k}\leq C_{i,j} \qquad\text{for every }j\text{ with }x_j>0. \]

This is the domination formulation of Scarf’s lemma stated as Theorem 5 in [Biró–Fleiner](https://real.mtak.hu/26264/7/BF16do_last.pdf). Its hypotheses do not require distinct values among the entries \(C_{i,k}\) with \(k>n\), so ties are permitted.

2. Encoding the given instance

Let \(E\) be the set of compatible unordered pairs. If \(m=0\), the zero assignment is feasible and stability is vacuous. Hence assume \(m>0\).

Introduce one variable \(q_e\) for each \(e\in E\), and one slack variable \(s_t\) for each \(t\in T\). For incidence purposes, a diagonal pair \(\{t,t\}\) has just the endpoint \(t\). The capacity equations are therefore
\[ s_t+\sum_{\substack{e\in E\\t\in e}}q_e=\mu_t \qquad(t\in T). \]

Write these equations as \(Ax=\mu\), with the slack variables first. Thus \(A\) has \(\tau\) rows and \(\tau+m\) columns, its first \(\tau\) columns form the identity matrix, and each remaining column is the incidence vector of its compatible pair. A diagonal column has one entry equal to one, exactly as required by the problem.

For every compatible partner \(u\) of \(t\), define
\[ r_t(u)=1+\text{the number of preference classes strictly below }u. \]
Since each list contains at most three types,
\[ 1\leq r_t(u)\leq 3, \qquad v\succeq_t u\ \Longleftrightarrow\ r_t(v)\geq r_t(u). \]

Define \(C\) as follows. On a slack column \(s_u\), set
\[ C_{t,s_u}= \begin{cases} 0,&u=t,\\ 5,&u\neq t. \end{cases} \]
On a pair column \(e\), set
\[ C_{t,e}= \begin{cases} r_t(u),&e=\{t,u\}\text{ is incident with }t,\\ 4,&t\notin e. \end{cases} \]
For a diagonal pair, the first case uses \(u=t\).

We now check all hypotheses of the lemma:

  • Every \(\mu_t\) is strictly positive by assumption.
  • The first \(\tau\) columns of \(A\) form the identity matrix.
  • The feasible set is nonempty: take \(s_t=\mu_t\) and \(q_e=0\).
  • It is bounded: every variable is nonnegative and occurs with coefficient one in at least one capacity equation, so it is at most the corresponding mass.
  • Every pair-column entry of \(C\) lies between one and four. Consequently, in each row, it is strictly above the own-slack entry zero and strictly below every other-slack entry five.

Scarf’s lemma therefore supplies a nonnegative solution \(x=(s,q)\) satisfying both the capacity equations and domination.

3. Domination implies precisely the required stability

Fix any compatible pair \(e\). Choose a row \(t\) that dominates its column:
\[ C_{t,e}\leq C_{t,j} \qquad\text{whenever }x_j>0. \]

First, \(t\) must be an endpoint of \(e\). Otherwise \(C_{t,e}=4\). Since
\[ s_t+\sum_{h\ni t}q_h=\mu_t>0, \]
either \(s_t>0\), whose column has entry zero in row \(t\), or some incident \(q_h>0\), whose column has entry at most three. Either alternative contradicts domination.

Write \(e=\{t,u\}\), allowing \(u=t\). Its entry in row \(t\) is \(r_t(u)\geq 1\). Thus domination also implies \(s_t=0\): a positive \(s_t\) would require \(r_t(u)\leq 0\).

Moreover, every positive incident assignment \(q_{tv}\) satisfies
\[ r_t(u)\leq r_t(v), \]
so \(v\succeq_t u\). All mass used at \(t\) is consequently assigned to partners weakly preferred to \(u\). Together with \(s_t=0\), this gives
\[ \sum_{v:\,v\succeq_t u}q_{tv} = \sum_{v:\,\{t,v\}\text{ compatible}}q_{tv} = \mu_t. \]
Hence \(B_t(u)=0\).

Every compatible pair therefore has an endpoint satisfying the required equality. For a diagonal pair, that endpoint is necessarily \(t\), giving \(B_t(t)=0\). The resulting assignment is stable.

4. Decision complexity

Every promised input is a YES-instance. The decision algorithm therefore returns YES; reading the input takes time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). If input validity is also checked, exact rational arithmetic and the compatibility and preference checks take polynomial time.

This conclusion concerns the requested existence decision. It uses no polynomial running-time claim for constructing a witness with Scarf’s algorithm.
