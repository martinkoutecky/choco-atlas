The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(X,Y\) with rational masses \((\alpha_x)_{x\in X}\) and \((\beta_y)_{y\in Y}\), each summing to \(1\), and one-world values \(v^A_{xy},v^B_{yx}\) and principal utilities \(u_{xy}\), compute a maximum-utility stable flow \(q\). The flow satisfies \(q_{xy}\ge 0\), \(\sum_y q_{xy}=\alpha_x\), and \(\sum_x q_{xy}=\beta_y\). It is stable if for each \((x,y)\), at least one of \(\sum_{y':\,v^A_{xy'}<v^A_{xy}}q_{xy'}\) and \(\sum_{x':\,v^B_{yx'}<v^B_{yx}}q_{x'y}\) is zero. Maximize \(\sum_{x,y}q_{xy}u_{xy}\), with the number of types allowed to vary.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(X,Y\) be nonempty, disjoint finite type sets. Each society is given by its support: its positive-mass types and their binary rational masses, with \(\sum_x\alpha_x=\sum_y\beta_y=1\). Set \(\tau=|X|+|Y|\), \(m=\max\{|X|,|Y|\}\); support size counts listed types, not possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
All pairs are admissible. Input values \(v^A_{xy},v^B_{yx}\in\mathbb Q\) induce preferences by larger value; ties are allowed and remain unresolved. Principal utilities satisfy \(u_{xy}\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\).
A flow \(q\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{X\times Y}\) is feasible when \(\sum_yq_{xy}=\alpha_x\) and \(\sum_xq_{xy}=\beta_y\).
It is stable when, for every \((x,y)\), at least one of \(\sum_{y':v^A_{xy'}<v^A_{xy}}q_{xy'}\) and \(\sum_{x':v^B_{yx'}<v^B_{yx}}q_{x'y}\) equals zero.
Write \(U(q)=\sum_{x,y}q_{xy}u_{xy}\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max\{U(q):q\text{ is feasible and stable}\}\).

For each fixed \(0<\epsilon<1\), can a feasible stable flow satisfying \(U(q)\geq m^{-(1-\epsilon)}\mathrm{OPT}\) be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is achieving this approximation NP-hard?

For discrete matching with ties, a \(n^{-(1-\epsilon)}\) utility-maximization inapproximability bound is stated even with one world, where \(n\) is the number of agents per side ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04846v1)); its cited original establishes an \(n^{1-\epsilon}\) bound for minimizing rank costs instead ([original](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/1066/1/ICALP99.pdf)).
Continuous bipartite stable allocation with strict preferences admits polynomial-time linear-objective optimization ([Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)); no result settling the stated approximation question with ties was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified binary rational values and utilities; agent values may have either sign.
  • Restricted principal utilities to nonnegative rationals so the anchor's multiplicative maximization approximation is meaningful.
  • Retained complete admissibility and unresolved ties, with blocking requiring strict improvement on both sides.
  • Used m, the larger side's support size, as the continuous analogue of the anchor's agent-count approximation parameter; normalized masses provide no agent count.
  • Restricted epsilon to the meaningful approximation range between zero and one, and defined L as total input bit length.

Result

For every fixed \(0<\epsilon<1\), achieving the stated approximation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Consequently, such a polynomial-time algorithm exists only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The hardness holds even when both sides have the same number of types, all masses are equal and dyadic, and every principal utility is a strictly positive integer.

1. Stable flows permit extraction of supported stable matchings

Suppose \(|X|=|Y|=N\) and every mass equals \(1/N\). For a feasible flow \(q\), define its support graph by
\[ E(q)=\{(x,y):q_{xy}>0\}. \]
Here and below, a discrete matching is weakly stable if no pair strictly prefers each other to their assigned partners.

Lemma. If \(q\) is stable, then:

  • Every perfect matching contained in \(E(q)\) is weakly stable.
  • Every edge of \(E(q)\) belongs to a perfect matching contained in \(E(q)\).

Conversely, every weakly stable perfect matching induces a stable flow by assigning mass \(1/N\) to its edges.

Proof. For the first assertion, let \(M\) be a supported perfect matching. If \((x,y)\) blocked \(M\), then
\[ v^A_{xM(x)}<v^A_{xy}, \qquad v^B_{yM(y)}<v^B_{yx}. \]
Since both matching edges have positive flow, both strict-worse sums in the stability condition for \((x,y)\) would be positive. This contradicts stability of \(q\).

For the second assertion, fix \((a,b)\in E(q)\), and put \(P=Nq\). Every row and column of \(P\) sums to \(1\), and \(P_{ab}>0\).

Delete \(a\) and \(b\) from the support graph. For any subset \(S\subseteq X\setminus\{a\}\), let \(T\subseteq Y\setminus\{b\}\) be its neighbors in the remaining graph. All positive entries in rows \(S\) lie in columns \(T\cup\{b\}\). Hence
\[ |S| = \sum_{x\in S}\sum_{y\in T}P_{xy} +\sum_{x\in S}P_{xb} \leq |T|+1-P_{ab} < |T|+1. \]
Because \(|S|\) and \(|T|\) are integers, \(|S|\leq|T|\). Hall’s marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching between the remaining equally sized sides. Adding \((a,b)\) proves the assertion.

Finally, for the flow induced by a perfect matching, the strict-worse sum on either side is positive exactly when that agent strictly prefers the proposed partner to its assigned partner. Weak stability therefore gives the required zero-sum disjunction. \(\square\)

2. An \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard source problem

We use the following established decision problem:

Given equally sized sets \(A,B\), mutually acceptable pairs, and preference lists with ties over acceptable partners, decide whether there is a perfect matching consisting of acceptable pairs with no mutually acceptable blocking pair.

This is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete by Theorem 1 of [Iwama–Manlove–Miyazaki–Morita](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/1066/1/ICALP99.pdf). Their definition requires a matching to cover everyone, and their stability notion requires strict improvement on both sides. Thus the theorem applies precisely to the source problem above.

Let a source instance have \(|A|=|B|=n\), where \(n\geq1\).

3. A designated-pair construction

Add a type \(a\) to the first side and a type \(b\) to the second side. Complete all preference lists as follows, with each listed tier strictly preferred to subsequent tiers:

| Type | Preferences, from best to worst |
|---|---|
| Original \(x\in A\) | Its original acceptable partners, preserving their order and ties; then \(b\); then all unacceptable original partners tied |
| Original \(y\in B\) | Its original acceptable partners, preserving their order and ties; then all unacceptable original partners and \(a\), tied |
| \(a\) | \(b\); then all original types in \(B\), tied |
| \(b\) | All original types in \(A\), tied; then \(a\) |

All pairs in this constructed instance are admissible.

Proposition. The source instance has a perfect weakly stable acceptable matching if and only if the constructed instance has a weakly stable perfect matching containing \((a,b)\).

Proof, forward direction. Extend a source matching by \((a,b)\).

An acceptable original pair cannot block, by source stability. An unacceptable original pair cannot block because each original type is assigned an acceptable partner, which it strictly prefers to every unacceptable partner. A pair \((x,b)\) cannot block because \(x\) prefers its acceptable assigned partner to \(b\). A pair \((a,y)\) cannot block because \(a\) prefers \(b\) to \(y\). These exhaust all possible blocking pairs.

Proof, reverse direction. Suppose a stable perfect matching contains \((a,b)\). Every original type must then be matched to an original type.

If an original \(x\in A\) were assigned an unacceptable partner, \(x\) would strictly prefer \(b\), while \(b\) would strictly prefer \(x\) to \(a\). Thus \((x,b)\) would block. Consequently, every original matching edge is acceptable.

Any blocking acceptable pair in the restricted original matching would also block the constructed matching, because all original comparisons among acceptable partners were preserved. The restriction is therefore a perfect weakly stable acceptable matching. \(\square\)

4. Uniform dyadic masses and explicit input size

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n+1\). Add \(N-(n+1)\) dummy pairs to the constructed instance.

Each dummy type ranks its designated dummy partner uniquely first and ties all other partners below it. Each existing type places all dummy partners in a final tier below its existing partners.

In every stable perfect matching, each dummy pair must be matched together: otherwise its two members would form a blocking pair. Conversely, adjoining all dummy pairs to a stable matching creates no blocking pair involving a dummy, because each dummy receives its uniquely best partner. Therefore the designated-pair equivalence remains valid.

Assign mass \(1/N\) to every type. These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to \(1\) on each side. Moreover,
\[ m=N,\qquad \tau=2N,\qquad N<2(n+1). \]

Every preference list can be represented by integer values preserving exactly its strict comparisons and ties. For example, assign consecutive integer values to its tiers in decreasing order. Their magnitudes are at most \(N\).

The construction lists all \(N^2\) pairs explicitly, and its total input bit length is polynomial in the source input length.

5. A positive-utility gap

Set the principal utilities to
\[ u_{ab}=N^2+1, \qquad u_{xy}=1\quad\text{for every other pair}. \]
Since every feasible flow has total mass \(1\),
\[ U(q)=1+N^2q_{ab}. \]

If the source instance is a yes-instance, the designated-pair proposition supplies a stable perfect matching containing \((a,b)\). Its induced flow has \(q_{ab}=1/N\). Conversely, the row constraint always gives \(q_{ab}\leq1/N\). Thus
\[ \mathrm{OPT}=N+1 \quad\text{in a yes-instance}. \]

If the source instance is a no-instance, no stable flow can have \(q_{ab}>0\). Indeed, the lemma would extract a supported stable perfect matching containing \((a,b)\), contradicting the designated-pair proposition. Hence every stable flow has utility \(1\).

A stable flow always exists in these constructed instances: refine every tie arbitrarily, apply the Gale–Shapley theorem to the complete strict lists, and use the resulting matching flow. Any blocking pair under the original preferences would remain blocking after refinement, so this matching is weakly stable under the original ties. Consequently,
\[ \mathrm{OPT}=1 \quad\text{in a no-instance}. \]

6. The requested approximation distinguishes the two cases

Suppose a polynomial-time algorithm always returned a feasible stable flow satisfying
\[ U(q)\geq m^{-(1-\epsilon)}\mathrm{OPT}. \]

On a yes-instance, since \(m=N\) and \(N^{1-\epsilon}\leq N\),
\[ U(q) \geq \frac{N+1}{N^{1-\epsilon}} \geq \frac{N+1}{N} = 1+\frac1N. \]
Using \(U(q)=1+N^2q_{ab}\), this implies \(q_{ab}\geq1/N^3\).

On a no-instance, every feasible stable output has \(q_{ab}=0\). Comparing the returned coordinate with \(1/(2N^3)\) therefore decides the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete source problem in polynomial time.

This proves the claimed approximation hardness. The argument uses the original paper’s perfect-matching existence theorem, rather than transferring its rank-cost minimization bound to utility maximization.

1. Supported stable matchings

Both assertions of the lemma are correct.

For the first assertion, a blocking pair for a supported perfect matching would give a positive term in each of the two strict-worse sums defining flow stability. The matching edges have positive flow by the definition of support, so the resulting contradiction is valid.

For the second assertion, \(P=Nq\) has nonnegative entries and every row and column sums to \(1\). After deleting \(a\) and \(b\), the stated inequality holds for every subset \(S\) of the remaining first side:

  • The total contribution from columns in \(T\) is at most \(|T|\), by their column sums.
  • The contribution to column \(b\) from rows in \(S\) is at most \(1-P_{ab}\), since \(a\notin S\).
  • Positivity of \(P_{ab}\) gives the strict inequality needed to conclude \(|S|\leq |T|\).

Thus Hall’s condition holds. The remaining sides have equal cardinality, so Hall’s theorem yields the asserted perfect matching. Adding \((a,b)\) produces a supported perfect matching containing the designated edge. The argument also covers the empty remaining graph when \(N=1\).

The converse is correct: for a matching-induced flow, each strict-worse sum is either \(0\) or \(1/N\), and is positive precisely when the corresponding agent strictly prefers the proposed partner to its assigned partner. This matches weak stability exactly.

The preference construction specifies every pair and preserves all original comparisons among acceptable partners.
