The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite valuation support \(V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) and rational masses \(\mu_v>0\) summing to \(1\), let \(Q\) be a common denominator and form, for each \(n=rQ\), the finite gain game with \(n\mu_v\) players of valuation \(v\). For a player \(i\) of type \(v\), determine and compute \(\Phi_\mu(v)=\lim_{r\to\infty} n\phi_i^{(n)}\), where \(\phi_i^{(n)}\) is that player’s Shapley value; establish the limit and an exact running-time bound in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\) of \(\tau\) distinct valuation types, each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_v\), where \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length. There is one object, so \(m=1\).
Let \(Q\) be the least common denominator of the masses. For each positive integer \(r\) with \(n=rQ\geq 2\), form a player set \(N_n\) containing exactly \(n\mu_v\) players of valuation \(v\). Write \(a_i\) for player \(i\)'s valuation.
The gain game has worth \(g_n(\varnothing)=0\) and, for every nonempty coalition \(S\subseteq N_n\), \(g_n(S)=\frac{n-|S|}{n^2}\sum_{i\in S}(\max_{j\in S}a_j-a_i)\). Equal valuations are allowed. Let \(\phi_i^{(n)}\) be the standard Shapley value; symmetry makes it identical for all players of a given type.

Do the limits \(\Phi_\mu(v)=\lim_{r\to\infty}(rQ)\phi_i^{(rQ)}\), with \(a_i=v\), exist as finite rational numbers for every supported type?
Is computing all these limits exactly solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is it NP-hard? In the polynomial case, what explicit bit-operation running-time bound holds?

For finite gain games, all Shapley values can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) arithmetic operations ([discrete algorithm](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.08068v4)); this bound does not specify bit complexity for a society encoded by masses.

Result

All limits exist and are finite rational numbers. Writing the society’s mean valuation as \(\bar v=\sum_{u\in V}\mu_u u\), the answer is

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\Phi_\mu(v)=\frac{\bar v-v}{2}\quad\text{for every }v\in V.}\)

All these numbers can be computed exactly, including reduction to lowest terms, in \(\boxed{O(\tau L^2)}\) bit operations using classical integer arithmetic.

1. Decomposition by valuation gaps

Order the supported valuations as \(v_1<\cdots<v_\tau\). If \(\tau=1\), every coalition has zero worth, so the asserted formula holds. Assume henceforth that \(\tau\geq2\).

For each \(k\in\{1,\ldots,\tau-1\}\), define

\(\displaystyle \Delta_k=v_{k+1}-v_k,\qquad \alpha_k=\sum_{j\leq k}\mu_{v_j},\qquad \beta_k=1-\alpha_k.\)

Positive masses imply \(0<\alpha_k,\beta_k<1\).

Fix an admissible population size \(n=rQ\). Partition its players into a low group, with valuations at most \(v_k\), and a high group, with valuations greater than \(v_k\). Their sizes are the positive integers

\(\displaystyle A_k=n\alpha_k,\qquad B_k=n\beta_k.\)

For a coalition \(S\), let \(\ell_k(S)\) count its low players and \(h_k(S)\) count its high players. Define the threshold game

\(\displaystyle F_{n,k}(S)=\frac{n-|S|}{n^2}\ell_k(S)\mathbf 1_{\{h_k(S)>0\}}.\)

This definition also gives \(F_{n,k}(\varnothing)=0\).

For a nonempty coalition, a player’s difference from its maximum valuation contains the gap \(\Delta_k\) precisely when that player belongs to the low group and the coalition contains a high player. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\bigl(\max_{j\in S}a_j-a_i\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1}\Delta_k\ell_k(S)\mathbf 1_{\{h_k(S)>0\}},\)

and therefore, for every coalition,

\(\displaystyle g_n=\sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1}\Delta_k F_{n,k}.\)

The Shapley value is linear because it averages marginal contributions. It thus suffices to calculate it for one threshold game.

2. Exact Shapley values for a threshold game

Consider a partition into \(A\) low players and \(B\) high players, where \(A+B=n\) and \(A,B\geq1\). Write

\(\displaystyle F(S)=\frac{n-|S|}{n^2}\ell(S)\mathbf 1_{\{h(S)>0\}}.\)

We will prove that its Shapley values per low and high player are respectively

\(\displaystyle \phi_L(F)=\frac{B}{2n^2}\left(1-\frac{2(n+1)}{(B+1)(B+2)}\right),\)

\(\displaystyle \phi_H(F)=-\frac{A}{2n^2}\left(1-\frac{2(n+1)}{(B+1)(B+2)}\right).\)

Random-order representation

Use the standard random-permutation definition of the Shapley value, also stated in the [cited gain-game paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1602.08068v4).

To generate a uniform random permutation, assign every player an independent uniform rank in \([0,1]\) and order the ranks. Conditional on player \(i\)’s rank being \(t\), each other player precedes \(i\) independently with probability \(t\). If \(T_t\) denotes this predecessor set, then, for any game \(w\),

\(\displaystyle \phi_i(w)=\int_0^1\mathbb E\bigl[w(T_t\cup\{i\})-w(T_t)\bigr]\,dt.\)

Indeed, independent continuous ranks have no ties, and all player orderings have equal probability.

The low player’s value

Introduce two auxiliary games:

\(\displaystyle f(S)=\frac{n-|S|}{n^2}\ell(S),\qquad z(S)=\frac{n-|S|}{n^2}\ell(S)\mathbf 1_{\{h(S)=0\}}.\)

Then \(F=f-z\).

Fix a low player \(i\). For a predecessor coalition \(T\), write \(s=|T|\) and \(\ell=\ell(T)\). Its marginal contribution to \(f\) is

\(\displaystyle f(T\cup\{i\})-f(T)=\frac{(n-s-1)(\ell+1)-(n-s)\ell}{n^2}=\frac{n-1-s-\ell}{n^2}.\)

Conditional on rank \(t\), we have \(\mathbb E[s]=(n-1)t\) and \(\mathbb E[\ell]=(A-1)t\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \phi_L(f)=\frac1{n^2}\int_0^1\bigl(n-1-(n-1)t-(A-1)t\bigr)\,dt=\frac{B}{2n^2}.\)

For \(z\), the marginal contribution is zero if a high player precedes \(i\). Otherwise, all predecessors are low, and the marginal contribution is

\(\displaystyle \frac{(n-s-1)(s+1)-(n-s)s}{n^2}=\frac{n-1-2s}{n^2}.\)

The probability that no high player precedes \(i\), conditional on rank \(t\), is \((1-t)^B\). This event is independent of the low players’ ranks, so the conditional expected number of low predecessors remains \((A-1)t\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \phi_L(z)=\frac1{n^2}\int_0^1(1-t)^B\bigl(n-1-2(A-1)t\bigr)\,dt.\)

The required integrals are

\(\displaystyle \int_0^1(1-t)^B\,dt=\frac1{B+1},\qquad \int_0^1t(1-t)^B\,dt=\frac1{(B+1)(B+2)}.\)

Thus, using \(A=n-B\),

\(\displaystyle \phi_L(z)=\frac{(n-1)(B+2)-2(A-1)}{n^2(B+1)(B+2)}=\frac{B(n+1)}{n^2(B+1)(B+2)}.\)

Subtracting proves the asserted formula for \(\phi_L(F)\).

The high player’s value

The threshold game is invariant under permutations within either group, so each group has a common Shapley value. Moreover, \(F(N)=F(\varnothing)=0\).

For every player ordering, the sum of marginal contributions telescopes to \(F(N)-F(\varnothing)\). Taking expectations gives

\(\displaystyle A\phi_L(F)+B\phi_H(F)=0.\)

Since \(B>0\), this proves the asserted formula for \(\phi_H(F)\).

3. Replication limits

Returning to threshold \(k\), substitute \(A=n\alpha_k\) and \(B=n\beta_k\). For a player of supported type \(v_j\), the exact scaled value is

\(\displaystyle n\phi_i^{(n)}=\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1}\Delta_k\left(\beta_k-\mathbf 1_{\{j>k\}}\right)\left(1-\frac{2(n+1)}{(n\beta_k+1)(n\beta_k+2)}\right).\)

Here the coefficient is \(\beta_k\) for a low player and \(\beta_k-1=-\alpha_k\) for a high player.

For every \(k\), the fixed number \(\beta_k\) is positive. In particular,

\(\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{2(n+1)}{(n\beta_k+1)(n\beta_k+2)}\leq\frac{4}{n\beta_k^2}\longrightarrow0.\)

There are only finitely many gaps. Therefore the limit along \(n=rQ\) exists and equals

\(\displaystyle \Phi_\mu(v_j)=\frac12\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1}\Delta_k\beta_k-\sum_{k<j}\Delta_k\right).\)

To identify this expression, expand each supported valuation:

\(\displaystyle v_j=v_1+\sum_{k<j}\Delta_k.\)

Averaging with the masses and interchanging finite sums gives

\(\displaystyle \bar v=v_1+\sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1}\Delta_k\sum_{j>k}\mu_{v_j}=v_1+\sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1}\Delta_k\beta_k.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \Phi_\mu(v_j)=\frac{\bar v-v_j}{2}.\)

Since all input valuations and masses are rational, every limit is rational and finite.

4. Exact algorithm and bit complexity

The algorithm needs only the weighted mean; it need not sort the support or compute \(Q\).

Write each input rational as

\(\displaystyle v=\frac{p_v}{q_v},\qquad \mu_v=\frac{c_v}{d_v},\)

with nonnegative valuation numerators and positive denominators and mass numerators. Define the common denominator and mean numerator

\(\displaystyle D=\prod_{v\in V}q_vd_v,\qquad M=\sum_{v\in V}c_vp_v\frac{D}{q_vd_v}.\)

Each division is exact, and \(\bar v=M/D\). Output, for each supported type,

\(\displaystyle \Phi_\mu(v)=\frac{M-p_v(D/q_v)}{2D},\)

reducing the numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor if desired.

To bound the bit cost:

  • Integer sizes. The total bit length of all input denominators is at most \(L\), so \(D<2^L\). Also \(\max_v v<2^L\). Positivity and \(\sum_v\mu_v=1\) imply

\(\displaystyle 0\leq M=D\bar v\leq D\max_v v<2^{2L}.\)
All products, sums, output numerators, and output denominators therefore have \(O(L)\) bits.

  • Number of arithmetic operations. Constructing \(D\), constructing \(M\), and constructing all output fractions require \(O(\tau)\) integer multiplications, exact divisions, additions, and subtractions.
  • Cost per operation. Classical multiplication and long division on \(O(L)\)-bit integers cost \(O(L^2)\) bit operations. Euclidean greatest-common-divisor computation also costs \(O(L^2)\) with classical arithmetic. For completeness, division of an \(a\)-bit integer by a \(b\)-bit integer costs \(O(b(a-b+1))\); along the Euclidean algorithm, the length differences telescope, and every two remainders at least halve the earlier positive remainder. Summing these division costs gives the stated quadratic bound.

There are at most \(\tau\) output reductions. Thus the entire computation costs

\(\displaystyle \boxed{O(\tau L^2)\text{ bit operations}.}\)

This settles the problem as polynomial-time solvable. The computation depends on the binary input size, without constructing any replicated player set.

Both integrals are correct. The critical simplification also checks:
