The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given τ resident cohorts with positive rational masses μ_i summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational interaction matrix \(q_{ij}\in[0,1]\), and one indivisible house per resident, with total supply \(1/2\) of each of two house types valued \(0\) and \(1\) by everyone, choose cohort fractions \(p_i\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\sum_i μ_i p_i=1/2\) to minimize \(E(p)=\frac12\sum_{i,j}μ_iμ_jq_{ij}[p_i(1-p_j)+(1-p_i)p_j]\).

Result

The continuous envy-threshold problem is $\mathsf{NP}$-complete, and therefore $\mathsf{NP}$-hard, already for $m=2$. Hardness holds even when all masses are equal and every entry of $Q$ belongs to $\{0,1\}$.

The essential step is a rounding lemma: when $q_{ii}=1$ for every cohort, equal masses and an even support size ensure that splitting cohorts cannot improve the optimum.

1. Quadratic form of the objective

Put $x_i=\mu_i p_i$, so $x_i$ is the mass of cohort $i$ receiving value-$1$ houses. Feasibility becomes

$$ 0\leq x_i\leq\mu_i, \qquad \sum_i x_i=\frac12. $$

Using symmetry of $Q$, the given objective simplifies to

$$ E(p) = \sum_{i,j}\mu_i\mu_jq_{ij}p_i - \sum_{i,j}\mu_i\mu_jq_{ij}p_ip_j. $$

Consequently, with $c=Q\mu$,

$$ E(p)=f(x):=c^\mathsf{T}x-x^\mathsf{T}Qx, \qquad \nabla f(x)=c-2Qx. $$

In particular, diagonal interactions contribute the genuine splitting penalty

$$ \mu_i^2q_{ii}p_i(1-p_i). $$

2. Exact rounding for equal masses

Lemma. Suppose $\tau$ is even, $\mu_i=1/\tau$, and $q_{ii}=1$ for every $i$. Then every feasible allocation $p$ admits a feasible allocation $\widehat p\in\{0,1\}^\tau$ satisfying

$$ E(\widehat p)\leq E(p). $$

Proof. Equal masses make the capacity constraint

$$ \sum_i p_i=\frac{\tau}{2}. $$

Suppose two coordinates $p_i,p_j$ lie strictly between $0$ and $1$. Consider the capacity-preserving move

$$ p(t)=p+t(e_i-e_j), $$

where $e_i$ denotes the $i$th coordinate vector. Its feasible interval is $[a,b]$, where

$$ a=\max\{-p_i,p_j-1\}, \qquad b=\min\{1-p_i,p_j\}. $$

Because both coordinates are fractional, $a<0<b$. At either endpoint, at least one of these coordinates becomes $0$ or $1$.

For equal masses, the quadratic part of the objective is $-\tau^{-2}p^\mathsf{T}Qp$. Hence

$$ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}E(p(t)) = -\frac{2}{\tau^2}(q_{ii}+q_{jj}-2q_{ij}) = -\frac{4}{\tau^2}(1-q_{ij}) \leq0. $$

Thus $E(p(t))$ is concave on $[a,b]$. Since $0$ is a convex combination of the endpoints, concavity implies

$$ \min\{E(p(a)),E(p(b))\}\leq E(p). $$

Choose such an endpoint. This decreases the number of fractional coordinates without increasing envy.

Repeating leaves at most one fractional coordinate. Exactly one is impossible: the coordinate sum is the integer $\tau/2$, and all other coordinates are integers. Therefore the resulting allocation is binary. $\square$

3. Polynomial reduction establishing hardness

We reduce from Minimum Bisection: given a simple undirected graph $G$ on an even number $n$ of vertices and an integer $k$, decide whether some partition into two sets of size $n/2$ cuts at most $k$ edges. This is the $\mathsf{NP}$-hard source problem used in [Inamdar, Jain, and Pandey, Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15864v1#Thmtheorem4.1).

We may restrict to $n\geq2$ and $0\leq k\leq |E(G)|$, since instances outside these bounds are immediately decidable.

Padding to a power-of-two support size

To ensure that the constructed numbers have finite binary expansions, first arrange that the number of vertices is a power of two.

Let $N$ be the smallest power of two satisfying

$$ N>2(n^2+n), $$

and put

$$ s=\frac{N-n}{2}. $$

Construct $H$ as the disjoint union of $G$ and two cliques, each with $s$ vertices. Then $H$ has $N$ vertices and

$$ s>n^2, \qquad s-1>k. $$

We claim that $G$ has a bisection cutting at most $k$ edges if and only if $H$ does.

For the forward direction, place the two cliques wholly on opposite sides and add one side of the original bisection to each. Each resulting side has size

$$ s+\frac n2=\frac N2, $$

and the cut size is unchanged.

Conversely, suppose a bisection of $H$ cuts at most $k$ edges. Splitting either clique into nonempty parts of sizes $a$ and $s-a$ would cut

$$ a(s-a)\geq s-1>k $$

edges. Thus both cliques are unsplit. They cannot occupy the same side, because

$$ 2s=N-n>\frac N2. $$

They therefore occupy opposite sides, leaving exactly $n/2$ original vertices on each side. These vertices give the required bisection of $G$.

Constructing the society

Create one resident type for each vertex of $H$, and set

$$ \tau=N, \qquad \mu_i=\frac1N. $$

Define the symmetric interaction matrix by

$$ q_{ij}= \begin{cases} 1,&i=j,\\ 1,&i\neq j\text{ and }\{i,j\}\in E(H),\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$

Use the prescribed two house types and supplies, and set

$$ B=\frac{k}{N^2}. $$

All masses are positive and sum to $1$. All entries of $Q$ belong to $\{0,1\}$. Since $N$ is a power of two, both the masses and $B$ have finite binary expansions.

For a binary feasible allocation $p$, precisely $N/2$ coordinates equal $1$. Let

$$ S=\{i:p_i=1\}. $$

Diagonal terms vanish. For each unordered graph edge, the two ordered terms in the envy formula contribute $1/N^2$ exactly when the edge crosses $S$. Therefore

$$ E(p)=\frac{|\delta_H(S)|}{N^2}, $$

where $\delta_H(S)$ is the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in $S$.

If $H$ has a bisection cutting at most $k$ edges, its binary allocation satisfies $E(p)\leq B$.

Conversely, if any real feasible allocation satisfies $E(p)\leq B$, the rounding lemma supplies a binary feasible allocation with no greater envy. Its associated bisection cuts at most $k$ edges.

This proves the equivalence. Moreover, $N=O(n^2)$, so writing the dense interaction matrix takes polynomial time, with output length $O(n^4)$. Thus the continuous problem is $\mathsf{NP}$-hard.

4. Membership in $\mathsf{NP}$

Allowing real fractions does not prevent polynomial-length rational certificates. We prove that a rational global minimizer of polynomial bit length always exists.

The feasible set

$$ P=\left\{x:0\leq x_i\leq\mu_i,\ \sum_i x_i=\frac12\right\} $$

is nonempty and compact. Thus the continuous function $f$ attains a global minimum at some $x^\ast$.

Let

$$ J=\{i:0<x_i^\ast<\mu_i\}. $$

Fix every coordinate outside $J$ to its value at $x^\ast$, which is either $0$ or $\mu_i$.

If $J$ is empty, $x^\ast$ is already rational with polynomial bit length. Otherwise, every sufficiently small perturbation supported on $J$ whose coordinate sum is zero is feasible in both directions. First-order optimality therefore gives a scalar $\lambda^\ast$ such that

$$ (c-2Qx^\ast)_j=\lambda^\ast \qquad \text{for every }j\in J. $$

Consider the rational linear system

$$ (c-2Qx)_j=\lambda \quad(j\in J), \qquad \sum_i x_i=\frac12, $$

together with the fixed coordinates, the box constraints, and $-1\leq\lambda\leq1$. This last bound is valid because, for feasible $x$,

$$ 0\leq c_j\leq1, \qquad 0\leq2(Qx)_j\leq1. $$

The system defines a nonempty bounded rational polytope.

The objective is constant on this polytope. Indeed, take two solutions $(x,\lambda)$ and $(y,\eta)$ and put $d=y-x$. Then $d$ is supported on $J$ and $\sum_i d_i=0$. Hence

$$ \nabla f(x)^\mathsf{T}d=0. $$

Subtracting the stationarity equations also gives

$$ -2d^\mathsf{T}Qd = d^\mathsf{T}\bigl(\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\bigr) = (\eta-\lambda)\sum_i d_i = 0. $$

The exact quadratic expansion now yields

$$ f(y)-f(x) = \nabla f(x)^\mathsf{T}d-d^\mathsf{T}Qd = 0. $$

A nonempty bounded rational polytope has a rational vertex. Here its defining coefficients have bit lengths polynomial in $\tau,L$. A vertex is determined by a full-rank subsystem of active linear constraints; Cramer’s rule and determinant bounds therefore give polynomial bit length for its coordinates.

Since the objective is constant on this polytope and it contains $x^\ast$, such a rational vertex is also a global minimizer.

A certificate can consequently specify rational $x$ of polynomial bit length. Feasibility and $f(x)\leq B$ can be checked exactly in $\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)$ bit operations. Thus the problem belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$.

5. Running times and conclusion

For the stated problem, $m=2$ is fixed.

The proof above also gives an exact algorithm taking

$$ 3^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L) $$

bit operations: enumerate whether each coordinate is at its lower bound, at its upper bound, or free; for each choice, solve the corresponding linear stationarity feasibility problem and evaluate one feasible solution. The objective is constant on each such stationary feasible set, and some enumerated set contains a global minimizer.

Thus:

  • The problem is $\mathsf{NP}$-complete already for $m=2$, with equal masses and $Q\in\{0,1\}^{\tau\times\tau}$.
  • It admits an exact $3^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)$ algorithm.
  • A running time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ would imply $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$.
