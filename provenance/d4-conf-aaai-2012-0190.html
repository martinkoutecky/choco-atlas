The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), a fixed tie-breaking order, target \(p\), rational strategic mass \(\epsilon>0\), truthful ranking \(t^\star\), and a rational nonstrategic mass profile \(\nu\) over complete rankings with total mass \(1-\epsilon\), is there a ranking \(r\) such that \(p\) wins when \(\operatorname{eliminate}(\mathrm{veto})\) is run on \(\nu+\epsilon\delta_r\)? Each round eliminates the remaining candidate with the greatest mass ranked last, using the given tie-breaker. The initial society is \(\nu+\epsilon\delta_{t^\star}\); the strategic cohort of mass \(\epsilon\) submits one common report.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq2\) candidates, with target \(p\in C\) and a supplied strict total order for breaking elimination ties. Types are strict complete rankings of \(C\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
The input supplies a nonnegative nonstrategic profile \(\nu\), rational \(0<\epsilon\leq1\) with \(\sum_t\nu(t)=1-\epsilon\), and a truthful ranking \(t^\star\). The initial society is \(\nu+\epsilon\delta_{t^\star}\), where \(\delta_t\) places unit mass on ranking \(t\); let \(\tau\) denote this society's support size and \(L\) the total input bit length.
The strategic cohort replaces its entire mass by one strict complete report \(r\), producing \(\nu+\epsilon\delta_r\). This report is fixed throughout the election; \(r=t^\star\) is allowed, and no improvement over the truthful outcome is required.
Under \(\operatorname{eliminate}(\mathrm{veto})\), restrict every ranking to the remaining candidates in each round and eliminate the candidate with greatest total mass ranking it last; among tied candidates eliminate the earliest in the supplied order. Continue until one candidate remains, who wins.

Is deciding whether some report \(r\) makes \(p\) win solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite unweighted electorates, deciding whether one manipulator can make a designated candidate win under this elimination rule is NP-complete. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.3918).
No result explicitly treating this rational-mass, common-report formulation was located in the literature search.

  • Specified that the earliest candidate in the supplied tie-breaking order is eliminated among candidates tied for greatest last-place mass.
  • Specified restriction of fixed rankings after elimination and termination only when one candidate remains.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The finite one-manipulator problem embeds into the stated rational-mass model by normalization. This embedding preserves the entire elimination sequence, including ties, and has polynomial output size.

1. The finite hardness theorem applies

We use the following known result:

Finite one-manipulator theorem. Given strict complete rankings of finitely many nonstrategic, unweighted voters and a designated candidate \(p\), deciding whether one additional voter can submit a strict complete ranking making \(p\) win under \(\operatorname{eliminate}(\mathrm{veto})\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

This is Theorem 3 of Davies, Narodytska, and Walsh. Their rule uses one fixed ballot per voter and eliminates until one candidate remains. Their hardness construction explicitly specifies a fixed tie-breaking priority, which can be supplied as the order in our instance. Thus neither adaptive reporting nor strategic selection among tied eliminations is needed. [Source: definitions and Theorem 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.3918#page=2), [fixed tie-breaking priority in the proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.3918#page=4).

We now give the reduction from this finite problem.

2. Constructing the rational-mass instance

Let the source instance have candidate set \(C\), target \(p\), nonstrategic rankings \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\), and fixed elimination tie order \(\prec\). We may restrict to the hard instances with \(n\geq1\).

For every ranking \(t\), define its multiplicity by \(a_t=|\{i:v_i=t\}|\). Construct the target instance using the same candidates, target, and tie order, and set

\( \epsilon=\frac{1}{n+1},\qquad \nu(t)=\frac{a_t}{n+1}. \)

Only rankings with \(a_t>0\) are listed in the support of \(\nu\). Choose any strict complete truthful ranking \(t^\star\), for example \(v_1\).

All required conditions hold:

  • Every listed nonstrategic mass is positive and rational.
  • The strategic mass satisfies \(0<\epsilon\leq1\).
  • Since \(\sum_t a_t=n\), we have \(\sum_t\nu(t)=n/(n+1)=1-\epsilon\).
  • The truthful ranking is supplied, and the strategic cohort may replace it by any strict complete ranking.

The truthful ranking imposes no additional restriction on the decision problem: the task explicitly allows every report, including \(t^\star\), and requires no improvement over the truthful outcome.

3. Every report preserves the elimination sequence

Fix any report \(r\). For a nonempty remaining candidate set \(S\subseteq C\), let \(\operatorname{last}_S(t)\) denote the candidate ranked last in the restriction of \(t\) to \(S\).

In the finite election, the last-place count of \(c\in S\), including the manipulator’s report, is

\( V_S(c;r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\operatorname{last}_S(v_i)=c\}+\mathbf{1}\{\operatorname{last}_S(r)=c\}. \)

In the constructed rational-mass election, its last-place mass is

\( M_S(c;r)=\sum_t\nu(t)\mathbf{1}\{\operatorname{last}_S(t)=c\}+\epsilon\mathbf{1}\{\operatorname{last}_S(r)=c\}=\frac{V_S(c;r)}{n+1}. \)

Multiplication by the positive constant \(1/(n+1)\) preserves every strict comparison and equality. Consequently, the candidates with greatest last-place mass are exactly those with greatest last-place count. Because both elections use the same tie order, they eliminate the same candidate whenever their remaining sets coincide.

Initially, both remaining sets equal \(C\). Induction over the rounds therefore shows that their complete elimination sequences coincide. In particular, for every report \(r\),

\( p\text{ wins in the finite election with }r\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\text{ wins under }\nu+\epsilon\delta_r. \)

Taking the existential quantifier over all strict complete reports proves that the reduction preserves both yes-instances and no-instances.

4. Support and encoding bounds

Let \(s\) be the number of distinct nonstrategic rankings in the source instance. Aggregating identical rankings can be done in polynomial time.

Because we chose \(t^\star=v_1\), the initial society has exactly these \(s\) supported rankings. Thus \(\tau=s\leq n\); the construction does not enumerate the \(m!\) possible rankings.

Every mass has numerator at most \(n\) and denominator \(n+1\), each representable with \(O(\log(n+1))\) binary bits. Using candidate indices, the constructed input length is

\( L=O\!\left((s+1)m\log m+(s+1)\log(n+1)\right). \)

This is polynomial in the length of the explicitly listed finite source instance. Hence a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) for the target problem would solve the finite \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem in polynomial time. The target problem is therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is a strict complete report \(r\), encoded as a permutation of the \(m\) candidates. Its length is \(O(m\log m)\).

To verify it, first check that it is a permutation, then simulate the election exactly. There are \(m-1\) elimination rounds. In each round, scan each supported nonstrategic ranking and \(r\) to identify its last remaining candidate, add the corresponding masses, and apply the supplied tie order.

Exact rational arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity here. Write the nonstrategic masses and \(\epsilon\) as \(a_j/b_j\), and let \(Q=\prod_j b_j\). The binary length of \(Q\) is at most the sum of the binary lengths of the denominators, hence \(O(L)\). Replace each mass by the integer weight \(a_j(Q/b_j)\).

These weights sum to \(Q\), because the election’s total mass is one. Every last-place score is therefore an integer between zero and \(Q\), requiring \(O(L)\) bits. Integer comparisons preserve the rational comparisons and ties exactly. A straightforward simulation uses \(O(m^2(\tau+1))\) ranking-inspection operations and polynomially many bit operations.

Thus a winning report can be verified in polynomial time, proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Conclusion

The rational-mass, common-report problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even on instances obtained by assigning the same mass \(1/(n+1)\) to each of \(n\) nonstrategic voters and the strategic cohort. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
