The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(S\), rational lower bounds \(b_s\), and a finite support of types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), linear preferences \(u_t(\rho,s)=v_{ts}\rho_s\), and ordered priority categories at each school, compute an RCE whose utility vector weakly dominates that of every other RCE, or report that none exists. An allocation consists of masses \(x_{ts}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\); for \(q_s=\sum_t x_{ts}>0\), require \(b_s\leq \rho_s\leq 1\) and \(\rho_s q_s=1\), and set \(\rho_s=1\) when \(q_s=0\). Every type assigned positive mass at multiple schools receives equal utility there; if a type strictly prefers a school to its assigned utility, that school must be at its lower bound and every type assigned positive mass there must have higher priority. Every empty school must be strictly worse for each type than its assigned outcome.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools, each with one unit of resources. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses are not normalized: write \(M=\sum_t\mu_t>0\).
Input includes positive rational valuations \(v_{ts}\), rational bounds \(0<b_s\leq1\) with \(\sum_s b_s^{-1}\geq M\), and a priority-category index \(c_s(t)\) for every type and school; smaller indices mean higher priority, and equal indices remain tied. Let \(K\) count all supplied categories and \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Impose no connection by indifference: there is no vector \(r\in[0,1]^S\), school sequence \(s_0,\ldots,s_k\) with distinct endpoints and consecutive schools distinct, and type sequence \(t_1,\ldots,t_k\), such that \(v_{t_j s_{j-1}}r_{s_{j-1}}=v_{t_j s_j}r_{s_j}\) for every \(j\), and \(r_{s_0}=1/a\), \(r_{s_k}=1/d\) for integers \(1\leq a,d\leq\lceil M\rceil\). Type labels may repeat, representing distinct individuals of the same type.
An allocation has real masses \(x_{ts}\geq0\), with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\), and school loads \(q_s=\sum_t x_{ts}\). Require \(b_s\leq\rho_s\leq1\) and \(\rho_s q_s=1\) whenever \(q_s>0\); set \(\rho_s=1\) when \(q_s=0\).
An allocation is a rationing crowding equilibrium (RCE) if each type has a common utility \(U_t=v_{ts}\rho_s\) at every school with \(x_{ts}>0\); whenever \(v_{ts}\rho_s>U_t\), require \(\rho_s=b_s\) and \(c_s(t')<c_s(t)\) for every \(t'\) with \(x_{t's}>0\); and whenever \(q_s=0\), require \(U_t>v_{ts}\) for every type. There is no outside option or additional tie-breaking.

Is computing an RCE whose utility vector weakly dominates that of every other RCE, or reporting that no such RCE exists, solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For finite indivisible students, positive rational linear valuations and no connection by indifference, a grid algorithm terminates at a welfare-greatest RCE in finitely many stages; this is not a polynomial bit-complexity guarantee ([source](https://www.econ.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dp/papers/e-21-006.pdf)).
No complexity result for exactly the divisible-mass problem specified above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Valuations are strictly positive binary rationals, preserving the discrete model's strict monotonicity in resource ratios.
  • Masses are absolute student masses rather than probabilities; total mass is positive and need not equal one. Schools retain one unit of resources, and aggregate capacity must cover total mass.
  • Bounds are strictly positive and at most one. Reciprocal capacities may be noninteger because the discrete integrality convention is explicitly motivated by indivisibility.
  • Priority categories are ordered, with equal-category types tied; the requirement that every admitted type have higher priority means strictly higher category, without tie-breaking.
  • The omitted NCBI promise is restored. Its student-count bound is replaced by the ceiling of total mass, retaining the original unit of student mass.
  • Indifference chains connect distinct schools and omit consecutive repetitions, excluding tautological self-connections that would make the discrete domain empty. Repeated type labels represent distinct individuals in a positive mass.
  • Reporting none includes both absence of any RCE and absence of a welfare-greatest RCE among existing RCEs.

NP-hardness of the requested computation

The requested computation is NP-hard, even on promised instances that always admit an RCE.

More precisely, deciding that no welfare-greatest RCE exists is NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. A single call to the requested computation therefore suffices to solve an NP-complete problem: inspect whether it returns an equilibrium or reports nonexistence.

The reduction below addresses the gap in the notes directly. Every constructed instance has a known baseline RCE. Additional equilibria exist exactly when an underlying hard matching instance is feasible, and any such equilibrium makes a welfare-greatest RCE impossible.

1. The starting problem

We use the following known NP-complete problem:

Given equally many students and schools, with mutually acceptable pairs, possibly tied student preferences, and strict school preferences over acceptable students, does there exist a perfect weakly stable matching?

Here a matching is *weakly stable* if no acceptable pair consists of a student who strictly prefers that school to their assigned school and a school that strictly prefers that student to its assigned student.

The proof of Lemma 2.1 in Manlove, Irving, Iwama, Miyazaki and Morita establishes NP-completeness of perfect weakly stable matching with ties on only one side and strict preferences on the other. Interchanging the sides gives precisely the version above. [*Hard variants of stable marriage*, Lemma 2.1 and its proof, pp. 6–7](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/15/1/hvsm.pdf).

Let the students be $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ and the schools be $s_1,\ldots,s_n$. We may assume $n\geq2$: adding an isolated mutually acceptable student–school pair preserves existence of a perfect weakly stable matching.

For an acceptable pair $(a,s)$, let $r_a(s)$ denote its student preference-group rank, starting at $1$. Let $\ell_s(a)$ denote the student's position in the school's strict acceptable-student order, also starting at $1$.

2. Construction of the RCE instance

Schools, bounds, and masses

Add two schools, $P$ and $R$, and index all schools as
$$ s_1,\ldots,s_n,\ s_{n+1}=P,\ s_{n+2}=R. $$

Choose the smallest positive integer $H$ satisfying
$$ 2^H>3n+1, $$
and set
$$ \varepsilon_i=2^{-iH}. $$

Write $h_s=b_s^{-1}$ for a school's maximum load. Define
$$ h_{s_i}=1+\varepsilon_i\quad(1\leq i\leq n), $$
$$ h_P=n+\varepsilon_{n+1}, \qquad h_R=n+\varepsilon_{n+2}, \qquad b_s=h_s^{-1}. $$

The supplied types are:

| Types | Mass |
|---|---:|
| Original types $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ | $1$ each |
| Guard types $d_1,\ldots,d_n$ | $1$ each |
| Control type $z$ | $n$ |
| Filler types $f_1,\ldots,f_{n+2}$ | $\varepsilon_i$ for $f_i$ |

Consequently,
$$ M=3n+\sum_{i=1}^{n+2}\varepsilon_i =\sum_{s\in S}h_s. $$
Because $H\geq1$ and the finite geometric sum is less than $1$,
$$ 3n<M<3n+1, \qquad \lceil M\rceil=3n+1. $$

Every mass is positive. Every bound satisfies $0<b_s<1$, and the required total-capacity inequality holds with equality.

Valuations

Define positive odd integers $w_{ts}$ and then set
$$ v_{ts}=h_s w_{ts}. $$
Thus, at the lower crowding bound,
$$ v_{ts}b_s=w_{ts}. $$

For an original type $a$, define
$$ w_{a s}= \begin{cases} 2(n+2-r_a(s))+1,& s\text{ is an acceptable original school},\\ 1,& s\text{ is an unacceptable original school}, \end{cases} $$
and
$$ w_{aP}=3,\qquad w_{aR}=1. $$
These values preserve the original student's strict comparisons and ties. Every acceptable-school value is at least $5$.

Put
$$ W=4n+9,\qquad F=4n+11. $$
For guard $d_i$, define
$$ w_{d_i s_i}=w_{d_i R}=W, \qquad w_{d_i s}=1\quad\text{at every other school}. $$

For the control type, define
$$ w_{z s_i}=2i-1\quad(1\leq i\leq n), \qquad w_{zP}=2n+1, \qquad w_{zR}=2n+3. $$
In particular, all its school values are distinct.

Finally, filler $f_i$ has
$$ w_{f_i s_i}=F, \qquad w_{f_i s}=1\quad(s\neq s_i). $$

Priority categories

Every filler has category $1$ at every school.

At an original school $s_i$, assign categories as follows:

  • Guard $d_i$ has category $2$.
  • An acceptable original type $a$ has category $\ell_{s_i}(a)+2$.
  • Every other nonfiller type has category $n+3$.

Thus the designated guard outranks every original type, while acceptable original types retain the school's original strict order.

At the two added schools, use:

| Types | Category at $P$ | Category at $R$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Fillers | $1$ | $1$ |
| Original types | $2$ | $4$ |
| Guards | $4$ | $2$ |
| Control type $z$ | $3$ | $3$ |

All equal indices remain tied, exactly as required.

Encoding size

The construction has
$$ m=n+2,\qquad \tau=3n+3. $$
There are at most $n+3$ distinct supplied categories per school.

Since $H=O(\log n)$, each dyadic denominator has $O(n\log n)$ bits. All effective values $w_{ts}$ are $O(n)$, and there are polynomially many masses, valuations, bounds, and category entries. Hence the construction has polynomial binary input length.

3. Structural properties of every RCE

The following claims allow arbitrary real mass splitting.

Claim 1: Every school is full at its lower bound

For an occupied school,
$$ q_s=\rho_s^{-1}\leq b_s^{-1}=h_s. $$
For an empty school, $q_s=0\leq h_s$ as well.

The allocation row sums imply
$$ \sum_s q_s=M=\sum_s h_s. $$
Every difference $h_s-q_s$ is nonnegative and their sum is zero. Therefore
$$ q_s=h_s,\qquad \rho_s=b_s $$
at every school.

In particular, every school is occupied, and every type's utility at school $s$ is the fixed value $w_{ts}$.

Claim 2: Each filler goes entirely to its designated school

A filler has only two possible attained utilities, $1$ and $F$.

If $U_{f_i}=1$, it strictly prefers $s_i$, where its value is $F$. Its category there is $1$. The RCE priority condition would require every occupant of this nonempty school to have category strictly smaller than $1$, but no supplied type has such a category.

Thus $U_{f_i}=F$, forcing its entire mass to $s_i$.

After these assignments, the load available to nonfiller types is exactly
$$ 1\quad\text{at each original school}, \qquad n\quad\text{at each of }P,R. $$

Claim 3: Each guard uses only its designated school and $R$

Guard $d_i$ can attain only utility $1$ or $W$.

Suppose its utility were $1$. It would strictly prefer $s_i$, where it has category $2$. That school receives one unit of nonfiller mass by Claims 1 and 2. Every nonfiller type has category at least $2$ there. Hence some positive occupant fails the required strict inequality
$$ c_{s_i}(t')<c_{s_i}(d_i)=2. $$
This contradicts the RCE condition.

Therefore $U_{d_i}=W$, and common utility restricts its positive assignments to $s_i$ and $R$.

Claim 4: Original types use only acceptable original schools and $P$

All effective values of an original type belong to
$$ \{1,3\}\cup\{5,7,\ldots,2n+3\}. $$

Suppose an original type $a$ attained utility $1$. It would strictly prefer $P$, where its value is $3$ and its category is $2$.

School $P$ receives $n$ units of nonfiller mass. Every nonfiller type has category at least $2$ there. Thus the strict priority requirement for $a$'s envy cannot hold.

Consequently $U_a\geq3$. The only schools with effective value at least $3$ for $a$ are $P$ and its acceptable original schools.

Claim 5: The control type goes entirely to either $P$ or $R$

All the control type's school values are distinct. By common utility, it can have positive allocation at only one school.

Its mass is $n\geq2$. Each original school has only one unit available after its forced filler assignment. The control type therefore cannot use an original school.

Its entire mass goes to $P$ or $R$.

4. The two possible allocation regimes

The baseline regime: $z$ goes to $R$

The control type consumes all $n$ units of nonfiller load at $R$.

By Claim 3, each guard $d_i$ must then send its entire unit to $s_i$. Those guards consume all nonfiller load at the original schools. By Claim 4, every original type must consequently send its entire unit to $P$.

Together with the forced fillers, this specifies a unique allocation, denoted $E_0$. Its utilities are
$$ U_a^0=3\quad\text{for every original type }a, $$
$$ U_{d_i}^0=W,\qquad U_{f_i}^0=F,\qquad U_z^0=2n+3. $$

This allocation is always an RCE.

Indeed, its loads equal the prescribed capacities, and each type has a common utility. Fillers, guards, and the control type attain their highest school values.

An original type strictly prefers only its acceptable original schools. At any such school, the occupants are its filler, of category $1$, and its designated guard, of category $2$. The original type has category at least $3$, so both occupants strictly outrank it. Every school is at its lower bound, and none is empty.

Thus $E_0$ exists independently of the answer to the matching problem.

The matching regime: $z$ goes to $P$

The control type consumes all nonfiller load at $P$. Claim 4 then forces all original-type mass to acceptable original schools.

The original types have total mass $n$, and the original schools have total nonfiller load $n$. Therefore the original-type allocation satisfies
$$ \sum_{s\text{ original}}x_{as}=1 \quad\text{for every original type }a, $$
$$ \sum_{a\text{ original}}x_{as}=1 \quad\text{for every original school }s. $$

There is no remaining load for guards at original schools. Claim 3 forces every guard to $R$.

We next prove that this regime exists exactly when the starting instance has a perfect weakly stable matching.

5. Equivalence with perfect weakly stable matching

An RCE in the matching regime yields a perfect weakly stable matching

Form the bipartite graph whose edges are the original pairs $(a,s)$ with $x_{as}>0$.

For any set $A$ of original students, let $N(A)$ be its neighboring schools in this graph. All $|A|$ units of its mass go to $N(A)$, and each neighboring school receives one unit of original-type mass. Hence
$$ |A| =\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{s\in N(A)}x_{as} \leq \sum_{s\in N(A)}\sum_{a\text{ original}}x_{as} =|N(A)|. $$

This verifies Hall's condition. Hall's marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching contained in the positive allocation support.

Every matched pair is acceptable. Common utility implies that this matching preserves each original type's RCE utility and hence its attained preference group.

Suppose an original student $a$ strictly prefers an acceptable school $s$ to its matched school. Then
$$ w_{as}>U_a. $$
The RCE priority condition says that every positive occupant of $s$ has strictly higher priority than $a$. In particular, the student matched to $s$ has strictly higher priority.

The school's original strict order is preserved by its categories. Thus $s$ strictly prefers its matched student to $a$, and $(a,s)$ does not block. This excludes every possible blocking pair, proving weak stability.

This argument shows explicitly why fractional splitting creates no additional matching-regime solutions.

A perfect weakly stable matching yields an RCE in the matching regime

Given a perfect weakly stable matching, allocate:

  • Each original type's unit according to the matching.
  • Every guard's unit to $R$.
  • The control type's mass $n$ to $P$.
  • Every filler to its designated school.

Set $\rho_s=b_s$ everywhere.

Every school's load is exactly $h_s$, and every type has a common utility. Fillers and guards attain their highest values.

An original type obtains utility at least $5$, so it strictly prefers neither $P$, $R$, nor any unacceptable original school. If it strictly prefers another acceptable school, weak stability and that school's strict preferences imply that the matched student there outranks it. The school's filler also outranks it. Thus the RCE priority condition holds.

The control type obtains $2n+1$ at $P$. Its only strictly preferred school is $R$, with value $2n+3$. The occupants of $R$ are its filler, of category $1$, and guards, of category $2$; all strictly outrank the control type, of category $3$.

All schools are at their lower bounds and occupied. Every RCE requirement therefore holds.

We have proved
$$ \text{an RCE with }z\text{ at }P\text{ exists} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the matching instance has a perfect weakly stable matching}. $$

6. Verification of the no-indifference promise

For a positive rational number $y$, write $\nu_2(y)$ for its exponent of $2$.

Each capacity has the form
$$ h_{s_i}=B_i+2^{-iH} =\frac{B_i2^{iH}+1}{2^{iH}}, $$
where $B_i$ is the positive integer $1$ or $n$. Its numerator is odd, so
$$ \nu_2(h_{s_i})=-iH. $$
Every effective value $w_{ts}$ is odd. Consequently,
$$ \nu_2(v_{t s_i})=-iH $$
for every type $t$ and school index $i$.

Suppose a prohibited indifference sequence existed, starting at $s_i$ and ending at a distinct school $s_j$.

The starting ratio is $1/a>0$. Each indifference equation gives
$$ \frac{r_{\mathrm{next}}}{r_{\mathrm{previous}}} = \frac{v_{t,\mathrm{previous}}}{v_{t,\mathrm{next}}}, $$
so it propagates a positive rational ratio. Taking $\nu_2$ and telescoping along the entire sequence gives
$$ \nu_2\!\left(\frac{r_{s_j}}{r_{s_i}}\right)=(j-i)H. $$
Since the endpoints are distinct, the absolute value is at least $H$. Repeated types or intermediate schools do not affect this telescoping identity.

But the required endpoint ratios also give
$$ \frac{r_{s_j}}{r_{s_i}}=\frac{a}{d}, \qquad 1\leq a,d\leq\lceil M\rceil=3n+1<2^H. $$
For any positive integer less than $2^H$, its exponent of $2$ lies between $0$ and $H-1$. Therefore
$$ \left|\nu_2(a/d)\right|\leq H-1, $$
a contradiction.

Thus every constructed instance satisfies the exact no-connection-by-indifference promise.

7. Greatest equilibria and the complexity conclusion

If the starting matching instance has no perfect weakly stable matching, Section 5 excludes the matching regime. Claims 1–5 then force every RCE to equal the baseline $E_0$. Hence $E_0$ is welfare-greatest.

If the starting instance does have a perfect weakly stable matching, there is an RCE $E_1$ in the matching regime. Every original type has
$$ U_a^1\geq5>3=U_a^0, $$
whereas the control type has
$$ U_z^1=2n+1<2n+3=U_z^0. $$

No RCE can dominate both $E_0$ and $E_1$. To dominate $E_0$, it would need
$$ U_z\geq2n+3. $$
By saturation and the control type's distinct effective values, this forces the control type to $R$. The resulting allocation is exactly $E_0$, which fails to dominate $E_1$ on every original-type coordinate.

Consequently,
$$ \text{the matching instance has a perfect weakly stable matching} $$
$$ \Longleftrightarrow $$
$$ \text{the constructed instance has no welfare-greatest RCE}. $$

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction to the decision problem “no welfare-greatest RCE exists,” with all constructed inputs satisfying the promise and admitting the baseline RCE.

Given an algorithm for the requested computation, run it once on the constructed instance. Answer yes to the NP-complete matching problem exactly when it reports that no welfare-greatest RCE exists.

Therefore the requested computation is NP-hard under a single-call polynomial-time reduction. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,K,L$ would imply $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.
