The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given goods \(\Omega\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over explicitly encoded multi-peak submodular valuation types \(v_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and fixed rational prices \(p_j\), decide whether there are assignments \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{S\subseteq\Omega}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j\) for every good \(j\), and \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(S\in\arg\max_{R\subseteq\Omega}(v_t(R)-p(R))\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\Omega\) be a set of \(m\ge1\) goods. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type explicitly lists a nonempty family \(\mathcal A_t=\{A_{t,1},\ldots,A_{t,k_t}\}\) of subsets of \(\Omega\), an integer \(1\le s_t\le m\), and a rational \(0\le\epsilon_t<1\), with \(|A_{t,i}|=s_t\) and \(|A_{t,i}\cap A_{t,i'}|\le\epsilon_ts_t\) for distinct indices.
Write \([z]_+=\max\{z,0\}\). A bundle \(S\) is close to \(A\in\mathcal A_t\) when \(|S\cap A|-|S\setminus A|>\epsilon_ts_t\); such an \(A\) is unique. With \(a=|S\cap A|\) and \(b=|S\setminus A|\), define \(v_t(S)=1-[1-a/s_t+\epsilon_t/2]_+[1-b/s_t-\epsilon_t/2]_+\) in this case, and otherwise \(v_t(S)=1-[1-|S|/(2s_t)]_+^2\). These valuations are nonnegative, normalized, monotone and submodular.
The input also gives binary-rational supplies \(q_j\ge0\) and fixed prices \(p_j\ge0\). Define \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\) and \(D_t(p)=\arg\max_{R\subseteq\Omega}\{v_t(R)-p(R)\}\); all utility ties are admissible, including the empty bundle.
Decide whether real masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) exist with \(\sum_{S\subseteq\Omega}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j\) for every \(j\), and \(x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow S\in D_t(p)\). Unused supply is allowed, regardless of its price.
Is this continuous envy-free feasibility problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(K=\sum_t k_t\), and the total input bit length \(L\).
For finite bidders with submodular valuations, polynomial-size characterizations of non-envy-freeness are conditionally ruled out under \(\mathrm{NP}\ne\mathrm{coNP}\); polynomial-time demand computation for multi-peak valuations is also stated ([Ben-Zwi and Newman](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00522v1)).
The truncated multi-peak construction is proved monotone and submodular ([Dobzinski and Vondrák](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.2792)). No result settling exactly the mass feasibility problem above was located.

1. Result

The stated continuous envy-free feasibility problem is in \(\mathrm P\).

More precisely, it reduces to an explicitly constructed rational linear program with \(\mathrm O(Km^3+\tau m^2)\) variables and constraints. Consequently, its total running time is

\[ \bigl(Km^3+\tau m^2+L\bigr)^{\mathrm O(1)} \]

bit operations. The construction includes every utility tie, including demand for the empty bundle, and permits unused supply.

The proof below establishes this directly for the specified valuations.

2. A lower bound valid for every bundle

Fix one type and temporarily suppress its index. Write

\(G(n)=1-[1-n/(2s)]_+^2\)

and

\(H(a,b)=1-[1-a/s+\epsilon/2]_+[1-b/s-\epsilon/2]_+.\)

Lemma 1. Every bundle \(S\) satisfies \(v(S)\ge G(|S|)\).

Proof. If \(S\) is not close to any peak, equality holds by definition.

Otherwise, let \(A\) be its close peak and write \(a=|S\cap A|\), \(b=|S\setminus A|\). Closeness means \(a-b>\epsilon s\). Since \(a\le s\), this implies

\(b<a-\epsilon s\le s(1-\epsilon)\)

and

\(a+b<2s.\)

Thus the positive-part operations in both \(H(a,b)\) and \(G(a+b)\) do not change their factors: \(1-a/s+\epsilon/2\ge0\), while \(1-b/s-\epsilon/2>0\). Expanding gives

\(H(a,b)=\dfrac{a+b}{s}-\dfrac{ab}{s^2}-\dfrac{\epsilon(a-b)}{2s}+\dfrac{\epsilon^2}{4}\)

and

\(G(a+b)=\dfrac{a+b}{s}-\dfrac{(a+b)^2}{4s^2}.\)

Therefore,

\(v(S)-G(|S|)=H(a,b)-G(a+b)=\dfrac{(a-b-\epsilon s)^2}{4s^2}>0.\)

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

The stipulated intersection condition also ensures uniqueness of a close peak: if \(S\) were close to distinct \(A,B\), adding the two strict closeness inequalities would give

\(2\epsilon s<2|S\cap A\cap B|-2|S\setminus(A\cup B)|\le2|A\cap B|\le2\epsilon s,\)

a contradiction.

3. An exact description of all demanded bundles

Continue with one fixed type. All minimum costs below are computable by sorting prices and taking prefix sums.

For each \(n\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\), define

\(c_n=\min\{p(S):|S|=n\}\)

and the nonempty family

\(\mathcal C_n=\{S:|S|=n,\ p(S)=c_n\}.\)

Give this family the score \(G(n)-c_n\).

For each listed peak \(A\), and each integer pair satisfying

\(0\le a\le s,\qquad 0\le b\le m-s,\qquad a-b>\epsilon s,\)

define

\(c_{A,a,b}=\min\{p(S):|S\cap A|=a,\ |S\setminus A|=b\}\)

and

\(\mathcal C_{A,a,b}=\{S:|S\cap A|=a,\ |S\setminus A|=b,\ p(S)=c_{A,a,b}\}.\)

Give this family the score \(H(a,b)-c_{A,a,b}\). Its cost is the sum of the costs of the \(a\) cheapest goods in \(A\) and the \(b\) cheapest goods outside \(A\).

Call each such indexed family a cell. Cells need not be disjoint.

Lemma 2. Let \(M\) be the largest cell score. Then

\(M=\max_{S\subseteq\Omega}\{v(S)-p(S)\},\)

and the entire demand family is exactly

\(D(p)=\bigcup_{\mathcal C:\,\operatorname{score}(\mathcal C)=M}\mathcal C.\)

Proof. Write \(U=\max_S\{v(S)-p(S)\}\).

First, every cell score is at most \(U\). For a cardinality cell, every \(S\in\mathcal C_n\) satisfies

\(v(S)-p(S)\ge G(n)-c_n\)

by Lemma 1. For a peak cell, every member is close to its indexing peak, so its utility equals that cell’s score. Thus \(M\le U\).

Conversely, consider any bundle \(S\).

If \(S\) is not close to a peak, then, with \(n=|S|\),

\(v(S)-p(S)=G(n)-p(S)\le G(n)-c_n\le M.\)

If \(S\) is close to \(A\), with counts \(a,b\), then

\(v(S)-p(S)=H(a,b)-p(S)\le H(a,b)-c_{A,a,b}\le M.\)

Hence \(U\le M\), proving \(U=M\).

Every member of a peak cell with score \(M\) has utility \(M\). Every member of a cardinality cell with score \(M\) has utility at least \(M\), and therefore exactly \(M\), since \(M=U\). This proves one inclusion in the asserted demand identity.

For the reverse inclusion, let \(S\) have utility \(M\). In the applicable chain of inequalities above, both endpoints equal \(M\); therefore every inequality is equality. Thus \(S\) has minimum price in its count class, and that class has score \(M\). \(\square\)

This identity handles all ties. In particular, selecting only one cheapest bundle from a tied cell would generally lose information needed for feasibility.

4. Convex combinations within a cell

For a bundle \(S\), let \(\mathbf1_S\in\{0,1\}^m\) denote its incidence vector.

A cardinality cell has one block, \(\Omega\), with prescribed count \(n\). A peak cell has two blocks, \(A\) and \(\Omega\setminus A\), with prescribed counts \(a,b\).

For either kind of cell, denote its blocks by \(B_1,\ldots,B_d\), its integer counts by \(r_1,\ldots,r_d\), and its minimum price by \(c\). Here \(d\le2\). Define

\(Q=\{w\in\mathbb R^m:0\le w_j\le1,\ \sum_{j\in B_\ell}w_j=r_\ell\text{ for every }\ell\}.\)

Lemma 3. The convex hull of the incidence vectors in this cell is exactly

\(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf1_S:S\in\mathcal C\}=\{w\in Q:\sum_jp_jw_j=c\}.\)

Proof. First, every vertex of \(Q\) is integral. Indeed, if a point of \(Q\) has a fractional coordinate in a block, that block must contain at least two fractional coordinates: its coordinate sum is an integer. Increasing one of those coordinates and decreasing the other by the same sufficiently small positive amount, and also making the opposite perturbation, preserves every constraint of \(Q\). The original point is therefore not a vertex.

Since \(Q\) is a bounded polytope, it is the convex hull of its vertices. Its integral points are precisely the incidence vectors of bundles having the prescribed block counts. Consequently, the minimum of \(\sum_jp_jw_j\) over \(Q\) equals \(c\).

Now take \(w\in Q\) with price \(c\), and express it as a convex combination of these incidence vectors. Every constituent bundle has price at least \(c\). Their weighted average has price exactly \(c\), so every constituent with positive weight has price \(c\). Those constituents all belong to the cell.

The opposite inclusion follows immediately from the defining equations. \(\square\)

5. The polynomial-size feasibility program

For every type \(t\), compute its maximum cell score \(M_t\), and retain precisely its cells with score \(M_t\). Denote their index set by \(\mathcal I_t\).

For each retained cell \(i\in\mathcal I_t\), introduce:

  • a mass variable \(y_{t,i}\ge0\);
  • variables \(z_{t,i,j}\), representing the mass in that cell receiving good \(j\).

Let that cell’s blocks, counts, and minimum price be \(B_{t,i,\ell}\), \(r_{t,i,\ell}\), and \(c_{t,i}\). Impose the following linear constraints:

\(0\le z_{t,i,j}\le y_{t,i}\qquad\text{for every }t,i,j;\)

\(\sum_{j\in B_{t,i,\ell}}z_{t,i,j}=r_{t,i,\ell}y_{t,i}\qquad\text{for every }t,i,\ell;\)

\(\sum_jp_jz_{t,i,j}=c_{t,i}y_{t,i}\qquad\text{for every }t,i;\)

\(\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_t}y_{t,i}=\mu_t\qquad\text{for every }t;\)

\(\sum_t\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_t}z_{t,i,j}\le q_j\qquad\text{for every }j.\)

Proposition. This linear program is feasible if and only if the requested envy-free masses exist.

Proof, forward direction. Suppose the requested masses \(x_{t,S}\) exist. By Lemma 2, every bundle with positive mass belongs to a retained cell. Assign each such bundle to one retained cell containing it, resolving overlaps arbitrarily. Let \(y_{t,i}\) be its cell’s total mass, and let \(z_{t,i,j}\) be the mass within that cell whose bundles contain \(j\).

Every bundle in a cell has the prescribed counts and price. Summing these identities establishes the cell constraints. The type-mass and supply constraints follow from the assumed feasibility of \(x\).

Proof, reverse direction. Suppose the linear program is feasible. If \(y_{t,i}=0\), the bounds force every \(z_{t,i,j}=0\).

If \(y_{t,i}>0\), define \(w_j=z_{t,i,j}/y_{t,i}\). The cell constraints and Lemma 3 express \(w\) as a convex combination of incidence vectors of bundles in that cell. Multiplying its weights by \(y_{t,i}\) gives a distribution of that cell’s mass over its bundles.

Perform this decomposition for every positive-mass cell and add contributions to identical bundles. The resulting masses satisfy the required type totals and supply inequalities. Every positively weighted bundle belongs to a retained cell and hence is demanded, by Lemma 2. \(\square\)

Supply appears only through inequalities, exactly as required.

6. Running time and bit complexity

Before retaining maximum-score cells, the total number of cells is at most

\(N=\tau(m+1)+\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}k_t(s_t+1)(m-s_t+1)\le\tau(m+1)+K(m+1)^2.\)

Thus \(N=\mathrm O(\tau m+Km^2)\). The strict closeness test only decreases this count.

Sort the goods once by price. For each peak, filter that ordering into its inside and outside lists and compute prefix costs. Enumerating and scoring all count pairs then takes

\(\mathrm O(m\log m+Km^2+\tau m)\)

rational arithmetic operations.

Each retained cell contributes \(m+1\) variables and \(\mathrm O(m)\) constraints. The complete program therefore has

\(\mathrm O(Km^3+\tau m^2)\)

variables and constraints.

All constructed rational coefficients have bit length \(\mathrm O(L+\log(m+1))\). To justify this bound, prefix costs are sums of input prices whose total encoding length is at most \(L\); a common denominator can be formed from their input denominators with \(\mathrm O(L)\) bits. The valuation formulas introduce only products of input rationals and integers bounded by a polynomial in \(m\). Exact score comparisons therefore also have polynomial bit complexity.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear-program feasibility applies to this explicitly given finite system of rational linear equalities and inequalities. Equalities may be replaced by two inequalities; no full-dimensionality assumption is needed. See [Khachiyan’s polynomial algorithm for linear programming](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319). Including construction and solution of the program gives the bound

\(\bigl(Km^3+\tau m^2+L\bigr)^{\mathrm O(1)}.\)

This is a bit-polynomial bound; it does not assert a strongly polynomial algorithm.

7. Relation to the cited results

[Ben-Zwi and Newman](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00522v1) discuss finite bidders receiving disjoint bundles and state a polynomial-time demand algorithm for multi-peak valuations. The proof here supplies the additional ingredient needed for this question: an exact representation of every demand tie and its convex hull.

The monotonicity and submodularity construction in [Dobzinski and Vondrák](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.2792) is consistent with the given premises. The present algorithm uses the explicit valuation formulas rather than an inference from submodularity.

Continuous masses admit precisely the convex combinations represented by the linear program. Thus hardness results for integral allocations do not settle this continuous feasibility problem.
