The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a prior log odds λ_π, a population of size \(N\), and a finite set of forecaster types Τ with rational masses μ_r satisfying \(Nμ_r ∈ ℤ\), type \(r\) has residual-signal precision \(q_r\) and observed mean residual log-likelihood ψ̄_r. If each member’s residual signal is conditionally independent with law \(ψ_{r,i}∣Y ∼ ℕ(±κ q_r,2κ q_r)\), compute the aggregate posterior log odds from the observed type means and its ex-ante conditional distribution: γ=λ_π+N∑_rμ_rψ̄_r\), and \(γ∣Y ∼ ℕ(λ_π±Nκ∑_rμ_rq_r,2Nκ∑_rμ_rq_r)\).

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_r\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also contains a binary-encoded integer \(N\geq1\), with \(n_r=N\mu_r\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), binary rationals \(\kappa>0\), \(\lambda_\pi\in\mathbb Q\), and, for each type, \(q_r\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\) and an observed mean \(\bar\psi_r\in\mathbb Q\).
The outcome is \(Y\in\{0,1\}\), with prior log odds \(\lambda_\pi\). All residual log-likelihood signals are independent conditional on \(Y\), with \(\psi_{r,i}\mid Y\sim\mathcal N((2Y-1)\kappa q_r,2\kappa q_r)\) for \(1\leq i\leq n_r\); the observed statistic is \(\bar\psi_r=n_r^{-1}\sum_i\psi_{r,i}\). Zero variance denotes a point mass, and \(q_r=0\) requires \(\bar\psi_r=0\).
Compute the posterior log odds \(\gamma=\log\frac{\Pr(Y=1\mid(\bar\psi_r)_{r\in T})}{\Pr(Y=0\mid(\bar\psi_r)_{r\in T})}\) and the mean and variance of its ex-ante conditional law for each outcome, exactly as rationals.

Is this computation polynomial-time in \(\tau\) and the total input bit length \(L\), including \(\log N\), or is it NP-hard? There is no separate parameter \(m\) in this model.

Result

The computation is polynomial-time in the support size \(\tau\) and total input bit length \(L\), including the encoding of \(N\).

Define the rational numbers
\(\displaystyle S=N\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r\bar\psi_r\)
and
\(\displaystyle M=\kappa N\sum_{r\in T}\mu_rq_r\).

Then the observed posterior log odds are
\(\displaystyle \boxed{\gamma=\lambda_\pi+S}\).

Writing \(\Gamma\) for the posterior log odds before observing the random means, its conditional law is
\(\displaystyle \boxed{\Gamma\mid Y=y\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\lambda_\pi+(2y-1)M,\;2M\right),\qquad y\in\{0,1\}}\).

Thus the conditional means are \(\lambda_\pi-M\) and \(\lambda_\pi+M\), and both conditional variances are \(2M\). All requested outputs are exact rationals.

1. Likelihood ratio of the observed means

Let \(X_r=n_r^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_r}\psi_{r,i}\) denote the random mean whose observed value is \(\bar\psi_r\). Conditional independence and the stated Gaussian distributions give
\(\displaystyle X_r\mid Y=y\sim\mathcal N\!\left((2y-1)\kappa q_r,\;\frac{2\kappa q_r}{n_r}\right)\).

Indeed, averaging preserves the individual mean, while the variance is
\(\displaystyle \frac{1}{n_r^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_r}2\kappa q_r=\frac{2\kappa q_r}{n_r}\).
The random means for distinct types are conditionally independent because they are functions of disjoint collections of conditionally independent signals.

Suppose first that \(q_r>0\). Set \(a_r=\kappa q_r>0\) and \(v_r=2a_r/n_r>0\). The conditional densities \(f_{r,1}\) and \(f_{r,0}\) have equal normalizing constants. Consequently,
\(\displaystyle \log\frac{f_{r,1}(x)}{f_{r,0}(x)} =\frac{(x+a_r)^2-(x-a_r)^2}{2v_r} =\frac{4a_rx}{2v_r} =n_rx\).

If \(q_r=0\), then \(X_r=0\) almost surely under both outcomes. Its likelihood ratio on its support is \(1\), so its log-likelihood contribution is zero. The input requires \(\bar\psi_r=0\), and hence \(n_r\bar\psi_r=0\) also gives the correct contribution.

Because \(\lambda_\pi\) is finite, both prior outcome probabilities are positive. Bayes’ rule and conditional independence therefore yield
\(\displaystyle \gamma =\lambda_\pi+\sum_{r\in T}n_r\bar\psi_r =\lambda_\pi+N\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r\bar\psi_r\).

For continuous coordinates, conditioning at an observed value is understood through the density-based posterior. More formally, use Lebesgue measure for coordinates with \(q_r>0\) and the point mass at zero for coordinates with \(q_r=0\). Both conditional laws have positive densities on this common support, so the displayed formula defines a posterior version at every permitted observation.

The equal-variance Gaussian likelihood calculation also appears in Observation 4 of the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13410v1#S3.SS3). The derivation here establishes it directly for the type means, including zero-variance coordinates.

2. Ex-ante conditional mean and variance

The posterior formula, applied to the random observations, gives
\(\displaystyle \Gamma=\lambda_\pi+\sum_{r\in T}n_rX_r\).

For each outcome \(y\),
\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[\Gamma\mid Y=y] =\lambda_\pi+\sum_{r\in T}n_r(2y-1)\kappa q_r =\lambda_\pi+(2y-1)M\).

Conditional independence makes all cross-covariances vanish. Thus
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Var}(\Gamma\mid Y=y) =\sum_{r\in T}n_r^2\frac{2\kappa q_r}{n_r} =2\kappa\sum_{r\in T}n_rq_r =2M\).

A linear combination of independent Gaussian variables is Gaussian; deterministic coordinates contribute only constants. This proves the asserted conditional law.

Since every \(\mu_r>0\), every \(q_r\geq0\), and \(\kappa N>0\), the case \(M=0\) occurs exactly when all \(q_r=0\). Then \(\Gamma=\lambda_\pi\) almost surely, and the displayed Gaussian law denotes that point mass.

3. Exact bit complexity

Assume the usual explicit binary encoding of a rational by its integer numerator and positive integer denominator. No expansion into \(N\) individual observations is necessary.

Write
\(\displaystyle \mu_r=\frac{u_r}{v_r},\qquad \bar\psi_r=\frac{c_r}{d_r},\qquad q_r=\frac{e_r}{f_r}\),
where all denominators are positive integers.

For the two weighted sums, choose the common denominators
\(\displaystyle D_\psi=\prod_{r\in T}v_rd_r,\qquad D_q=\prod_{r\in T}v_rf_r\).

Then compute the integer numerators
\(\displaystyle A=\sum_{r\in T}u_rc_r\frac{D_\psi}{v_rd_r},\qquad B=\sum_{r\in T}u_re_r\frac{D_q}{v_rf_r}\).

Each indicated division is exact, and
\(\displaystyle \sum_{r\in T}\mu_r\bar\psi_r=\frac{A}{D_\psi},\qquad \sum_{r\in T}\mu_rq_r=\frac{B}{D_q}\).

The sum of the bit lengths of all input denominators is at most \(L\). Therefore each common denominator has \(O(L)\) bits. Each summand defining \(A\) or \(B\) also has \(O(L)\) bits: it is a product of a bounded number of integers whose bit lengths are \(O(L)\). Summing \(\tau\) such integers gives numerators with \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits.

Multiplication by the binary-encoded \(N\), multiplication by \(\kappa\), and addition of \(\lambda_\pi\) preserve this polynomial bound on intermediate and output bit lengths. The subsequent operations producing the two means and their common variance do likewise.

The construction uses \(O(\tau)\) integer arithmetic operations on integers of polynomially bounded bit length. Binary addition, multiplication, exact division, and, if reduced output fractions are desired, greatest-common-divisor computation all have polynomial bit complexity. Hence the entire computation is polynomial-time in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

In particular, a potentially enormous value of \(N\) costs only its binary bit length; the algorithm never iterates over individuals. No additional parameter is needed. NP-hardness under standard polynomial-time reductions would therefore imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
