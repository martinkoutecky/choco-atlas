The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\) and a distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses over a finite support of complete rankings, assume every pair has a strict majority. Under asynchronous rate-one updates, each unit of mass swaps a uniformly chosen adjacent pair when its order opposes the fixed majority tournament \(G_\mu\). Determine whether the ranking distribution \(\nu_s\) converges to a point mass \(\delta_\pi\); output the consensus ranking \(\pi\) if it does, or a directed cycle in \(G_\mu\) otherwise.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq 2\) candidates. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) strict complete rankings with positive binary rational masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Define \(p_{ab}=\mu\{\sigma:a\text{ precedes }b\text{ in }\sigma\}\). Assume \(p_{ab}\neq 1/2\) for every distinct pair, and let the fixed tournament \(G_\mu\) have edge \(a\to b\) exactly when \(p_{ab}>1/2\).
Let \(\nu_s\) be the law at time \(s\geq 0\) of the continuous-time Markov chain on all complete rankings, initially distributed as \(\mu\). At rate \(1\), choose uniformly among the \(m-1\) adjacent positions; swap their candidates exactly when their current order opposes \(G_\mu\), and otherwise leave the ranking unchanged.
Decide whether there exists a ranking \(\pi\) such that \(\lim_{s\to\infty}\|\nu_s-\delta_\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=0\); output such a \(\pi\) if so, and a directed cycle in \(G_\mu\) otherwise.

Is this continuous consensus decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with the requested outputs computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies with an odd number of voters, sequential majority-opposing adjacent swaps reach consensus exactly when the initial majority tournament is acyclic, giving a polynomial-time consensus test. [Source](https://yaakobi.net.technion.ac.il/files/2015/04/29_Network_Voting_ISIT13.pdf)
No result settling exactly the continuous process defined above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the distribution normalization explicit: positive support masses sum to one.
  • Restricted to at least two candidates so that uniform selection of an adjacent position is defined.
  • Interpreted asynchronous rate-one updates as a continuous-time Markov chain whose deterministic law is the evolving mass distribution; unsuccessful proposals leave the ranking unchanged.
  • Specified that uniform selection ranges over all adjacent positions, rather than only eligible swaps.
  • Defined convergence as total-variation convergence as time tends to infinity, allowing the evolving distribution to occupy rankings outside the initial support.

Result

The problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Consensus occurs if and only if \(G_\mu\) is acyclic. Both the consensus ranking and, otherwise, a directed triangle can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The related finite-voter criterion appears as Theorem 8 of [Farnoud et al., *Building Consensus via Iterative Voting*](https://yaakobi.net.technion.ac.il/files/2015/04/29_Network_Voting_ISIT13.pdf). The proof below establishes the criterion directly for the continuous process specified here.

1. Absorption of the continuous process

Write a ranking as \(\sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m)\), with earlier candidates preferred. Define its disagreement count by
\[ D(\sigma)=\bigl|\{(a,b):a\to b\text{ in }G_\mu,\ b\text{ precedes }a\text{ in }\sigma\}\bigr|. \]
Thus \(0\leq D(\sigma)\leq N\), where \(N=\binom m2\).

An adjacent swap changes the relative order of exactly the two swapped candidates: every other candidate lies either before both or after both. Every permitted swap corrects their order according to \(G_\mu\). Consequently, each genuine state change decreases \(D\) by exactly one.

A ranking is absorbing precisely when every consecutive pair follows a tournament edge, that is, when it is a directed Hamiltonian path of \(G_\mu\).

At a nonabsorbing ranking, let \(k\) be the number of permitted adjacent swaps. The genuine transition rate is \(k/(m-1)\), so the waiting time until the next genuine transition is exponential with mean \((m-1)/k\leq m-1\). These rates follow directly by thinning the rate-one Poisson update process according to the uniformly chosen positions.

There can be at most \(N\) genuine transitions. Each waiting time before absorption is almost surely finite, and the conditional expected duration of each is at most \(m-1\). Therefore the absorption time \(T\) satisfies
\[ \mathbb E_\mu[T]\leq N(m-1). \]
In particular, absorption occurs almost surely. This argument applies even when \(G_\mu\) is cyclic.

2. Acyclicity implies consensus

Suppose \(G_\mu\) is acyclic. Every finite acyclic directed graph admits a topological ordering: repeatedly remove a vertex of indegree zero. Such a vertex exists because otherwise repeatedly following incoming edges would eventually produce a directed cycle.

Let \(\pi=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)\) be a topological ordering. Since \(G_\mu\) is a tournament, its edges necessarily satisfy \(a_i\to a_j\) whenever \(i<j\). This also makes the topological ordering unique: every pair has its order prescribed by its edge.

Now consider an absorbing ranking \(\sigma\). Every consecutive pair follows an edge of \(G_\mu\), so the positions of \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m\) in \(\pi\) are strictly increasing. They are a permutation of \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\); hence \(\sigma=\pi\). Thus \(\pi\) is the unique absorbing ranking.

By Section 1, the process reaches \(\pi\) almost surely. More explicitly, for \(s>0\),
\[ \|\nu_s-\delta_\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}} =1-\nu_s(\{\pi\}) =\Pr_\mu(T>s) \leq \frac{N(m-1)}{s}, \]
where the final inequality is Markov’s inequality. The required total-variation convergence follows.

3. A cycle prevents consensus

Fix an edge \(a\to b\), and let \(A_{ab}\) be the set of rankings placing \(a\) before \(b\).

Along any sample path, membership in \(A_{ab}\) can change only when \(a\) and \(b\) swap with each other. The rule permits that swap only from the order \(b,a\) to the order \(a,b\). Therefore the indicator of \(A_{ab}\) is nondecreasing along every sample path, and
\[ \nu_s(A_{ab})\geq \mu(A_{ab})=p_{ab}>\frac12 \quad\text{for every }s\geq0. \]

Suppose \(G_\mu\) contains a directed cycle. For any ranking \(\pi\), some edge \(a\to b\) must oppose \(\pi\): otherwise all tournament edges would go forward in a linear order, which excludes directed cycles.

For this edge, \(\delta_\pi(A_{ab})=0\). Using the definition of total variation as the supremum over events gives
\[ \|\nu_s-\delta_\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \geq |\nu_s(A_{ab})-\delta_\pi(A_{ab})| \geq p_{ab}>\frac12. \]
Thus convergence to \(\delta_\pi\) is impossible for every ranking \(\pi\).

Together, Sections 2 and 3 prove the equivalence.

4. Polynomial-time algorithm and outputs

First compute every pairwise probability exactly and construct \(G_\mu\).

To check the bit complexity, write the supplied masses as \(u_i/v_i\), with positive integers encoded in binary. Set \(Q=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}v_i\) and \(w_i=u_i(Q/v_i)\). The bit length of \(Q\) is bounded by the total denominator encoding length, hence by \(L\). Since the masses sum to one, \(\sum_iw_i=Q\), so all partial sums also have polynomial bit length. Determine each edge by comparing
\[ 2\sum_{i:\,a\text{ precedes }b\text{ in }\sigma^{(i)}}w_i \quad\text{with}\quad Q. \]
The no-ties assumption makes every comparison strict. Recording candidate positions in each input ranking lets all pairwise sums be computed using \(O(\tau m^2)\) integer additions and comparisons, together with polynomially many bit operations to construct the integer weights.

Next enumerate all candidate triples and test for a directed triangle. This costs \(O(m^3)\) graph operations.

Every cyclic tournament contains a directed triangle. Indeed, choose a shortest directed cycle \((v_1,\ldots,v_r,v_1)\). If \(r>3\), the chord between \(v_1\) and \(v_3\) either points from \(v_3\) to \(v_1\), producing the triangle \((v_1,v_2,v_3,v_1)\), or from \(v_1\) to \(v_3\), producing a shorter directed cycle. Both contradict minimality. Hence \(r=3\).

The algorithm therefore returns:

  • A directed triangle if one is found. Section 3 proves that consensus is impossible.
  • Otherwise, the unique topological ordering \(\pi\), computed in \(O(m^2)\) graph operations. Section 2 proves convergence to \(\delta_\pi\).

All arithmetic and graph operations have total bit complexity polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Strict complete rankings give \(p_{ab}+p_{ba}=1\). Together with the no-ties assumption, this ensures that \(G_\mu\) is a tournament: every unordered pair has exactly one directed edge.

The proof consistently uses the fixed tournament determined by the initial distribution. It also correctly treats \(\tau\) as the number of supplied rankings, without enumerating all \(m!\) states.

The definition of \(D(\sigma)\) counts each tournament edge whose endpoints occur in the opposite order in \(\sigma\). Thus \(0\leq D(\sigma)\leq \binom m2\).

The adjacent-swap argument is correct. Swapping adjacent candidates changes their mutual order and leaves every other pairwise order unchanged. A permitted swap corrects precisely their disagreement with \(G_\mu\). Therefore each genuine transition decreases \(D\) by exactly one.

This argument does not require acyclicity. Consequently, even in the cyclic case, there can be at most \(N=\binom m2\) genuine transitions.

A ranking has no permitted swap exactly when every consecutive pair follows a tournament edge. This is precisely the stated directed Hamiltonian-path condition; it does not assert that all nonconsecutive pairs follow tournament edges.

\[ \mathbb E_\mu[T]\leq N(m-1). \]
Every such waiting period is almost surely finite, so absorption occurs almost surely. Initial absorbing states are covered by \(T=0\).
