The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite buyer types \(I\) and seller types \(J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\ge0\) with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\), values \(v_{ij}\), budgets \(b_i\), and reserves \(r_j\), choose common type prices \(p_j\) and mass flows \(x_{ij}\ge0\). Require \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\), \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\), and \(x_{ij}>0\Rightarrow r_j\le p_j\le\min\{b_i,v_{ij}\}\); if seller type \(j\) has leftover mass, require \(p_j=r_j\). Require core stability: whenever \(x_{ij}>0\), \(v_{ij}-p_j\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every \(k\) with \(p_k<b_i\); if type \(i\) has unmatched mass, require \(0\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every such \(k\). For a rational threshold \(K\), does such an outcome exist with \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j)\ge K\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: an explicit list of types with positive binary-rational masses. Here the disjoint buyer and seller supports are \(I,J\), with \(m=|J|\), \(\tau=|I|+|J|\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses satisfy \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\).
Inputs are binary-encoded values \(v_{ij}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), per-buyer hard budgets \(b_i\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), reserves \(r_j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), and a threshold \(K\in\mathbb Q\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An outcome consists of common seller-type prices \(p_j\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) and flows \(x_{ij}\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), satisfying \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\), \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\), and \(x_{ij}>0\Rightarrow r_j\le p_j\le\min\{b_i,v_{ij}\}\).
Require \(\sum_ix_{ij}<\beta_j\Rightarrow p_j=r_j\). Unmatched buyers have utility zero; a buyer matched to seller type \(j\) has utility \(v_{ij}-p_j\). Flows divide a type's population among matches; budgets apply to each matched buyer.
Define core stability by \(x_{ij}>0\Rightarrow v_{ij}-p_j\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every \(k\in J\) with \(p_k<b_i\), and \(\sum_jx_{ij}<\alpha_i\Rightarrow 0\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every such \(k\). Utility ties are allowed without prescribed tie-breaking.
The decision problem asks whether such an outcome exists with \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j)\ge K\).

Is this continuous decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

The discrete counterpart with indivisible matches is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, including equal numbers of buyers and sellers; with nonbinding budgets it admits a polynomial-time algorithm ([Batziou–Bichler–Fichtl](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.06132v2)).
No complexity result for exactly the mass model defined above was located in the literature search.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(|I|=|J|=N\) and every supported type has mass \(1/N\), with \(N\) a power of two.

The key is that, at fixed prices, fractional flows can be rounded to an indivisible matching without losing stability or decreasing surplus after normalization.

1. A matching decomposition lemma. Let \(Q\) be an \(N\times N\) nonnegative matrix whose row and column sums are at most one. Then \(Q\) is a convex combination of partial matching matrices:

\(\displaystyle Q=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\lambda_\ell M^\ell,\qquad \lambda_\ell>0,\qquad \sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\lambda_\ell=1.\)

Here every \(M^\ell\) has entries in \(\{0,1\}\), with at most one entry equal to one in each row and column. Moreover:

  • If \(Q_{ij}=0\), then \(M^\ell_{ij}=0\) for every \(\ell\).
  • If a row or column of \(Q\) sums to one, the corresponding row or column of every \(M^\ell\) sums to one.

*Proof.* Write \(a_i=1-\sum_jQ_{ij}\) and \(c_j=1-\sum_iQ_{ij}\). The block matrix with block rows \((Q,\operatorname{diag}(a))\) and \((\operatorname{diag}(c),Q^{\mathsf T})\) is doubly stochastic: every row and column sums to one.

For completeness, a doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices. Indeed, its positive entries define a bipartite graph. For any set \(S\) of rows, their total mass is \(|S|\), and lies entirely in their neighboring columns, whose total capacity is \(|N(S)|\). Hence \(|S|\leq |N(S)|\), so Hall’s marriage theorem supplies a perfect matching of positive entries. Let \(P\) be its permutation matrix and let \(\varepsilon\) be the minimum entry on that matching. If \(\varepsilon=1\), the matrix equals \(P\). Otherwise, subtracting \(\varepsilon P\) and dividing by \(1-\varepsilon\) gives another doubly stochastic matrix with fewer positive entries. Induction proves the decomposition.

Restricting these permutation matrices to the upper-left block gives the claimed partial matching decomposition of \(Q\). Nonnegativity proves the assertion about zero entries. Finally, a convex combination of numbers in \(\{0,1\}\) can equal one only if every positively weighted number equals one. This proves the saturation assertions. \(\square\)

2. Rounding preserves stability at the same prices. Consider an instance with \(N\) buyer types and \(N\) seller types, all of mass \(1/N\). Suppose \((p,x)\) is a feasible, core-stable outcome. Set \(Q_{ij}=Nx_{ij}\) and apply the lemma.

Each partial matching \(M^\ell\), together with the same price vector \(p\), is a feasible, core-stable indivisible outcome.

To verify this assertion, first suppose \(M^\ell_{ij}=1\). The support property gives \(x_{ij}>0\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle r_j\leq p_j\leq\min\{b_i,v_{ij}\},\)

and, for every \(k\) satisfying \(p_k<b_i\),

\(\displaystyle v_{ij}-p_j\geq v_{ik}-p_k.\)

Thus every selected match satisfies individual feasibility and the required stability inequalities.

Next, suppose buyer \(i\) is unmatched in \(M^\ell\). Its row in \(Q\) cannot sum to one, because every saturated row remains saturated in every matching. Hence \(\sum_jx_{ij}<1/N\). Stability of the original flow therefore gives

\(\displaystyle 0\geq v_{ik}-p_k\quad\text{for every }k\text{ with }p_k<b_i.\)

This is exactly the unmatched-buyer condition.

Likewise, if seller \(j\) is unmatched in \(M^\ell\), its column in \(Q\) is not saturated. Thus \(\sum_ix_{ij}<1/N\), and the original outcome requires \(p_j=r_j\).

These verifications prove the assertion. In particular, the strict budget cutoff causes no difficulty: prices remain unchanged throughout the rounding.

Define the indivisible surplus of \(M\) by

\(\displaystyle W(M)=\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j).\)

The decomposition gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_\ell\lambda_\ell W(M^\ell)=\sum_{i,j}Q_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j)=N\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j).\)

Therefore some core-stable matching \(M^\ell\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle W(M^\ell)\geq N\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j).\)

Conversely, any feasible core-stable indivisible matching \(M\) yields a continuous outcome by taking \(x_{ij}=M_{ij}/N\), with surplus \(W(M)/N\). We have therefore proved the exact equivalence

\(\displaystyle \text{continuous surplus at least }K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{indivisible surplus at least }NK\)

for equal-mass instances.

3. A polynomial reduction with dyadic masses. Use the discrete hardness premise in the question: the indivisible counterpart is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete with equally many buyers and sellers. This is the MBSBM problem stated in Section 7 and Theorem 7.1 of [Batziou–Bichler–Fichtl](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.06132v2#S7). Its core condition uses the same strict cutoff \(p_k<b_i\); we use the counterpart’s individual feasibility and unsold-seller reserve convention.

Take such a discrete instance with \(n\geq1\) buyers, \(n\) sellers, and surplus threshold \(k\). Let

\(\displaystyle N=2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil},\)

so \(n\leq N<2n\). Pad the instance to \(N\) buyers and \(N\) sellers as follows:

  • Give each new buyer budget one.
  • Give each new seller reserve zero.
  • Set every valuation involving a new buyer or new seller to zero.
  • Preserve all original data.

This padding preserves the existence of a discrete core-stable outcome with surplus at least \(k\).

For the forward implication, leave all new agents unmatched and set new seller prices to zero. A new buyer values every seller at zero, so every available alternative has utility \(-p_j\leq0\). Original buyers receive nonnegative utility, and new sellers offer utility zero, so their addition introduces no blocking alternative.

For the reverse implication, take any stable padded outcome and delete every match involving a new agent, retaining original seller prices. Every deleted match has utility zero: feasibility and valuation zero force its price to be zero.

An original buyer whose match is deleted therefore had utility zero before deletion. Its stability inequalities against original sellers remain exactly the inequalities required of an unmatched buyer. An original seller whose match is deleted had price zero; feasibility forces its reserve to be zero, so its retained price satisfies the unsold-seller convention. All other feasibility and stability conditions persist.

Finally, every match involving a new agent contributes zero surplus. For a new seller this follows from valuation and reserve zero. If an original seller is matched to a new buyer, feasibility forces its reserve to be zero as well. Thus projection preserves surplus, proving the padding equivalence.

Now form the continuous instance from this padded data by setting

\(\displaystyle \alpha_i=\beta_j=\frac1N\quad\text{for all supported types},\qquad K=\frac{k}{N}.\)

All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one on each side. By Section 2, this continuous instance is a yes-instance exactly when the padded discrete instance admits surplus at least \(k\), and hence exactly when the original discrete instance is a yes-instance.

The reduction is polynomial: \(N<2n\), the enlarged valuation matrix has polynomial size, and each mass requires only \(O(\log n)\) bits. Its output has \(m=N\), \(\tau=2N\), and total bit length polynomial in the source input length.

4. Conclusion. This is a polynomial many-one reduction from the given \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete discrete problem to the continuous mass problem. Therefore the continuous problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
