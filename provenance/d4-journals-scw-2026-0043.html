The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(E\subseteq D\times L\times G\), rational vote masses \(\mu_e>0\) summing to \(1\), a seat supply \(h>0\) per unit of population, feasible target marginal seat masses \(m_{i,v}\) summing to \(h\) in each dimension, and rational cell bounds \(a_e\le b_e\), compute the minimizer \(x\) of \(\sum_{e\in E}x_e\log(x_e/\mu_e)-x_e\), subject to \(\sum_{e:e_i=v}x_e=m_{i,v}\) and \(a_e\le x_e\le b_e\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(N_1=D,N_2=L,N_3=G\) be finite nonempty sets of districts, lists, and demographic groups, and put \(m=|D|+|L|+|G|\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(E\subseteq D\times L\times G\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_e\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau=|E|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The remaining inputs are binary rationals \(h>0\), \(m_{i,v}\ge0\) with \(\sum_{v\in N_i}m_{i,v}=h\) for each \(i\), and finite bounds \(0\le a_e\le b_e\). Let \(L\) denote the total input bit length.
Promise that \(P=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{E}:\sum_{e\in E:e_i=v}x_e=m_{i,v},\ a_e\le x_e\le b_e\}\) is nonempty; types outside \(E\) receive zero seats.
Define \(F(x)=\sum_{e\in E}[x_e\log(x_e/\mu_e)-x_e]\), using the natural logarithm and \(0\log(0/\mu_e)=0\). The computational problem is to compute its unique minimizer \(x^*\) on \(P\) exactly, with real algebraic coordinates represented by minimal polynomials and rational isolating intervals.

Is computing \(x^*\) polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this exact optimization problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For integral seats, bounded generalized Jefferson proportional apportionments exist whenever the seat-allocation relaxation is feasible and the nonnegative integer marginal deviations satisfy \(\sum_{i=1}^{d}1/(\alpha_i+2)\le1\). [Cembrano et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-025-01623-9)
For continuous entropy optimization with interior optimal solutions, Potra and Ye give an \(\epsilon\)-optimal algorithm with \(O(\sqrt n\,|\log\epsilon|)\) iterations; this is an approximation guarantee rather than an exact algebraic-output guarantee. [Potra and Ye](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/0803043)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required nonnegative real seat masses, nonnegative rational marginals, and nonnegative finite rational cell bounds, following the discrete seat domains.
  • Interpreted feasibility as joint feasibility of the marginals and cell bounds, rather than feasibility of the marginals alone.
  • Assigned zero seats outside the positive-mass support and used the continuous extension of the objective at zero; boundary-feasible instances remain allowed.
  • Defined m as the total number of dimension labels and L as total input bit length.
  • Interpreted computing the minimizer literally as exact computation and fixed the standard minimal-polynomial-and-isolating-interval encoding of real algebraic coordinates.

1. Proven conclusions and remaining gap

Under the standard interpretation that minimal polynomials are output as explicit binary coefficient lists, the requested exact computation cannot run in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. This obstruction holds even when $h=1$ and every seat bound lies in $[0,1]$.

I do not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. Exponential output size does not establish that classification.

Beyond the output-size obstruction, the following results are proved here:

  • The optimizer has a polynomial-size characterization by cubic polynomial equations and inequalities, after identifying coordinates forced to zero.
  • Every optimal coordinate has algebraic degree at most $3^{\min(m,\tau)}$.
  • Comparing an optimal coordinate with a rational threshold is at least as hard as the square-root-sum comparison problem, under polynomial-time many-one reductions.

Throughout, bounds are understood to be rational inputs encoded in binary.

2. Existence, uniqueness, and coordinates forced to zero

Let $A$ be the marginal incidence matrix. Its rows are indexed by pairs $(i,v)$, and
$$ A_{(i,v),e}= \begin{cases} 1,&e_i=v,\\ 0,&e_i\ne v. \end{cases} $$
Write the marginal vector as $M$, so that
$$ P=\{x:Ax=M,\ a_e\le x_e\le b_e\}. $$

For $\mu>0$, define
$$ f_\mu(t)=t\log(t/\mu)-t,\qquad f_\mu(0)=0. $$
This function is continuous on $[0,\infty)$ because $t\log t$ tends to zero as $t$ tends to zero. It is strictly convex on $(0,\infty)$ because
$$ f_\mu''(t)=\frac1t>0. $$
Strict convexity also holds on a segment with one endpoint zero: for $b>0$ and $0<\theta<1$,
$$ f_\mu(\theta b)-\theta f_\mu(b) =\theta b\log\theta<0. $$
Consequently, $F$ is strictly convex on the nonnegative orthant.

The promised set $P$ is nonempty, convex, closed, and bounded by the finite coordinate bounds. Continuity gives existence of a minimizer, and strict convexity gives uniqueness.

Define
$$ J=\{e\in E:\text{there exists }y\in P\text{ with }y_e>0\}. $$

Claim. The optimizer satisfies
$$ x_e^*>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e\in J. $$

The reverse implication requires proof. Suppose $x_e^*=0$ but some $y\in P$ satisfies $y_e>0$. Consider the feasible segment
$$ x(t)=(1-t)x^*+ty,\qquad 0<t<1. $$
Let $Z=\{f:x_f^*=0\}$. For a coordinate $f\in Z$ with $y_f>0$,
$$ \frac{f_{\mu_f}(ty_f)-f_{\mu_f}(0)}t =y_f\log t+y_f\bigl(\log(y_f/\mu_f)-1\bigr). $$
Coordinates with $x_f^*>0$ contribute quantities having finite limits as $t$ tends to zero. Hence
$$ \frac{F(x(t))-F(x^*)}{t} =\left(\sum_{f\in Z}y_f\right)\log t+O(1). $$
The coefficient of $\log t$ is positive because it includes $y_e>0$. Therefore this quotient tends to negative infinity, contradicting optimality for sufficiently small positive $t$.

The other implication follows directly from the definition of $J$: every coordinate outside $J$ is zero throughout $P$.

Moreover, $J$ can be found in polynomial time by solving, for every $e$, the rational linear program
$$ \max\{x_e:x\in P\}. $$
Its optimum is positive exactly when $e\in J$. Exact rational linear programming has polynomial bit complexity; these programs are feasible and bounded by the hypotheses.

3. A polynomial-size cubic characterization

Remove coordinates outside $J$, which are identically zero. For each marginal row introduce a positive real variable $s_{i,v}$. For $e\in J$, put
$$ w_e=\mu_e s_{1,e_1}s_{2,e_2}s_{3,e_3}. $$

Consider the following formula in $x$ and $s$:

  • The marginal equalities hold, with removed coordinates set to zero.
  • For every $e\in J$,

$$ x_e>0,\qquad a_e\le x_e\le b_e. $$

  • Every $s_{i,v}$ is positive.
  • For every $e\in J$, at least one of the following conditions holds:

$$ x_e=a_e,\qquad w_e\le a_e; $$
$$ x_e=w_e; $$
$$ x_e=b_e,\qquad w_e\ge b_e. $$

The fourth condition is a disjunction, imposed separately for each coordinate. If $a_e=b_e>0$, its first and third alternatives together impose no restriction on $w_e$, as required for a fixed coordinate.

Claim. The projection of the solution set of this formula onto the $x$ coordinates is exactly $\{x^*\}$.

Necessity

On the retained coordinates, $F$ is differentiable at $x^*$, with gradient
$$ g_e=\log(x_e^*/\mu_e). $$
Let
$$ B_-=\{e\in J:x_e^*=a_e\},\qquad B_+=\{e\in J:x_e^*=b_e\}. $$
The feasible-direction cone at $x^*$ is
$$ T=\{d:A_Jd=0,\ d_e\ge0\text{ for }e\in B_-,\ d_e\le0\text{ for }e\in B_+\}. $$
Indeed, every such direction admits sufficiently small feasible steps in the positive direction: inactive bounds have positive slack. Optimality therefore gives
$$ g^\mathsf Td\ge0\qquad\text{for every }d\in T. $$

We use the following polar-cone form of Farkas’ lemma: for the cone just displayed, this inequality holds if and only if
$$ g=A_J^\mathsf T\lambda+\alpha-\beta, $$
where $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, $\alpha$ is supported on $B_-$, and $\beta$ is supported on $B_+$. This statement requires no rank assumption or strict feasibility assumption.

Set
$$ s_{i,v}=\exp(\lambda_{i,v}). $$
Then
$$ w_e=\mu_e\exp\bigl((A_J^\mathsf T\lambda)_e\bigr). $$
For a coordinate at neither bound, $g_e=(A_J^\mathsf T\lambda)_e$, so $x_e^*=w_e$.

At a lower bound only,
$$ \log(x_e^*/w_e)=\alpha_e\ge0, $$
which gives $w_e\le a_e$. At an upper bound only,
$$ \log(x_e^*/w_e)=-\beta_e\le0, $$
which gives $w_e\ge b_e$. Fixed coordinates satisfy one of these two inequalities automatically. Thus the formula is satisfied.

Sufficiency

Conversely, suppose $(x,s)$ satisfies the formula. Put
$$ \lambda_{i,v}=\log s_{i,v},\qquad \gamma_e=\log(x_e/\mu_e)-(A_J^\mathsf T\lambda)_e. $$
The alternatives in the formula imply:

  • $\gamma_e=0$ when $x_e=w_e$;
  • $\gamma_e\ge0$ in the lower-bound alternative;
  • $\gamma_e\le0$ in the upper-bound alternative.

For every feasible $y$, the marginal equalities give
$$ (A_J^\mathsf T\lambda)^\mathsf T(y-x)=0. $$
Each coordinate also satisfies
$$ \gamma_e(y_e-x_e)\ge0. $$
For a fixed coordinate this product is zero. Therefore
$$ \nabla F(x)^\mathsf T(y-x)\ge0. $$
The supporting-hyperplane inequality for a convex function, applied at the positive point $x$, gives
$$ F(y)\ge F(x)+\nabla F(x)^\mathsf T(y-x)\ge F(x). $$
This inequality remains valid when some coordinates of $y$ are zero, by continuity of $F$. Hence $x=x^*$ by uniqueness.

Consequences

There are at most $\tau+m$ variables and $O(\tau+m)$ polynomial conditions. Each $w_e$ has degree three. Clearing rational denominators gives integer coefficients of polynomial bit length.

Thus, after polynomial-time preprocessing, the optimizer is characterized by an existential real formula of polynomial size and degree at most three.

In particular, for a rational $q$, both predicates
$$ x_e^*\le q \qquad\text{and}\qquad x_e^*>q $$
reduce in polynomial time to real feasibility: append the corresponding inequality to the formula. On the promised domain, the coordinate-threshold decision problem therefore lies in
$$ \exists\mathbb R\cap\operatorname{co}\exists\mathbb R, $$
where $\exists\mathbb R$ denotes polynomial-time reducibility to feasibility of Boolean combinations of integer polynomial equations and inequalities.

This characterization also proves algebraicity. The Tarski–Seidenberg theorem states that a projection of a semialgebraic set defined over $\mathbb Q$ is semialgebraic over $\mathbb Q$. Its hypotheses apply to the displayed formula. See [Basu, Theorem 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1409.1534#S2.SS1).

Each coordinate projection is the singleton $\{x_e^*\}$. A singleton semialgebraic over $\mathbb Q$ must consist of an algebraic number: otherwise none of the finitely many nonzero rational polynomials in a defining sign formula would vanish at that point, so all their signs would remain unchanged in a neighborhood. That would contradict the set being a singleton.

4. An exponential upper bound on algebraic degree

A more quantitative argument gives
$$ [\mathbb Q(x_e^*):\mathbb Q]\le 3^{\min(m,\tau)}. $$

Let
$$ I=\{e:a_e<x_e^*<b_e\}. $$
Coordinates outside $I$ equal rational bounds. Fixing them leaves
$$ A_Iy=c, $$
with rational $c$ and positive $y=x_I^*$.

If $I$ is empty, all coordinates are rational. Otherwise, put $r=\operatorname{rank}(A_I)$ and choose $r$ independent rows, forming a matrix $B$.

Every direction in $\ker A_I$ admits sufficiently small perturbations of both signs, because coordinates in $I$ are strictly between their bounds. First-order stationarity therefore implies
$$ \bigl(\log(y_e/\mu_e)\bigr)_{e\in I}\in\operatorname{row}(B). $$
There is consequently a positive vector $s\in\mathbb R_{>0}^r$ such that
$$ y_e=\mu_e\prod_{j=1}^r s_j^{B_{j,e}}. $$
Each column of $B$ contains at most three ones. Define
$$ y_e(s)=\mu_e\prod_{j=1}^r s_j^{B_{j,e}} $$
and
$$ H_j(s)=\sum_{e\in I}B_{j,e}y_e(s)-c_j,\qquad 1\le j\le r. $$
These are rational polynomials of degree at most three.

The system $H(s)=0$ has exactly one positive solution. To see this, suppose $s,t$ are positive solutions, and write $y=y(s)$ and $z=y(t)$. Then
$$ B(y-z)=0 $$
and
$$ \log(y/\mu)-\log(z/\mu) =B^\mathsf T(\log s-\log t). $$
Taking the inner product gives
$$ 0=\sum_{e\in I}(y_e-z_e)(\log y_e-\log z_e). $$
Each summand is nonnegative, and it is positive whenever $y_e\ne z_e$. Thus $y=z$. Since $B$ has full row rank, $B^\mathsf T$ is injective, so $s=t$.

The singleton argument from Section 3 now shows that every $s_j$ is algebraic.

The Jacobian at this positive solution is
$$ DH(s)=B\operatorname{diag}(y)B^\mathsf T \operatorname{diag}(s_1^{-1},\ldots,s_r^{-1}). $$
The first matrix is positive definite: for $v\ne0$,
$$ v^\mathsf TB\operatorname{diag}(y)B^\mathsf Tv =\sum_{e\in I}y_e(B^\mathsf Tv)_e^2>0. $$
Here positivity follows from $y_e>0$ and injectivity of $B^\mathsf T$. Hence the Jacobian is nonsingular.

The affine Bézout bound states that $r$ polynomials in $r$ variables, of positive degrees $d_1,\ldots,d_r$, have at most $\prod_jd_j$ isolated common zeros over $\mathbb C$. See [Mondal, Theorem I.2](https://pinakimondal.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/p1_to_16.pdf). Applied here, this bound is at most $3^r$.

Let $K=\mathbb Q(s_1,\ldots,s_r)$. Every embedding of $K$ into $\mathbb C$ sends $s$ to a common zero of $H$ with nonzero Jacobian determinant. Different embeddings give different tuples because the coordinates generate $K$. Since characteristic zero implies separability,
$$ [K:\mathbb Q]\le3^r. $$
Every $y_e$ belongs to $K$, and the remaining optimal coordinates are rational. Therefore
$$ [\mathbb Q(x_e^*):\mathbb Q]\le3^r\le3^{\min(m,\tau)}. $$

This is a degree bound, not a polynomial-time output guarantee.

5. Square-root-sum reduces to an optimal-coordinate threshold

Define the decision problem $\mathsf{Threshold}$ as follows: given a promised instance, a supported type $e$, and a rational $q$, decide whether
$$ x_e^*\le q. $$

I prove a polynomial-time many-one reduction to this problem from
$$ \text{given binary integers }A_1,\ldots,A_n,K,\quad \text{decide whether }\sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{A_i}\le K. $$
Square-root-sum comparisons are a standard exact-computation problem; see [Eisenbrand, Haeberle, and Singer](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.54).

Remove radicands zero, and remove radicands one while subtracting their number from $K$. Cases with no remaining radicands are decidable directly. We can therefore assume $A_i\ge2$.

Write
$$ A_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^nA_i. $$
If $K<n$, the answer is no because each square root exceeds one. If $K\ge A_{\mathrm{tot}}$, the answer is yes because $\sqrt{A_i}<A_i$. Such cases map to fixed yes or no instances of $\mathsf{Threshold}$.

It remains to handle
$$ n\le K<A_{\mathrm{tot}}. $$

Construction

Introduce an auxiliary index $0$, and set
$$ A_0=1,\qquad C_0=2,\qquad C_i=A_i-1\quad(1\le i\le n). $$
Put
$$ C=\sum_{i=0}^nC_i=2+A_{\mathrm{tot}}-n,\qquad R=1+A_{\mathrm{tot}}-K. $$
The assumed range of $K$ gives
$$ 2\le R\le C-1. $$

For every $i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}$, create district vertices $d_{i,0},d_{i,1}$ and list vertices $\ell_{i,0},\ell_{i,1}$. Use two common demographic vertices $g_+,g_-$ and four supported types:
$$ p_i=(d_{i,0},\ell_{i,0},g_+),\qquad r_i=(d_{i,1},\ell_{i,1},g_+), $$
$$ q_i=(d_{i,0},\ell_{i,1},g_-),\qquad s_i=(d_{i,1},\ell_{i,0},g_-). $$
Give each of these district and list vertices marginal $C_i$, and bound each of the four coordinates between zero and $C_i$.

The district and list equations imply exactly
$$ x_{p_i}=x_{r_i}=y_i,\qquad x_{q_i}=x_{s_i}=C_i-y_i. $$
Indeed, the equations at $d_{i,0}$ and $\ell_{i,0}$ first give $x_{q_i}=x_{s_i}$; the remaining equations then give the displayed form.

Give $g_+$ marginal $2R$ and $g_-$ marginal $2(C-R)$. Their equations are equivalent to
$$ \sum_{i=0}^ny_i=R. $$

Add a type $e_\dagger$ using three new vertices, including a third demographic vertex. Fix its seat count and its three marginals at one. The common marginal total is
$$ h=2C+1. $$
Feasibility is witnessed by
$$ y_i=\frac{RC_i}{C}, $$
which lies strictly between zero and $C_i$.

For the masses, put
$$ M=\sum_{i=0}^n(A_i+3)=A_{\mathrm{tot}}+3n+4. $$
Choose an integer $b$ with $2^b>M$, and let $c=2^{-b}$. Assign
$$ \mu_{p_i}=A_ic,\qquad \mu_{r_i}=\mu_{q_i}=\mu_{s_i}=c, $$
and
$$ \mu_{e_\dagger}=1-Mc. $$
All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one.

This construction has
$$ \tau=4n+5,\qquad m=4n+9. $$
All margins and bounds are integers, and $b$ and all numerical bit lengths are polynomially bounded by the square-root-sum input length. Thus the construction is a polynomial-time reduction and satisfies the feasibility promise.

Optimizer calculation

Let $f_i(y)$ be the objective contribution from the four types at index $i$. For $0<y<C_i$,
$$ f_i'(y)=2\log\!\left(\frac{y}{C_i-y}\right)-\log A_i. $$
For $s>0$, define
$$ y_i(s)=\frac{C_i\sqrt{A_i}\,s}{1+\sqrt{A_i}\,s}, \qquad T(s)=\sum_{i=0}^ny_i(s). $$
The function $T$ is continuous and strictly increasing, since
$$ T'(s)=\sum_{i=0}^n \frac{C_i\sqrt{A_i}}{(1+\sqrt{A_i}\,s)^2}>0. $$
Its limits at zero and infinity are zero and $C$. Hence there is a unique $s^*>0$ satisfying $T(s^*)=R$.

At this point,
$$ f_i'(y_i(s^*))=2\log s^* $$
for every $i$. For any other feasible vector $z$, convexity gives
$$ \sum_i f_i(z_i) \ge \sum_i f_i(y_i(s^*)) +2\log s^*\sum_i(z_i-y_i(s^*)) = \sum_i f_i(y_i(s^*)). $$
Thus the optimizer is $y_i=y_i(s^*)$. In particular, every nonfixed supported coordinate is strictly between its bounds.

At $s=1$, for $i\ge1$,
$$ y_i(1) =\frac{(A_i-1)\sqrt{A_i}}{1+\sqrt{A_i}} =A_i-\sqrt{A_i}, $$
while $y_0(1)=1$. Consequently,
$$ T(1)=1+A_{\mathrm{tot}}-\sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{A_i}. $$
It follows that
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{A_i}\le K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T(1)\ge R \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s^*\le1. $$
Finally,
$$ x_{p_0}^*=y_0(s^*)=\frac{2s^*}{1+s^*}, $$
so
$$ \boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{A_i}\le K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{p_0}^*\le1.} $$
Equality is preserved: equality on the left holds exactly when $s^*=1$, hence exactly when $x_{p_0}^*=1$.

This proves the claimed many-one reduction to $\mathsf{Threshold}$, including on instances with only three demographic groups and interior nonfixed optimal coordinates. It does not constitute an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.

6. Exponential explicit output, even with normalized seats

For completeness, the output-size obstruction is proved here, with the normalization $h=1$.

A support with a large one-dimensional kernel

Fix $n\ge1$. Start with variables
$$ u_0,\ldots,u_n,\qquad v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1} $$
and equations
$$ u_i-v_i=0,\qquad u_i+v_i-u_{i+1}=0, \qquad 0\le i<n. $$
Let $B$ be their coefficient matrix. Its kernel is one-dimensional, generated by
$$ w_{u_i}=2^i,\qquad w_{v_i}=2^i, $$
because the equations force $v_i=u_i$ and $u_{i+1}=2u_i$.

There are $5n$ variable occurrences in these equations. Let $\Omega$ index them. For an occurrence $o$, write $c(o)$ for its variable, $r(o)$ for its equation, $\sigma(o)\in\{1,-1\}$ for its coefficient, and $w_o=w_{c(o)}$. Cyclically order the occurrences of each variable, and let $s(o)$ be the successor.

Create district vertices $d_o$, list vertices $\ell_o$, and demographic vertices $g_r$, together with an additional vertex $g_\circ$. Introduce two supported types per occurrence:

  • If $\sigma(o)=1$,

$$ p_o=(d_o,\ell_o,g_{r(o)}),\qquad q_o=(d_o,\ell_{s(o)},g_\circ). $$

  • If $\sigma(o)=-1$,

$$ p_o=(d_o,\ell_o,g_\circ),\qquad q_o=(d_o,\ell_{s(o)},g_{r(o)}). $$

These types are distinct. Different occurrences have different districts. Within one occurrence, the list vertices differ unless the occurrence cycle has length one; in that case the demographic vertices differ.

Let $A$ be the resulting incidence matrix. Its kernel is generated by
$$ z_{p_o}=w_o,\qquad z_{q_o}=-w_o. $$
To verify this, let $\eta\in\ker A$. The district equation gives
$$ \eta_{q_o}=-\eta_{p_o}. $$
The list equation at $\ell_o$ gives
$$ \eta_{p_o}+\eta_{q_{s^{-1}(o)}}=0. $$
Thus all $p$ coordinates belonging to one original variable agree. Calling their common value $\xi_c$, the equation at $g_r$ becomes precisely the original equation
$$ \sum_{o:r(o)=r}\sigma(o)\xi_{c(o)}=0. $$
The equation at $g_\circ$ is redundant: the sum of all demographic-row expressions is $\sum_o(\eta_{p_o}+\eta_{q_o})=0$. Therefore $\ker A$ is identified with $\ker B$, proving the assertion.

Put
$$ S=\sum_{o\in\Omega}w_o. $$
For each $i$, the first equation contributes $2\cdot2^i$ and the second contributes $4\cdot2^i$. Hence
$$ S=6(2^n-1). $$

Margins, bounds, and masses

Write $z=z^+-z^-$, with
$$ z^+_{p_o}=w_o,\quad z^+_{q_o}=0,\qquad z^-_{p_o}=0,\quad z^-_{q_o}=w_o. $$
Since $Az=0$, we have $Az^+=Az^-$.

Set
$$ H=2^{n+4}. $$
Choose the ordinary margins to be $Az^-/H$, and impose bounds
$$ 0\le x_{p_o},x_{q_o}\le\frac{w_o}{H}. $$
The equality solutions form the affine line
$$ x=\frac{z^-+tz}{H}. $$
The bounds restrict its parameter exactly to $0\le t\le1$, giving
$$ x_{p_o}=\frac{w_ot}{H},\qquad x_{q_o}=\frac{w_o(1-t)}H. $$

Add a type $e_\dagger$ on three new vertices and fix its seat count and its three marginals at
$$ 1-\frac SH. $$
Each marginal family now sums to one, so $h=1$. All seat bounds lie in $[0,1]$: ordinary bounds are at most $2^n/H=1/16$, and the fixed additional seat lies strictly between zero and one.

Choose an occurrence $o_0$ of $u_0$, whose weight is one. Assign
$$ \mu_{p_o}=\mu_{q_o}=\frac{w_o}{H}, $$
except that
$$ \mu_{p_{o_0}}=\frac2H. $$
Finally, assign
$$ \mu_{e_\dagger}=1-\frac{2S+1}{H}. $$
These masses sum to one and are positive because
$$ 2S+1=12(2^n-1)+1<2^{n+4}=H. $$

The instance is feasible, for example at $t=1/2$. Its sizes are
$$ \tau=10n+1,\qquad m=12n+4,\qquad L=O(n^2). $$
Indeed, there are $O(n)$ numerical entries, each with $O(n)$ bits, and support identifiers need only $O(\log n)$ bits. All numerical inputs are dyadic rationals.

Optimizer and minimal polynomial

Let $\Phi(t)$ be the objective on this feasible segment. The fixed additional type contributes a constant. Each ordinary pair contributes
$$ \frac{w_o}{H}\log\!\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right) $$
to its derivative. Doubling the selected mass subtracts $\log2/H$. Therefore
$$ \Phi'(t)=\frac1H \left[ S\log\!\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)-\log2 \right], $$
and
$$ \Phi''(t)=\frac SH\left(\frac1t+\frac1{1-t}\right)>0. $$
The derivative tends to negative infinity at zero and positive infinity at one. The unique optimizer is consequently interior and satisfies
$$ \left(\frac{t^*}{1-t^*}\right)^S=2. $$
Writing $\alpha=2^{1/S}$,
$$ t^*=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}. $$

We use Eisenstein’s criterion in this precise form: a primitive integer polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ if a prime divides every coefficient except the leading coefficient, does not divide the leading coefficient, and its square does not divide the constant coefficient. For $Y^S-2$, the prime two meets all these conditions. Hence
$$ [\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=S. $$
The transformations
$$ t^*=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha},\qquad \alpha=\frac{t^*}{1-t^*} $$
have nonzero denominators, so
$$ [\mathbb Q(t^*):\mathbb Q]=S. $$

The selected optimal coordinate is
$$ x_{p_{o_0}}^*=\frac{t^*}{H}. $$
It also has degree $S$, because multiplication by the nonzero rational $H$ preserves its generated field.

Since $S$ is even, its monic minimal polynomial is
$$ P(X)=H^{-S}\left[2(1-HX)^S-(HX)^S\right]. $$
This polynomial vanishes at the selected coordinate, has degree $S$, and is monic. The degree calculation therefore identifies it as the minimal polynomial.

For every $0\le k<S$, its coefficient of $X^k$ is
$$ 2(-1)^k\binom Sk H^{k-S}, $$
