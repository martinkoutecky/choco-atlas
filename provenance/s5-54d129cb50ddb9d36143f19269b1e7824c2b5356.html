The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite worker and project type sets with rational masses, capacities, acceptable type pairs, and strict rankings of counterpart types, represent feasible mass assignments by distributions over integral partner-type bundles and compatible pairings. For each pair of assignments \(M,N\), couple the bundle distributions within each type in the way that minimizes the integrated paper-style vote: cancel common partner types, pad with nulls, and use the least favorable bijection, treating copies of one type as equivalent. Find a robustly popular assignment, whose worst-coupling margin against every feasible \(N\) is nonnegative, with maximum matched-pair mass, or report that none exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(W\) and \(P\) be finite worker and project type sets, with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq W\times P\), \(m=|E|\), and strict rankings of acceptable counterpart types. Each type \(t\) has a positive integer per-person capacity \(c_t\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|W|+|P|\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Capacities are binary encoded; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A bundle \(S\) for type \(t\) is a multiset of acceptable counterpart types with \(|S|\leq c_t\); repeated types represent distinct partners of that type. Write \(n_s(S)\) for the multiplicity of \(s\). An assignment \(M\) specifies a probability distribution \(p_t^M\) over bundles for each type. It is feasible when \(\mu_w\sum_S p_w^M(S)n_p(S)=\mu_p\sum_T p_p^M(T)n_w(T)\) for every \((w,p)\in E\). These equalities define compatible pairings of partner incidences in the nonatomic populations; one individual cannot partner twice with the same individual. Its matched-pair mass is \(Q(M)=\sum_{w\in W}\mu_w\sum_Sp_w^M(S)|S|\).
For bundles \(S,T\) of type \(t\), cancel common occurrences with multiplicity, then pad the shorter remainder with nulls. Every acceptable type ranks above null; copies of the same type are equivalent. Define \(\delta_t(S,T)\) as the minimum, over bijections between the remainders, of the sum of comparison votes: \(+1\) for a preferred first entry, \(-1\) for a preferred second entry, and \(0\) for equivalent entries; empty remainders contribute \(0\).
Let \(\Gamma(p,q)\) denote all couplings with marginals \(p,q\). Define \(D(M,N)=\sum_t\mu_t\min_{\gamma_t\in\Gamma(p_t^M,p_t^N)}\sum_{S,T}\gamma_t(S,T)\delta_t(S,T)\). Couplings are chosen independently within types and need not preserve partner identities or pairings across assignments. An assignment \(M\) is robustly popular if \(D(M,N)\geq0\) for every feasible \(N\).
Is finding a robustly popular assignment of maximum matched-pair mass, or reporting that none exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?
For finite societies with strict individual rankings and positive integer capacities, a maximum-size popular many-to-many matching under the same least-favorable bundle comparison is computable in linear time. [Brandl–Kavitha](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.07531v2)
No result settling the precise nonatomic problem with adversarial within-type couplings was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Capacities are positive integers per individual, as in the discrete definition, and are binary encoded.
  • Masses are positive binary rationals normalized jointly across both sides to sum to one; the two sides need not have equal total mass.
  • Bundles are multisets: multiple partners of one type are allowed, but they represent distinct individuals, preserving the discrete prohibition on repeated individual pairs.
  • Feasibility means equality of worker and project partner-incidence masses for each acceptable type pair; compatible pairings impose no additional aggregate bounds in the nonatomic model.
  • Bundle distributions and couplings may have arbitrary real probabilities; only the input masses and numerical parameters must be rational.
  • Worst-case couplings are independent across types and unconstrained by pairings between individuals in the two assignments.
  • The size objective counts each matched pair once, through the worker-side incidence mass; empty bundles and incomplete assignments are allowed.

Status and new results

The general polynomial-time-versus-$\mathrm{NP}$-hardness question remains unresolved. I prove two tractable cases:

  • Forest type graphs: maximum-mass robust popularity, including reporting nonexistence, is solvable in polynomial time for arbitrary positive rational masses and binary capacities.
  • Equal effective capacities: the problem is polynomial-time solvable on arbitrary bipartite type graphs when $\mu_t c_t$ is the same for every type.

The forest result addresses certificate selection directly. Its algorithm represents each relevant subtree optimization function using only polynomially many affine functions indexed by paths.

1. An exact characterization by incidence flows

Put
$$ b_t=\mu_t c_t, \qquad \mathcal O_t=N(t)\cup\{\bot\}, $$
where $\bot$ is null. Extend the strict ranking of type $t$ by putting $\bot$ last, and let $v_t(a,d)$ be its comparison vote between options $a,d\in\mathcal O_t$.

For a feasible assignment, define
$$ f_{wp} = \mu_w\mathbb E[n_p(S)] = \mu_p\mathbb E[n_w(T)], \qquad u_t=b_t-\sum_{e\ni t}f_e. $$
Thus
$$ f_e\geq0, \qquad u_t\geq0, \qquad \sum_{e\ni t}f_e+u_t=b_t, \qquad Q=\sum_{e\in E}f_e. $$

An option is active when its expected padded multiplicity is positive. Consequently, a real option is active exactly when its edge has positive flow, and null is active exactly when $u_t>0$.

For $\lambda\in\mathbb R^E$, define
$$ h_w(p)=\lambda_{wp}, \qquad h_p(w)=-\lambda_{wp}, \qquad h_t(\bot)=0. $$

1.1 Certificate theorem

Theorem. A feasible assignment is robustly popular if and only if there exists $\lambda$ satisfying
$$ h_t(d)-h_t(a)\leq v_t(a,d) $$
for every type $t$, every active option $a$, and every option $d\in\mathcal O_t$.

Proof. For a bundle $S$ of type $t$, let $r_t(S)\in\mathbb R^E$ be its signed incidence vector: worker incidences have positive sign and project incidences negative sign.

For every source bundle having positive probability, put
$$ x_{t,S}=\mu_t p_t^M(S)>0. $$
Consider the finite linear program
$$ \min\sum_{t,S,T}z_{t,S,T}\delta_t(S,T) $$
subject to
$$ \sum_Tz_{t,S,T}=x_{t,S}, \qquad \sum_{t,S,T}z_{t,S,T}r_t(T)=0, \qquad z_{t,S,T}\geq0. $$

Its target marginal defines a challenger by
$$ p_t^N(T)=\frac1{\mu_t}\sum_Sz_{t,S,T}. $$
The first constraints make these probability distributions, and the second constraint expresses challenger feasibility. Conversely, every feasible challenger and every family of within-type couplings give such variables. The program therefore has value $\min_N D(M,N)$.

There are finitely many bundles, even though their number can be large. The feasible region is nonempty, because the diagonal choice $z_{t,S,S}=x_{t,S}$ is feasible, and bounded, because each row has fixed nonnegative sum. Linear-programming strong duality applies.

The dual has free variables $\alpha_{t,S}$ and $\lambda$, with constraints
$$ \alpha_{t,S}+\lambda\cdot r_t(T)\leq\delta_t(S,T). $$
Its objective is $\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\alpha_{t,S}$.

If $M$ is robust, the optimum is zero: robustness gives a lower bound of zero, while the diagonal primal solution has value zero. Taking $T=S$ in the dual gives
$$ \alpha_{t,S}\leq-\lambda\cdot r_t(S). $$
Feasibility of $M$ gives
$$ \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}r_t(S)=0. $$
Since all retained $x_{t,S}$ are positive, attaining dual objective zero forces
$$ \alpha_{t,S}=-\lambda\cdot r_t(S) $$
for every retained source bundle. Hence
$$ \lambda\cdot\bigl(r_t(T)-r_t(S)\bigr)\leq\delta_t(S,T). $$

Pad a supported source bundle to capacity $c_t$ using nulls. If it contains an occurrence of an active option $a$, replace that occurrence by $d$. This produces a valid bundle, and cancellation leaves precisely the comparison $v_t(a,d)$. The preceding inequality becomes
$$ h_t(d)-h_t(a)\leq v_t(a,d). $$
This proves necessity.

For sufficiency, pad both compared bundles to capacity and cancel common occurrences, including common nulls. This produces exactly the comparison specified in the question: nulls remain only on the shorter real remainder.

Every remaining source entry is active. Summing the certificate inequalities along any bijection between the remainders gives
$$ \lambda\cdot\bigl(r_t(T)-r_t(S)\bigr) \leq \text{the vote sum of that bijection}. $$
The left side is independent of the bijection, so the inequality holds with the minimum vote sum $\delta_t(S,T)$.

Integrating against arbitrary within-type couplings and summing over types yields
$$ \sum_t\mu_t\sum_{S,T}\gamma_t(S,T)\delta_t(S,T) \geq \lambda\cdot \left( \sum_t\mu_t\mathbb E_N[r_t(T)] - \sum_t\mu_t\mathbb E_M[r_t(S)] \right) =0. $$
The equality follows from feasibility of both assignments. Thus every family of couplings has nonnegative total margin, proving robustness. $\square$

In particular, robustness depends only on $f$ and $u$, since these determine the active options.

1.2 Two admissible options and five-valued certificates

For fixed $\lambda$, call an option $a$ admissible at $t$ when
$$ h_t(d)-h_t(a)\leq v_t(a,d) \qquad \text{for every }d\in\mathcal O_t. $$

There are at most two admissible options at any type. Indeed, suppose
$$ a\succ_t d\succ_t z $$
were three admissible options. Admissibility gives
$$ h_t(a)-h_t(d)\leq-1, \qquad h_t(d)-h_t(z)\leq-1, \qquad h_t(z)-h_t(a)\leq1. $$
Their sum is the contradiction $0\leq-1$.

Therefore every robust flow has at most two positive real edges at a type. If $u_t>0$, null is also active, so there is at most one positive real edge.

Every robust assignment has a certificate in
$$ V^E, \qquad V=\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}. $$
Here is a proof of the finite domain.

For every active real option, comparison with null gives $h_t(a)\geq-1$. A positive edge is active at both endpoints, whose potentials are opposite, so
$$ -1\leq\lambda_e\leq1 $$
on positive edges. Hence every active option, including null, has potential in $[-1,1]$.

Every type has an active option because its padded bundles have positive total size $c_t$. Comparing an active option at a worker with any incident edge gives $\lambda_e\leq2$. The analogous project comparison gives $-\lambda_e\leq2$. Thus all certificate coordinates lie in $[-2,2]$.

After substituting the definitions of $h_t$, every certificate constraint is a difference inequality between two $\lambda$ coordinates, or an individual bound, with integer right side. If $x-y\leq k$ for integer $k$, then
$$ \lfloor x\rfloor-\lfloor y\rfloor\leq k. $$
Integer individual bounds are also preserved by flooring. Flooring all coordinates therefore gives a certificate in $V^E$.

1.3 The resulting finite optimization problem

We can now optimize directly over certified flows:
$$ \lambda\in V^E, \qquad f_e,u_t\geq0, \qquad \sum_{e\ni t}f_e+u_t=b_t, $$
subject to admissibility of every option carrying positive flow or positive unused capacity.

Conversely, any certified flow produces an original assignment. At type $t$, give probability
$$ \frac{f_{ts}}{b_t} $$
to the bundle of $c_t$ copies of $s$, and probability $u_t/b_t$ to the empty bundle. These probabilities sum to one, and the incidence mass is
$$ \mu_t c_t\frac{f_{ts}}{b_t}=f_{ts}. $$
Its active options are admissible, so the certificate theorem proves robustness.

This establishes an exact equivalence, with objective $\sum_e f_e$. It also handles binary capacities without expanding them.

2. Polynomial-time optimization on forests

Theorem. If the bipartite type graph $(W\cup P,E)$ is a forest, a maximum-mass robustly popular assignment, or a correct report of nonexistence, can be computed in polynomial time in $m,\tau,L$.

I give the dynamic program and prove its polynomial representation.

2.1 Subtree states

Root each tree. For a nonroot vertex $v$, write $r$ for its parent and $C(v)$ for its children. Let $T_v$ be the subtree rooted at $v$.

A subtree configuration includes its internal edge flows, unused capacities, and certificate coordinates. It also includes the boundary flow $x$ on $rv$ and its prescribed certificate value $\lambda_{rv}=\ell$.

The objective of a subtree configuration is the sum of flows on edges wholly inside $T_v$; it excludes the boundary flow $x$.

For every $\ell\in V$, define:

  • $Z_v(\ell)$: the maximum subtree objective when $x=0$. The parent option need not be admissible at $v$.
  • $F_v^\ell(x)$: the maximum subtree objective when the boundary flow is $x$, with the additional requirement that the parent option is admissible at $v$, even when $x=0$.

An infeasible scalar state has value $-\infty$, and an infeasible function value is $-\infty$.

The additional requirement in $F$ is deliberate. A positive boundary flow always requires parent-option admissibility. Requiring it also at zero makes each case valid on a closed interval; genuine zero-boundary configurations are covered separately by $Z$.

At the root, there is no boundary edge. The root optimum is computed by the same routine as a $Z$ state, omitting the parent option.

2.2 A local calculation

Suppose we prescribe:

  • the boundary certificate value, if a parent exists;
  • a set $R$ of at most two options required to be admissible at $v$;
  • the certificate value $\eta_i\in V$ of every selected child edge $vi$.

Every unselected child edge has flow zero.

Let
$$ K_v(\ell,R,\eta) $$
be the maximum sum of the $Z$ values of all unselected child subtrees, subject to all comparisons required by $R$ at $v$. At a root, omit $\ell$.

This quantity is polynomial-time computable:

  • Check comparisons from each $a\in R$ to null, the parent option, and all selected child options. Their potentials are already fixed.
  • For each unselected child $j$, independently choose $\zeta\in V$ maximizing $Z_j(\zeta)$ subject to

$$ h_v(j)-h_v(a)\leq v_v(a,j) \qquad \text{for every }a\in R. $$

  • Add these independent maxima.

If a required comparison fails or an unselected child has no feasible choice, set $K_v=-\infty$.

The independence in step 2 is exact: all local inequalities have a first option in $R$. Once its potentials are fixed, the inequalities involving an unselected child depend only on that child's edge coordinate. Different child subtrees have no connecting edges because the graph is a tree.

There are only five choices per unselected edge.

2.3 Recurrence for the positive-boundary function

Fix $\ell$. Because the parent option is required to be admissible, at most one further option can be admissible. Thus the following cases exhaust $F_v^\ell$.

Terminal case with no unused capacity. The parent edge fills $v$:
$$ x=b_v, \qquad R=\{r\}. $$
This contributes the singleton-domain function
$$ F_v^\ell(b_v)\geq K_v(\ell,\{r\},\varnothing). $$

Terminal case allowing unused capacity. There is no selected child edge:
$$ 0\leq x\leq b_v, \qquad u_v=b_v-x, \qquad R=\{r,\bot\}. $$
This contributes the constant function
$$ K_v(\ell,\{r,\bot\},\varnothing) $$
on $[0,b_v]$.

Continuation through one child. Select $i\in C(v)$ and $\eta\in V$. Require
$$ R=\{r,i\}, \qquad u_v=0. $$
The child-edge flow must be
$$ y=b_v-x. $$
The contribution is
$$ K_v(\ell,\{r,i\},\eta) + (b_v-x) + F_i^\eta(b_v-x), $$
where $0\leq x\leq b_v$ and the child function is feasible.

The recurrence takes the maximum of all these contributions.

To verify completeness, consider any feasible configuration in the definition of $F_v^\ell(x)$. If null is active, no child can have positive flow, because parent, null, and such a child would be three admissible options. This is the second terminal case. If null is inactive, either no child has positive flow, giving the first terminal case, or exactly one child has positive flow, giving a continuation.

The recurrence sometimes requires an option to be admissible when its flow is zero. Such requirements only restrict a case; they cannot produce an invalid certified flow. At a zero-flow boundary of a continuation, the corresponding terminal case covers configurations that do not satisfy the extra child-option requirement. Hence these closed-domain recurrences remain complete.

2.4 Recurrence for a zero-boundary state

When the parent flow is zero, the actual active options at $v$ are among null and its children. There are at most two.

The following cases therefore exhaust $Z_v(\ell)$.

No selected child. Require $R=\{\bot\}$ and set $u_v=b_v$. The contribution is
$$ K_v(\ell,\{\bot\},\varnothing). $$

One selected child, with null admissible. For $i\in C(v)$ and $\eta\in V$, require $R=\{i,\bot\}$ and optimize
$$ K_v(\ell,\{i,\bot\},\eta) + \max_{0\leq x\leq b_v} \bigl(x+F_i^\eta(x)\bigr). $$

One selected child, filling $v$. Require $R=\{i\}$ and use
$$ K_v(\ell,\{i\},\eta) + b_v+F_i^\eta(b_v). $$

Two selected children. For distinct $i,j\in C(v)$ and $\eta_i,\eta_j\in V$, require $R=\{i,j\}$. Their flows sum to $b_v$, giving
$$ K_v(\ell,\{i,j\},\eta_i,\eta_j) + b_v + \max_{0\leq x\leq b_v} \left( F_i^{\eta_i}(x)+F_j^{\eta_j}(b_v-x) \right). $$

Take the maximum over all feasible cases. The root uses these same expressions with the parent option absent from the local comparisons.

Soundness follows because every option with positive flow or unused capacity belongs to the prescribed admissible set. Completeness follows by taking the actual active set: null alone, one real option with or without null, or two real options.

2.5 Polynomial representation of every function

A record represents an affine function
$$ a x+d $$
on a nonempty closed rational interval $J$, possibly a singleton. A function $F_v^\ell$ is represented as the maximum of its records at each argument.

Lemma. Each $F_v^\ell$ needs at most $2|T_v|$ records. Every record has slope
$$ a\in\{0,-1\}. $$

Proof. A terminal case creates a constant record, with slope zero. A continuation transforms a child record
$$ a y+d, \qquad y\in J, $$
into
$$ K+(b_v-x)+a(b_v-x)+d = -(1+a)x+(1+a)b_v+d+K $$
on
$$ [0,b_v]\cap\{x:b_v-x\in J\}. $$
If $a\in\{0,-1\}$, the new slope is again in $\{0,-1\}$.

Repeated continuation follows a downward path until a terminal vertex $z$. There are two terminal modes: the singleton case and the case allowing null.

For a fixed $z$ and fixed terminal mode:

  • The downward path from $v$ to $z$ is unique.
  • The flow transformation at each internal vertex is $y=b_s-x$.
  • The domain therefore depends only on that path's masses and terminal mode.
  • The slope depends only on the number of continuations.
  • Certificate choices affect only feasibility and the additive constant.

Consequently, all records with the same terminal vertex and mode have identical domains and slopes. Retaining the largest additive constant preserves their maximum. There are at most two records for each $z\in T_v$, proving the bound. $\square$

The record bound also gives a direct implementation: generate transformed records bottom-up, index them by terminal vertex and terminal mode, and retain the largest constant for each index. There is no need to enumerate all certificate sequences along a path.

2.6 Computing the remaining maxima

For a record $a x+d$ on $J$, the one-child optimization restricts it to
$$ J\cap[0,b_v]. $$
The objective $x+F_i(x)$ is affine on that restricted record, so its maximum occurs at an endpoint.

For two child records
$$ a_i x+d_i \quad\text{on }J_i, \qquad a_j y+d_j \quad\text{on }J_j, $$
their common feasible domain is
$$ [0,b_v]\cap J_i\cap\{x:b_v-x\in J_j\}. $$
On this interval, the expression being optimized is
$$ (a_i-a_j)x+a_j b_v+d_i+d_j. $$
Again, its maximum occurs at an endpoint. Enumerating all record pairs is polynomial because each child has only linearly many records.

All other choices are polynomial as well: five boundary values, at most two selected children, five values per selected edge, and independent five-way choices for unselected edges.

2.7 Bit complexity, reconstruction, and correctness

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of the rational numbers $b_t$, after writing them as fractions. Its bit length is polynomial in the input length.

Every interval endpoint is obtained by additions, subtractions, maxima, and minima involving the $b_t$. Thus every endpoint is an integer multiple of $1/D$ and has polynomial bit length.

All scalar maxima are attained at such endpoints. Objective values are sums of feasible edge flows and are bounded by $\sum_t b_t$. Record constants also have polynomial bit length: their slopes are zero or minus one, their arguments lie in $[0,b_v]$, and their represented feasible objective values are bounded by subtree capacity sums.

Therefore the algorithm uses polynomially many operations on polynomial-length rationals.

Store maximizing choices in the local calculations and recurrences. Backtracking from the root reconstructs a flow and a five-valued certificate. The construction in Section 1.3 then gives the original bundle distributions. At most two bundles per type have positive probability, and repeated partners are specified by binary multiplicities.

If the root value is $-\infty$, the recurrence proves that no certified flow exists. The certificate theorem then proves nonexistence of a robust assignment. Otherwise, completeness and soundness show that the root value is exactly the maximum robust matched-pair mass. Different tree components are independent, so their optima add. This proves the theorem. $\square$

As a computational check, an implementation of these recurrences agreed with exhaustive five-valued certificate enumeration on sixteen random rational-mass trees with two to five vertices. The proof above does not depend on that check.

3. Equal effective capacities on arbitrary graphs

Theorem. Suppose
$$ b_t=\mu_t c_t=b $$
for every type. Then a maximum-mass robust assignment exists and can be found in polynomial time. Its mass is
$$ b\cdot \bigl(\text{maximum cardinality of a finite popular matching on the type graph}\bigr). $$

3.1 Integrality for a fixed certificate

Fix a certificate $\lambda$. Divide all flows and unused capacities by $b$:
$$ g_e=\frac{f_e}{b}, \qquad s_t=\frac{u_t}{b}. $$
Their constraints are
$$ g_e,s_t\geq0, \qquad \sum_{e\ni t}g_e+s_t=1, $$
with variables set to zero whenever their options are inadmissible.

This polytope is integral. To verify this, multiply every project row of its equality matrix by $-1$. Each edge column then has one entry $1$ and one entry $-1$, while each slack column has one nonzero entry equal to $1$ or $-1$.

Every square submatrix has determinant in $\{-1,0,1\}$. Indeed, if a column has at most one nonzero entry, expansion along that column reduces the assertion inductively. Otherwise every column has two opposite entries, so the sum of its rows is zero and its determinant is zero. The matrix is therefore totally unimodular.

Deleting forbidden columns preserves this property. With integral right sides, every vertex of the bounded nonnegative equality polytope is integral. At such a vertex, each type has either one incident edge of value one or slack of value one. Thus it represents an ordinary matching.

Every point is a convex combination of vertices. Moreover, each resulting matching uses only admissible options, so it satisfies the same robustness certificate.

3.2 Ordinary popularity equals robustness for deterministic unit-capacity assignments

Consider the auxiliary society with capacities one and equal masses $1/\tau$. A finite matching specifies one deterministic option at each type.

For a deterministic source assignment, the coupling against any challenger is uniquely determined within each type. Its margin is therefore linear in the challenger probabilities.

Every feasible fractional challenger is a convex combination of ordinary matchings, by the same integrality argument with no forbidden columns. Consequently, a deterministic source defeats every fractional challenger exactly when it defeats every ordinary matching. This is ordinary finite popularity.

Thus, in Section 3.1, all integral matchings in the decomposition are popular. It follows that every robust flow satisfies
$$ Q = b\sum_e g_e \leq b\cdot \bigl(\text{maximum finite popular cardinality}\bigr). $$

Conversely, a maximum-cardinality finite popular matching is robust in the auxiliary society. By the certificate theorem, it has an admissibility certificate. Give flow $b$ to each of its edges and unused capacity $b$ to its unmatched types. The same certificate proves robustness in the original society, attaining the displayed bound.

The finite instance is bipartite, has strict rankings, and has capacity one at every vertex. Hence the maximum-size popular-matching algorithm of [Brandl–Kavitha](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.07531) applies. Scaling its incidence flow by $b$ and constructing the original bundles requires polynomial bit complexity. $\square$

4. What remains open

The forest algorithm selects certificates in polynomial time, rather than enumerating $5^m$ possibilities. Its crucial compression uses the uniqueness of the downward path to each terminal vertex.

For arbitrary type graphs, the certificate theorem still bounds the positive-flow degree by two. However, all acceptable edges remain in the certificate inequalities, including zero-flow edges. The forest proof therefore does not extend merely by observing that positive-flow components are paths and cycles: selecting those components and satisfying the comparisons on the remaining edges are still coupled.

No polynomial-time algorithm or valid hardness reduction for that general selection problem is established here.
