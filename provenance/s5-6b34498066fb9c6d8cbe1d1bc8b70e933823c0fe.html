The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many hidden task-quality types \(q\) with rational masses \(\mu_q\), fresh workers drawn independently from \(F\), and expected response budget \(\gamma\) per unit task mass, what is the minimum limiting average classification error achievable by a policy that assigns further queries using observed response histories? For the Theorem 2.2 counterpart, assume the policy is also supplied the exact average difficulty of the unresolved mass at each round, and characterize the achievable error rate under the paper’s bounded-support-ratio conditions.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass task-quality types \(q\in[0,1]\setminus\{1/2\}\), with binary-rational qualities and masses \(\mu_q>0\), normalized by \(\sum_q\mu_q=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The worker law \(F\) is encoded by \(s\) rational support points \(p\in[0,1]\) and positive binary-rational probabilities; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A task of hidden quality \(q\) has true label \(t(q)=\operatorname{sign}(2q-1)\) and difficulty \(d(q)=(2q-1)^2\). Each fresh worker independently draws one hidden \(p\sim F\), answers an assigned subset of tasks, and departs permanently. Conditional on all qualities, responses are independent, with \(\Pr(A=+1\mid q,p)=qp+(1-q)(1-p)\); each response costs one unit.
Write \(\mu=\mathbb E_F[2p-1]>0\), \(\sigma^2=\mathbb E_F[(2p-1)^2]\), and \(\lambda=(\sum_q\mu_q/d(q))^{-1}\). Let \(d_{\min}\) and \(d_{\max}\) be the extreme supported difficulties. Partition difficulties into dyadic intervals \((d_{\max}2^{-(a+1)},d_{\max}2^{-a}]\), discard empty bins, and denote their masses by \(\delta_a\). Promise \(d_{\max}/d_{\min}\le K_d\) and \(\delta_{\max}/\delta_{\min}\le K_\delta\), for fixed constants \(K_d,K_\delta\ge1\).
The support lists describe the environment; a policy initially receives only \(\mu,\sigma^2,\lambda,d_{\min},d_{\max}\), the mean difficulty, and the dyadic bin endpoints and masses. It observes worker identities, assignments, and responses, but no realized qualities. It may randomize, use the entire observed history, and permanently classify tasks between rounds. Before each round it also receives the exact mean difficulty of all unresolved tasks; assignments within that round are fixed before its fresh workers respond.
Define the population limit using \(m\) tasks with independent qualities drawn from the given masses. For binary-rational \(\gamma>0\), let \(R(\gamma)=\inf_{(\pi_m)}\limsup_{m\to\infty}m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Pr_{\pi_m}(\hat t_i\ne t_i)\), where each admissible policy terminates almost surely, labels every task, and collects at most \(m\gamma\) responses in expectation. For finite task lists, an efficient adaptive scheme with the same difficulty oracle has asymptotic error at most \(C\exp(-c\gamma\lambda\sigma^2)\) under bounded ratios and \(\gamma=\Theta(\log m)\); this is an achievable-rate guarantee, not an exact optimization algorithm. Partial result: an exact heterogeneous-worker subclass in polynomial time

The general polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question remains unresolved here. I prove two further results:

  • The balanced, equal-difficulty case is exactly solvable when all workers have the same absolute reliability, even if some workers are negatively oriented.
  • Its exact decision problem is solvable in $\operatorname{poly}(\tau,s,L)$ bit operations, without a dependence on the numerical value of $\gamma$.

The second result also improves the encoding-sensitive running time for the homogeneous special case in the notes.

1. The subclass and its exact population optimum

Assume that the task support is
$$ q_+=\frac{1+a}{2}, \qquad q_-=\frac{1-a}{2}, \qquad \mu_{q_+}=\mu_{q_-}=\frac12, $$
where $a\in(0,1]$ is rational. Assume also that, for some rational $b\in(0,1]$,
$$ |2p-1|=b \qquad\text{for every supported worker quality }p. $$
The worker law may place positive mass on both
$$ p_+=\frac{1+b}{2}, \qquad p_-=\frac{1-b}{2}. $$
The assumption $\mu>0$ means that the positive orientation has greater probability.

All task difficulties equal $a^2$. Consequently,
$$ \lambda=a^2, \qquad \sigma^2=b^2, \qquad r:=ab=\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2}>0. $$
The difficulty oracle always returns $a^2$ while any task remains unresolved. Both ratio conditions hold with ratios equal to one.

For $0<r<1$, define
$$ z=\frac{1-r}{1+r}, \qquad h_n=\frac{1-z^n}{1+z^n}, \qquad e_n=\frac{z^n}{1+z^n}, \qquad c_n=\frac{nh_n}{r} $$
for integers $n\ge0$. Let $H_r$ be the piecewise-linear function joining the points $(c_n,e_n)$. Thus, if
$$ c_n\le x<c_{n+1}, \qquad \alpha=\frac{x-c_n}{c_{n+1}-c_n}, $$
then
$$ H_r(x)=(1-\alpha)e_n+\alpha e_{n+1}. $$
For $r=1$, define
$$ H_1(x)=\frac12\max\{1-x,0\}. $$

Theorem. In the subclass above,
$$ R(\gamma)=H_r(\gamma). $$
Moreover, deciding $R(\gamma)\le\varepsilon$ takes $\operatorname{poly}(\tau,s,L)$ bit operations. There are admissible finite-population policies with deterministic response count at most $\lfloor m\gamma\rfloor$, control time polynomial in $L$ and $m$, and average error converging to $H_r(\gamma)$.

The proofs occupy the following sections.

2. An exact homogeneous-channel benchmark

Consider balanced independent task signs $T_i\in\{-1,+1\}$. Suppose each collected response $B$ about task $i$ satisfies
$$ \Pr(B=T_i\mid T_i)=\frac{1+r}{2}, $$
and all responses are independent conditional on the task signs. Policies may couple their decisions across tasks and use arbitrary independent randomization.

I first prove that the optimal average error under expected budget $m x$ is exactly $H_r(x)$.

2.1 Posterior and cost identities

Assume $0<r<1$. At termination, let $N_i$ be the response count for task $i$ and let $S_i$ be its response sum.

For a fixed terminal transcript, the likelihood ratio between $T_i=+1$ and $T_i=-1$ is
$$ \left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right)^{S_i}=z^{-S_i}. $$
Indeed, each response about task $i$ contributes its corresponding likelihood factor. Policy actions depend on observed history and independent randomization, so their factors cancel between the two hypotheses. Responses concerning other tasks supply no further factor involving $T_i$, because the prior signs are independent and the channel is homogeneous.

Hence
$$ \mathbb E[T_i\mid\text{terminal transcript}] = \begin{cases} \operatorname{sign}(S_i)h_{|S_i|},&S_i\ne0,\\ 0,&S_i=0. \end{cases} $$
The minimum conditional classification error is therefore $e_{|S_i|}$, giving
$$ \Pr(\widehat T_i\ne T_i)\ge \mathbb E[e_{|S_i|}]. $$

There is also the identity
$$ \mathbb E[N_i]=\mathbb E[c_{|S_i|}]. $$
To prove it, condition additionally on all true task signs. Every newly collected response about task $i$ then satisfies
$$ \mathbb E[T_iB\mid\text{information before that response}]=r. $$
Assignments fixed before a round's responses permit exposing the responses in any fixed order within that round. Summing the conditional expectations gives
$$ \mathbb E[T_iS_i]=r\,\mathbb E[N_i]. $$
The expected budget implies $\mathbb E[N_i]<\infty$, which justifies this summation: the sum of expected absolute increments is at most $\mathbb E[N_i]$.

On the other hand, the posterior formula and $|S_i|\le N_i$ give
$$ \mathbb E[T_iS_i] = \mathbb E[|S_i|h_{|S_i|}]. $$
Combining these equalities proves the cost identity.

2.2 Convexity of the benchmark

The sequence $h_n$ increases strictly. Since $h_1=r$, we have $c_n\ge n$ for $n\ge1$. Thus the costs increase strictly and tend to infinity.

Direct subtraction yields
$$ h_{n+1}-h_n = \frac{2z^n(1-z)}{(1+z^n)(1+z^{n+1})} $$
and consequently
$$ \frac{e_n-e_{n+1}}{c_{n+1}-c_n} = \frac{r} {2n+1+\dfrac{z^{-n}-z^{n+1}}{1-z}}. $$
The denominator's increment from $n$ to $n+1$ equals
$$ 2+z^{-(n+1)}+z^{n+1}>0. $$
Therefore successive segment slopes of $H_r$ increase. The function is convex and decreasing.

Its largest absolute slope is the first one:
$$ \frac{e_0-e_1}{c_1-c_0}=\frac r2. $$
In particular, $H_r$ is Lipschitz with constant at most $1/2$.

Choose a uniformly random task index $I$, independently of everything else, and put $K=|S_I|$. Every policy with expected total cost at most $m x$ satisfies
$$ \frac1m\sum_i\Pr(\widehat T_i\ne T_i) \ge \mathbb E[e_K] = \mathbb E[H_r(c_K)] \ge H_r(\mathbb E[c_K]) \ge H_r(x). $$
Jensen's inequality applies because $\mathbb E[c_K]\le x<\infty$ and $H_r$ is bounded.

2.3 Achievability

For $n\ge1$, query a task until its response sum first reaches $+n$ or $-n$, and output that sign.

The stopping time has finite expectation. Conditional on either true sign, every block of $2n$ positive responses has probability at least
$$ \left(\frac{1-r}{2}\right)^{2n}>0. $$
Such a block forces absorption from every interior state. Applying this bound to successive blocks gives a geometric upper bound on the stopping-time tail.

At termination, $|S|=n$. The posterior and cost identities therefore give error $e_n$ and expected cost $c_n$. Threshold zero means no response and a fair random label.

Randomizing independently between adjacent thresholds achieves every segment of $H_r$. This proves the benchmark exactly, for every finite population under an expected budget.

For $r=1$, one response reveals the sign. On a task receiving no response, its balanced sign remains independent of the transcript. Thus
$$ \frac1m\sum_i\Pr(\widehat T_i\ne T_i) \ge \frac{1}{2m}\sum_i\Pr(N_i=0) \ge \frac12\max\{1-x,0\}. $$
Querying each task with probability $\min\{x,1\}$ attains this bound.

3. A lower bound for arbitrary worker laws in the equal-difficulty case

Continue to assume balanced task qualities $q_+,q_-$ with common difficulty $a^2$, but now allow an arbitrary worker law. Define
$$ b_*=\max_{p\in\operatorname{supp}(F)}|2p-1|, \qquad r_*=ab_*. $$
Since $\mu>0$, we have $b_*>0$.

Proposition.
$$ R(\gamma)\ge H_{r_*}(\gamma). $$

Proof. Start with the homogeneous channel of reliability $r_*$. For each fresh worker, independently sample a latent $p$ from the given worker law. For every response assigned to that worker, independently sample $X\in\{-1,+1\}$ conditional on $p$, with
$$ \mathbb E[X\mid p]=\frac{2p-1}{b_*}. $$
This defines a probability distribution because the right-hand side belongs to $[-1,1]$.

If $B$ is the homogeneous response, return $A=XB$ to the original policy. For a task with sign $T$,
$$ \mathbb E[A\mid T,p] = \frac{2p-1}{b_*}\,r_*T = a(2p-1)T. $$
Since $2q-1=aT$, this is precisely
$$ (2q-1)(2p-1), $$
the mean response prescribed by the question. A binary response distribution is determined by its mean. Conditional on all signs and worker qualities, the constructed responses are independent, so their complete joint law is also correct.

Thus any policy in the original environment can be simulated in the stronger homogeneous channel, preserving assignments, responses observed by that policy, costs, and labels. The simulator's extra variables are independent randomization; the benchmark lower bound allows such randomization. Therefore every original policy has average error at least $H_{r_*}(\gamma)$. The same bound passes to $R(\gamma)$. $\square$

In the constant-absolute-reliability subclass, $b_*=b$, proving the lower half of the theorem.

4. Exact comparisons without expanding enormous rational powers

The benchmark involves $z^n$, and $n$ can be exponentially large in its binary encoding length. Expanding that rational power is unnecessary.

4.1 A comparison lemma

Lemma. Given a rational $z\in(0,1)$, a nonnegative integer $n$ in binary, and a rational polynomial $P$ of degree at most two, the sign of
$$ P(z^n) $$
can be determined in time polynomial in the total binary input length.

Proof. Handle $n=0$ and constant polynomials directly. Clear coefficient denominators, obtaining a nonconstant integer polynomial
$$ P(X)=a_dX^d+\cdots+a_0, \qquad 1\le d\le2. $$
This increases the encoding length only polynomially. Let $B$ bound the resulting total encoding length, and write $z=u/v$ in lowest terms, so $v\ge2$.

First handle possible equality. Let $\ell$ be the binary length of $|a_d|$. If $n<\ell$, expand $z^n$ and evaluate exactly; the resulting numbers have polynomially many bits.

If $n\ge\ell$, then
$$ v^n\ge2^n\ge2^\ell>|a_d|. $$
The rational-root theorem says that the denominator of a reduced rational root divides $a_d$. The reduced denominator of $z^n$ is $v^n$. Hence
$$ P(z^n)\ne0. $$

The real roots of a polynomial of degree at most two can be isolated using rational arithmetic and square-root approximation in polynomial time. Their ordering relative to $0$ and $1$ can likewise be determined exactly. Roots outside $(0,1)$ need no further comparison because $0<z^n<1$.

Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be a remaining root. Its degree is at most two. Its logarithmic Weil height is $O(B)$: for an irreducible quadratic this follows from its coefficients and the Cauchy root bound; for a rational root it follows from the rational-root theorem.

Here is the quantitative theorem used. For fixed integers $k,D$, there is an effectively computable constant $C_{k,D}$ such that, for positive real algebraic numbers $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k$ generating a field of degree at most $D$, and integers $b_j$, every nonzero form
$$ \Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^k b_j\log\alpha_j $$
satisfies
$$ \log|\Lambda| \ge -C_{k,D} \left(\prod_{j=1}^k\max\{1,\mathfrak h(\alpha_j)\}\right) \log\max\{3,|b_1|,\ldots,|b_k|\}. $$
Here $\mathfrak h$ denotes logarithmic Weil height. This is a standard quantitative bound for linear forms in logarithms; [Bugeaud's Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00275) states the needed form.

Apply it to
$$ \Lambda=n\log z-\log\alpha. $$
Its hypotheses hold: both bases are positive algebraic numbers, their generated field has degree at most two, the coefficients are integers, and $\Lambda\ne0$ by the equality test above. Both heights are $O(B)$, and $\log n=O(B)$. Consequently, for an effective absolute constant $C$,
$$ |\Lambda|\ge2^{-C(B+1)^3}. $$

The two logarithms can be approximated to this accuracy in polynomial time. To justify this computational step explicitly, nonzero quadratic roots have magnitude at least $2^{-O(B)}$, by applying a root bound to the reciprocal polynomial after removing any zero root. Thus polynomially many additional bits of absolute root accuracy give the required relative accuracy. Scaling a positive number by a power of two brings it into $[1,2]$. On this interval, writing
$$ y=\frac{x-1}{x+1} $$
gives $|y|\le1/3$ and
$$ \log x = 2\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{y^{2j+1}}{2j+1} +\mathcal R_M, \qquad |\mathcal R_M| \le \frac{2|y|^{2M+1}}{(2M+1)(1-y^2)}. $$
Polynomially many terms suffice. The same series evaluates $\log2$.

Approximate each logarithm to $C(B+1)^3+B+O(1)$ bits. Multiplication by $n$ then leaves an error smaller than half the certified lower bound on $|\Lambda|$. Its sign is determined, and this is the sign of $z^n-\alpha$.

Finally, the leading coefficient and the location of $z^n$ relative to the real roots determine the sign of $P(z^n)$. All operations use polynomially many bits and polynomially many arithmetic steps. $\square$

4.2 Applying the lemma to the benchmark decision

Assume $0<r<1$ and put
$$ \Gamma=1+\lfloor\gamma\rfloor. $$
Because $c_j\ge j$, we have $c_\Gamma>\gamma$. The relevant segment index can therefore be found by binary search among integers from $0$ through $\Gamma-1$, using $O(\log(\Gamma+1))$ comparisons.

For an index $j$, rearrangement gives
$$ c_j\le\gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad j-\gamma r\le(j+\gamma r)z^j. $$
If $j\le\gamma r$, this holds automatically. Otherwise it is the comparison
$$ z^j\ge\frac{j-\gamma r}{j+\gamma r}, $$
which the lemma decides using a polynomial of degree one. All quantities except the unexpanded power have polynomial encoding length.

After finding $n$ with $c_n\le\gamma<c_{n+1}$, set
$$ u=z^n, \qquad t=1-2\varepsilon. $$
If $\varepsilon\ge1/2$, the answer is immediately positive. Otherwise $t\in(0,1)$.

Writing $1-2H_r(\gamma)$ for the interpolated posterior magnitude, direct substitution gives
$$ 1-2H_r(\gamma) = \frac{ 1-(1+z)u+zu^2+2\gamma r(1-z)u }{ 1+(2n+1)(1-z)u-zu^2 }. $$
The denominator is positive: divided by $(1+u)(1+zu)$, it equals
$$ (n+1)h_{n+1}-nh_n = r(c_{n+1}-c_n)>0. $$
Therefore
$$ H_r(\gamma)\le\varepsilon $$
is equivalent to
$$ (1-t) + \bigl(-(1+z)+(2\gamma r-t(2n+1))(1-z)\bigr)u + z(1+t)u^2 \ge0. $$
This is precisely a degree-two comparison covered by the lemma.

The binary search uses polynomially many calls on polynomial-length inputs. Thus the exact decision takes $\operatorname{poly}(L)$ bit operations after preprocessing. For $r=1$, compare $\frac12\max\{1-\gamma,0\}$ directly with $\varepsilon$.

The same algorithm tests the lower-bound certificate
$$ H_{ab_*}(\gamma)>\varepsilon $$
in polynomial time for every worker law in the balanced equal-difficulty setting.

5. Achievability with heterogeneous worker orientations

I now prove that constant absolute worker reliability permits attaining the lower bound in the population limit.

Write a worker's orientation as
$$ W=\frac{2p-1}{b}\in\{-1,+1\}. $$
For a task with sign $T$,
$$ \mathbb E[A\mid T,W]=rTW. $$
Thus, if $W$ were known, the corrected response $WA$ would be a homogeneous response of reliability $r$.

The construction estimates $W$ after each worker's batch. It assigns reference tasks and target tasks together, so it respects the restriction that assignments are fixed before responses.

5.1 Parameters and reference tasks

For sufficiently large $m$, put
$$ h=\lceil m^{1/8}\rceil, \qquad g=\lceil m^{1/2}\rceil, \qquad \xi=\max\{\gamma-1/h,0\}. $$
For small populations with $h\ge m$, use the zero-response policy.

Keep $h$ reference tasks unresolved until the end. For each reference, collect $h$ responses using distinct fresh singleton workers, and estimate its sign by majority vote. This costs $h^2$ responses.

A singleton response has reliability
$$ r_0=a\mu>0. $$
Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound therefore give
$$ \Pr(\text{some reference sign estimate is wrong}) \le h\exp(-hr_0^2/2). $$

5.2 Efficient threshold randomization

For each target task, independently choose the homogeneous threshold mixture for budget $\xi$, with a slight downward approximation of its mixing probability.

This approximation can be computed in polynomial time. Suppose its exact mixing probability is $\alpha$, between thresholds $n$ and $n+1$. For a rational candidate $\beta\in[0,1]$,
$$ \alpha\ge\beta $$
is equivalent to
$$ \xi r-(1-\beta)nh_n-\beta(n+1)h_{n+1}\ge0. $$
After multiplying by $(1+z^n)(1+z^{n+1})$, this becomes a polynomial of degree at most two in $z^n$:
$$ \xi r(1+u)(1+zu) -(1-\beta)n(1-u)(1+zu) -\beta(n+1)(1-zu)(1+u) \ge0. $$
The comparison lemma permits binary bisection for $\alpha$.

Choose a dyadic approximation $\alpha'\le\alpha$ satisfying
$$ (\alpha-\alpha')(c_{n+1}-c_n)\le1/h. $$
Only $O(L+\log m)$ bits are needed, since
$$ c_{n+1}-c_n\le c_{n+1}\le\frac{\Gamma+1}{r}. $$
The resulting ideal threshold policy has expected cost at most $\xi$ and error at most
$$ H_r(\xi)+\frac{1}{2h}, $$
by the Lipschitz bound from Section 2. For $r=1$, use a dyadic approximation of the mixture between thresholds zero and one; if $\xi\ge1$, use threshold one.

5.3 Worker calibration within each round

Run at most $h$ target rounds. In each round, partition the unresolved target tasks into groups of at most $g$ tasks. Assign each group to one fresh worker, together with all $h$ reference tasks.

After receiving the entire batch, estimate that worker's orientation by
$$ \widehat W = \operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_{j=1}^h\widetilde T_jA_j\right), $$
where $\widetilde T_j$ is the reference sign estimate. Break ties arbitrarily.

If all reference estimates are correct, the summands, multiplied by $W$, are independent signs with mean $r$. Hence each worker's orientation estimate is wrong with probability at most
$$ \exp(-hr^2/2). $$
This bound remains valid conditional on the preceding history: the worker is fresh, and its calibration responses are independent conditional on its orientation and the true reference signs.

There are at most
$$ h\left\lceil\frac mg\right\rceil $$
target workers. Let $E$ be the event that all reference estimates and all target-worker orientation estimates are correct. A union bound gives
$$ \Pr(E^c) \le h\exp(-hr_0^2/2) + h\left\lceil\frac mg\right\rceil\exp(-hr^2/2). $$

Use $\widehat W A$ as each target's corrected response. Permanently label targets when their response sums hit their selected thresholds. After $h$ target rounds, label any remaining targets arbitrarily, and label the references.

5.4 Coupling to independent homogeneous responses

Conditional on true signs and worker orientations, the actual responses can be generated as
$$ A=WY, $$
where the target variables $Y$ are independent homogeneous responses of reliability $r$. Their conditional distribution does not depend on $W$.

Generate in advance independent homogeneous response streams for every target, together with its independent threshold choice. Calibration randomness and worker orientations can then be generated as needed. This produces the correct joint law in the heterogeneous environment.

On $E$, every corrected target response equals its corresponding $Y$. Consequently, until truncation or budget exhaustion, the target process agrees with the ideal threshold process.

Let $N_i^\circ$ be the untruncated response count of the ideal policy for target $i$, and put
$$ X_i=\min\{N_i^\circ,h\}. $$
The variables $X_i$ are independent, satisfy $0\le X_i\le h$, and have
$$ \mathbb E[X_i]\le\xi, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)\le h^2. $$
Furthermore,
$$ \Pr(N_i^\circ>h)\le\frac{\mathbb E[N_i^\circ]}h\le\frac{\gamma}{h}. $$
Thus truncating the ideal process increases average target error by at most $\gamma/h$.

5.5 Enforcing the finite-population budget

The reference and calibration responses total at most
$$ C_m = h^2+h^2\left\lceil\frac mg\right\rceil = O(m^{3/4}). $$
Before any round, count its planned responses. If the round would exceed the remaining allowance $\lfloor m\gamma\rfloor$, collect no further responses and label every unresolved task.

This guarantees, for every realization,
$$ \text{total response count}\le\lfloor m\gamma\rfloor. $$
Every policy terminates after at most one training round and $h$ target rounds.

For all sufficiently large $m$, $\xi=\gamma-1/h$ and
$$ \lfloor m\gamma\rfloor-C_m-m\xi \ge\frac{m}{2h}. $$
If $E$ occurs and
$$ \sum_iX_i+C_m\le\lfloor m\gamma\rfloor, $$
the cap cannot abort a round: every prefix cost is at most the final cost.

Since $\mathbb E[\sum_iX_i]\le m\xi$, Chebyshev's inequality gives
$$ \Pr\left( \sum_iX_i+C_m>\lfloor m\gamma\rfloor \right) \le \frac{mh^2}{(m/(2h))^2} = \frac{4h^4}{m}. $$
Consequently the additional error from calibration failures or the cap is at most
$$ \Pr(E^c)+\frac{4h^4}{m}. $$

The references contribute at most $h/m$ to average error. Combining all bounds gives, for sufficiently large $m$,
$$ \frac1m\sum_i\Pr(\widehat T_i\ne T_i) \le H_r(\xi) +\frac{1}{2h} +\frac{\gamma}{h} +\frac hm +\frac{4h^4}{m} +h\exp(-hr_0^2/2) +h\left\lceil\frac mg\right\rceil\exp(-hr^2/2). $$
For each fixed instance, $r_0,r>0$. With the chosen $h,g$, every term following $H_r(\xi)$ tends to zero. Also $\xi\to\gamma$, and $H_r$ is continuous. Therefore
$$ \limsup_{m\to\infty} \frac1m\sum_i\Pr(\widehat T_i\ne T_i) \le H_r(\gamma). $$
Together with Section 3, this proves
$$ R(\gamma)=H_r(\gamma). $$

The construction uses only permitted summaries: $a=\sqrt{d_{\min}}$, $b=\sqrt{\sigma^2}$, and the supplied $\mu$. It does not require the support lists or any realized quality.

6. Running times, example, and remaining question

Recognizing the subclass and computing its rational parameters take $\operatorname{poly}(\tau,s,L)$ bit operations. The exact decision algorithm has the same polynomial bound. It compares succinct expressions rather than writing out rational powers with exponentially many bits. Both ratio conditions already hold with ratios one.
