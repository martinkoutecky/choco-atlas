The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(C\), \(q_I\), a finite support of types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), responsive course rankings \(\succ_t\), rational bids \(b_{tc}\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\), with each type's mass distributed uniformly over a shared atomless tie-breaking rank \(r\in[0,1]\), compute a measurable assignment \(S(t,r)\subseteq C\) of integral schedules, with \(|S(t,r)|\le q_I\) and mass assigned to each course \(c\) at most \(\kappa_c\), that is stable under bid-and-rank priorities and weakly Pareto-dominates every stable assignment; determine whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) courses and let \(1\leq q_I\leq m\) be an integer. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type supplies a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(C\cup\{\varnothing\}\), interpreted through responsive schedule preferences, and nonnegative rational bids \(b_{tc}\) satisfying \(\sum_c b_{tc}=B\) for a common rational \(B>0\). Course capacities \(\kappa_c>0\) are rational.
Students are pairs \(x=(t,r)\), with measure \(d\nu(t,r)=\mu_t\,dr\) on \(r\in[0,1]\). Course \(c\) prioritizes higher \(b_{tc}\), then smaller \(r\), then smaller type index; every student is acceptable to every course.
An assignment gives each student an integral schedule \(S(x)\subseteq C\) of at most \(q_I\) courses, all ranked above \(\varnothing\), with enrollment \(e_c=\nu\{x:c\in S(x)\}\leq\kappa_c\).
A student wants an unassigned desirable course \(c\) if \(|S(x)|<q_I\) or \(c\succ_t d\) for some \(d\in S(x)\). Stability means that, for almost every student, each such course is full and its enrolled students of lower priority have measure zero.
An assignment weakly Pareto-dominates another if almost every student weakly prefers its schedule under every strict responsive extension of that student's course ranking. Assignments are identified up to null sets; exact output is a finite partition of each type's rank interval into intervals with rational endpoints and an integral schedule on each interval.

Does every instance admit a stable assignment weakly Pareto-dominating every measurable stable assignment? Is computing such an assignment polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L,q_I\), or is the computation NP-hard?

In finite societies, student-proposing deferred acceptance yields a stable assignment weakly preferred by every student to every stable assignment, and the course-bidding result gives dominance over every market outcome. [Sönmez–Ünver](https://www.tayfunsonmez.net/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/coursebidding.pdf)
For continuum students taking at most one college, stable matchings have a cutoff characterization and extremal market-clearing cutoffs; this concerns unit-demand admission rather than the course-schedule computation above. [Azevedo–Leshno](https://eduardomazevedo.github.io/papers/Azevedo-Leshno-Supply-and-Demand-Matching-WP.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total student mass to one, so the supplied per-capita capacities are enrollment bounds.
  • Included the outside option in each strict course ranking and excluded undesirable courses from assigned schedules.
  • Restored nonnegative bids with a common positive rational endowment, positive capacities, and a positive integer schedule quota bounded by the number of courses.
  • Specified that smaller ranks win bid ties; type index resolves the remaining null-set ties, including ties at zero bids.
  • Defined continuum stability almost everywhere using positive mass of lower-priority enrollees, making stability invariant under null-set changes.
  • Course rankings leave comparisons between arbitrary schedules unspecified; interpreted weak dominance as holding under every compatible strict responsive extension.
  • Specified exact output as schedules on finite rational interval partitions and required dominance against all measurable stable assignments, not only assignments with that representation.

Partial solution: existence, exact computation with an NP oracle, and polynomial special cases

Every instance admits a stable assignment weakly Pareto-dominating every measurable stable assignment.

I also prove a general computational upper bound: the required exact assignment can be computed in $\mathsf{FP}^{\mathsf{NP}}$, meaning deterministic polynomial time with an $\mathsf{NP}$ decision oracle. In addition, computation is polynomial-time when bids are independent of type, for arbitrary $q_I$.

These results do not settle polynomial-time solvability versus NP-hardness for unrestricted bids. The arguments below are self-contained, including the existence and representation results used from the notes.

1. Scores, availability, and an interval formula for demand

For each course $c$, enumerate its distinct bids increasingly as groups $1,\ldots,K_c$, and let $k_{tc}$ denote the group containing $b_{tc}$. Thus $K_c\leq\tau$. Define

$$ s_c(t,r)=k_{tc}-r. $$

Outside rank endpoints, higher scores reproduce the specified course priorities. Equal scores within a bid group are resolved by type index, but each score level contains at most one rank per type and therefore has measure zero. Consequently, score comparisons suffice for the measure-based stability condition.

For every real $z$,

$$ \nu\{x:s_c(x)=z\}=0. $$

Moreover, for $a<b$,

$$ \nu\{x:a<s_c(x)\leq b\}\leq b-a. $$

Indeed, the relevant ranks within each type form an interval of length at most $b-a$, and the type masses sum to $1$.

Let

$$ P=\prod_{c\in C}[0,K_c]. $$

For $p\in P$, define the available desirable courses and the selected schedule by

$$ A_p(t,r)=\{c:c\succ_t\varnothing,\ s_c(t,r)>p_c\}, $$

$$ S_p(t,r)=\text{the best }\min\{q_I,|A_p(t,r)|\}\text{ courses in }A_p(t,r). $$

Write

$$ D_c(p)=\nu\{x:c\in S_p(x)\}. $$

The availability prefix for type $t$ at course $c$ has length

$$ a_{tc}(p)=\min\{1,\max\{0,k_{tc}-p_c\}\}. $$

For a desirable course $c$, let $h_{tc}(p)$ be the $q_I$-th largest value among the availability lengths of desirable courses preferred to $c$. Set $h_{tc}(p)=0$ if there are fewer than $q_I$ such courses.

Except at finitely many ranks, at least $q_I$ courses preferred to $c$ are available precisely when $r<h_{tc}(p)$. Therefore

$$ \{r:c\in S_p(t,r)\} = [h_{tc}(p),a_{tc}(p)) $$

up to null sets, with the interval empty when its upper endpoint does not exceed its lower endpoint. In particular,

$$ D_c(p)= \sum_{t:c\succ_t\varnothing} \mu_t\max\{0,a_{tc}(p)-h_{tc}(p)\}. $$

Thus each type-course enrollment set is a single interval. The formula also shows that demand at rational cutoffs can be evaluated exactly in polynomial time: compute the availability lengths, determine the relevant order statistics, and perform rational arithmetic.

Demand properties

For every course $c$:

  • $D_c$ is nonincreasing in $p_c$;
  • $D_c$ is nondecreasing in each $p_d$ with $d\neq c$;
  • demand is continuous, with

$$ |D_c(p)-D_c(p')| \leq\sum_{d\in C}|p_d-p'_d|. $$

To justify the first two properties, removing availability of $c$ cannot create a selection of $c$, while removing another course cannot displace an already selected $c$.

For continuity, a student's schedule can change only if some course changes availability. The measure affected by a change in $p_d$ is at most $|p_d-p'_d|$, by the score-distribution bound. The union bound proves the displayed inequality.

Changing only $p_c$ gives the sharper bound

$$ |D_c(p)-D_c(p')|\leq |p_c-p'_c|. $$

Finally,

$$ N(p)=\sum_cD_c(p)=\int |S_p(x)|\,d\nu(x) $$

is nonincreasing in every cutoff coordinate, because removing available courses cannot increase the selected schedule's cardinality.

2. A monotone nonexpansive map and least feasible cutoffs

Define

$$ F=\{p\in P:D_c(p)\leq\kappa_c\text{ for all }c\} $$

and the map

$$ T_c(p)=\max\{0,p_c+D_c(p)-\kappa_c\}. $$

Lemma 1. The map $T$ maps $P$ into itself, is componentwise monotone, and is nonexpansive in $\ell_1$.

Proof. Holding other coordinates fixed, demand at $p_c=K_c$ is zero. The own-coordinate Lipschitz bound consequently gives

$$ D_c(p)\leq K_c-p_c. $$

Thus

$$ 0\leq T_c(p)\leq \max\{0,K_c-\kappa_c\}<K_c, $$

so $T(P)\subseteq P$.

Put $G_c(p)=p_c+D_c(p)-\kappa_c$. Increasing $p_j$ by $\delta\geq0$ increases $G_j$ by

$$ \delta+D_j(p+\delta e_j)-D_j(p)\geq0, $$

using the own-coordinate Lipschitz bound. It increases every other $G_c$ by demand monotonicity. Hence $G$, and therefore $T$, is componentwise monotone.

For the same coordinate change,

$$ \sum_c\bigl(G_c(p+\delta e_j)-G_c(p)\bigr) = \delta+N(p+\delta e_j)-N(p) \leq\delta. $$

Every summand on the left is nonnegative. Applying $z\mapsto\max\{0,z\}$ preserves nonnegativity of each coordinate change and cannot increase it. Therefore

$$ \|T(p+\delta e_j)-T(p)\|_1\leq\delta. $$

Connecting arbitrary $p,p'\in P$ by successive changes of individual coordinates and applying the triangle inequality yields

$$ \|T(p)-T(p')\|_1\leq\|p-p'\|_1. $$

This proves the lemma. $\square$

Call $p$ clearing when

$$ D_c(p)\leq\kappa_c, \qquad p_c>0\Longrightarrow D_c(p)=\kappa_c. $$

Directly from its definition,

$$ T(p)=p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\text{ is clearing}, $$

and

$$ T(p)\leq p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\in F. $$

For the latter equivalence, a capacity violation makes $p_c+D_c(p)-\kappa_c>p_c\geq0$, so the maximum with zero remains strictly above $p_c$.

Lemma 2. The feasible set $F$ has a componentwise least member $p^\star$, and $p^\star$ is clearing.

Proof. Start with $p^{(0)}=0$ and iterate

$$ p^{(n+1)}=T(p^{(n)}). $$

Since $T(0)\geq0$ and $T$ is monotone, the sequence increases componentwise. It lies in the bounded box $P$, so it converges to some $p^\star\in P$.

Continuity gives $T(p^\star)=p^\star$. Hence $p^\star$ is clearing and belongs to $F$.

If $v\in F$, then $T(v)\leq v$. Induction gives

$$ p^{(n)}\leq v $$

for every $n$: it holds initially, and monotonicity gives

$$ p^{(n+1)}=T(p^{(n)})\leq T(v)\leq v. $$

Taking limits proves $p^\star\leq v$. Thus $p^\star$ is the componentwise least feasible vector. $\square$

This iteration establishes existence. Section 8 below shows why it does not itself supply a polynomial-time algorithm.

3. Cutoff characterization of all measurable stable assignments

Lemma 3. Every clearing cutoff induces a stable assignment.

Proof. Feasibility follows from $D_c(p)\leq\kappa_c$.

If a student wants an unassigned desirable course $c$, then $c$ cannot be available: selecting the best available courses up to quota precludes wanting an omitted available course. Thus $s_c(x)\leq p_c$.

Scores are positive almost everywhere. Consequently, outside a null set, $p_c>0$, so clearing makes $c$ full. Every enrolled student at $c$ has score strictly above $p_c$, hence above this student's score. Lower-priority enrollment therefore has measure zero, as required. $\square$

Lemma 4. Every measurable stable assignment equals $S_p$ almost everywhere for some clearing cutoff $p$.

Proof. Let $S$ be stable, and write

$$ E_c=\{x:c\in S(x)\}, \qquad e_c=\nu(E_c). $$

If $e_c<\kappa_c$, set $p_c=0$. If $e_c=\kappa_c$, then $e_c>0$, and set

$$ p_c=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{x\in E_c}s_c(x). $$

Assigned students have score at least $p_c$ almost everywhere. Atomlessness of score levels makes this inequality strict almost everywhere. The same strict inequality holds at slack courses with cutoff zero.

For a full course and every $z>p_c$, the definition of essential infimum gives

$$ \nu(E_c\cap\{x:s_c(x)<z\})>0. $$

Hence a student with $s_c(x)>p_c$ who wants the unassigned course $c$ would face positive lower-priority enrollment, contradicting stability. At a slack course, wanting an unassigned desirable course directly contradicts stability.

Discarding the finitely many courses' exceptional null sets, we obtain:

  • every assigned course is available;
  • no omitted available desirable course is wanted.

If $|S(x)|<q_I$, the second condition excludes every omitted available course. If $|S(x)|=q_I$, a schedule differing from the best $q_I$ available courses would omit a course preferred to an assigned course, again violating the second condition.

Thus $S=S_p$ almost everywhere. Its enrollment is $D_c(p)$, and every positive cutoff belongs to a full course. Therefore $p$ is clearing. $\square$

This characterization applies to arbitrary measurable stable assignments; no interval representation was assumed.

4. Universal dominance and enrollment invariance

A strict responsive extension requires two comparisons used here: adding a desirable course, within quota, improves a schedule; replacing a course by a more preferred course improves it.

Lemma 5. Enlarging availability weakly improves the selected schedule under every strict responsive extension.

Proof. Let $Y\subseteq X$ consist of desirable courses, and let $U(Y),U(X)$ select their best courses up to quota.

If $|U(X)|<q_I$, then $U(X)=X$ and $U(Y)\subseteq U(X)$. Add the missing desirable courses.

Otherwise $|U(X)|=q_I$. Every course in $U(X)\setminus U(Y)$ is preferred to every course in $U(Y)\setminus U(X)$: the former belong to the best $q_I$ courses of $X$, while the latter do not. Also,

$$ |U(X)\setminus U(Y)|\geq|U(Y)\setminus U(X)|. $$

Replace each discarded course by a distinct newly selected course, then add any remaining newly selected courses. Every operation is required to improve the schedule by responsiveness, and every intermediate schedule satisfies the quota. $\square$

Theorem 1. Universal student-optimal stability exists.

Proof. Lemmas 2 and 3 show that $S_{p^\star}$ is stable.

Let $S$ be any measurable stable assignment. By Lemma 4, $S=S_p$ almost everywhere for some clearing $p$. Since $p\in F$,

$$ p^\star\leq p, \qquad A_p(x)\subseteq A_{p^\star}(x). $$

Lemma 5 proves the required weak preference under every strict responsive extension, almost everywhere. $\square$

The following additional invariance result is useful computationally.

Theorem 2. All stable assignments have identical course enrollments and identical schedule cardinalities almost everywhere.

Furthermore, every student with an incomplete schedule receives the same schedule in every stable assignment, up to null sets.

Proof. Compare any clearing $p$ with $p^\star\leq p$.

If $p_c>0$, clearing gives

$$ D_c(p^\star)\leq\kappa_c=D_c(p). $$

If $p_c=0$, then $p^\star_c=0$, and raising only the other cutoffs cannot decrease demand for $c$. Thus the same inequality holds. Summing gives

$$ \sum_cD_c(p^\star)\leq\sum_cD_c(p). $$

Availability inclusion also gives

$$ |S_{p^\star}(x)|\geq|S_p(x)| $$

pointwise. Integrating yields the reverse inequality between total enrollments. Both total inequalities are therefore equalities.

Each course's enrollment difference is nonnegative, so every such difference is zero. Likewise, the nonnegative measurable function

$$ |S_{p^\star}(x)|-|S_p(x)| $$

has integral zero, so it vanishes almost everywhere.

If this common cardinality is less than $q_I$, then $S_{p^\star}(x)=A_{p^\star}(x)$ and

$$ S_p(x)\subseteq A_p(x)\subseteq A_{p^\star}(x)=S_{p^\star}(x). $$

Equal cardinalities force equality of schedules. Comparing each stable assignment with $S_{p^\star}$ proves all assertions. $\square$

5. Rationality and polynomial output length

For each course choose a band

$$ j_c\leq p_c\leq j_c+1, \qquad j_c\in\{0,\ldots,K_c-1\}, $$

and put

$$ u_c=j_c+1-p_c. $$

Within these bands,

$$ a_{tc}(p)= \begin{cases} 0,&k_{tc}\leq j_c,\\ u_c,&k_{tc}=j_c+1,\\ 1,&k_{tc}\geq j_c+2. \end{cases} $$

Choose a permutation $\sigma$ and impose

$$ u_{\sigma(1)}\leq\cdots\leq u_{\sigma(m)}. $$

These closed regions cover $P$. There are at most

$$ \left(\prod_cK_c\right)m!\leq\tau^m m! $$

of them.

Set $z_0=0$, $z_i=u_{\sigma(i)}$ for $1\leq i\leq m$, and $z_{m+1}=1$. In each rank interval $(z_i,z_{i+1})$, every type's availability and selected schedule are fixed by the bands and permutation. Denote that schedule by $V_{ti}$. Therefore

$$ D_c(p)= \sum_t\mu_t\sum_{i=0}^{m} \mathbf{1}_{\{c\in V_{ti}\}}(z_{i+1}-z_i). $$

This identity remains valid on region boundaries: tied endpoints merely create zero-length intervals. Each demand is consequently affine with rational coefficients on each region.

Intersect a region with the capacity inequalities. The result is a bounded rational polytope with $m$ variables, polynomially many constraints, and polynomial encoding length.

Choose such a polytope $Q$ containing $p^\star$. Every $v\in Q$ is feasible, so $v\geq p^\star$. Hence $p^\star$ uniquely minimizes

$$ \sum_cp_c $$

over $Q$. A unique linear-objective minimizer on a polytope is a vertex: any nontrivial convex decomposition would force both constituents to attain the same objective value.

At a vertex, $m$ linearly independent active constraints determine the coordinates. For completeness, their bit bounds can be made uniform. Let $W$ be the product of the input denominators of the masses and capacities; then $W\leq2^L$. In the displayed affine-demand formula, constants are rational combinations of the masses with integer coefficients bounded by a polynomial in $m,\tau$, and linear coefficients have the same property. Multiplying all constraints by $W$ therefore gives integer coefficients and right-hand sides bounded by $2^{h_0}$ for some explicitly computable

$$ h_0=O(L+\log(m\tau+1)). $$

Cramer's rule bounds every coordinate's numerator and denominator by

$$ m!\,2^{mh_0}. $$

Thus an explicitly computable polynomial bound $H$ exists such that each reduced coordinate of $p^\star$ has numerator and denominator at most $2^H$.

For each type, availability changes only at its at most $m$ prefix endpoints. Hence $S_{p^\star}$ has at most $m+1$ intervals per type, with rational endpoints of polynomial bit length. Listing the schedules gives polynomial total output length.

This also justifies the notes' finite algorithm: minimize $\sum_cp_c$ on every nonempty regional feasible polytope and select the smallest optimum. Its unique global minimizer is $p^\star$. It uses at most $\tau^m m!$ polynomial-size rational linear programs, and is polynomial-time for fixed $m$.

6. General exact computation in $\mathsf{FP}^{\mathsf{NP}}$

Consider the decision problem with an instance, a course $c$, and a rational $z$:

$$ \mathcal E(I,c,z): \quad \text{Does some }p\in F\text{ satisfy }p_c\leq z? $$

Lemma 6. The problem $\mathcal E$ belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$.

Proof. A certificate is a rational cutoff vector satisfying the stated inequalities.

If the answer is yes, choose a cutoff region containing a feasible witness and intersect its feasible polytope with $p_c\leq z$. This is a nonempty bounded rational polytope, so it has a vertex. The determinant argument from Section 5, now including the numerator and denominator of $z$, gives a certificate of polynomial bit length in the complete query input.

The verifier evaluates demand using the interval formula in Section 1 and checks $p\in P$, every capacity inequality, and $p_c\leq z$. All operations have polynomial bit complexity. $\square$

Least feasibility gives the crucial equivalence

$$ \mathcal E(I,c,z)\text{ holds} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^\star_c\leq z. $$

The reverse direction uses $p^\star$ itself as a witness. The forward direction follows from $p^\star\leq p$ for every feasible $p$.

Theorem 3. The exact universally student-optimal stable assignment can be computed in $\mathsf{FP}^{\mathsf{NP}}$.

Proof. Let $Q_0=2^H$, using the uniform denominator bound from Section 5.

For each course, binary-search $[0,K_c]$ using the $\mathsf{NP}$ decision procedure for $\mathcal E$. After polynomially many queries, obtain a rational interval $[\ell_c,u_c]$ containing $p^\star_c$ and having width

$$ u_c-\ell_c<\frac{1}{4Q_0^2}. $$

All query endpoints have polynomial bit length.

Two distinct reduced rationals with denominators at most $Q_0$ differ by at least $1/Q_0^2$. Thus this interval contains exactly one such rational, namely $p^\star_c$.

It can be recovered in polynomial time by continued fractions. Specifically, let $w_c$ be the interval midpoint. If $p^\star_c=a/b$ in lowest terms, then

$$ \left|w_c-\frac ab\right| < \frac{1}{8Q_0^2} < \frac{1}{2b^2}. $$

The classical continued-fraction approximation theorem states that a reduced rational $a/b$ satisfying $|w-a/b|<1/(2b^2)$ is a convergent of $w$. Therefore compute the convergents of the rational $w_c$ and select the unique one lying in $[\ell_c,u_c]$ with denominator at most $Q_0$. The Euclidean algorithm performs this computation in polynomial time.

Having recovered every coordinate exactly, compute the availability endpoints and schedules. Section 5 proves that the resulting output has polynomial length. $\square$

This upper bound applies to variable $m,\tau,q_I$. It neither eliminates the oracle nor establishes NP-hardness.

7. Polynomial computation for common rank priorities

Assume now that

$$ b_{tc}=b_c \qquad \text{for every type }t\text{ and course }c. $$

The common budget requirement remains satisfied by $\sum_cb_c=B$. At every course, students are then prioritized by smaller $r$, with type-index ties confined to null sets.

Theorem 4. Under this assumption, the stable assignment is unique up to null sets and is computable exactly in polynomial time for arbitrary $q_I$.

Construction. Sweep the rank coordinate from $0$ to $1$.

At the start of a phase, let $U$ be the courses with positive remaining capacity. For each type, let $V_t(U)$ be its best desirable courses in $U$, up to quota. Define the enrollment rate

$$ \lambda_c=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}_{\{c\in V_t(U)\}}. $$

If the current rank is $\rho$ and remaining capacities are $R_c$, advance by

$$ \Delta= \min\left\{ 1-\rho,\ \min_{c\in U:\lambda_c>0}\frac{R_c}{\lambda_c} \right\}, $$

interpreting the inner minimum as infinite when there is no positive rate. Assign $V_t(U)$ throughout the phase's rank interval, subtract $\Delta\lambda_c$ from each remaining capacity, and remove courses whose remaining capacity becomes zero.

Every phase ending before rank $1$ exhausts at least one course. Thus there are at most $m+1$ phases.

Feasibility and stability. The definition of $\Delta$ preserves nonnegative remaining capacities, and schedules satisfy desirability and quota constraints.

If a student wants an omitted course, that course was already exhausted: while a course remains in $U$, choosing the best available courses precludes wanting it unassigned. All enrolled students of an exhausted course have earlier ranks than any later student wanting it, apart from null endpoint sets. Hence the constructed assignment is stable.

Uniqueness. In this case $K_c=1$. Let $S=S_p$ be any stable assignment, and put

$$ a_c=1-p_c. $$

Compare it with $S^\star=S_{p^\star}$. Availability under $S^\star$ contains availability under $S$, and Theorem 2 gives equal schedule sizes and equal course enrollments.

At each student where the schedules differ, pair the gained courses in $S^\star\setminus S$ bijectively with the lost courses in $S\setminus S^\star$. This can be done measurably by using course-index order. A gained course $c$ was unavailable under $p$, while a lost course $d$ was available. Therefore every such pair satisfies

$$ a_c\leq r<a_d. $$

Let $f_{cd}$ be the measure of these paired gains and losses. Equal enrollment in each course gives

$$ \sum_df_{cd}=\sum_df_{dc}. $$

Consequently,

$$ \sum_{c,d}f_{cd}(a_d-a_c)=0. $$

But every nonzero term has $a_d-a_c>0$. Thus all $f_{cd}$ vanish, proving $S=S^\star$ almost everywhere.

The sweep construction is stable, so it is this unique assignment.

Bit complexity. Each phase uses polynomially many rational operations. More explicitly, put the input masses and capacities over a common denominator $W$. Every positive rate is $A_c/W$, where $A_c$ is a positive integer of at most $O(L)$ bits. A capacity-exhaustion step introduces only a factor $A_c$ into the current common denominator; updating capacities introduces no additional denominator beyond this factor. There are at most $m$ exhaustion steps. All resulting endpoints and capacities therefore have polynomial bit length. This proves polynomial-time exact computation. $\square$

The independent-course case $q_I=m$ is also polynomial-time without the common-bid assumption. Every student takes every available desirable course. At each course, admit desirable types by decreasing bid group, fully admitting groups until capacity is reached. If a boundary group of total desirable mass $M$ follows already admitted mass $H$, admit its common rank prefix of length

$$ \rho=\frac{\kappa_c-H}{M}. $$

If total desirable mass does not exceed capacity, admit everyone desiring the course. Any excluded desirable course is wanted when $q_I=m$, so stability forces exactly these priority-prefix enrollments. Sorting, rational arithmetic, and assembling the interval output all take polynomial time.

8. Two concrete obstacles to straightforward algorithms

These examples identify failures of particular approaches; neither establishes hardness.

The feasible cutoff set need not be convex

Take one type, three courses $a,b,c$, quota $q_I=1$, ranking

$$ a\succ b\succ c\succ\varnothing, $$

and bids $1$ at every course, so $B=3$. Let capacities be

$$ (\kappa_a,\kappa_b,\kappa_c) = \left(\frac35,\frac35,\frac1{10}\right). $$

The cutoff vectors

$$ p=\left(\frac25,1,\frac3{10}\right), \qquad p'=\left(1,\frac25,\frac3{10}\right) $$

have demands respectively

$$ D(p)=\left(\frac35,0,\frac1{10}\right), \qquad D(p')=\left(0,\frac35,\frac1{10}\right). $$

Both belong to $F$. Their midpoint has availability lengths

$$ \left(\frac3{10},\frac3{10},\frac7{10}\right). $$

Students below rank $3/10$ choose $a$; students between ranks $3/10$ and $7/10$ choose $c$. Thus midpoint demand for $c$ is $2/5$, exceeding its capacity $1/10$. Hence $F$ is nonconvex.

The iteration in Section 2 can require exponentially many steps

Take two courses, two types, and $q_I=1$. Let both types bid $1$ at both courses. Type $1$ ranks

$$ a\succ\varnothing\succ b, $$

and type $2$ ranks

$$ b\succ\varnothing\succ a. $$

For an integer $h\geq1$, let

$$ \mu_1=\alpha=2^{-h}, \qquad \mu_2=1-\alpha, \qquad \kappa_a=\frac{\alpha}{2}, \qquad \kappa_b=1. $$

Starting the iteration at zero keeps $p_b^{(n)}=0$ and gives

$$ p_a^{(n+1)}=(1-\alpha)p_a^{(n)}+\frac{\alpha}{2}. $$

Therefore

$$ p_a^{(n)} = \frac12\bigl(1-(1-\alpha)^n\bigr), \qquad p_a^\star=\frac12. $$

No finite iterate reaches the exact cutoff. Also, Bernoulli's inequality gives

$$ (1-\alpha)^n\geq1-n\alpha. $$

For $n\leq2^{h-1}$, the cutoff error is consequently at least $1/4$. Thus this iteration needs exponentially many steps even for fixed cutoff accuracy, although the instance has input length $O(h)$ and belongs to the polynomial special case of Section 7.

9. Remaining question

The existence question is settled affirmatively. Exact output has polynomial length, and unrestricted computation lies in $\mathsf{FP}^{\mathsf{NP}}$.

The missing step is a general polynomial-time algorithm eliminating the oracle, or a valid NP-hardness reduction. Nonconvexity of the feasible set and slow convergence of one iteration prove neither alternative.
