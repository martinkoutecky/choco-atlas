The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(S\subseteq\mathfrak S(C)\) of ranking types, and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\rho\) summing to \(1\), compute a ranking \(\pi\in\mathfrak S(C)\) minimizing \(\sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho\,\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho)\), or decide whether that minimum is at most a supplied rational threshold.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set with \(m=|C|\), and let \(\mathfrak S(C)\) denote its strict complete rankings. Use unnormalized Kendall–Tau distance \(\mathrm{KT}\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(S\subseteq\mathfrak S(C)\) of \(\tau=|S|\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\rho\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible types.
Given a positive binary-rational threshold \(b\), decide whether \(\min_{\pi\in\mathfrak S(C)}\sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho)\le b\); the optimization version returns any minimizing ranking.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length and \(k=\lceil b\rceil\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and \(k\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? If it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, is it nevertheless fixed-parameter tractable with parameter \(k\)?

For discrete elections with \(n\) votes and integer score threshold \(K\), an algorithm runs in \(O(1.5079^K+m^2n)\) time.
Discrete Kemeny Score is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with four complete rankings ([Dwork et al.](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~naor/PAPERS/rank_www10.html)).
Expected-distance Kemeny medians of ranking distributions have also been studied ([Irurozki et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10640v1)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The threshold domain was unspecified; use a positive binary rational, matching the positive discrete threshold.
  • The continuous parameter was unspecified; use the ceiling of the normalized score threshold as the integer parameter.
  • Specify unnormalized Kendall–Tau distance, matching the discrete score.
  • Allow any minimizing ranking when several rankings tie; impose no additional tie-breaking.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(\tau\leq 4\). Nevertheless, it is fixed-parameter tractable with parameter \(k=\lceil b\rceil\).

A self-contained algorithm has bit running time
\[ O\!\left( \bigl(L+m^2\tau+m^2(k+1)64^k\bigr) \bigl(L+\log(m+1)\bigr)^2 \right). \]
On every yes-instance, the algorithm can also return a globally minimizing ranking.

Here, “binary-rational” may mean either dyadic rationals or rationals with binary-encoded numerators and denominators: the proof covers both interpretations.

1. Objective and exact arithmetic

Write
\[ F(\pi)=\sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho), \qquad \mathrm{OPT}=\min_{\pi\in\mathfrak S(C)}F(\pi). \]
The minimum exists because \(\mathfrak S(C)\) is finite.

For distinct candidates \(x,y\), define the cost of placing \(x\) before \(y\) by
\[ w_{xy}=\sum_{\rho:\,y\text{ precedes }x\text{ in }\rho}\mu_\rho. \]
Because Kendall–Tau distance counts disagreeing unordered pairs,
\[ F(\pi)=\sum_{x\text{ precedes }y\text{ in }\pi}w_{xy}. \]
Thus all pair costs, and then the scores of all support rankings, can be computed using \(O(m^2\tau)\) arithmetic operations.

Exact arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity. To see this, write the masses as \(\mu_\rho=a_\rho/d_\rho\) and the threshold as \(b=u/v\), and use the common denominator
\[ D=v\prod_{\rho\in S}d_\rho. \]
The binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). The scaled pair costs \(Dw_{xy}\) are integers between \(0\) and \(D\), and every scaled ranking score is at most \(D\binom m2\). Consequently, all relevant integers have
\[ B=O\!\left(L+\log(m+1)\right) \]
bits. Schoolbook integer arithmetic suffices for the running-time bound stated above.

2. Proof of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is a strict complete ranking \(\pi\). Its expected distance can be evaluated and compared with \(b\) exactly using the preceding integer representation, in polynomial time.

Hardness with at most four support types

We use the supplied theorem that discrete Kemeny Score is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete with four complete rankings; this is also stated in Theorem 11 of [Dwork et al., Appendix B](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~naor/PAPERS/rank_www10.html).

Let a source instance consist of four rankings \(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_4\) on \(C_0\) and a nonnegative integer threshold \(K\). Its score is
\[ T(\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^4\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho_i). \]

The target requires a strictly positive threshold, so we include a small gadget that also handles \(K=0\). Add candidates \(x,y\), placing both before every candidate of \(C_0\) in all four rankings. In the first two rankings place \(x\) before \(y\); in the last two place \(y\) before \(x\).

For any extended ranking:

  • The pairs within \(C_0\) contribute the score of its restriction to \(C_0\).
  • The pair \(\{x,y\}\) contributes exactly \(2\), whichever way it is ordered.
  • Pairs between \(\{x,y\}\) and \(C_0\) contribute a nonnegative amount.

Conversely, putting \(x,y\) first and following them by any ranking of \(C_0\) makes every cross-pair contribution zero. Therefore the new minimum discrete score is exactly
\[ \min_\pi T(\pi)+2. \]

Give each extended vote mass \(1/4\), merging identical rankings by adding their masses. This produces a support of size at most four with positive dyadic masses. Set
\[ b=\frac{K+2}{4}>0. \]
Then
\[ \min_\pi F(\pi)\leq b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \min_\pi T(\pi)\leq K. \]
The construction has polynomial input length. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, and hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

3. A reference ranking close to every optimum

Choose a support ranking \(r\in S\) minimizing \(F(r)\) over the support.

We prove two inequalities:
\[ F(r)\leq 2\mathrm{OPT}, \qquad \mathrm{KT}(r,\pi^\star)\leq 3\mathrm{OPT} \]
for every globally minimizing ranking \(\pi^\star\).

Kendall–Tau distance satisfies the triangle inequality: for each unordered pair, if two rankings disagree, at least one disagrees with any third ranking; summing these pairwise inequalities proves the assertion. Thus
\[ \mathrm{KT}(\rho,\sigma) \leq \mathrm{KT}(\rho,\pi^\star)+\mathrm{KT}(\pi^\star,\sigma). \]
Multiplying by \(\mu_\rho\mu_\sigma\) and summing gives
\[ \sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho F(\rho) \leq 2F(\pi^\star)=2\mathrm{OPT}. \]
The minimum support score is at most this weighted average, proving \(F(r)\leq2\mathrm{OPT}\).

Similarly, averaging
\[ \mathrm{KT}(r,\pi^\star) \leq \mathrm{KT}(r,\sigma)+\mathrm{KT}(\sigma,\pi^\star) \]
over \(\sigma\) gives
\[ \mathrm{KT}(r,\pi^\star) \leq F(r)+\mathrm{OPT} \leq3\mathrm{OPT}. \]

Consequently:

  • If \(F(r)>2b\), the instance is a no-instance.
  • If \(\mathrm{OPT}\leq b\), every optimum lies within Kendall–Tau distance \(3b\) of \(r\).

The reference ranking is computable in \(O(m^2\tau)\) arithmetic operations by evaluating the pair-cost formula on every support ranking.

4. Dynamic programming with bounded displacement

Relabel the candidates so that the reference ranking is \(r=(1,\ldots,m)\), and put
\[ R=\min\{3k,m-1\}. \]

If candidate \(i\) occupies position \(j\) in a ranking \(\pi\), then
\[ |i-j|\leq\mathrm{KT}(r,\pi). \]
Indeed, its sets of predecessors in the two rankings have sizes \(i-1\) and \(j-1\). Their symmetric difference has size at least \(|i-j|\), and every element of that difference forms a disagreeing pair with \(i\).

Therefore, whenever \(\mathrm{OPT}\leq b\), every optimum satisfies
\[ |\operatorname{pos}_\pi(i)-i|\leq R \qquad\text{for every }i. \]
We now minimize \(F\) exactly over all rankings satisfying these displacement bounds.

States

After \(t\) positions have been filled, let \(P\) be the set of placed candidates. Any admissible complete ranking must satisfy
\[ |P|=t, \qquad \{1,\ldots,\max(0,t-R)\}\subseteq P \subseteq\{1,\ldots,\min(m,t+R)\}. \]
The first inclusion holds because candidate \(i\leq t-R\) has latest allowed position \(i+R\leq t\). The second holds because a candidate \(i>t+R\) has earliest allowed position \(i-R>t\).

Only the candidates in the interval
\[ \{\max(0,t-R)+1,\ldots,\min(m,t+R)\} \]
have undetermined membership in \(P\). This interval contains at most \(2R\) candidates, so there are at most \(2^{2R}\) states at each layer.

Recurrence and correctness

Initialize the empty-prefix state with cost \(0\). From state \(P\) at layer \(t\), append an unplaced candidate \(x\) satisfying
\[ |x-(t+1)|\leq R, \]
provided \(P\cup\{x\}\) satisfies the state conditions at layer \(t+1\). The transition adds
\[ \sum_{y\in P}w_{yx}. \]
Keep the minimum cost for each resulting state.

This recurrence is exact. Appending \(x\) fixes precisely the pairs between \(x\) and previously placed candidates; their cost depends only on \(P\). Future contributions likewise depend on the placed set, rather than the internal order of an earlier prefix. Every path to the final state produces a ranking satisfying the displacement bounds. Conversely, every ranking satisfying those bounds passes through the stated prefix states and transitions. Finally, each unordered pair is charged exactly once, when its later candidate is appended.

Hence the final state contains the minimum expected distance over all displacement-bounded rankings. Predecessor pointers recover a minimizing ranking.

Running time

There are at most \(m+1\) layers, \(2^{2R}\) states per layer, and \(2R+1\) possible appended candidates per state. Evaluating a transition directly takes \(O(m)\) arithmetic operations. Thus the dynamic program uses
\[ O\!\left(m^2(R+1)2^{2R}\right) \]
arithmetic operations.

Because \(R\leq3k\), this is
\[ O\!\left(m^2(k+1)64^k\right). \]

5. Decision, optimization, and normalization

The decision algorithm first rejects if \(F(r)>2b\). Otherwise it runs the dynamic program and accepts exactly when the resulting minimum is at most \(b\).

If the original instance is a yes-instance, a global optimum belongs to the searched class, so the dynamic-programming minimum equals \(\mathrm{OPT}\). If it is a no-instance, every searched ranking still has score greater than \(b\). This proves correctness in both directions.

Including preprocessing and exact arithmetic gives the bit running time
\[ O\!\left( \bigl(L+m^2\tau+m^2(k+1)64^k\bigr) \bigl(L+\log(m+1)\bigr)^2 \right). \]
This is fixed-parameter tractable in \(k\), with polynomial dependence on the actual support size \(\tau\).

For optimization, the same algorithm returns a global median whenever \(b\) is an upper bound on \(\mathrm{OPT}\). Without a supplied upper bound, run it with integer thresholds \(1,2,\ldots\), stopping at the first yes-instance. With
\[ k_\star=\max\{1,\lceil\mathrm{OPT}\rceil\}, \]
the total running time satisfies the same bound with \(k_\star\) in place of \(k\), by summing the geometric exponential terms. Thus unrestricted optimization is FPT in the ceiling of the optimum.

An arbitrary threshold cannot parameterize unrestricted optimization: fixing \(b=1\) while requiring a median on every instance would still require solving the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard optimization problem.

Finally, clearing denominators and expanding masses into discrete votes does not establish FPT in \(k\). For example, two masses \(1-2^{-q}\) and \(2^{-q}\), with \(b=1/2\), have \(k=1\), but denominator clearing produces \(n=2^q\) votes and discrete threshold \(K=2^{q-1}\). The supplied discrete running time is therefore not an FPT bound in the normalized threshold. The argument above avoids this expansion entirely.

Verification report

The main argument is correct: it establishes that the decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even for support size \(\tau\leq4\), and fixed-parameter tractable in \(k=\lceil b\rceil\). The dynamic program also returns a global optimum on every yes-instance.

There is one minor issue: the assertion that an arbitrary threshold “cannot parameterize unrestricted optimization” needs the qualification “unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).” No substantive mathematical repair is needed.

1. Objective and exact arithmetic

Verified. The definition of \(w_{xy}\) has the correct orientation: placing \(x\) before \(y\) incurs precisely the mass of rankings placing \(y\) before \(x\). Consequently,
\[ F(\pi)=\sum_{x\text{ precedes }y\text{ in }\pi}w_{xy} \]
counts every disagreeing unordered pair exactly once.

Computing the pair costs requires \(O(m^2\tau)\) arithmetic operations. Once those costs are available, evaluating all \(\tau\) support rankings requires another \(O(m^2\tau)\) operations.

The denominator
\[ D=v\prod_{\rho\in S}d_\rho \]
is divisible by every mass denominator and by the threshold denominator. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), because the sum of the encoded denominator lengths is bounded by the total input length. No assumption of reduced fractions is necessary.

The bounds on scaled pair costs and ranking scores are correct:
\[ 0\leq Dw_{xy}\leq D, \qquad 0\leq DF(\pi)\leq D\binom m2. \]
The scaled threshold \(Db\) also has \(O(L)\) bits. Thus the stated bound
\[ B=O(L+\log(m+1)) \]
covers the score arithmetic and comparisons. Schoolbook arithmetic costs \(O(B^2)\) per operation, which is sufficient for the claimed bit-time bound.

The interpretation covering both explicitly encoded dyadic fractions and binary-encoded rational numerators and denominators is valid.

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and hardness

Membership verified. A complete ranking is a polynomial-length certificate, and its expected distance can be evaluated and compared with \(b\) exactly in polynomial time.

The cited hardness theorem is applicable. Theorem 11 in [Dwork et al., Appendix B](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~naor/PAPERS/rank_www10.html) states NP-hardness for even numbers of full lists at least four, and NP-completeness of the corresponding decision problem. In particular, it covers four strict complete rankings. The prover states the needed specialization and uses matching hypotheses.

The gadget is correct. In every extended vote, both new candidates precede all original candidates. Therefore:

  • Restricting an extended output ranking to \(C_0\) preserves its contribution from original candidate pairs.
  • The new pair disagrees with exactly two of the four votes, independently of its orientation.
  • Cross-pair contributions are nonnegative.
  • Placing both new candidates first makes all cross-pair contributions zero.

These observations justify exactly the claimed additive shift of \(2\) in the optimum discrete score.

Assigning mass \(1/4\) to each extended vote divides the discrete score by \(4\). Merging identical rankings preserves the objective and produces positive masses summing to \(1\), with support size at most four. The threshold
\[ b=\frac{K+2}{4} \]
is strictly positive, including when \(K=0\), and the stated decision equivalence follows.

The construction has polynomial encoding length. This establishes the claimed restricted NP-hardness and, together with membership, NP-completeness.

3. Reference ranking inequalities

Verified, including the quantifier “every optimum.”

The pairwise explanation of the Kendall–Tau triangle inequality is sufficient. Multiplying by \(\mu_\rho\mu_\sigma\) and summing is valid because the masses are nonnegative and sum to \(1\). The resulting inequality
\[ \sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho F(\rho)\leq2\mathrm{OPT} \]
holds for any chosen global optimum.

Since \(r\) minimizes \(F\) over the nonempty support, its score is at most that weighted average. Hence
\[ F(r)\leq2\mathrm{OPT}. \]

The second averaging argument correctly gives
\[ \mathrm{KT}(r,\pi^\star)\leq F(r)+\mathrm{OPT}\leq3\mathrm{OPT} \]
for each global optimum \(\pi^\star\). Neither inequality depends on which optimum is selected.

Both algorithmic consequences follow: \(F(r)>2b\) permits rejection, and every optimum of a yes-instance lies within distance \(3b\) of \(r\).

4. Displacement bounds and states

Verified. The predecessor-set argument correctly establishes
\[ |i-j|\leq\mathrm{KT}(r,\pi). \]
Every element of the symmetric difference contributes a distinct disagreeing pair incident to candidate \(i\).

For a yes-instance, the distance bound and \(b\leq k\) yield displacement at most \(3k\). Displacement is also always at most \(m-1\), so the choice
\[ R=\min\{3k,m-1\} \]
is justified.

The prefix inclusions have the correct boundary indices. Candidates at most \(t-R\) must already have appeared; candidates greater than \(t+R\) cannot yet have appeared. Together with \(|P|=t\), these give the stated necessary state conditions.

The interval of candidates with undetermined membership has length at most \(2R\), including at the initial and final layers. Thus \(2^{2R}\) is a valid upper bound on states per layer. Counting all subsets only overestimates the number satisfying the cardinality condition.

5. Recurrence and correctness

Verified. Appending \(x\) incurs
\[ \sum_{y\in P}w_{yx}, \]
with the correct orientation. These are precisely the pairs whose later candidate has just been placed.

The state-merging argument is sound: future feasibility depends on the placed set and layer, and future pair contributions depend on membership in the placed set, not the order of its already placed candidates. Keeping only the cheapest prefix for a state therefore preserves optimality.

Every completed transition path satisfies the displacement restriction because each appended candidate is checked at its actual position. Conversely, every displacement-bounded ranking satisfies the prefix conditions and is represented by a path. Each unordered pair is charged once. The final state therefore has exactly the minimum score over the searched class.

The reference ranking itself belongs to that class, so a final state always exists.

6. Running time and edge cases

Verified. There are at most \(m+1\) layers, \(2^{2R}\) states per layer, and \(2R+1\) candidate transitions per state. Direct transition evaluation uses \(O(m)\) arithmetic operations. The stated bound
\[ O\!\left(m^2(R+1)2^{2R}\right) \]
follows.

Because \(R\leq3k\),
\[ 2^{2R}\leq64^k, \qquad R+1\leq3(k+1). \]
Combining this with preprocessing and the arithmetic cost gives the claimed bit-time bound.

Routine state encoding and predecessor bookkeeping fit within this bound: \(R\leq m-1\), and the explicit support input contains a complete ranking, so state descriptions have polynomial length bounded by the input size.

The case \(m=1\) is handled: \(R=0\), the unique ranking has score zero, and the recurrence reaches it. A singleton support and instances with \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\) also cause no failure. Since \(b>0\), the decision parameter satisfies \(k\geq1\).

7. Decision and optimization claims

Decision correctness verified. On a yes-instance, every global optimum belongs to the searched class, so its restricted minimum equals \(\mathrm{OPT}\). On a no-instance, no searched ranking can have score at most \(b\). The preliminary rejection is also sound.

Optimization guarantees verified. The write-up correctly limits the same-threshold global optimization guarantee to instances where \(b\) bounds \(\mathrm{OPT}\). It does not claim that the restricted minimum is globally optimal on arbitrary no-instances.

Trying positive integer thresholds until the first acceptance terminates, since every expected distance is at most \(\binom m2\). The first accepted threshold is
