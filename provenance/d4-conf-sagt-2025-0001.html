The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, \(k=2\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings with a common line realization, compute the mass version \(S_\mu\) of the Polar Comparison Rule using pairwise masses \(\mu(a\succ b)\). For every compatible candidate placement \(y\) and unit-mass voter-location measure \(\nu\) whose ranking marginal is \(\mu\), require \(\sup_{(y,\nu)\models\mu}\frac{\int \sum_{a\in S_\mu}|z-y_a|\,d\nu(t,z)}{\min_{S\in\binom{C}{2}}\int \sum_{a\in S}|z-y_a|\,d\nu(t,z)}\le 1+\sqrt{2}\).

Let \(C=[m]\), with \(m\ge 2\), and fix committee size \(k=2\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) strict complete rankings with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; masses sum to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A compatible realization consists of distinct candidate locations \(y_a\in\mathbb R\) and a probability measure \(\nu\) on ranking–location pairs \((t,z)\), with ranking marginal \(\mu\), finite first moment, and \(|z-y_a|<|z-y_b|\) whenever \(a\succ_t b\), almost surely. Existence of such a realization is promised; locations are not supplied.
Write \(p_{ab}=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu(t)\). To fix ordinal choices, select the lexicographically least candidate sequence admitting a compatible realization in that left-to-right order. Orient majority arcs by \(p_{ab}>1/2\), breaking equal-mass ties toward the earlier candidate in this sequence; let the majority order be the lexicographically least Hamiltonian path of this tournament.
Define the mass Polar Comparison Rule as follows. Let \(a,b\) be the first two candidates in the majority order, and remove every candidate \(e\) for which \(p_{e'e}=1\) for some \(e'\notin\{e,a\}\). Among the remaining candidates strictly opposite \(b\) across \(a\) in the selected candidate sequence, let \(c\) be the nearest to \(a\) in that sequence. Set \(S_\mu=\{a,b\}\) if \(c\) does not exist, or \(p_{cb}\le 1/(1+\sqrt2)\), or \(p_{ba}\ge 1/(1+\sqrt2)\); otherwise set \(S_\mu=\{a,c\}\).
For a compatible realization define \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu(S;y)=\int\sum_{a\in S}|z-y_a|\,d\nu(t,z)\) and \(D_\mu(S)=\sup_{(y,\nu)\models\mu}\operatorname{SC}_\nu(S;y)/\min_{T\in\binom C2}\operatorname{SC}_\nu(T;y)\).
Can the mass Polar Comparison Rule be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) while guaranteeing \(D_\mu(S_\mu)\le 1+\sqrt2\) for every promised input, or does either requirement fail?
For finite electorates, the Polar Comparison Rule guarantees utilitarian additive distortion at most \(1+\sqrt2\); this bound is tight for deterministic two-winner rules when \(m\ge4\). Result

Both requirements hold. The mass Polar Comparison Rule is computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and every promised input satisfies \(D_\mu(S_\mu)\le 1+\sqrt2\).

The proof requires two separate arguments: an exact polynomial algorithm for the prescribed candidate order, and a transfer of the finite-electorate distortion theorem to arbitrary compatible probability measures.

1. Realizable candidate orders depend only on the support

Fix distinct candidate locations \(y\). For a ranking \(t\), define its location region by

\(\displaystyle I_t(y)=\{z\in\mathbb R: |z-y_a|<|z-y_b|\text{ whenever }a\succ_t b\}.\)

For distinct locations, each pairwise inequality defines an open half-line whose boundary is the corresponding candidate midpoint. Thus \(I_t(y)\) is an open interval, possibly unbounded or empty.

A candidate order admits a compatible realization of \(\mu\) if and only if it admits a Euclidean embedding with one voter for each supported ranking:

  • A compatible measure gives positive mass to each supported ranking, so its corresponding interval is nonempty. Choose one point from each interval.
  • Conversely, place mass \(\mu(t)\) at a chosen point of each nonempty interval.

The latter measure has finite support and therefore finite first moment. Consequently, candidate-order feasibility depends only on the supported rankings, not on their positive masses.

Call a candidate order a *single-peaked axis* if every initial segment of every supported ranking is consecutive in that order. Every realizable candidate order is such an axis: candidates closer than a given distance to a voter form an interval on the line.

2. A characterization permitting lexicographic optimization

We use the following structural part of Knoblauch’s Euclidean-recognition algorithm.

Known structural result. For a Euclidean-realizable finite profile of strict complete rankings, one can compute in polynomial time a subset \(B\subseteq C\) and an order \(\pi_B\) such that:

  • Every Euclidean embedding of the restricted profile on \(B\) orders \(B\) as \(\pi_B\) or its reversal.
  • Every candidate in \(U=C\setminus B\) is unanimously comparable with every other candidate.

These are the properties established in Step 3 and Lemma 2 of [Knoblauch’s working paper](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2008-52.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here by Section 1. The algorithm uses the \(\tau\) supported rankings as its finite profile.

The following extension lemma supplies the additional fact needed for our lexicographic requirement.

Lemma 1: characterization of all realizable axes

A candidate order \(\sigma\) is realizable if and only if:

  • \(\sigma\) is a single-peaked axis for the supported rankings; and
  • its restriction to \(B\) is \(\pi_B\) or its reversal.

Proof. Necessity follows from the preceding observations and the structural result.

For sufficiency, first suppose \(B\ne\varnothing\). Start with an embedding of the restricted profile on \(B\), reflected if necessary to match \(\sigma|_B\). Use one voter location \(z_t\) for each original supported ranking.

Candidates in \(U\) have the same relative ranking in every vote. Insert them in that unanimous preference order, from best to worst, always following their positions in \(\sigma\).

Consider an insertion of \(u\). Among the candidates already present, let \(P\) consist of those unanimously preferred to \(u\), and let \(Q\) consist of those unanimously worse than \(u\). These sets partition the present candidates because \(u\) is unanimously comparable with every candidate.

Suppose first that \(P\ne\varnothing\). In the restriction of \(\sigma\) to the present candidates and \(u\), both \(P\) and \(P\cup\{u\}\) are initial ranking segments. Hence both are consecutive. Thus \(u\) is immediately outside \(P\), on one specified side, and every candidate in \(Q\) lies outside the interval occupied by \(P\).

Keep \(P\) and all voters fixed. Translate every member of \(Q\) to the left of \(P\) leftward by \(H\), and every member to the right rightward by \(H\).

This preserves the current rankings:

  • Every voter lies to the left of every right-side candidate \(q\in Q\). Indeed, choosing any \(p\in P\), unanimous preference for \(p\) over \(q\) gives \(z_t<(y_p+y_q)/2<y_q\). The analogous statement holds on the left.
  • Consequently, translations preserve comparisons within either side of \(Q\).
  • Comparisons between opposite sides of \(Q\) are preserved because their midpoints remain unchanged.
  • Every distance to a member of \(Q\) increases by \(H\), so comparisons favoring \(P\) over \(Q\) remain valid.

Choose a location \(w\) for \(u\), outside \(P\) on its specified side, sufficiently far away that every voter prefers every member of \(P\) to \(u\). Then choose \(H\) sufficiently large that every voter prefers \(u\) to every member of \(Q\), and \(u\) lies before the translated \(Q\) candidates on its side. This realizes all required comparisons and the prescribed order.

If \(P=\varnothing\), then \(u\) is universally best among the current candidates. Choose a point \(h\) in the gap prescribed for \(u\) by \(\sigma\). Translate the candidates to its left by \(-H\), and those to its right by \(H\). Cross-side midpoints remain unchanged. Within either side, the rankings are unanimously monotone toward \(h\), because the axis is single-peaked with common top candidate \(u\). For sufficiently large \(H\), all voters lie between the two sides, those within-side rankings hold, and every voter prefers a new candidate at \(h\) to every translated candidate.

These insertions finish the construction.

If \(B=\varnothing\), unanimous comparability means all rankings coincide. Place their best candidate and all voters at zero. Insert subsequent candidates on their prescribed sides, choosing each new distance sufficiently large. The same nonempty-\(P\) insertion argument applies, with \(Q\) empty. This realizes every single-peaked axis. \(\square\)

3. Computing the least realizable candidate sequence

For each supported ranking and each initial segment, impose the requirement that its candidate set be consecutive. There are \(\tau m\) such sets, with total explicit size polynomial in \(\tau,m\).

The [Booth–Lueker PQ-tree theorem](https://www.cs.uoi.gr/~loukas/courses/grad/Algorithmic_Graph_Theory/index.files/Booth-Lueker-PQtrees.pdf) gives a polynomial algorithm constructing a tree whose frontiers are exactly the permutations satisfying these consecutivity constraints. At a P-node its children may be permuted arbitrarily; at a Q-node their listed order may be reversed.

By Lemma 1, we must find the least frontier whose restriction to \(B\) is \(\pi_B\), and separately the least whose restriction is its reversal.

Here is an explicit polynomial procedure for either prescribed core order.

For every node, its descendant \(B\)-leaves must be consecutive in the prescribed core order, since all its descendant leaves form one block in a frontier. Reject a node if this condition fails. Process the tree from leaves upward.

  • P-node. Children containing core leaves must occur in the order of their disjoint core intervals. Children containing no core leaves are unrestricted. Having computed each child’s least feasible frontier, concatenate child blocks greedily: among unrestricted children and the next available core-containing child, choose the block with the smallest first candidate.
  • Q-node. Check each of its two child orders. Retain those respecting the prescribed core order, substitute each child’s least feasible frontier, and choose the smaller concatenation.

The greedy P-node step is exact. Its available child blocks have distinct first candidates, and selecting any available block leaves a feasible ordering of the remaining blocks. Therefore the smallest available first candidate determines the lexicographically best next block. Internal child choices are independent once their core subsequences are fixed.

Induction proves that each node returns its least feasible frontier. All operations are polynomial. Taking the smaller root frontier from the two core orientations gives exactly the least realizable candidate sequence required by the question. If \(B=\varnothing\), run the unconstrained version once.

4. Majority order and numerical comparisons are polynomial

Compute all \(p_{ab}\) directly from the support using exact rational arithmetic.

The tournament specified in the question is transitive. To prove this, take an embedding in the selected candidate order with one voter point per supported ranking. Clear the rational mass denominators, obtaining a finite electorate for this argument.

Let \(z_*\) be its lower median voter location. For candidates with \(y_a<y_b\), write \(h=(y_a+y_b)/2\). Strict compatibility excludes voters at \(h\). The tournament directs \(a\) toward \(b\) precisely when at least half the electorate lies to the left of \(h\), including equality because \(a\) occurs earlier on the axis. This is equivalent to \(z_*<h\).

Thus the tournament is exactly the strict distance ranking of the lower median voter. It is transitive and has a unique Hamiltonian path. Its lexicographically least Hamiltonian path therefore requires only sorting or topological ordering.

After this, the domination test and the search for \(c\) are direct polynomial scans.

The irrational threshold also presents no computational difficulty. Since \(1/(1+\sqrt2)=\sqrt2-1\), for a nonnegative rational \(r=u/v\), with \(v>0\),

\(\displaystyle r\le\frac1{1+\sqrt2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (u+v)^2\le 2v^2.\)

The corresponding greater-than-or-equal comparison is obtained by reversing the inequality.

A common denominator has bit length at most the sum of the input denominator lengths. Hence all rational sums and integer comparisons have polynomial bit complexity. The whole rule is therefore computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

5. Checking the finite theorem’s ordinal conventions

Before transferring the distortion bound, we verify that the selected axis does not cause a mismatch with the geometric Polar Comparison Rule in another compatible embedding.

Let \(a,b\) be the first two majority candidates, and let \(R\) be the surviving candidates other than \(a\).

The candidate \(a\) survives: a unanimous dominator would have a majority arc toward \(a\), contradicting its first position in the transitive tournament. Also \(b\in R\): any unanimous dominator other than \(a\) would precede \(b\).

Any two distinct members of \(R\) are not unanimously comparable; otherwise one would remove the other. Therefore, if \(|R|\ge2\), every member of \(R\) belongs to \(B\).

Every tied-majority pair likewise belongs to \(B\), since a candidate outside \(B\) is unanimously comparable with every candidate. Reflect any compatible embedding, if necessary, so that its order on \(B\) agrees with the selected axis. Reflection preserves all costs. All majority ties then point toward the geometrically left candidate.

The surviving order also agrees:

  • If \(a\in B\), all surviving candidates belong to \(B\), so their order agrees.
  • If \(a\notin B\), its first majority position and unanimous comparability imply that every ranking places \(a\) first. On either side of \(a\), a nearer candidate unanimously dominates every farther candidate. For example, if \(y_a<y_r<y_s\), preference for \(a\) over \(r\) forces every voter to satisfy \(z<(y_a+y_r)/2<y_r\), and consequently to prefer \(r\) over \(s\). Thus \(R\) contains at most one candidate on each side. If it has two, they lie on opposite sides of \(a\), fixing the surviving order once their core orientation is fixed.
  • If \(|R|=1\), the surviving set is just \(\{a,b\}\), and \(c\) does not exist.

Consequently, in every compatible finite embedding, after the harmless reflection, our majority choices and geometric choice of \(c\) coincide with Algorithm 3 of the cited paper.

We may therefore apply its finite-electorate theorem: for strict complete rankings and distinct candidate locations on the line, that two-winner Polar Comparison Rule has utilitarian additive distortion at most \(1+\sqrt2\). The domination exclusion and both threshold branches are exactly those in this question after normalizing voter counts. See [Algorithm 3 and Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.13720).

6. Transferring the bound to probability measures

Fix an arbitrary compatible realization \((y,\nu)\). Define

\(\displaystyle F_a=\int |z-y_a|\,d\nu(t,z).\)

Every \(F_a\) is finite by the first-moment assumption.

Lemma 2: finite compression preserving all costs

There exists a compatible finite atomic measure with the same ranking marginal and exactly the same \(F_a\) for every candidate.

Proof. Order the candidate locations and partition the line into the \(m+1\) intervals cut by those locations, assigning each boundary consistently to an adjacent interval.

For each supported ranking \(t\), restrict its conditional location distribution to each partition cell of positive mass. Replace that restriction by one atom at its conditional mean.

Every function \(z\mapsto |z-y_a|\) is affine on each cell. Thus conditional averaging preserves its integral.

The new atom remains in \(I_t(y)\): that set is an open interval, and the mean of an integrable random variable lying almost surely strictly between its finite endpoints also lies strictly between them. Therefore every atom remains strictly compatible.

There are at most \(\tau(m+1)\) atoms. Their masses preserve the ranking marginal and every candidate’s cost. \(\square\)

Denote these atoms by \((t,z_{tj})\), with masses \(\alpha_{tj}>0\). They satisfy \(\sum_j\alpha_{tj}=\mu(t)\).

Choose a positive integer \(Q\) with \(Q\mu(t)\) integral for every supported ranking. For each positive integer \(N\), approximate the atom masses by multiples of \(1/(NQ)\), preserving each ranking’s total exactly. Explicitly, round down all but the last atom count within each ranking, and assign the remaining count to its last atom.

The resulting counts \(n_{tj}^{(N)}\) are nonnegative integers satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_j n_{tj}^{(N)}=NQ\mu(t),\qquad \frac{n_{tj}^{(N)}}{NQ}\longrightarrow\alpha_{tj}.\)

Place that many voters at each \(z_{tj}\). This gives a compatible finite electorate with exactly the original normalized ranking masses. Hence it selects exactly \(S_\mu\): its support, candidate-order feasibility, majority comparisons, domination tests, and thresholds are unchanged.

Let \(\nu_N\) be its normalized empirical measure. Because the atom locations are fixed and finite in number,

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_{\nu_N}(T;y)\longrightarrow\operatorname{SC}_{\nu}(T;y)\)

for every committee \(T\). There are finitely many two-candidate committees, so their minimum costs converge as well.

The finite theorem gives, for every \(N\),

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_{\nu_N}(S_\mu;y)\le(1+\sqrt2)\min_{T\in\binom C2}\operatorname{SC}_{\nu_N}(T;y).\)

Passing to the limit proves the same inequality for \(\nu\).

Finally, its denominator is positive. For distinct candidates \(r,s\), the triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_{\nu}(\{r,s\};y)\ge |y_r-y_s|>0.\)

Taking the minimum over finitely many pairs preserves positivity. Dividing and then taking the supremum over all compatible realizations establishes \(D_\mu(S_\mu)\le1+\sqrt2\).

The potentially enormous finite electorates appear only in the proof of the bound; the polynomial algorithm never constructs them.
