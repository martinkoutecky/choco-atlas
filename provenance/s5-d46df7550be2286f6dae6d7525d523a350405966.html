The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite item set \(V\), complete bidder types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), monotone supermodular quadratic utilities \(f_t\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\in[0,1]\), compute a welfare-maximizing mass assignment \(y_{t,S}\ge 0\) to integral bundles \(S\subseteq V\). Maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}f_t(S)\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v}y_{t,S}=q_v\) for every \(v\); determine the exact complexity in \(n=|V|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and input bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(V=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) be a nonempty item set. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types.
Each type is specified by nonnegative binary rational coefficients \(a_t(u,v),b_t(v)\), defining the monotone supermodular quadratic utility \(f_t(S)=\sum_{u<v,\,u,v\in S}a_t(u,v)+\sum_{v\in S}b_t(v)\), so \(f_t(\varnothing)=0\). Every bundle \(S\subseteq V\), including the empty bundle, is admissible.
Supplies \(q_v\in[0,1]\) are binary rationals. A mass assignment has \(y_{t,S}\geq0\), \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni v}y_{t,S}=q_v\). Its welfare is \(\sum_t\sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}f_t(S)\).
The computational problem is to compute an exact welfare-maximizing assignment, represented by its nonzero bundle masses; unlisted entries are zero. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Is this problem solvable in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite bidders receiving a partition of the items, maximizing welfare with these utilities is NP-hard even with three bidders, and polynomial-time solvable with two bidders ([Shioura–Suzuki](https://orsj.org/wp-content/or-archives50/pdf/e_mag/Vol.55_01_092.pdf)).
No result settling the exact mass-assignment problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified utilities by binary rational pair and singleton coefficients, all nonnegative, and fixed the empty-bundle utility to zero, following the discrete utility definition.
  • Interpreted complete types as permitting every subset of items, including the empty bundle; no bundle-size restriction is imposed.
  • Specified the output as a list of nonzero bundle masses, since requiring the full exponentially indexed array would introduce an output-size obstruction.
  • Took the item set to be nonempty and defined L as the total bit length of masses, supplies, and utility coefficients.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem is solvable exactly in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\). An optimal assignment can be returned with at most \(\tau(n+1)\) nonzero bundle masses.

The key is that, within each type, a nested family of bundles simultaneously maximizes every pair’s joint allocation mass for prescribed item marginals.

1. Optimal welfare for prescribed marginals

Fix a type \(t\), write \(m=\mu_t\), and prescribe item marginals \(x_v\) satisfying \(0\leq x_v\leq m\).

For any assignment of total mass \(m\) with these marginals, define the pair marginal

\(p_{uv}=\sum_{S\supseteq\{u,v\}}y_{t,S}\).

Every bundle counted in \(p_{uv}\) is counted in both item marginals. Consequently,

\(p_{uv}\leq\min\{x_u,x_v\}\).

Expanding the utility and interchanging finite sums gives the type’s welfare as

\(\sum_S y_{t,S}f_t(S)=\sum_v b_t(v)x_v+\sum_{u<v}a_t(u,v)p_{uv}\).

Since every \(a_t(u,v)\) is nonnegative, this welfare is at most

\(F_t(x)=\sum_v b_t(v)x_v+\sum_{u<v}a_t(u,v)\min\{x_u,x_v\}\).

We now construct an assignment attaining this bound.

Choose an ordering \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\) with

\(x_{v_1}\geq x_{v_2}\geq\cdots\geq x_{v_n}\),

and set \(x_{v_{n+1}}=0\). Let \(S_k=\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\}\). Assign masses

\(y_{t,\varnothing}=m-x_{v_1}\),

\(y_{t,S_k}=x_{v_k}-x_{v_{k+1}}\quad(1\leq k\leq n)\),

with all other entries zero. These masses are nonnegative, and their sum telescopes to \(m\).

The bundles containing \(v_i\) are exactly the prefixes \(S_k\) with \(k\geq i\), so its marginal is

\(\sum_{k=i}^n(x_{v_k}-x_{v_{k+1}})=x_{v_i}\).

Similarly, if \(i<j\), the bundles containing both \(v_i\) and \(v_j\) are exactly those with \(k\geq j\). Their joint mass is

\(\sum_{k=j}^n(x_{v_k}-x_{v_{k+1}})=x_{v_j}=\min\{x_{v_i},x_{v_j}\}\).

Thus every pair bound is attained simultaneously. This proves:

Marginal lemma. For any prescribed marginals in \([0,\mu_t]^n\), the maximum welfare of type \(t\) is exactly \(F_t(x)\), attained using at most \(n+1\) bundles.

Ties and zero marginals cause no difficulty: the resulting zero masses are omitted.

2. A polynomial-size linear program

Introduce variables \(x_{t,v}\) for item marginals and \(z_{t,uv}\) for each type and unordered pair \(u<v\). Consider the linear program with objective

\(\max\ \sum_t\sum_v b_t(v)x_{t,v}+\sum_t\sum_{u<v}a_t(u,v)z_{t,uv}\),

subject to

\(0\leq x_{t,v}\leq\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t,v\),

\(\sum_t x_{t,v}=q_v\quad\text{for every }v\),

\(0\leq z_{t,uv}\leq x_{t,u},\qquad z_{t,uv}\leq x_{t,v}\quad\text{for every }t,u<v\).

This program is feasible: take \(x_{t,v}=\mu_tq_v\) and every \(z_{t,uv}=0\). The marginal equalities hold because \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). All variables are bounded, so the feasible region is nonempty and compact; hence an optimum exists.

Let \(W^\star\) denote the original assignment optimum and \(P^\star\) this linear program’s optimum.

For any feasible mass assignment, set

\(x_{t,v}=\sum_{S\ni v}y_{t,S}\),

\(z_{t,uv}=\sum_{S\supseteq\{u,v\}}y_{t,S}\).

These variables satisfy all the linear constraints, and their objective equals the assignment’s welfare by expansion of the utilities. Therefore,

\(W^\star\leq P^\star\).

Conversely, take an optimal linear-program solution \((x^\star,z^\star)\). Apply the marginal lemma separately to each type, using marginals \(x^\star_{t,v}\). The resulting assignment has total mass \(\mu_t\) for each type and satisfies every supply equality. Its welfare is

\(\sum_t F_t(x^\star_t)=\sum_t\sum_v b_t(v)x^\star_{t,v}+\sum_t\sum_{u<v}a_t(u,v)\min\{x^\star_{t,u},x^\star_{t,v}\}\).

Because \(z^\star_{t,uv}\leq\min\{x^\star_{t,u},x^\star_{t,v}\}\) and the pair coefficients are nonnegative, this welfare is at least \(P^\star\). Hence,

\(W^\star\geq P^\star\).

Combining the inequalities proves \(W^\star=P^\star\), and proves that the reconstruction yields an optimal assignment.

3. Exact computation and output size

The linear program has

\(D=\tau\left(n+\binom n2\right)\)

variables and \(O(\tau n^2+n)\) constraints. Its constraint coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\); its remaining numerical data are the input masses, supplies, and utility coefficients. Its total binary encoding length is therefore polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\).

We use the standard rational linear programming theorem: a linear program with explicitly encoded rational data can be solved in polynomial bit time, returning an exact rational optimum whenever a finite optimum exists. The polynomial-time result for rational data is stated in [Adler–Beling, *Polynomial Algorithms for Linear Programming over the Algebraic Numbers*](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf). Here the required hypotheses hold: the program is explicitly represented, rational, feasible, and bounded.

For each type, sort the exact optimal marginals and apply the prefix construction from Section 1. Sorting rational numbers and subtracting rational coordinates take polynomial bit time. The optimal coordinates have polynomial encoding length, and each reconstructed mass is a difference of two such coordinates, or of a coordinate and \(\mu_t\). Thus every output mass also has polynomial encoding length.

There are at most \(n+1\) output bundles per listed type. Encoding each bundle by its item incidence vector gives a total output length polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\). This computes the requested assignment itself, including the empty bundle whenever its mass is positive.

4. Why the finite-bidder hardness does not transfer

The [Shioura–Suzuki problem](https://orsj.org/wp-content/or-archives50/pdf/e_mag/Vol.55_01_092.pdf) requires bidders to receive a partition of the items. Here each type’s mass may be split among several bundles, and supplies constrain aggregate item marginals. The feasible sets differ substantially.

For example, take three items and three types, each of mass \(1/3\), with every supply equal to \(1/3\). Let the types’ only nonzero coefficients be

\(a_1(1,2)=a_2(2,3)=a_3(1,3)=1\).

If each type must place its entire mass on one bundle, the supply constraints force those bundles to partition the items. The three valued pairs intersect pairwise, so at most one type obtains its valued pair. The maximum welfare under this restriction is \(1/3\).

In the mass-assignment problem, give each type mass \(1/6\) on its valued pair and mass \(1/6\) on the empty bundle. Each item then receives mass \(1/3\), and welfare is \(1/2\).

This value is optimal: for each type, its valued pair’s joint mass is at most half the sum of the two corresponding item marginals. Summing these inequalities and then including the remaining nonnegative marginals gives

\(W\leq\frac12\sum_t\sum_v x_{t,v}=\frac12\sum_v q_v=\frac12\).

Thus splitting type masses changes the optimum even in a small instance, and the polynomial algorithm above fully settles the stated problem.
