The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given household types \(i\in[\tau]\), each with positive rational population mass \(\alpha_i\), weight \(w_i>0\), and nonnegative utility \(u_i(h)\) for each house type \(h\in[\sigma]\), and per-capita house capacities \(\beta_h\geq 0\) with \(\sum_h\beta_h\geq 1\), decide whether there are assignment masses \(x_{ih}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_h x_{ih}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ih}\leq\beta_h\), such that for every pair of positive-mass assignments \(x_{ih}>0\) and \(x_{jg}>0\), \(u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\) or \(u_i(h)/w_i\geq u_i(g)/w_j\). Determine the complexity in \(\tau\), \(\sigma\), and the binary input length \(L\), including when there are at most two weight values and three utility values. Each household still receives one whole house; the assignment masses describe cohorts receiving each house type.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\geq1\) household types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\alpha_i\), with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=\sigma\geq1\) house types. Household type \(i\) has positive binary-rational weight \(w_i\) and binary-encoded nonnegative integer utilities \(u_i(h)\). House capacities \(\beta_h\geq0\) are binary rationals with \(\sum_h\beta_h\geq1\); unused capacity is allowed. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
An assignment consists of real masses \(x_{ih}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_hx_{ih}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ih}\leq\beta_h\). Each household receives one whole house; masses describe cohorts and may split across house types.
The assignment is SumAvg-envy-free if, for every ordered pair \((i,h),(j,g)\) with \(x_{ih}>0\) and \(x_{jg}>0\), including \(i=j\), we have \(u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\) or \(u_i(h)/w_i\geq u_i(g)/w_j\). Equality is permitted.

Is deciding whether a SumAvg-envy-free assignment exists in \(\mathrm{P}\) or NP-hard, both in general and under the simultaneous restrictions \(|\{w_i\}|\leq2\) and \(|\{u_i(h):i,h\}|\leq3\)? State running times in \(m=\sigma\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).

For finite households and individually listed houses, SumAvg-envy-free house-allocation existence is NP-hard even with two weight values and three utility values; it is polynomial-time solvable with binary utilities or identical utility functions. [Liu and Xiao](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05353v2)
No result settling this exact cohort-mass feasibility problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized household masses to sum to one, matching the distribution interpretation and per-capita capacities.
  • Specified nonnegative integer utilities, as in the discrete model, and positive rational weights encoded in binary because the weight encoding was unspecified.
  • Specified real assignment masses without integrality requirements; household cohorts may split while each household receives a whole house.
  • Explicitly included comparisons between cohorts of the same household type and permitted equality, preserving the pairwise fairness definition.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, both in general and under the simultaneous restrictions of at most two weight values and three utility values. This remains true when all masses and capacities are dyadic rationals and total capacity equals one.

Exact algorithms have running times

\( \displaystyle \min\{m^\tau,(\tau+1)^m\}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) \)

in general, and

\( \displaystyle 3^{\min\{m,\tau\}}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) \)

under the simultaneous restrictions.

The key to hardness is that an integral allocation can be recovered on the support of a fractional assignment. Since the envy conditions depend only on that support, they survive this recovery.

1. Utility levels and polynomial verification

Lemma 1. In every SumAvg-envy-free assignment, each household type receives houses of a single utility value, although it may receive several house types.

Proof. Suppose \(x_{ih}>0\) and \(x_{ig}>0\). Applying the condition to these two cohorts of the same type gives

\( \displaystyle u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{u_i(h)}{w_i}\geq\frac{u_i(g)}{w_i}. \)

Because \(w_i>0\), both alternatives are equivalent to \(u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\). Reversing the ordered pair gives the opposite inequality. Thus \(u_i(h)=u_i(g)\). Since \(\alpha_i>0\), every type has at least one positive assignment entry. \(\square\)

Let \(V_i=\{u_i(h):1\leq h\leq m\}\). For a proposed vector \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_\tau)\), with \(v_i\in V_i\), define the allowed pairs by

\( \displaystyle E(v)=\left\{(j,g):u_j(g)=v_j,\ \text{and for every }i,\ v_i\geq u_i(g)\ \text{or}\ v_iw_j\geq u_i(g)w_i\right\}. \)

Lemma 2. A SumAvg-envy-free assignment exists if and only if, for some such vector \(v\), the mass constraints are feasible using only pairs in \(E(v)\).

Proof. Given an envy-free assignment, Lemma 1 defines \(v_i\). For any positive entry \(x_{jg}\) and any household type \(i\), choose a positive entry \(x_{ih}\). The envy condition, together with \(u_i(h)=v_i\), gives exactly the condition defining \(E(v)\).

Conversely, suppose an assignment uses only \(E(v)\). For any positive entries \(x_{ih}\) and \(x_{jg}\), we have \(u_i(h)=v_i\). Membership of \((j,g)\) in \(E(v)\) then gives

\( \displaystyle u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\quad\text{or}\quad u_i(h)w_j\geq u_i(g)w_i, \)

which is the required condition because both weights are positive. \(\square\)

For a fixed allowed set \(E\), feasibility is a maximum-flow problem. Introduce a source \(s\), household vertices \(i\), house vertices \(h\), and a sink \(t\), with capacities:

  • \(s\to i\): \(\alpha_i\);
  • \(i\to h\), for \((i,h)\in E\): \(1\);
  • \(h\to t\): \(\min\{\beta_h,1\}\).

A flow of value one saturates every source arc, since their capacities sum to one. Its middle-arc flows therefore constitute exactly a feasible assignment supported on \(E\). Conversely, every such assignment produces a flow of value one. Clipping house capacities at one is harmless because total assigned mass is one.

All comparisons defining \(E(v)\) can be performed exactly by integer cross multiplication. Clearing the mass and capacity denominators produces integer capacities of polynomial bit length. Maximum flow is therefore computable in polynomial time. The integral-flow theorem also ensures a rational feasible assignment of polynomial encoding length. These standard flow properties and the Edmonds–Karp running-time bound are stated in [Har-Peled’s network-flow notes](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs473/fa2018/lec/lec/14_flow_II.pdf).

A nondeterministic verifier can guess one house index per household type, thereby specifying \(v_i\), and perform this flow test. The certificate has \(O(\tau\log(m+1))\) bits. Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

2. A support-preserving transfer from finite allocations

Consider a finite instance with \(n\) individually listed households and \(q\) individually listed houses, where \(q\geq n\).

Group households that have identical weights and utility functions. Let \(a_i\) be the number in household type \(i\). Group houses whose utility columns are identical across all households, and let \(b_h\) be the number in house type \(h\). These operations preserve every utility comparison.

Construct a cohort instance with

\( \displaystyle \alpha_i=\frac{a_i}{n},\qquad \beta_h=\frac{b_h}{n}. \)

Lemma 3. The finite instance admits a SumAvg-envy-free allocation if and only if this cohort instance admits a SumAvg-envy-free assignment.

Proof: finite to cohort. Let \(c_{ih}\) count the households of type \(i\) assigned houses of type \(h\). Set \(x_{ih}=c_{ih}/n\). The row sums and capacity bounds follow immediately.

For any two positive entries, choose finite households realizing those entries. Their envy condition is precisely the corresponding cohort condition. If both entries refer to the same type and house, the condition is an equality; if the houses differ, the selected finite households are distinct. Thus comparisons within a household type are also preserved.

Proof: cohort to finite. Let \(x\) be a feasible envy-free cohort assignment, and retain only its positive support

\( \displaystyle E=\{(i,h):x_{ih}>0\}. \)

The scaled matrix \(y_{ih}=nx_{ih}\) satisfies

\( \displaystyle \sum_h y_{ih}=a_i,\qquad \sum_i y_{ih}\leq b_h. \)

Form a flow network with source capacities \(a_i\), sink capacities \(b_h\), and middle arcs only in \(E\), each of capacity \(n\). The matrix \(y\) provides a real flow of value \(n\). Every capacity is an integer, and the source capacities sum to \(n\). The integral-flow theorem therefore supplies an integer flow of value \(n\), whose middle-arc entries \(z_{ih}\) satisfy

\( \displaystyle \sum_hz_{ih}=a_i,\qquad \sum_iz_{ih}\leq b_h,\qquad z_{ih}>0\Longrightarrow x_{ih}>0. \)

Assign exactly \(z_{ih}\) distinct households of type \(i\) to distinct houses of type \(h\). Integer row sums supply one house to each household, and integer column bounds ensure enough houses are available.

Every pair of resulting allocations belongs to the original positive support. Its envy condition therefore holds in \(x\), and hence also in the recovered finite allocation. \(\square\)

This proves that allowing real cohort masses does not remove the finite obstruction in these normalized instances. The argument also handles genuine type aggregation; it does not require keeping duplicate household profiles as separate support elements.

3. Restricted hardness, including dyadic masses

We use the finite hardness theorem supplied in the question. Specifically, [Liu and Xiao, Theorem 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05353v2#S5), gives \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness with two weights and three utility values. Their construction has equally many households and houses, weights in \(\{1,U\}\), utilities in \(\{0,1,U\}\), for a fixed integer \(U>1\), and every household values at least one house positively.

Lemma 3 already transfers hardness if arbitrary binary-encoded rational masses are allowed. The following padding makes the reduction valid even under the stricter dyadic interpretation of “binary-rational.”

Take one of these hard finite instances with \(N\) households and \(N\) houses. Choose the smallest power of two \(P\) strictly larger than \(N\), and put \(D=P-N\). Thus \(P\leq2N\).

Add \(D\) dummy households and \(D\) dummy houses:

  • Original households value every dummy house at zero.
  • Dummy households have weight \(1\), value every original house at zero, and value every dummy house at \(U\).

The weight and utility sets remain \(\{1,U\}\) and \(\{0,1,U\}\).

Padding preserves existence. An envy-free original allocation extends by assigning dummy households to dummy houses. Original households value the new recipients’ houses at zero, while dummy households receive their maximum utility, so all additional comparisons hold.

Conversely, any allocation of the padded instance uses every house: there are exactly \(P\) households and \(P\) houses. No household can receive utility zero in an envy-free allocation. Indeed, every household values some house positively, and that house is allocated to someone. Against its recipient, a household receiving zero would fail both envy inequalities, since all weights are positive.

Consequently, original households receive only original houses, and dummy households receive only dummy houses. Restricting the allocation to the original instance preserves envy-freeness. This proves the converse.

Now apply Lemma 3 to the padded instance. Every mass and capacity has the form

\( \displaystyle \alpha_i=\frac{a_i}{P},\qquad \beta_h=\frac{b_h}{P}, \)

so all are dyadic rationals, and total capacity equals one. The dummy households form one household type, and the dummy houses form one house type. Thus

\( \displaystyle \tau\leq N+1,\qquad m=\sigma\leq N+1. \)

Their masses and capacities need only \(O(\log N)\) bits. The construction, aggregation, and output encoding are polynomial in the finite input size.

We have therefore established a polynomial reduction preserving the simultaneous restrictions. Together with membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of both the restricted and general problems.

4. Exact running times

Enumerating household utility levels

Lemma 2 gives an algorithm enumerating all vectors \(v\). Their number is

\( \displaystyle \prod_{i=1}^{\tau}|V_i|\leq m^\tau. \)

Each vector requires \(O(\tau^2m)\) exact comparisons and one maximum-flow computation. Hence the running time is

\( \displaystyle m^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). \)

With at most three utility values globally, the enumeration has at most \(3^\tau\) vectors.

Enumerating minimum recipient weights

There is a complementary algorithm exponential in the number of house types.

Let \(W=\{w_i:1\leq i\leq\tau\}\). For each house type \(g\), guess either a value \(a_g\in W\) or an unused marker \(\bot\). For this vector, allow \((i,h)\) exactly when:

  • \(a_h\neq\bot\) and \(w_i\geq a_h\);
  • for every \(g\) with \(a_g\neq\bot\),

\( \displaystyle u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\quad\text{or}\quad u_i(h)a_g\geq u_i(g)w_i. \)

Test mass feasibility on these allowed pairs.

Completeness. Given an envy-free assignment, mark its unused house types by \(\bot\). For each used \(g\), choose

\( \displaystyle a_g=\min\{w_j:x_{jg}>0\}. \)

The minimum is attained because there are finitely many household types. Comparing any positive source cohort with a recipient attaining that minimum proves the stated edge condition. Thus the assignment passes this flow test.

Soundness. Suppose a flow test succeeds, and consider positive entries \(x_{ih}\) and \(x_{jg}\). The edge conditions give \(w_j\geq a_g\). If the ordinary utility inequality holds, the envy condition is satisfied. Otherwise,

\( \displaystyle u_i(h)a_g\geq u_i(g)w_i. \)

Because \(u_i(h)\geq0\) and \(w_j\geq a_g\),

\( \displaystyle u_i(h)w_j\geq u_i(h)a_g\geq u_i(g)w_i. \)

This is the required weighted inequality. A house guessed active but receiving no mass causes no problem: the test merely imposes additional sufficient conditions.

There are \((|W|+1)^m\leq(\tau+1)^m\) guesses. Each requires \(O(\tau m^2)\) comparisons and one flow computation. With at most two weights, there are at most \(3^m\) guesses.

Bit-complexity bounds

For an explicit common polynomial factor, one may take

\( \displaystyle B(m,\tau,L)=L^3+ L^2\left[\tau m(\tau+m)+(\tau+m+2)(\tau m+\tau+m)^2\right]. \)

Indeed, denominator clearing takes polynomial time bounded by \(O(L^3)\) using elementary arithmetic. Comparisons use \(O(L)\)-bit integers and cost \(O(L^2)\) each. The flow network has at most \(\tau+m+2\) vertices and \(\tau m+\tau+m\) arcs; Edmonds–Karp uses \(O(VE^2)\) arithmetic operations. The displayed factor bounds both verification procedures.

Choosing the smaller enumeration gives:

| Input class | Proven exact running time |
|---|---|
| General | \(O\!\left(B(m,\tau,L)\min\{m^\tau,(\tau+1)^m\}\right)\) |
| At most two weights and three utility values | \(O\!\left(B(m,\tau,L)3^{\min\{m,\tau\}}\right)\) |

Thus the restricted problem has an exact algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in \(\min\{m,\tau\}\), while remaining \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete when both parameters vary. A polynomial-time algorithm for either full input class would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
