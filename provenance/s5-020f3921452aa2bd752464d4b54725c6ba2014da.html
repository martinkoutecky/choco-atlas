The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite good classes \(G\), lexicographic types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and type valuations \(v_t\) over integral class-count bundles \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{|G|}\), determine whether a finite-support lottery over feasible mass allocations exists and, when it does, compute one. In each state \(\omega\), masses \(x^\omega_{t,B}\ge 0\) must satisfy \(\sum_B x^\omega_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x^\omega_{t,B}B_g=q_g\) for each \(g\in G\). Every state must be EFX across all occupied type-bundle pairs: if \(x^\omega_{t,B},x^\omega_{s,C}>0\), then \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(C-\mathbf e_g)\) for every class \(g\) with \(C_g>0\). Every state must also be Pareto optimal: there is no mass-preserving reassignment, represented by a coupling from assigned to new bundles, that weakly improves every unit of mass for its type and strictly improves a positive mass. Across the lottery, require \(\sum_\omega p_\omega\sum_B x^\omega_{t,B}v_t(B)/\mu_t\ge \frac{9}{10}\sum_\omega p_\omega\sum_C x^\omega_{s,C}v_t(C)/\mu_s\) for every pair \(t,s\). What is the existence and computational complexity of this problem in \(|G|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\), independently of the replica scale?

1. Polynomial-time construction for proportional valuations

Let
$$ G_+=\{g\in G:q_g>0\}, \qquad m_+=|G_+|. $$

Every feasible state assigns zero copies of every class outside $G_+$ on every positive-mass entry. The same holds for every supply-preserving competing coupling: nonnegative destination counts for a zero-supply class must have integral zero. Thus zero-supply classes may be omitted throughout this section.

Theorem. Suppose the valuations restricted to $G_+$ are proportional: there are positive numbers $c_t$ and positive rational weights $a_g$ such that
$$ a_{t,g}=c_ta_g \qquad(t=1,\ldots,\tau,\ g\in G_+). $$
Then a single admissible state can be computed in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. It achieves exact envy-freeness and uses at most $m_++1$ common bundles, hence at most $\tau(m_++1)$ positive type–bundle entries.

In particular, the theorem covers every one-type instance and every instance with only one positive-supply class.

If $G_+$ is empty, assigning the empty bundle to every type proves the theorem. Hence assume $G_+$ is nonempty.

1.1. Every feasible state is Pareto optimal in this subclass

Multiply the base weights by a common denominator and absorb the scaling into the constants $c_t$. We may therefore use positive integer weights $b_g$ and write, on bundles supported in $G_+$,
$$ v_t(B)=c_t\bar v(B), \qquad \bar v(B)=\sum_{g\in G_+}b_gB_g. $$

A weak improvement for any type is a weak improvement in $\bar v$; a strict improvement is a strict improvement in $\bar v$. Supply preservation fixes aggregate base value:
$$ \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}\bar v(B) = \sum_{g\in G_+}b_gq_g. $$

The corresponding destination integral has the same value. Finite first moments ensure both integrals are finite. A nonnegative value increase that is positive on a positive mass has positive integral, so a Pareto-improving coupling would contradict this equality.

Thus every feasible state is Pareto optimal under the question’s coupling definition.

1.2. The finite greedy EFX lemma

Consider finitely many identical recipients with valuation $\bar v$. Process all copies in nonincreasing order of their weights, assigning each copy to a recipient of minimum current value.

This produces an EFX allocation.

Indeed, fix a nonempty final bundle $C$, and let $h$ be its last assigned copy. Immediately before that assignment, the recipient’s value was
$$ \bar v(C)-b_h, $$
and was minimum among all current values. Since subsequent values only increase, every final bundle $B$ satisfies
$$ \bar v(B)\geq \bar v(C)-b_h. $$
The processing order makes $b_h$ a minimum copy weight in $C$. Consequently, for every class $g$ occurring in $C$,
$$ \bar v(B) \geq \bar v(C)-b_h \geq \bar v(C)-b_g. $$
These are precisely the EFX inequalities.

The remaining task is to simulate this allocation without enumerating recipients or copies.

1.3. Batched greedy assignment of one class

Maintain a finite distribution
$$ (B_1,w_1),\ldots,(B_k,w_k), \qquad w_i>0,\qquad \sum_iw_i=1. $$
Initially the distribution consists only of the empty bundle with mass $1$.

Process classes in nonincreasing order of $b_g$. Fix the current class $g$, write $b=b_g$, and let its supply be $q=q_g>0$. Define current integer loads
$$ \ell_i=\bar v(B_i). $$

A recipient initially in group $i$ would receive successive copies of this class at pre-assignment loads
$$ \ell_i,\ \ell_i+b,\ \ell_i+2b,\ \ldots. $$

For an integer $z$, define the total mass of assignment events at loads at most $z$:
$$ F(z)= \sum_{i=1}^k w_i \max\left\{ 0,\ \left\lfloor\frac{z-\ell_i}{b}\right\rfloor+1 \right\}. $$

Let $z$ be the smallest integer for which $F(z)\geq q$. This integer exists. For example,
$$ U=\max_i\ell_i+b\lceil q\rceil $$
satisfies $F(U)\geq q$: every recipient has at least $\lceil q\rceil$ events at loads at most $U$.

The number of events strictly below $z$ for a recipient in group $i$ is
$$ n_i= \max\left\{ 0,\ \left\lceil\frac{z-\ell_i}{b}\right\rceil \right\}. $$
Because loads, weights, and $z$ are integral,
$$ \sum_iw_in_i=F(z-1). $$

Set
$$ \delta=q-\sum_iw_in_i. $$
Minimality of $z$ gives
$$ 0<\delta\leq \sum_{i:\,\ell_i+n_ib=z}w_i. $$

Thus, after all events below $z$, precisely a mass $\delta$ of events at load $z$ remains to be selected. Choose numbers $u_i$ satisfying
$$ 0\leq u_i\leq w_i, \qquad u_i=0\ \text{unless }\ell_i+n_ib=z, \qquad \sum_i u_i=\delta. $$
These numbers can be chosen by filling eligible groups in any fixed order. At most one group is partially filled.

Replace group $i$ by
$$ (B_i+n_i\mathbf e_g,\ w_i-u_i) $$
and
$$ (B_i+(n_i+1)\mathbf e_g,\ u_i), $$
omitting zero masses.

This update adds exactly the required supply:
$$ \sum_i\bigl((w_i-u_i)n_i+u_i(n_i+1)\bigr) = \sum_iw_in_i+\delta = q. $$
It preserves total population mass and all previously assigned classes.

Every group produces one positive output group, except possibly the single partially filled group, which produces two. Therefore each processed class increases support size by at most one. The final distribution has at most $m_++1$ bundles.

1.4. Why the batched procedure is a genuine greedy simulation

Let $D$ be a common denominator of all supplies. Every maintained mass is an integer multiple of $1/D$.

This holds initially. Inductively, $n_i$ is integral, so $\delta$ is an integer multiple of $1/D$; the sequential filling rule makes every $u_i$ an integer multiple of $1/D$ as well.

For verification only, interpret the distribution as $D$ identical recipients, with $Dw_i$ recipients in group $i$. The current class has $Dq$ copies.

The update selects every assignment event below $z$, followed by exactly $D\delta$ events at $z$. These events can be executed in nondecreasing order of their pre-assignment loads. Each selected event’s predecessors have strictly smaller loads and have already been executed. At each step, the selected event therefore belongs to a recipient of minimum current value.

Thus the batched update realizes the finite greedy rule. Across all classes it realizes processing copies in nonincreasing weight order. The lemma in Section 1.2 consequently proves EFX for the final distribution.

The algorithm never constructs these $D$ recipients.

1.5. The resulting state and exact envy-freeness

Write the final distribution as $(B_i,w_i)_{i=1}^k$, and assign it identically within every type:
$$ x_{t,B_i}=\mu_tw_i. $$

The type-mass equalities hold because $\sum_iw_i=1$. Supplies hold because
$$ \sum_{t,i}\mu_tw_iB_{i,g} = \sum_iw_iB_{i,g} = q_g. $$

Positive scaling preserves the base EFX inequalities, so the state is EFX for every type. It is Pareto optimal by Section 1.1.

For any types $t,s$,
$$ \frac{\sum_i x_{t,B_i}v_t(B_i)}{\mu_t} = \sum_iw_iv_t(B_i) = \frac{\sum_i x_{s,B_i}v_t(B_i)}{\mu_s}. $$
Thus the state is exactly envy-free. Since values are nonnegative, exact envy-freeness implies the required $\frac9{10}$ inequalities.

A lottery consisting of this state with probability $1$ is admissible.

1.6. Polynomial bit complexity

Take the integer base weights by clearing denominators of one input type’s weights on $G_+$. The resulting integers $b_g$ have polynomial bit length.

Suppose the maximum load before a phase is $M$. The threshold satisfies
$$ z\leq M+b_g\lceil q_g\rceil. $$
Every updated load is at most $\max\{M,z+b_g\}$. Inductively, every load throughout the algorithm is bounded by
$$ S=\sum_{g\in G_+}b_g(\lceil q_g\rceil+1). $$
This integer has polynomial bit length. Since every $b_g\geq1$, every bundle coordinate is at most $S$.

The threshold is found by integer binary search between $\min_i\ell_i$ and $U$. The number of search steps is polynomial in the input length. Each evaluation of $F$ uses at most $m_++1$ terms.

All masses retain common denominator $D$, whose bit length is at most the sum of the input supply-denominator lengths. Multiplication by $\mu_t$ also preserves polynomial encoding length.

There are at most $m_+$ phases. Every arithmetic operation therefore involves polynomial-length integers or rationals, and the entire computation is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, independently of $D$’s numerical magnitude.

Proportionality itself can be checked by rational cross-products against one reference type and one positive-supply class.

2. Arbitrary valuations when total supply is at most one

This section reproves the corresponding result from the notes, including the zero-supply clarification.

Theorem. If
$$ Q=\sum_gq_g\leq1, $$
a single admissible state achieving exact envy-freeness can be computed in polynomial time.

For $Q=0$, assign the empty bundle to every type. Otherwise, run the following eating process for time $Q$: each type consumes its favorite available class at rate $\mu_t$. “Available” means having positive remaining supply.

Aggregate consumption has rate $\sum_t\mu_t=1$, so all supplies are exhausted exactly at time $Q$. Write $h_t(u)$ for type $t$’s chosen class at time $u$, ignoring event times of measure zero, and define
$$ x_{t,\mathbf e_g} = \mu_t\,\operatorname{length} \{u\in[0,Q]:h_t(u)=g\}, \qquad x_{t,\mathbf0}=\mu_t(1-Q). $$

These masses satisfy all feasibility equalities.

2.1. Exact envy-freeness and EFX

At almost every time $u$, both $h_t(u)$ and $h_s(u)$ are available. Type $t$ chooses its favorite available class, so
$$ a_{t,h_t(u)}\geq a_{t,h_s(u)}. $$
Integration gives
$$ \frac{\sum_Bx_{t,B}v_t(B)}{\mu_t} = \int_0^Qa_{t,h_t(u)}\,du \geq \int_0^Qa_{t,h_s(u)}\,du = \frac{\sum_Cx_{s,C}v_t(C)}{\mu_s}. $$

Every supported bundle is empty or a singleton. Removing a copy from a nonempty supported bundle leaves the empty bundle. Nonnegative valuations therefore prove EFX.

2.2. Pareto optimality against countable couplings

The initially nonempty mass is exactly $Q$. Under a weakly improving coupling, every initially nonempty unit must remain nonempty, because its initial value is positive.

Supply preservation fixes total destination cardinality at $Q$. Consequently, every initially nonempty unit must receive exactly one copy, and every initially empty unit must remain empty. Otherwise, the integral of destination cardinality would exceed $Q$.

For each positive-supply class $g$, let $\theta_g$ be its exhaustion time. If type $t$ consumes $g$ for positive time and prefers another positive-supply class $h$, then
$$ \theta_h<\theta_g. $$
Indeed, $h$ must already have been exhausted when the positive-duration consumption of $g$ begins, whereas $g$ finishes exhausting strictly later.

Zero-supply classes cannot occur in positive-mass destinations. Since singleton values are strictly ordered, every weakly improving singleton transition either retains its class or moves to a preferred positive-supply class. Every strict transition therefore strictly decreases the exhaustion-time potential.

But supply preservation fixes that potential at
$$ \sum_{g\in G_+}q_g\theta_g. $$
A weakly improving coupling with strict improvement on positive mass would strictly decrease this fixed quantity, a contradiction.

2.3. Running time

There are at most $m$ phases, each ending with at least one class exhausting.

Let $D$ clear the denominators of the masses and supplies. A nonzero consumption rate is $R/D$, where $R$ is an integer satisfying $1\leq R\leq D$. Each phase introduces at most multiplication of the common denominator by one such $R$. Hence, after at most $m$ phases, denominator bit length is polynomial in $m,L$.

The construction outputs at most $\tau(m+1)$ entries and runs in polynomial time.

3. General bounds and support preservation

We now return to arbitrary instances.

3.1. A polynomial-bit bound on EFX bundles

Define
$$ V_t=\sum_ga_{t,g}q_g, \qquad A_t=\max_ga_{t,g}. $$

Let a nonempty bundle $B$ occur for type $t$ in an EFX state, and choose $g$ with $B_g>0$. For every bundle $C$ assigned to that same type, the within-type EFX comparison gives
$$ v_t(C)\geq v_t(B)-a_{t,g}. $$
Averaging over type $t$ yields
$$ v_t(B) \leq \frac{\sum_Cx_{t,C}v_t(C)}{\mu_t}+A_t \leq \frac{V_t}{\mu_t}+A_t. $$
The last inequality holds because type $t$’s aggregate holdings are coordinatewise bounded by total supplies.

Thus every coordinate of every supported bundle is at most
$$ H= \left\lceil \max_{t,g} \frac{V_t/\mu_t+A_t}{a_{t,g}} \right\rceil. $$

Every positive input rational is between $2^{-L}$ and $2^L$, and every supply is at most $2^L$. Therefore
$$ H\leq m2^{4L}+2^{2L}+1. $$
In particular, $H$ has polynomial bit length. This bounds encoding length, not numerical magnitude.

3.2. Support preservation of EFX and Pareto optimality

Lemma. Let $x$ and $z$ be feasible states for the same instance. Suppose every positive type–bundle entry of $z$ occurs positively in $x$. If $x$ is EFX and Pareto optimal, then so is $z$.

EFX follows because support reduction removes required comparisons.

For Pareto optimality, suppose a coupling $\Gamma$ improves $z$. Since the supports are finite, choose $\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$ \varepsilon z_{t,B}\leq x_{t,B} $$
for every entry. Apply $\varepsilon\Gamma$ to that portion of $x$ and leave all remaining mass unchanged.

The original and destination portions both use supplies $\varepsilon q$, so the resulting coupling preserves supplies. It weakly improves every transported unit and strictly improves positive mass. Its first moments remain finite. This contradicts Pareto optimality of $x$.

4. A sharper polynomial-size lottery theorem

Theorem. Whenever an admissible lottery exists, one exists with at most
$$ W=(\tau-1)m+1 $$
positive-probability states, each having at most
$$ K=\tau+m $$
positive type–bundle entries. All bundle coordinates, state masses, and probabilities have polynomial encoding length.

The improvement over the previous $\tau(m+1)$ bound comes from decomposing each state before reducing the lottery’s number of states.

4.1. Decompose each state into sparse feasible states

Fix one state of an existing admissible lottery and restrict variables to its finite type–bundle support. Impose only its type-mass and supply equations:
$$ \sum_Bz_{t,B}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}B_g=q_g, \qquad z_{t,B}\geq0. $$

This is a nonempty bounded polytope: every variable lies between $0$ and $1$. Its equality matrix has at most $\tau+m$ rows.

Every point in this polytope is a finite convex combination of points whose positive-variable columns are linearly independent. Here is a direct justification. If the current positive columns are dependent, choose a nonzero null vector supported on them. The type-mass equations imply that this vector has both positive and negative coordinates. Moving in either direction until a coordinate reaches zero gives two feasible points with smaller supports, of which the current point is a convex combination. Repeating this argument terminates because support size strictly decreases.

Every terminal point has at most $\tau+m$ positive variables. Its support is contained in the original state’s support, so Section 3.2 proves that it remains EFX and Pareto optimal.

Replace the original lottery state by its finite convex decomposition. Doing this for every state preserves expected type-specific goods totals, hence preserves all fairness inequalities.

4.2. Polynomial encoding of the sparse states

Every supported bundle is bounded by $H$. At a terminal point, let $r\leq\tau+m$ be the number of positive variables. Their columns are independent, so select $r$ rows giving a nonsingular $r$-by-$r$ subsystem.

The matrix entries are integers bounded by $\max\{1,H\}$. Clear denominators of the right-hand side, consisting of input masses and supplies, using a common denominator of polynomial bit length.

For an integer matrix whose entries have absolute value at most $C$, the determinant satisfies
$$ |\det M|\leq r!C^r. $$
This follows by expanding the determinant into $r!$ signed products. Applying the same bound to the numerator determinants in Cramer’s rule shows that every state mass has polynomial numerator and denominator bit length.

Thus all sparse states obtained above have polynomial encoding length, even if their total number is large.

4.3. Reduce to at most $W$ states

For a state $j$, define
$$ r^j_{t,g}=\sum_Bx^j_{t,B}B_g. $$
These vectors satisfy
$$ \sum_t r^j_{t,g}=q_g \qquad(g\in G), $$
and therefore lie in an affine space of dimension at most $(\tau-1)m$.

Additivity makes every fairness expression depend only on the expected vector $r$. For example,
$$ \mathbb E\left[ \frac{\sum_Bx^\omega_{s,B}v_t(B)}{\mu_s} \right] = \frac1{\mu_s} \sum_ga_{t,g}\mathbb E[r^\omega_{s,g}]. $$

We use the finite-set affine-dimension form of convex Carathéodory: a convex combination of points lying in an affine space of dimension at most $d$ can be expressed using at most $d+1$ of those points.

For completeness, if more than $d+1$ points have positive coefficients, there is a nonzero affine dependence
$$ \sum_j\alpha_j=0, \qquad \sum_j\alpha_jr^j=0. $$
Subtract a positive multiple of $\alpha$ from the coefficient vector until one positive coefficient becomes zero, preserving nonnegativity, total probability, and the represented point. Repetition proves the assertion.

Applying it here retains at most $W$ sparse states and preserves expected $r$. Thus fairness remains valid.

4.4. Polynomial encoding of probabilities

The preceding reduction need not control probability bit lengths. Fix the retained states and consider the polytope of probabilities satisfying
$$ p_j\geq0, \qquad \sum_jp_j=1, $$
and, for every $t,s$,
$$ \sum_jp_j \left( \frac{\sum_Bx^j_{t,B}v_t(B)}{\mu_t} - \frac9{10} \frac{\sum_Cx^j_{s,C}v_t(C)}{\mu_s} \right) \geq0. $$

It is nonempty and bounded. It has polynomially many variables and constraints, and all coefficients have polynomial bit length by Section 4.2.

At a vertex, a full-rank set of active constraints determines the probabilities. Clearing denominators and applying the determinant bound from Section 4.2 gives polynomial probability bit lengths. Discard zero-probability states.

This proves the theorem. It does not supply an algorithm for discovering the required supports.

5. Pareto-optimality verification and the general complexity upper bound

5.1. Non-Pareto-optimality has polynomial-size certificates

Fix an explicitly represented feasible state. Index its positive entries by $i$, with origin type $t_i$, bundle $B_i$, and value
$$ u_i=v_{t_i}(B_i). $$

For any weakly improving integral destination $C$, delete copies while retaining value at least $u_i$, until a coordinatewise minimal destination $D\leq C$ is reached.

If $D_g>0$, minimality implies
$$ v_{t_i}(D)-a_{t_i,g}<u_i. $$
Also,
$$ a_{t_i,g}(D_g-1) \leq v_{t_i}(D)-a_{t_i,g}. $$
Hence
$$ D_g\leq \left\lceil\frac{u_i}{a_{t_i,g}}\right\rceil. $$
If $u_i=0$, the minimal destination is the empty bundle.

Consequently, every minimal destination lies in a finite box whose coordinate bounds have polynomial bit length.

Form a finite collection of labelled move generators:

  • For each origin $i$, include every move $D-B_i$ with $D$ in its bounded box and $v_{t_i}(D)\geq u_i$.
  • Fix one positive origin $i_0$, and include $\mathbf e_g$ as the move from $B_{i_0}$ to $B_{i_0}+\mathbf e_g$.

Mark a generator strict when its represented move strictly improves the originating type. Every coordinate-increment generator is strict.

5.2. Equivalence with a finite zero-sum combination

A countable Pareto-improving coupling gives a nonnegative combination of these generators summing to zero. Indeed, every transition decomposes as
$$ C-B_i = (D-B_i)+ \sum_g(C_g-D_g)\mathbf e_g. $$

There are finitely many bounded generators. Finite first moments ensure that all aggregated increment coefficients are finite. Supply preservation makes the aggregate resource-change vector zero.

If an original transition is strict, either $D$ is already strictly better than $B_i$, or $C-D$ contains a copy and therefore contributes a strict increment generator. Thus at least one strict generator has positive coefficient.

Conversely, suppose a finite nonnegative combination of represented weakly improving moves sums to zero and gives positive coefficient to a strict move. Scale all coefficients by a sufficiently small positive number so that the total mass moved from each origin fits its assigned positive mass. Execute those moves and leave the remaining mass unchanged.

The zero-sum condition preserves supplies. Every moved unit weakly improves, and positive mass strictly improves. Hence the state is not Pareto optimal.

5.3. At most $m+1$ moves suffice

Take such a zero-sum combination, choose a strict generator $d_j$ having positive coefficient, and divide all coefficients by that coefficient. Then
$$ -d_j $$
lies in the cone generated by the remaining generators.

For a finite collection of vectors in $\mathbb R^m$, every vector in its nonnegative cone has a representation using at most $m$ linearly independent generators. To see this, start with a representation having positive coefficients. If its used columns are dependent, subtract a suitable multiple of a dependence until one coefficient becomes zero, retaining nonnegativity and the represented vector. Repeat until the columns are independent.

Therefore the zero-sum improvement can use at most $m+1$ moves, including $d_j$.

The selected generator coordinates are integers of polynomial bit length. For the independent columns, select a nonsingular square subsystem of rows. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound give nonnegative rational coefficients of polynomial bit length.

A polynomial-time verifier checks:

  • that each move has a positive assigned origin;
  • weak improvement for every move;
  • strict improvement for a positive-coefficient move;
  • nonnegative coefficients and zero aggregate resource change.

These conditions suffice by Section 5.2. Thus non-Pareto-optimality is in $\mathrm{NP}$, and Pareto optimality is in $\mathrm{coNP}$.

5.4. Existence belongs to $\Sigma_2^p$

By Section 4, existence has a polynomial-size lottery witness. Feasibility, EFX, and all fairness inequalities can be checked directly in polynomial time.

Pareto optimality of every positive-probability state is a $\mathrm{coNP}$ condition by Sections 5.1–5.3. Equivalently, after guessing the lottery, universally exclude every polynomial-size improving-move certificate for its states.

Therefore
$$ \text{admissible-lottery existence}\in\Sigma_2^p. $$

This upper bound establishes neither polynomial-time decidability nor $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

6. Why ordinary integral Pareto optimality is insufficient

The supplied [discrete guarantee](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16209) concerns distinct goods and lexicographic bundle comparisons. Independently of the repeated-copy issue, ordinary integral Pareto optimality does not imply the coupling-based property required here.

Consider three classes $h,b,c$, two types of mass $\frac12$, and weights
$$ a_1=(8,4,3), \qquad a_2=(6,2,3). $$
Their orders are respectively $h,b,c$ and $h,c,b$, and the required inequalities hold:
$$ 8>4+3,\quad 4>3, \qquad 6>3+2,\quad 3>2. $$

Assign type $1$ the bundle $B=(0,2,1)$ and type $2$ the bundle $C=(1,0,0)$. Supplies are
$$ q=\left(\frac12,1,\frac12\right). $$

The state is EFX. Type $1$ compares against a singleton. For type $2$, removing $b$ or $c$ from $B$ leaves values $5$ or $4$, both at most its own value $6$.

The corresponding allocation to two integral recipients with total goods $(1,2,1)$ is Pareto optimal. If recipient $2$ retains $h$, recipient $1$ needs all of $2b+c$ to retain its value $11$. If recipient $2$ loses $h$, it needs all of $2b+c$ to reach value at least $6$, because every proper subbundle has value at most $5$; recipient $1$ then receives at most $h$, worth $8$.

Nevertheless, the mass state has the following improvement, each transition on mass $\frac14$:
$$ B\longmapsto(0,3,0), \qquad B\longmapsto(1,1,0), \qquad C\longmapsto(0,0,2). $$
Leave the remaining type-$2$ mass unchanged. The two type-$1$ destinations have value $12>11$, and the type-$2$ destination has value $6$. Resource changes sum to zero:
$$ (0,1,-1)+(1,-1,-1)+(-1,0,2)=(0,0,0). $$

Thus the state fails coupling-based Pareto optimality. This example is an obstruction to transferring integral Pareto arguments; it is not a nonexistence example.

The remaining instances have nonproportional valuations and total supply greater than one. For those instances, the arguments above establish neither universal existence nor a counterexample. They also do not establish hardness or a general polynomial-time construction.
