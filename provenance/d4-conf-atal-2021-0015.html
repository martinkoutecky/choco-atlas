The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite worker types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), additive type costs \(c_t(e)\) for chore categories \(e\in E\), per-capita chore supplies \(q_e\), and workload cap \(r\), decide whether there exists finite-support rational mass variables \(x_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{E}\) with \(\sum_e b_e\le r\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}b_e x_{t,b}=q_e\), and \(c_t(b)\le(3/2)\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,b\uplus b')\) for every positively supported pair \((t,b),(u,b')\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) worker types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) chore categories \(E\), nonnegative binary rational costs \(c_t(e)\) and per-capita supplies \(q_e\), and a binary-encoded integer workload cap \(r\ge0\). Costs are additive and need not be normalized.
Let \(B_r=\{b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{E}:\sum_e b_e\le r\}\). Each coordinate counts indivisible copies of its category; empty bundles are allowed.
For a multiset represented by \(s\), define \(\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,s)=\min_{a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{E},\,a\le s}\max\{c_t(a),c_t(s-a)\}\), with coordinatewise inequalities; these partitions have no workload restriction.
A feasible allocation consists of finitely supported nonnegative rational masses \(x_{t,b}\), indexed by \(t\in T\) and \(b\in B_r\), satisfying \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_ex_{t,b}=q_e\) for every \(e\in E\).
It must also satisfy \(c_t(b)\le\frac32\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,b+b')\) for every ordered pair with \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(x_{u,b'}>0\), including identical support entries; \(b+b'\) represents multiset union.

Is deciding whether such a feasible allocation exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(r\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

For finite lists of agents with nonnegative additive chore costs, round-robin computes a \(\frac32\)-PMMS allocation in polynomial time ([verified result](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10458-023-09618-5)); this result imposes no workload cap.
No result settling this exact mass feasibility problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total worker mass to one so that the stated supply equations use per-capita supplies.
  • Specified costs and supplies as nonnegative binary rationals; zero costs are allowed and costs are not normalized, matching the additive fairness setting.
  • Specified the workload cap as a nonnegative binary-encoded integer, with at least one worker type and one chore category.
  • Specified allocation masses as nonnegative rationals and allowed empty bundles.
  • Interpreted category multiplicities as indivisible copies and multiset union as coordinatewise addition; the MMS partitions may be empty and are not subject to the allocation workload cap.
  • Applied fairness to every ordered pair of positive support entries, including identical entries and pairs of the same worker type.
  • Defined the total input bit length and retained the workload cap as an explicit running-time parameter.

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable. More precisely, a feasible allocation exists if and only if

\( \displaystyle \sum_{e\in E}q_e\le r. \)

The key observation is that round-robin bounds each worker’s number of chores by the ceiling of the average workload. Thus the workload cap is compatible with the given fairness guarantee. The proof below establishes the needed round-robin properties directly, including for zero and non-normalized costs.

1. Necessity

Suppose a feasible allocation exists. Summing the supply equations gives

\( \displaystyle \sum_{e\in E}q_e =\sum_{t,b}\left(\sum_{e\in E}b_e\right)x_{t,b} \le r\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b} =r\sum_t\mu_t =r. \)

The inequality uses the workload restriction on every supported bundle. Consequently, total supply exceeding \(r\) makes feasibility impossible.

2. Two properties of finite round-robin

Consider finitely many workers with nonnegative additive costs and finitely many individually labeled chore copies. Fix a cyclic order of the workers. On each turn, the current worker takes a remaining chore of minimum cost according to their own costs. Stop when every chore has been allocated.

Write \(A_i\) for worker \(i\)’s bundle and \(h_i=|A_i|\).

Workload bound

If there are \(N\) workers and \(H\) chores, every complete round gives each worker one chore, and the final incomplete round gives each worker at most one. Therefore,

\( \displaystyle h_i\le\left\lceil\frac{H}{N}\right\rceil. \)

Fairness bound

We first prove that, for any distinct workers \(i,j\) with \(A_i\ne\varnothing\), some chore \(g\in A_i\) satisfies

\( \displaystyle c_i(A_i\setminus\{g\})\le c_i(A_j). \)

Let \(i_k\) and \(j_k\) denote their respective chores picked in round \(k\). Choose \(g=i_{h_i}\), worker \(i\)’s last pick.

If \(i\) precedes \(j\) in the cyclic order, then \(h_i-1\le h_j\). For every \(k\le h_i-1\), chore \(j_k\) was still available when \(i\) picked \(i_k\). The minimum-cost choice therefore gives \(c_i(i_k)\le c_i(j_k)\). Summing and using nonnegativity gives

\( \displaystyle c_i(A_i\setminus\{g\}) =\sum_{k=1}^{h_i-1}c_i(i_k) \le\sum_{k=1}^{h_i-1}c_i(j_k) \le c_i(A_j). \)

If \(i\) follows \(j\), then \(h_i\le h_j\). For every \(k\le h_i-1\), chore \(j_{k+1}\) was still available when \(i\) picked \(i_k\). Hence

\( \displaystyle c_i(A_i\setminus\{g\}) \le\sum_{k=1}^{h_i-1}c_i(j_{k+1}) \le c_i(A_j). \)

This proves the asserted inequality in both cases, including when removing \(g\) leaves the empty bundle.

Now put \(X=c_i(A_i)\), \(Y=c_i(A_j)\), and \(M=\operatorname{MMS}_i(2,A_i\cup A_j)\). Every partition of the union has total cost \(X+Y\), so \(2M\ge X+Y\). Moreover, the partition part containing \(g\) has cost at least \(c_i(g)\), so \(M\ge c_i(g)\). The preceding inequality implies

\( \displaystyle X-Y\le c_i(g)\le M. \)

Combining these bounds yields

\( \displaystyle 2X=(X+Y)+(X-Y)\le 2M+M=3M. \)

Thus \(c_i(A_i)\le\frac32M\). If \(A_i=\varnothing\), the same conclusion follows immediately from nonnegative costs.

This proves the required round-robin guarantee without normalization or strict positivity assumptions. It agrees with the supplied paper’s Corollary 3.1. [Fairness criteria for allocating indivisible chores](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10458-023-09618-5)

3. Sufficiency by exact finite replication

Assume \(Q:=\sum_{e\in E}q_e\le r\).

Because all masses and supplies are rational, choose a positive integer \(N\) such that every \(N\mu_t\) and every \(Nq_e\) is integral. Twice the product of their denominators is one valid choice.

Construct a finite instance with:

  • exactly \(N\mu_t\) workers of each listed type \(t\);
  • exactly \(Nq_e\) individually labeled chore copies of each category \(e\);
  • cost \(c_t(e)\) for every category-\(e\) copy evaluated by a type-\(t\) worker.

There are exactly \(N\) workers and \(H=NQ\) chores. Run the round-robin procedure from Section 2. Each worker receives at most

\( \displaystyle \left\lceil\frac{H}{N}\right\rceil=\lceil Q\rceil\le r \)

chores, where the last inequality uses that \(r\) is an integer.

For each worker \(i\), let \(b(i)\) be the vector counting the categories in their bundle. Define

\( \displaystyle x_{t,b} =\frac{|\{i:\text{worker }i\text{ has type }t,\ b(i)=b\}|}{N}. \)

These are nonnegative rational masses with finite support, and every supported bundle belongs to \(B_r\). Counting workers and chore copies gives

\( \displaystyle \sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,b}b_ex_{t,b}=q_e. \)

It remains to check every required ordered support pair.

For distinct entries \((t,b)\ne(u,b')\) with positive mass, choose workers representing those entries. They are distinct workers, so Section 2 gives

\( \displaystyle c_t(b)\le\frac32\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,b+b'). \)

Here the MMS values for labeled copies and category-count vectors coincide: every partition of labeled copies determines a vector \(a\le b+b'\), and every such vector can be realized by selecting the specified number of copies of each category. Corresponding partitions have equal costs.

For an identical support entry, observe that

\( \displaystyle \operatorname{MMS}_t(2,2b)=c_t(b). \)

Indeed, the total cost is \(2c_t(b)\), giving the lower bound \(c_t(b)\), while the partition into two copies of \(b\) attains that bound. Hence the identical-entry constraint also holds.

The aggregated allocation therefore satisfies every requirement. This proves sufficiency, including when \(r=0\) or all costs vanish.

4. Decision algorithm and running time

The algorithm reads the input, computes \(Q=\sum_e q_e\) exactly, and accepts precisely when \(Q\le r\). It does not construct the replicated population.

For an explicit bit bound, write \(q_e=a_e/d_e\) with \(d_e>0\). Accumulate the sum without reducing fractions: adding \(a/d\) to \(A/D\) replaces the accumulator by numerator \(Ad+aD\) and denominator \(Dd\). All intermediate integers have \(O(L)\) bits: denominator lengths are bounded by the total input denominator length, and numerator lengths have the same bound up to the input numerator lengths and \(O(\log m)\).

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, the \(m\) updates and final comparison require

\( \displaystyle O(L+mL^2)\subseteq O(L^3) \)

bit operations. Thus the running time is polynomial in the total input bit length \(L\). Dependence on \(\tau\) and on the numerical value of \(r\) occurs only through reading their binary-encoded input; there is no enumeration of \(B_r\) and no factor polynomial in the numerical value of \(r\).

The potentially large replication is an existence proof, while the decision algorithm is the exact supply comparison.
