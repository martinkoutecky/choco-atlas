The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite compatibility graph \(H=(T,F)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational equal-sharing rewards \(r_{ts}\), find a stable mass matching \(x\) with welfare \(W(x)\geq \tfrac12 OPT\), in time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), \(|F|\), and input bit length \(L\). Here \(x_{ts}\geq 0\), \(\sum_{s:\{t,s\}\in F}x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\), and \(OPT\) maximizes \(W(x)=\sum_{\{t,s\}\in F}r_{ts}x_{ts}\) over feasible mass matchings. Stability means there are no compatible types \(t,s\) with positive-mass groups assigned partner types \(u,v\), respectively, such that \(r_{ts}>r_{tu}\) and \(r_{ts}>r_{sv}\), taking the unmatched payoff to be \(0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The masses satisfy \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\).
The compatibility graph \(H=(T,F)\) is finite, simple, undirected, and need not be bipartite; same-type matches are excluded. Put \(m=|F|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Each edge has a nonnegative binary rational reward \(r_{ts}=r_{st}\), received equally by both partners; there are no friendship utilities.
A mass matching assigns a divisible mass \(x_{ts}=x_{st}\geq0\) to each edge, with \(\sum_{s:\{t,s\}\in F}x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\). Its unmatched mass is \(x_{t\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_s x_{ts}\), and unmatched payoff is \(r_{t\bot}=0\).
Stability means that no edge \(\{t,s\}\in F\) admits partner states \(u,v\in T\cup\{\bot\}\) with \(x_{tu}>0\), \(x_{sv}>0\), \(r_{ts}>r_{tu}\), and \(r_{ts}>r_{sv}\). Thus either or both deviating groups may be unmatched; ties do not block.
Define \(W(x)=\sum_{\{t,s\}\in F}r_{ts}x_{ts}\) and \(OPT=\max\{W(y):y\text{ is a feasible mass matching}\}\); this welfare is half the sum of individual rewards.

Can a stable mass matching satisfying \(W(x)\geq\tfrac12 OPT\) always be found in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is achieving this guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete equal-sharing matching without friendship, best-blocking-pair dynamics from a maximum-weight matching compute a stable matching with at least half the optimal welfare in polynomial time ([Anshelevich–Bhardwaj–Hoefer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.5780)).
For bipartite stable allocations with quotas, edge capacities, and distinct globally ranked edges, random best-response dynamics reach stability in expected polynomial time ([Cseh–Skutella](https://real.mtak.hu/83554/1/MTDP1820.pdf)); this does not establish the stated welfare guarantee on general graphs with ties.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The reward domain was unspecified; rewards are nonnegative binary rationals, allowing zero rewards.
  • The graph convention was unspecified; use a simple undirected graph without loops, so same-type matching is excluded.
  • The graded stability definition omitted explicit unmatched partner states; include positive unmatched mass on either or both sides of a blocking deviation, with payoff zero.
  • The graded question omitted friendship parameters; specialize the anchor to all friendship parameters zero, matching its stated blocking inequalities.
  • Interpret each edge variable as matched pair mass and each reward as the common individual payoff; retain the graded welfare normalization, which is half total individual reward.
  • Define the requested running-time parameter m as the number of compatibility edges and L as total input bit length.

Result

The guarantee is polynomial-time solvable. Process edges in nonincreasing reward order, assigning as much mass as possible to each edge. This produces a stable mass matching on any graph, including graphs with ties.

Moreover, every stable mass matching satisfies \(W(x)\geq \tfrac12 OPT\).

1. Stability implies the welfare bound

Fix a feasible mass matching \(x\). For each type \(t\), define its minimum realized payoff by
\[ p_t=\min\{r_{tu}:u\in T\cup\{\bot\},\ x_{tu}>0\}. \]
This minimum exists: the set of partner states is finite, and their masses sum to \(\mu_t>0\). Since all rewards are nonnegative, \(p_t\geq0\).

For any edge \(\{t,s\}\), both endpoints have positive mass receiving strictly less than \(r_{ts}\) precisely when \(p_t<r_{ts}\) and \(p_s<r_{ts}\). Consequently, the stated definition of stability is equivalent to
\[ r_{ts}\leq\max\{p_t,p_s\}\qquad\text{for every }\{t,s\}\in F. \]

Now suppose \(x\) is stable, and let \(y\) be any feasible mass matching. Nonnegativity of the minimum payoffs gives
\[ r_{ts}\leq\max\{p_t,p_s\}\leq p_t+p_s. \]
Multiplying by \(y_{ts}\geq0\), summing, and using feasibility of \(y\), we obtain
\[ W(y)\leq\sum_{\{t,s\}\in F}(p_t+p_s)y_{ts} =\sum_{t\in T}p_t\sum_{s:\{t,s\}\in F}y_{ts} \leq\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t. \]

Every positive-mass partner state of type \(t\) gives payoff at least \(p_t\). Thus
\[ \mu_t p_t \leq\sum_{u\in T\cup\{\bot\}}r_{tu}x_{tu} =\sum_{s:\{t,s\}\in F}r_{ts}x_{ts}, \]
where the last equality uses \(r_{t\bot}=0\). Summing over types counts every edge twice, so
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t\leq2W(x). \]
Combining these inequalities proves \(W(y)\leq2W(x)\) for every feasible \(y\), and hence
\[ W(x)\geq\tfrac12 OPT. \]

The maximum defining \(OPT\) exists because the feasible set is nonempty, closed, and bounded in finite dimension, and welfare is continuous.

2. A greedy construction of a stable matching

Order the edges as \(e_1,\ldots,e_m\) so that \(r_{e_1}\geq\cdots\geq r_{e_m}\), breaking ties arbitrarily.

Initialize residual masses \(R_t=\mu_t\). For each edge \(e_i=\{t,s\}\), assign
\[ x_{ts}=\min\{R_t,R_s\}, \]
and subtract this amount from both residual masses. At termination, set \(x_{t\bot}=R_t\).

Feasibility follows from the invariant
\[ R_t=\mu_t-\sum_{\substack{e\ni t\\e\text{ already processed}}}x_e\geq0. \]

To prove stability, fix an edge \(e_i=\{t,s\}\). Immediately after it is processed, at least one endpoint, say \(t\), has residual mass zero. That endpoint receives no further allocations. All its positive matched mass therefore lies on edges processed at or before \(e_i\), whose rewards are at least \(r_{e_i}\). It has no unmatched mass because its residual mass is zero.

Thus type \(t\) has no positive-mass partner state with payoff strictly below \(r_{e_i}\). This prevents \(e_i\) from blocking. The argument applies to every edge, proving stability.

Ties cause no difficulty: equal rewards do not constitute strict improvements. The proof uses no bipartiteness assumption.

By Section 1, the constructed matching satisfies \(W(x)\geq\tfrac12 OPT\).

3. Polynomial bit complexity

Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), with positive integers \(a_t,b_t\), and define
\[ D=\prod_{t\in T}b_t,\qquad c_t=a_t(D/b_t). \]
The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Each \(c_t\) is an integer, and \(\sum_t c_t=D\).

Run the greedy algorithm on these integer capacities. Each edge requires one minimum operation and two subtractions. All residual capacities and allocated amounts lie between zero and \(D\), so they have \(O(L)\) bits. Divide the resulting allocations by \(D\) to obtain the required rational matching.

Rewards can be sorted exactly by comparing rational numbers through integer cross multiplication. There are \(O(m\log(m+1))\) comparisons, each involving integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Constructing the common denominator, running the allocation steps, and writing the output also take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

No expansion into individual agents is needed, even when \(D\) is exponentially large in value.

4. The factor is sharp

Take a four-vertex path \(a-b-c-d\), with every type having mass \(\tfrac14\). Give the outer edges reward \(1\) and the middle edge reward \(1+\varepsilon\), where \(0<\varepsilon<1\) is rational.

In a stable matching, the middle edge requires at least one of \(b,c\) to have minimum realized payoff at least \(1+\varepsilon\). Its only partner state with that payoff is the middle edge. Therefore \(x_{bc}=\tfrac14\), saturating both middle vertices. Every stable matching consequently has welfare \((1+\varepsilon)/4\).

For any feasible matching, putting \(z=x_{bc}\) gives \(x_{ab},x_{cd}\leq\tfrac14-z\), and hence
\[ W(x)\leq(1+\varepsilon)z+2(\tfrac14-z) =\tfrac12-(1-\varepsilon)z\leq\tfrac12. \]
Allocating \(\tfrac14\) to each outer edge attains \(\tfrac12\), so \(OPT=\tfrac12\). The stable-to-optimal welfare ratio is \((1+\varepsilon)/2\), approaching \(\tfrac12\). Thus no uniformly larger factor is possible.

\(r_{ts}\leq\max\{p_t,p_s\}\).
This equivalence correctly includes unmatched states and excludes improvements consisting only of ties.

\(r_{ts}\leq p_t+p_s\)
follows from the stability characterization and nonnegativity of the minimum payoffs.

Multiplication by \(y_{ts}\geq0\) preserves this inequality. The subsequent identity counts each edge once at each endpoint, and feasibility gives
\[ \sum_t p_t\sum_s y_{ts}\leq\sum_t\mu_t p_t. \]
This use of feasibility is valid because \(p_t\geq0\).
