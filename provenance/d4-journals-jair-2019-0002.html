The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m+1\) candidates, scoring vector \(\alpha\), target \(p\), rational nonmanipulator distribution \(\nu\) of mass \(1-\rho\) over complete rankings, and rational manipulator mass \(\rho\) of one repeated true type with admissible \(p\)-first reports, choose a distribution \(q\) over those reports so that \(\mu^q=\nu+\rho q\) minimizes \(T_\infty(q)=\max_{c\ne p}\operatorname{sc}_{\mu^q}(c)\), equivalently the score margin, or decide whether \(p\) is a co-winner.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C=\{p,c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), with \(m\ge1\), and use positional scoring with an input vector \(\alpha_0\ge\cdots\ge\alpha_m\ge0\) of integers satisfying \(\alpha_0\le m^{O(1)}\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The input consists of a rational nonmanipulator measure \(\nu\) of mass \(1-\rho\), where \(0<\rho\le1\), and manipulator mass \(\rho\) sharing one strict complete true ranking with \(p\) first. Every strict complete ranking with \(p\) first is an admissible report, with no further restriction from the true ranking.
Choose a rational probability distribution \(q\) over admissible reports, represented by its positive support, and set \(\mu^q=\nu+\rho q\). Scores are mass-weighted positional scores.
Define \(T_\infty(q)=\max_{c\ne p}\operatorname{sc}_{\mu^q}(c)\) and \(T^*=\min_qT_\infty(q)\). The score of \(p\) is fixed at \(\operatorname{sc}_\nu(p)+\rho\alpha_0\); equality with a competitor counts as winning.
For a fixed constant \(d\ge2\), put \(\beta=\min\{m-1,\lceil d\sqrt{m\ln m}\rceil\}\) and \(g(\alpha)=\max_{1\le j\le m-\beta}(\alpha_j-\alpha_{j+\beta})\).

Can a randomized algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) return \(q\) satisfying \(T_\infty(q)\le T^*+\rho g(\alpha)\) with exponentially small failure probability, or is achieving this additive guarantee NP-hard?

For indivisible unweighted manipulators, a randomized polynomial-time algorithm achieves additive error \(kg(\alpha)\), with exponentially small failure probability ([Keller–Hassidim–Hazon, 2019](https://www.ariel.ac.il/wp/noam-hazon/wp-content/uploads/sites/317/2021/02/19.jair_.new_approx_borda.pdf)).
For divisible votes, co-winner feasibility and minimum total manipulator weight are polynomial-time solvable; these results address different objectives from the fixed-mass competitor-score approximation above ([Xia–Conitzer–Procaccia, 2010](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The unspecified admissible reports are all strict complete p-first rankings; the repeated true ranking also places p first and imposes no additional reporting constraint.
  • The scoring vector uses nonnegative integer coefficients with polynomially bounded maximum, retaining the domain required by the discrete algorithm's dynamic programming.
  • The total society mass is one, with positive manipulator mass and possibly zero nonmanipulator mass.
  • The discrete additive error is normalized by total society mass, replacing manipulator count k by manipulator mass rho.
  • The index displacement in g(alpha) is rounded upward and capped at m-1 so that it is defined for small candidate sets.
  • The chosen report distribution has rational masses and is represented by its positive support; L includes all input encodings.
  • Ties use the co-winner convention.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is exactly solvable in deterministic polynomial time. An algorithm can return a rational distribution \(q^*\), supported on at most \(m^2\) admissible reports, such that
\(T_\infty(q^*)=T^*\).

Consequently, the requested additive guarantee holds with failure probability zero.

1. Candidate–position marginals

Write \(b_i=\operatorname{sc}_\nu(c_i)\) for each \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\). These rational numbers are computable from the given support in polynomial time.

For a distribution \(q\) over admissible reports, define
\(x_{ij}=\Pr_{R\sim q}[c_i\text{ occupies position }j\text{ among the competitors}]\),
where \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\); that position awards score \(\alpha_j\).

Every report places each competitor in exactly one position and each position contains exactly one competitor. Therefore its marginal matrix \(X=(x_{ij})\) satisfies

\(x_{ij}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^m x_{ij}=1\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^m x_{ij}=1\quad\text{for every }j.\)

Such a matrix is called doubly stochastic. Linearity of mass-weighted scores gives

\(\operatorname{sc}_{\mu^q}(c_i)=b_i+\rho\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jx_{ij}.\)

Thus the objective depends only on these marginals.

2. A polynomial-size linear program

Consider the following rational linear program, with variables \(t\) and \(x_{ij}\):

\(\displaystyle \text{minimize }t\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle b_i+\rho\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jx_{ij}\le t\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^m x_{ij}=1\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^m x_{ij}=1\qquad(j=1,\ldots,m),\)

\(\displaystyle x_{ij}\ge0\qquad(i,j=1,\ldots,m),\qquad 0\le t\le\alpha_0.\)

The bound on \(t\) loses no relevant solution: the total society mass is one, and every positional score lies between zero and \(\alpha_0\), so every candidate’s total score lies in that interval.

The program is feasible. Indeed, take \(x_{ij}=1/m\) and \(t=\alpha_0\). For every competitor,

\(\displaystyle b_i+\frac{\rho}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_j \le(1-\rho)\alpha_0+\rho\alpha_0=\alpha_0.\)

The feasible region is closed and bounded, because \(0\le x_{ij}\le1\) and \(0\le t\le\alpha_0\).

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: a linear program with binary-encoded rational data can be solved deterministically in time polynomial in its encoding length, returning an exact rational optimum of polynomial bit length whenever an optimum exists. This is the classical polynomial-time solvability of rational linear programming. [Khachiyan, 1979](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319)

Here there are \(m^2+1\) variables and polynomially many constraints. All coefficients have polynomial bit length:

  • Each \(b_i\) is a sum of at most \(\tau\) input masses multiplied by input scores. Using the product of the input denominators as a common denominator gives a polynomial bit-length bound.
  • The remaining coefficients are \(\rho\), the scores, their products, and constants.
  • The hypothesis \(\alpha_0\le m^{O(1)}\) ensures polynomial score encoding length.

Therefore an exact rational optimal solution \((X^*,t_{\mathrm{LP}})\) is computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

It remains to construct the required distribution over actual reports.

3. Constructive rational decomposition

We prove that every rational doubly stochastic matrix \(X\) can be decomposed, in polynomial time, into at most \(m^2\) permutation matrices with positive rational coefficients.

Initialize a residual matrix \(R=X\). Its row sums and column sums all equal \(r=1\). While \(r>0\), form a bipartite graph whose left vertices are competitors, whose right vertices are positions, and whose edges are precisely the pairs \((i,j)\) with \(R_{ij}>0\).

For any set \(S\) of left vertices, let \(N(S)\) denote its neighborhood. Since each row sums to \(r\),

\(\displaystyle |S|r=\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in N(S)}R_{ij}.\)

Nonnegativity and the column sums imply

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in N(S)}R_{ij} \le\sum_{j\in N(S)}\sum_{i=1}^mR_{ij}=|N(S)|r.\)

Dividing by \(r>0\) gives \(|S|\le|N(S)|\). Hall’s marriage theorem therefore guarantees a perfect matching, which can be found by a polynomial-time bipartite matching algorithm.

Let \(P\) be its permutation matrix and set

\(\displaystyle \lambda=\min\{R_{ij}:P_{ij}=1\}>0.\)

Record the pair \((\lambda,P)\) and replace \(R\) by \(R-\lambda P\). This preserves nonnegativity, and every row sum and column sum becomes \(r-\lambda\).

At least one positive entry becomes zero in every iteration; no zero entry becomes positive. Hence there are at most \(m^2\) iterations. The process can continue whenever \(r>0\), by the matching argument above, so it terminates with \(r=0\). Nonnegativity then forces \(R=0\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle X=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\lambda_\ell P_\ell,\qquad \lambda_\ell>0,\qquad \sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\lambda_\ell=1,\qquad s\le m^2.\)

Each \(P_\ell\) specifies an admissible report: put \(p\) first and assign each competitor to its matched position. Assign probability \(\lambda_\ell\) to that report. The resulting distribution has marginal matrix exactly \(X\).

Bit complexity. Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the entries of \(X\). Its bit length is at most the sum of their denominator bit lengths. Throughout the algorithm, every residual entry and every \(\lambda_\ell\) is an integer multiple of \(1/D\) lying between zero and one. Thus their numerators and denominators have polynomial bit length. Together with the polynomial number of matching computations, this proves polynomial running time and polynomial output length.

4. Equality with the target optimum

For every rational admissible distribution \(q\), its marginal matrix makes the linear program feasible with \(t=T_\infty(q)\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{LP}}\le T_\infty(q).\)

Conversely, apply the decomposition above to the rational optimal matrix \(X^*\). It returns an admissible rational distribution \(q^*\) whose competitor scores satisfy

\(\displaystyle T_\infty(q^*) =\max_{1\le i\le m}\left(b_i+\rho\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jx^*_{ij}\right) \le t_{\mathrm{LP}}.\)

Applying the first inequality to \(q^*\) proves equality. Since the first inequality holds for every admissible \(q\), the distribution \(q^*\) attains the minimum, and

\(\displaystyle T_\infty(q^*)=t_{\mathrm{LP}}=T^*.\)

Finally, \(0\le\beta\le m-1\), so the maximum defining \(g(\alpha)\) is nonempty. Monotonicity of the scoring vector gives \(\alpha_j-\alpha_{j+\beta}\ge0\), hence \(g(\alpha)\ge0\). Thus

\(\displaystyle T_\infty(q^*)=T^*\le T^*+\rho g(\alpha).\)

This also covers \(m=1\), when \(\beta=0\) and \(g(\alpha)=0\). The deterministic algorithm is a randomized algorithm that ignores its random bits, with failure probability zero. The decisive hypotheses are divisibility and the admissibility of every report placing \(p\) first: they allow every rational doubly stochastic marginal matrix to be realized.
