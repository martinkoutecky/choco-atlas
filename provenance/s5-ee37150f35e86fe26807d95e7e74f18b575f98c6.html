The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of sellers, a target seller \(i\), fixed strategies for the other sellers, and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete buyer types encoding ratings, eligibility, and effort costs, compute a mass allocation of bribery actions that maximizes \(i\)'s payoff under the paper's budget and shared-buyer constraints. Is the analogue of Proposition 1's greedy rule—bribing the lowest-rated eligible mass first, allowing a partial mass at the cutoff—always a best response?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\geq1\) sellers and a designated seller \(i\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type records its historical seller \(h_t\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}\), where \(0\) means no previous purchase, its historical rating \(r_t\in[0,1]\), and its eligibility for bribery by each seller. Historical buyers may be bribed only by their historical seller; previously unserved buyers may become new raters. All numerical input is rational.
The fixed, jointly feasible strategies of sellers other than \(i\) reserve mass \(q_{tj}\geq0\) of each previously unserved type, with \(\sum_{j\ne i}q_{tj}\leq\mu_t\). Reserved mass cannot also be bribed by \(i\). Define \(c_t=\mu_t-\sum_{j\ne i}q_{tj}\) for \(h_t=0\).
Let \(H=\sum_{h_t=i}\mu_t>0\), \(S=\sum_{h_t=i}\mu_t r_t\), \(P_0=\sum_{h_t=0}\mu_t\), and \(P=\sum_{h_t=0}c_t\). The purchase profit \(k>0\) and budget cap \(B>0\) are input parameters.
For each eligible historical type with \(h_t=i\), choose total rating effort \(0\leq e_t\leq\mu_t(1-r_t)\). This costs \(e_t\) and increases its rating integral by \(e_t\), without adding raters or consuming potential buyers.
For each eligible previously unserved type, choose recruited mass \(0\leq x_t\leq c_t\) and rating integral \(0\leq y_t\leq x_t\), with \(y_t>0\) whenever \(x_t>0\). This costs \(y_t\), adds rater mass \(x_t\), and removes that mass from the potential market. Ineligible types receive no action; mass may be split and rating effort may be partial.
Write \(E=\sum_t e_t\), \(X=\sum_t x_t\), and \(Y=\sum_t y_t\). Feasibility requires \(E+Y\leq B\); unused budget is allowed. The payoff is \(\pi=k(P-X)(S+E+Y)/(H+X)-kP_0S/H-E-Y\).

Is the continuous best-response problem—deciding, for a rational threshold \(T\), whether some feasible allocation has \(\pi\geq T\)—in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, where \(L\) is the total input bit length?

For discrete buyers, Zhou et al. claim that updating historical ratings in ascending order and then recruiting non-raters dominates alternatives with the same budget; the stated greedy procedure runs in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.10014v4)
Known society-continuum bribery results establish tractability and hardness for election attacks, rather than this seller-payoff problem; no result settling this precise continuous problem was located. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.25444)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the society to total mass one, with budget and utility measured in these population units.
  • Used disjoint historical buyer populations, encoded by a unique historical seller, so subtracting their masses from the total market gives a nonnegative potential-buyer mass.
  • Retained historical-rating updates as well as recruitment, following the greedy procedure despite the introductory restriction to buyers who have not interacted with the seller.
  • Interpreted eligibility as a supplied permission for an otherwise permitted action; historical buyers can update only their own seller's rating, and recruitment draws from previously unserved buyers.
  • Fixed effort costs to the stated unit cost per unit of rating increase, without introducing heterogeneous cost coefficients.
  • Represented fixed competing recruitment by rational reservations within each type; reservations are disjoint and unavailable to the target seller.
  • Required positive historical rater mass, ratings in the unit interval, positive purchase profit and budget, and the linear utility specialization.
  • Allowed unused budget because the requested object is a payoff-maximizing best response under a budget cap, whereas the discrete dominance claim compares strategies at the same budget.
  • Assigned new raters no pre-existing target rating and required positive payment for positive recruited mass; partial ratings and partial recruitment remain permitted.
  • Used the original pre-bribery utility as the payoff baseline and updated both the rating denominator and remaining potential market after recruitment.
  • Formulated exact optimization through a rational payoff-threshold decision problem, allowing real mass allocations without assuming an optimal allocation has rational coordinates.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact polynomial-time solvability

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). The proof gives an exact algorithm using rational arithmetic, with running time polynomial in \(\,m,\tau,L\,\). It accounts for eligibility, reservations, partial effort, unused budget, and the strict positivity requirement for recruited types.

1. Aggregate capacities

Let \(\mathcal H\) be the eligible types whose historical seller is \(\,i\,\), and let \(\mathcal N\) be the previously unserved types eligible for bribery by \(\,i\,\). Define

\[ A=\sum_{t\in\mathcal H}\mu_t(1-r_t), \qquad C=\sum_{t\in\mathcal N}c_t, \qquad K=\frac{kP_0S}{H}. \]

Here \(\,A\,\) is the total available historical rating effort, \(\,C\,\) is the total recruitable mass after reservations, and \(\,K\,\) is the fixed baseline term. In particular,

\[ 0\leq A\leq H-S,\qquad 0\leq C\leq P. \]

Every total historical effort \(\,E\in[0,A]\,\) is realizable: enumerate the eligible historical types and fill their effort capacities until the prescribed total is reached, using partial effort on the last type if necessary.

Likewise, every recruited mass \(\,x\in[0,C]\,\) is realizable with rating integral equal to recruited mass. Fill the capacities \(\,c_t\,\) and set \(\,y_t=x_t\,\) for every recruited type. This satisfies \(\,y_t>0\,\) whenever \(\,x_t>0\,\), and respects all reservations.

2. A dominance lemma at fixed expenditure

Lemma. Every feasible allocation with total expenditure \(\,z=E+Y\,\) is weakly dominated by a feasible allocation with

\[ E'=\min\{z,A\}, \qquad X'=Y'=\max\{0,z-A\}. \]

Proof. The original allocation satisfies

\[ E\leq A,\qquad Y\leq X\leq C. \]

Consequently,

\[ z=E+Y\leq A+C, \qquad X\geq Y=z-E\geq z-A. \]

Together with \(\,X\geq0\,\), this gives \(\,X\geq X'\,\).

The proposed replacement has the same expenditure because \(\,E'+Y'=z\,\). It is realizable by the capacity-filling constructions above: \(\,E'\leq A\,\), and \(\,X'=Y'\leq C\,\) follows from \(\,z\leq A+C\,\). Its budget is unchanged, and its recruited ratings satisfy the strict positivity requirement.

At fixed expenditure \(\,z\,\), the payoff is

\[ k(S+z)\frac{P-X}{H+X}-K-z. \]

For \(\,0\leq x_1\leq x_2\,\),

\[ \frac{P-x_1}{H+x_1}-\frac{P-x_2}{H+x_2} = \frac{(P+H)(x_2-x_1)}{(H+x_1)(H+x_2)} \geq0. \]

Both denominators are positive because \(\,H>0\,\). Since \(\,k>0\,\) and \(\,S+z\geq0\,\), decreasing recruited mass from \(\,X\,\) to \(\,X'\,\) cannot decrease the payoff. This proves the lemma.

Thus, at any fixed expenditure, it suffices to spend on historical effort first and recruit only with the remaining expenditure, giving every recruited buyer rating \(\,1\,\). Historical types need not be sorted: their contributions depend only on total effort.

3. Reduction to one variable

Set

\[ D=\min\{B,A+C\}, \qquad x(z)=\max\{0,z-A\}. \]

Every feasible expenditure lies in \(\,[0,D]\,\), and the lemma’s canonical allocation is feasible for every \(\,z\in[0,D]\,\). Therefore the original threshold question is exactly equivalent to

\[ \text{Does some }z\in[0,D]\text{ satisfy } F(z)=k(P-x(z))\frac{S+z}{H+x(z)}-K-z\geq T? \]

This equivalence also resolves the potential issue caused by strict recruitment constraints. Although the original feasible set need not be closed, canonical allocations realize every point of the closed interval \(\,[0,D]\,\), and \(\,F\,\) is continuous there. Hence a best response is attained.

We now decide the threshold question separately on the two branches.

Historical-effort branch

Let \(\,M=\min\{A,B\}\,\). For \(\,z\in[0,M]\,\), no recruitment is needed, so

\[ F(z)=\frac{kPS}{H}-K+ \left(\frac{kP}{H}-1\right)z. \]

Define \(\,\alpha=kP/H-1\,\). The maximum on this interval occurs at \(\,z_h=M\,\) if \(\,\alpha>0\,\), and at \(\,z_h=0\,\) otherwise.

Thus this branch contains a successful allocation exactly when \(\,F(z_h)\geq T\,\). This is an exact rational comparison.

Recruitment branch

If \(\,B<A\,\), this branch is empty.

Suppose instead that \(\,B\geq A\,\). Write

\[ U=\min\{C,B-A\}, \qquad R=S+A. \]

The branch consists of \(\,z=A+x\,\), where \(\,x\in[0,U]\,\), with payoff

\[ G(x)=k(P-x)\frac{R+x}{H+x}-K-A-x. \]

Because \(\,H+x>0\,\), the threshold inequality \(\,G(x)\geq T\,\) is equivalent to

\[ Q_T(x):=k(P-x)(R+x)-(K+A+T+x)(H+x)\geq0. \]

Expanding gives

\[ Q_T(x)=-(k+1)x^2+\beta x+\gamma, \]

where

\[ \beta=k(P-R)-(K+A+T+H), \qquad \gamma=kPR-H(K+A+T). \]

Since \(\,k+1>0\,\), this quadratic is strictly concave. Its unrestricted maximizer is the rational number

\[ v=\frac{\beta}{2(k+1)}. \]

Indeed, completing the square gives

\[ Q_T(x)=Q_T(v)-(k+1)(x-v)^2. \]

Its maximum on \(\,[0,U]\,\) therefore occurs at

\[ w=\min\{U,\max\{0,v\}\}. \]

Consequently, the recruitment branch contains a successful allocation if and only if \(\,Q_T(w)\geq0\,\).

The algorithm maximizes the threshold polynomial \(\,Q_T\,\), rather than the payoff itself. This distinction allows exact threshold decisions using only rational arithmetic, including equality cases.

4. Algorithm and bit complexity

The complete decision algorithm is:

  • Compute \(\,H,S,P_0,P,A,C,K\,\).
  • Compute the historical-branch candidate \(\,z_h\,\). Accept if \(\,F(z_h)\geq T\,\).
  • If \(\,B<A\,\), reject.
  • Otherwise compute \(\,U,R,\beta,\gamma,w\,\). Accept if \(\,Q_T(w)\geq0\,\); otherwise reject.

Correctness follows from the dominance lemma and the two exact branch tests.

The aggregates require polynomially many rational additions and multiplications: at most \(\,O(m\tau)\,\) operations to process explicitly listed reservations, and \(\,O(\tau)\,\) further operations for the capacities and historical totals. After aggregation, the decision uses only a constant number of rational operations and comparisons.

All intermediate rationals have polynomial bit length. To see this for the aggregates, a common denominator can be obtained by multiplying the input denominators; its bit length is at most the sum of their bit lengths. Products such as \(\,\mu_t r_t\,\) likewise have polynomial bit length. The subsequent formulas have constant arithmetic depth, and division is only by explicitly positive rationals such as \(\,H\,\) and \(\,2(k+1)\,\). Standard exact integer arithmetic therefore implements the algorithm in \(\,\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\,\) time.

On acceptance, a rational feasible allocation can also be constructed. Use historical effort \(\,z_h\,\) for the first branch, or historical effort \(\,A\,\) and recruited mass \(\,w\,\) with \(\,y_t=x_t\,\) for the second branch. The capacity-filling construction produces polynomial-size rational coordinates.
