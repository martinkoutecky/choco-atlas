The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of contributor types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and work weights \(q_t\), take finite populations with \(n_t/N \to \mu_t\), one distinguished founder, and coalition value \(\nu(S)=0\) without the founder and \(\nu(S)=\rho(\sum_{i\in S}q_i)^2\) otherwise. What are the limits, as fractions of grand-coalition value, of the founder’s Shapley payoff and each type’s aggregate Shapley payoff?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) contributor types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Each type has a fixed binary-rational work weight \(q_t\geq0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t>0\). Let \(\rho>0\) be binary rational and \(L\) the total input bit length.
For each integer \(N\), take \(N\) contributors, partitioned into sets \(C_{t,N}\) of sizes \(n_t(N)\), with \(\sum_t n_t(N)=N\) and \(n_t(N)/N\to\mu_t\), together with one founder \(g\). The weights remain fixed as \(N\) grows; the founder contributes no work weight.
Write \(A_N=\{g\}\cup\bigcup_t C_{t,N}\). Define \(\nu_N(S)=0\) when \(g\notin S\), and \(\nu_N(S)=\rho\bigl(\sum_t q_t|S\cap C_{t,N}|\bigr)^2\) otherwise. Let \(\phi_i(\nu_N)\) denote the standard Shapley payoff.
The quantities to compute are \(F=\lim_{N\to\infty}\phi_g(\nu_N)/\nu_N(A_N)\) and \(P_t=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i\in C_{t,N}}\phi_i(\nu_N)/\nu_N(A_N)\) for every \(t\in T\).

Do these limits exist independently of the population sequence, and is their exact computation from the support in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete weighted quadratic games with one indispensable founder, closed-form asymptotic normalized Shapley payoffs are known; diffuse-workload asymptotics for the founder and individual contributors are also established. Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The limits exist for every admissible population sequence, and their exact computation is polynomial-time solvable. With \(m=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t>0\), they are

\[ F=\frac13,\qquad P_t=\frac{2\mu_tq_t}{3m}\quad(t\in T). \]

The argument below proves exact finite-population identities before taking limits. These identities agree with the weighted quadratic formulas in Section 3.1.2 of [Assif, Kennedy and Saniee](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/330050/1/games-14-00057.pdf).

1. Shapley payoffs for elementary coalition terms

For a finite player set \(A\) and a nonempty subset \(R\subseteq A\), define the unanimity game \(u_R\) by \(u_R(S)=1\) if \(R\subseteq S\), and \(u_R(S)=0\) otherwise.

The Shapley payoff is the expected marginal contribution when the players enter in a uniformly random permutation. A player outside \(R\) always has marginal contribution zero in \(u_R\). A player in \(R\) has marginal contribution one exactly when it enters last among the players in \(R\). Their relative order is uniform, so each player in \(R\) receives \(1/|R|\).

Shapley payoffs are linear in the coalition-value function, because marginal contributions and their expectations are linear. We will apply these facts only to sets \(R\) of sizes two and three.

2. Exact finite-population formulas

Fix \(N\). Write \(I_N=A_N\setminus\{g\}\), and assign weight \(w_i=q_t\) to each \(i\in C_{t,N}\). Define

\[ W_N=\sum_{i\in I_N}w_i=\sum_{t\in T}n_t(N)q_t, \qquad D_N=\sum_{i\in I_N}w_i^2=\sum_{t\in T}n_t(N)q_t^2. \]

Expanding the square in the definition of the game gives the following identity of coalition-value functions:

\[ \nu_N = \rho\sum_{i\in I_N}w_i^2u_{\{g,i\}} + 2\rho\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq I_N,\ i\ne j}w_iw_j u_{\{g,i,j\}}. \]

The second sum runs over unordered pairs. Indeed, if a coalition lacks \(g\), every term vanishes; if it contains \(g\), the terms are exactly the diagonal and cross terms of its squared total work weight.

The founder receives half of each two-player term and one third of each three-player term. Consequently,

\[ \phi_g(\nu_N) = \frac{\rho D_N}{2} + \frac{2\rho}{3}\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq I_N,\ i\ne j}w_iw_j = \rho\left(\frac{W_N^2}{3}+\frac{D_N}{6}\right), \]

where the last equality uses \(W_N^2=D_N+2\sum_{\{i,j\}}w_iw_j\).

A contributor \(i\) receives half of its diagonal term and one third of every cross term involving it. Therefore,

\[ \phi_i(\nu_N) = \frac{\rho w_i^2}{2} + \frac{2\rho}{3}w_i\sum_{j\in I_N\setminus\{i\}}w_j = \rho\left(\frac{2w_iW_N}{3}-\frac{w_i^2}{6}\right). \]

Since \(\nu_N(A_N)=\rho W_N^2\), whenever \(W_N>0\) the normalized formulas are

\[ \frac{\phi_g(\nu_N)}{\nu_N(A_N)} = \frac13+\frac{D_N}{6W_N^2}, \]

and, for every \(t\in T\),

\[ \frac{\sum_{i\in C_{t,N}}\phi_i(\nu_N)}{\nu_N(A_N)} = \frac{2n_t(N)q_t}{3W_N} - \frac{n_t(N)q_t^2}{6W_N^2}. \]

These identities also cover zero-weight contributors: their payoffs are exactly zero.

3. Existence and independence of the limits

By the assumed convergence of the population proportions and finiteness of \(T\),

\[ \frac{W_N}{N}\longrightarrow m=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t>0, \qquad \frac{D_N}{N}\longrightarrow s=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t^2<\infty. \]

In particular, \(W_N>0\) for all sufficiently large \(N\). The normalized expressions may be undefined for finitely many initial populations with no positive-weight contributors; this does not affect their limits.

Now

\[ \frac{D_N}{W_N^2} = \frac{D_N/N}{N(W_N/N)^2} \longrightarrow0, \]

because its numerator converges to a finite number and \(W_N/N\) converges to the positive number \(m\). Thus \(F=1/3\).

For each fixed type \(t\),

\[ \frac{n_t(N)q_t}{W_N} = \frac{(n_t(N)/N)q_t}{W_N/N} \longrightarrow\frac{\mu_tq_t}{m}, \]

whereas

\[ \frac{n_t(N)q_t^2}{W_N^2} = \frac{(n_t(N)/N)q_t^2}{N(W_N/N)^2} \longrightarrow0. \]

Substitution into the exact type formula proves \(P_t=2\mu_tq_t/(3m)\). These expressions depend only on the given support data, so the limits are independent of the population sequence.

As a consistency check, \(F+\sum_{t\in T}P_t=1/3+2m/(3m)=1\).

4. Exact computation and bit complexity

It remains to establish polynomial bit complexity, rather than merely a polynomial number of rational operations.

Write the input rationals as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\) and \(q_t=c_t/d_t\), using binary-encoded integers with positive denominators. Put \(A_t=a_tc_t\), \(B_t=b_td_t\), and compute

\[ B=\prod_{t\in T}B_t,\qquad H_t=A_t(B/B_t),\qquad H=\sum_{t\in T}H_t. \]

All divisions \(B/B_t\) are exact integer divisions. Moreover, \(m=H/B\), so \(H>0\), and the required outputs are simply

\[ F=\frac13,\qquad P_t=\frac{2H_t}{3H}. \]

The bit length of \(B\) is bounded by the sum of the input denominator bit lengths. Each \(H_t\) is a product of input integers whose total bit length is \(O(L)\). Summing \(\tau\) such integers gives \(H\) bit length \(O(L+\log\tau)\). Thus all intermediate integers and all output numerators and denominators have polynomially bounded bit lengths.

The algorithm uses \(O(\tau)\) integer multiplications, exact divisions, and additions on those integers. Standard integer arithmetic performs these operations in polynomial time; reducing the output fractions by greatest common divisors also takes polynomial time.

Hence the support-encoded exact limit problem belongs to the class of polynomial-time computable functions. No population enumeration is needed.
