The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set \(\Theta\) of complete valuation types with population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta_{\mathbb{Q}}(\Theta)\), an ordered stream of indivisible item classes with per-capita supplies, and normalized DAB buffer capacity \(\beta\), construct a compact deterministic policy that, for every sufficiently large integral replication \(q\), allocates the \(q\mu_\theta\) agents and integral item copies online, maintains individual EF at every nonterminal update, ends EF1, and supplies an integral lift of the type-level assignment.

There are \(m\) item classes. Type \(\theta\) has additive values \(v_{\theta j}\in\mathbb{Q}\), possibly positive, negative, or zero, with at most \(k\) distinct values. The complete valuation table is known initially. An ordered stream of class blocks, each with positive binary-rational per-capita supply \(s_t\), is revealed block by block; future blocks are unavailable. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Deferred allocation of batches (DAB) permits simultaneous irrevocable allocation of any subset of the buffered copies and the current arrival; the capacity bound must hold before the next arrival. After an explicit end signal, all remaining copies must be allocated in one terminal batch.

A compact policy at buffer rate $k$, and a nearly matching lower bound

The general polynomial-time versus $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness question remains open. I prove a new sufficient condition that addresses the compactness issue:

$$ \boxed{\beta\geq \sum_\theta\mu_\theta d_\theta,} $$

where $d_\theta$ is the number of distinct values in type $\theta$’s valuation row. Since $d_\theta\leq k$, this gives a uniform compact policy whenever $\beta\geq k$.

I also prove that, even with one type and positive valuations, the necessary buffer rate can approach $k$ arbitrarily closely. Thus $k$ is the sharp worst-case sufficient rate, although deciding feasibility for a particular table below this rate remains unresolved.

1. Definitions and the main sufficient condition

Assume $m\geq1$; with no classes there are no arrivals.

For an admissible replication $q$, let

$$ n_\theta=q\mu_\theta. $$

Every $n_\theta$ is a positive integer, and $\sum_\theta n_\theta=q$. Identify an agent by its type $\theta$ and rank $r\in\{1,\ldots,n_\theta\}$. Its bundle is represented by an integer vector of class counts.

An allocation $A$ is individually EF if

$$ v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_\ell) $$

for every ordered pair of agents $i,\ell$. It is mixed-manna EF1 if each strict envy comparison can be repaired by removing either an item valued positively by $i$ from $A_\ell$, or an item valued negatively by $i$ from $A_i$.

Define

$$ d_\theta=\left|\{v_{\theta j}:1\leq j\leq m\}\right|, \qquad D=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta d_\theta, \qquad d_{\max}=\max_\theta d_\theta. $$

Theorem 1. If $\beta\geq D$, there is a uniform deterministic policy satisfying every requirement of the task. It works for every admissible $q\geq2$, with an internal buffer capacity

$$ b_q=\sum_\theta n_\theta d_\theta-d_{\max}=qD-d_{\max}. $$

Its records and computations are polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L,\log q$. Each revealed block produces at most one nonuniform nonterminal batch record.

In particular, $\beta\geq k$ suffices.

The proof occupies Sections 2–4. It includes both the integral allocation and its compressed representation.

We first record the fairness composition facts used throughout.

Lemma 2. The componentwise union of two EF allocations on disjoint item sets is EF. The componentwise union of an EF allocation and an EF1 allocation on disjoint item sets is EF1.

Proof. For an ordered pair $i,\ell$, utility differences add:

$$ v_i(A_i\cup B_i)-v_i(A_\ell\cup B_\ell) = \bigl(v_i(A_i)-v_i(A_\ell)\bigr) + \bigl(v_i(B_i)-v_i(B_\ell)\bigr). $$

If both allocations are EF, both summands are nonnegative.

If $A$ is EF and a removal repairs the comparison in $B$, the same removal remains available in the union, with the same sign and location. Adding the nonnegative difference contributed by $A$ preserves the repaired inequality. If the comparison in $B$ needs no removal, neither does the comparison in the union. $\square$

2. A weighted matching lemma with binary multiplicities

For a nonempty multiset $R$ of available copies, an agent’s *top copies* are the copies attaining its largest value in $R$. Form the bipartite graph joining each agent to its top copies.

A matching covering all agents gives an EF increment: each agent receives one of its top copies, and every other agent receives a copy from the same multiset.

The following lemma supplies such a matching after temporarily hiding some copies.

Lemma 3. Consider $n$ agents. Agent $i$ assigns at most $d_i$ distinct values to the classes. Put

$$ d_*=\max_i d_i, \qquad B=\sum_{i=1}^n d_i-d_*. $$

Every multiset $S$ containing at least $B+1$ copies has a nonempty submultiset $R$ whose top-choice graph admits a matching covering all agents.

When agents are represented by types and copies by class multiplicities, $R$ and the matching can be computed in polynomial time in the binary input length.

2.1 Existence

Start with $R=S$. If the top-choice graph admits a matching covering all agents, stop.

Otherwise, Hall’s theorem gives a nonempty set $X$ of agents whose neighborhood $H$ satisfies

$$ |H|<|X|. $$

Temporarily hide every copy in $H$ and repeat.

For every agent in $X$, all its current top copies have been hidden. If copies remain, its new top value is strictly smaller. Charge this deletion once to each agent in $X$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that the process exhausts $S$ after $r$ deletions. Let $t_i$ be the number of charges to agent $i$. The values charged to an agent strictly decrease, so

$$ t_i\leq d_i. $$

Also $t_i\leq r$. Each deletion hides at most one fewer copy than the number of charges it makes. Since the hidden sets are disjoint,

$$ |S|\leq\sum_i t_i-r. $$

Choose an agent $i_*$ with $d_{i_*}=d_*$. Then

$$ \sum_i(d_i-t_i)+r \geq d_*-t_{i_*}+r \geq d_*. $$

Consequently,

$$ |S| \leq \sum_i d_i-\left(\sum_i(d_i-t_i)+r\right) \leq \sum_i d_i-d_* =B, $$

contradicting $|S|\geq B+1$.

Thus the process finds a matching before exhausting the copies.

2.2 Computation without listing agents or copies

Suppose the current class multiplicities are $C_j$. For each type, compute its top available classes. Use the flow network with:

  • an arc from the source to type $\theta$ of capacity $n_\theta$;
  • an arc from type $\theta$ to each of its top classes of capacity $n+1$;
  • an arc from class $j$ to the sink of capacity $C_j$.

All capacities are nonnegative integers. The network has polynomially many vertices and arcs, with binary-encoded capacities.

A flow of value $n$ yields integers $f_{\theta j}$ satisfying

$$ \sum_j f_{\theta j}=n_\theta, \qquad \sum_\theta f_{\theta j}\leq C_j. $$

Assigning the indicated copies to the agents of each type gives the required matching.

If the maximum flow is less than $n$, take a minimum cut. No type-to-class arc can cross this cut because its capacity is $n+1$. Let $X$ be the types on the source side, and let $H$ be the union of their top classes. The cut inequality implies

$$ \sum_{j\in H}C_j<\sum_{\theta\in X}n_\theta. $$

Thus all agents of the types in $X$, together with all copies of the classes in $H$, give the required Hall witness. Hide those classes.

Every deletion hides at least one whole available class. Therefore there are at most $m$ deletions. Integer maximum flow and minimum cut are computable in polynomial time in this network’s binary encoding. Fixing all algorithmic choices and arc orders makes the procedure deterministic. $\square$

For the replicated society, Lemma 3 applies with

$$ \sum_i d_i=\sum_\theta n_\theta d_\theta, \qquad d_*=d_{\max}. $$

The matching can be recorded exactly: for each type, order the classes and assign its $f_{\theta j}$ copies to consecutive rank intervals. Each agent receives one copy, and the record uses at most $m$ intervals per type.

3. The online policy and its compact records

Set

$$ b=b_q=qD-d_{\max}. $$

This is an integer. Removing one agent with $d_i=d_{\max}$ from the sum defining $b$ shows that

$$ b\geq q-1. $$

If $\beta\geq D$, then

$$ b\leq qD\leq\lfloor q\beta\rfloor, $$

because $qD=\sum_\theta n_\theta d_\theta$ is integral.

Maintain residual class counters $C_j$, initially zero. Upon an arrival of class $j$:

  • If its residual count reaches $q$, allocate those $q$ copies simultaneously, one to every agent.
  • Otherwise, if the live residual count reaches $b+1$, apply Lemma 3 to the live copies and allocate the resulting one-copy-per-agent matching simultaneously.
  • Otherwise, retain the arrival.

Hidden copies in the matching procedure remain buffered.

3.1 Capacity and nonterminal fairness

Before an arrival, the buffer contains at most $b$ copies. The current arrival therefore raises the live count to at most $b+1$.

Either allocation rule removes exactly $q$ copies. After such an update, the residual count is at most

$$ b+1-q\leq b. $$

If no allocation occurs, the residual count is at most $b$. Hence the internal capacity is respected before the next arrival.

A class-only batch gives every agent the same class-count increment, so it is EF regardless of signs.

For a matching batch, let $R$ be the final multiset obtained by Lemma 3. Agent $i$ receives a top copy $g_i$ in $R$. Every recipient’s copy belongs to $R$, so

$$ v_i(g_i)\geq v_i(g_\ell) $$

for all agents $\ell$. Thus this increment is EF. The hidden copies do not enter this comparison because they remain unallocated.

Starting from empty bundles, Lemma 2 proves that every nonterminal allocation is individually EF.

3.2 At most one nonuniform batch per block

Consider a block of class $j$. Let $F$ be the number of buffered copies of all other classes.

Suppose a matching batch occurs in this block. It removes $q$ copies from a live pool of size $b+1$. Immediately afterward,

$$ F\leq b+1-q. $$

During the rest of this block, only class $j$ arrives. Whenever its residual count reaches $q$, the policy uses a class-only batch. Between such batches, its residual count is at most $q-1$. Therefore the total buffered count is at most

$$ F+(q-1)\leq b. $$

A second matching batch cannot occur in this block.

This argument is the central compression step: a block may contain arbitrarily many class-only batches, but it contains at most one nonuniform batch.

3.3 Polynomial computation even for a long block

After each arrival is handled, every class residual satisfies

$$ C_j<q. $$

Indeed, a class-only batch fires when the incoming class first reaches $q$, resetting its count to zero. A matching batch only decreases residual counts.

At the beginning of a block of class $j$, let $T=\sum_h C_h$. The distances to the next class-only trigger and the next overflow trigger are respectively

$$ a=q-C_j, \qquad c=b+1-T. $$

Both are positive integers.

If the block has fewer than $\min\{a,c\}$ remaining arrivals, they can all be accounted for by one counter update. Otherwise:

  • If $a\leq c$, the first event is a class-only batch. At that event, $F+q\leq b+1$, hence $F\leq b+1-q$. All subsequent batches in this block are class-only.
  • If $c<a$, the first event is a matching batch. Section 3.2 again shows that all subsequent batches are class-only.

Subsequent class-only batches occur every $q$ arrivals and can be represented using quotient and remainder arithmetic.

Thus a block’s schedule and records can be computed in polynomial time without enumerating its arrivals or its batches. At an actual individual arrival or allocation update, the corresponding counters and records are updated in polynomial time.

3.4 Exact individual bundles and type totals

Store:

  • common counters $u_j$, recording the number of class-$j$ copies given to every agent by class-only batches;
  • for each matching batch, its consecutive rank intervals and their assigned classes.

There is at most one matching record per block. If there are $T$ blocks, these records use $O(T\tau m)$ intervals. Since the blocks are explicitly encoded, $T$ is bounded by the input length.

For agent $(\theta,r)$, its nonterminal class-$j$ count is

$$ u_j+ \sum_e \mathbf{1}\{r\text{ lies in the class-}j\text{ interval for type }\theta \text{ in record }e\}. $$

This is an exact integer bundle query.

If record $e$ assigns $f^e_{\theta j}$ copies of class $j$ to type $\theta$, its exact nonterminal total is

$$ n_\theta u_j+\sum_e f^e_{\theta j}. $$

Dividing by $q$ gives the required $x^q_{\theta j}$. All counters have bit length polynomial in $L+\log q$, and the records are polynomially sized.

4. A compact terminal EF1 allocation

The terminal step needs a binary-multiplicity allocation algorithm. I give its construction and fairness proof explicitly.

Lemma 4. Given binary-encoded type multiplicities $n_\theta$ and class multiplicities $C_j$, a mixed-manna EF1 allocation can be computed with polynomial time and space in the binary input length. Its records support exact individual bundle queries and type totals.

4.1 The two-phase rule

Order agents by type and then by rank, breaking item-value ties by class index.

Partition the real classes into:

  • classes valued nonpositively by every type;
  • the remaining classes, each valued positively by at least one type.

In the first phase, pad the universally nonpositive copies with zero-valued dummy copies until their number is a multiple of $q$. Allocate them by greedy round-robin in the chosen agent order.

In the second phase, use the reverse agent order. On each turn, an agent chooses its favorite positively valued remaining real copy, if one exists; otherwise it takes a conceptual zero-valued dummy. Complete the final round with conceptual dummies. Stop when all real copies have been allocated.

Finally delete all dummies.

4.2 EF1 proof

Let $i$ precede $\ell$ in the first-phase order. Write their first-phase bundles as $C_i,C_\ell$, including dummies, and let the number of rounds be $R$.

Within each round, $\ell$’s eventual pick was available when $i$ chose. Therefore

$$ v_i(C_i)\geq v_i(C_\ell). $$

For the reverse comparison, $\ell$’s pick in round $r$ precedes $i$’s pick in round $r+1$. Greediness gives

$$ v_\ell(c_{\ell,r})\geq v_\ell(c_{i,r+1}) \qquad (1\leq r<R). $$

All first-phase picks have nonpositive value to every agent. Consequently, when $R>0$,

$$ v_\ell(C_\ell\setminus\{c_{\ell,R}\}) \geq \sum_{r=2}^R v_\ell(c_{i,r}) \geq v_\ell(C_i). $$

If the removed pick has value zero, the original comparison already holds. Otherwise it is a real, negatively valued item in $\ell$’s own bundle. An empty first phase contributes zero.

Now let $G_i,G_\ell$ be the second-phase bundles, including conceptual dummies, with $S$ rounds. The reverse order puts $\ell$ before $i$. Greedy choice, with a zero-valued dummy always available, gives

$$ v_\ell(G_\ell)\geq v_\ell(G_i). $$

For the other direction, $i$’s pick in round $r$ precedes $\ell$’s pick in round $r+1$. Thus

$$ v_i(g_{i,r})\geq v_i(g_{\ell,r+1}) \qquad (1\leq r<S). $$

Every own second-phase pick has nonnegative value. For $S>0$, summing therefore gives

$$ v_i(G_i) \geq \sum_{r=2}^S v_i(g_{\ell,r}) = v_i(G_\ell)-v_i(g_{\ell,1}). $$

If $g_{\ell,1}$ is positive to $i$, removing it repairs the comparison. If it is nonpositive to $i$, the original comparison already holds. Again, an empty phase contributes zero.

Combining phases:

  • $i$’s first-phase comparison is envy-free, and its second-phase comparison needs at most one positive-item removal from $\ell$’s bundle;
  • $\ell$’s second-phase comparison is envy-free, and its first-phase comparison needs at most one negative-item removal from its own bundle.

Hence the union is mixed-manna EF1. Removing dummies changes no utility, and every required removal has a strict sign, so its witness is a real item.

4.3 Compression

Each round visits each type as a contiguous group of identical-preference agents, possibly in descending rank order.

At the start of a round, compute each type’s selected class. In the second phase this can be the conceptual dummy. For each finite class $j$, let $D_j$ be the total number of agents currently selecting it.

While finite copies remain, some finite class has positive demand. In the second phase this follows because some present type values a remaining real class positively.

Compute

$$ h=\min_{j:D_j>0}\left\lfloor\frac{C_j}{D_j}\right\rfloor. $$

If $h>0$, execute $h$ complete rounds at once. Every agent of a type receives $h$ copies of that type’s selected finite class; conceptual dummy increments are ignored.

This is an exact greedy execution. Each selected class has enough stock for all its designated picks. Inductively, no designated favorite disappears before its designated pick, and a favorite that remains available continues to be selected.

If $h=0$, simulate one full round using rank intervals. A segment ends when a selected finite class is exhausted or a type group ends. Thus one simulated round has at most $m+\tau+1$ segments, allowing for the finite dummy class in the first phase.

Every simulated round exhausts a finite class. Otherwise no favorite would change during the round, contradicting $C_j<D_j$ for a class attaining $h=0$.

Every positive fast-forward either exhausts a finite class or leaves a demanded class with stock below its next-round demand. If no class was exhausted, favorites are unchanged and the next step is an exhaustion round.

Therefore there are only $O(m)$ fast-forward stages and explicitly simulated rounds. Record fast-forwards as common increments for each type, and simulated segments as rank intervals. These records give exact bundles and type totals in polynomial time. $\square$

4.4 Completion of Theorem 1

After the end signal, apply Lemma 4 to all buffered copies and allocate its output in one terminal batch.

The preceding allocation is EF by Section 3. Lemma 2 therefore makes the final allocation EF1. Append the terminal records to the nonterminal records.

If $T_{\theta j}$ is the terminal class-$j$ total for type $\theta$, then

$$ x^q_{\theta j} = \frac{n_\theta u_j+\sum_e f^e_{\theta j}+T_{\theta j}}{q}. $$

The individual records lift this expression exactly.

Every operation uses revealed information only. The policy is a fixed algorithm whose description is independent of $q$, and all records, queries, and computations have the required polynomial bounds. This proves Theorem 1.

Moreover, since $D\leq d_{\max}\leq k$,

$$ b_q=qD-d_{\max} \leq(q-1)d_{\max} \leq(q-1)k. $$

Thus this construction supplies compact integral records within the finite-agent buffer guarantee stated in the question’s [Theorem 4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01159v1#Thmtheorem4.2).

5. Necessary bounds and worst-case sharpness

5.1 Homogeneous-prefix obstruction

For class $j$, let

$$ p_j=\sum_{\theta:v_{\theta j}>0}\mu_\theta, $$

and define

$$ \gamma_j= \begin{cases} p_j,&p_j>0,\\ 0,&p_j=0\text{ and some }v_{\theta j}=0,\\ 1,&v_{\theta j}<0\text{ for every }\theta. \end{cases} $$

Proposition 5. Every feasible policy satisfies

$$ \beta\geq\max_j\gamma_j. $$

Proof. Consider a stream prefix consisting only of class $j$. If an agent values the class positively, its EF inequalities require its copy count to be at least every other agent’s count. Hence all positively valuing agents have the same maximal count. Any nonempty EF allocation must give at least one copy to each of these $qp_j$ agents.

If all agents value the class negatively, EF requires all copy counts to be equal. Any nonempty EF allocation then contains at least $q$ copies.

When $\beta$ is strictly below the corresponding rate, sufficiently large admissible $q$ satisfy

$$ \lfloor q\beta\rfloor+1<qp_j $$

or, in the all-negative case, the same inequality with $q$ on the right. Choose a fixed homogeneous block with supply exceeding $\beta+1$. At arrival $\lfloor q\beta\rfloor+1$, no nonempty EF allocation is possible, the capacity is exceeded, and another arrival remains. This is impossible. $\square$

5.2 A stronger obstruction for identical positive valuations

Theorem 6. Suppose there is one type of mass $1$, and the classes have strictly increasing positive values

$$ 0<a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_m. $$

Every feasible policy satisfies

$$ \boxed{ \beta\geq m-\sum_{i=2}^m\frac{a_{i-1}}{a_i}. } $$

This conclusion does not require any computational or compactness restriction.

Proof. Put

$$ t_1=1, \qquad t_i=1-\frac{a_{i-1}}{a_i} \quad (2\leq i\leq m). $$

All $t_i$ are positive, and telescoping gives

$$ \sum_{j=1}^i a_jt_j=a_i \qquad (1\leq i\leq m). $$

Suppose $\beta<\sum_i t_i$. Choose fixed positive dyadic supplies $s_i<t_i$, sufficiently close to $t_i$ that

$$ \sum_i s_i>\beta. $$

Such choices exist by density of dyadic rationals. They satisfy

$$ \sum_{j=1}^i a_js_j<a_i \qquad (1\leq i\leq m). $$

Present these $m$ class blocks in increasing value order, followed by one more positive-supply block.

I claim that no nonempty subset of the copies in the first $m$ blocks admits an EF allocation. Because all valuations are identical, an EF allocation must give every agent a common utility $u$. If it is nonempty, strict positivity gives $u\geq a_1$.

Let $i$ be the largest index with $a_i\leq u$. No bundle of utility $u$ can contain a copy of a class with index greater than $i$: that copy alone would have value greater than $u$, and all other values are positive.

The total assigned utility is therefore at most the available utility in the first $i$ classes, which is strictly less than

$$ qa_i\leq qu. $$

But equal utility $u$ for all $q$ agents requires total assigned utility exactly $qu$. This contradiction proves the claim.

Every earlier prefix is a subset of these copies, so no nonempty nonterminal EF update can occur during the first $m$ blocks. All their copies must remain buffered. Their number is

$$ q\sum_i s_i>q\beta\geq\lfloor q\beta\rfloor. $$

The following block ensures that this is a nonterminal capacity obstruction. $\square$

5.3 The rate $k$ is sharp in the worst case

Take one type and $m=k=r$ classes with values

$$ 1,h,h^2,\ldots,h^{r-1}, $$

where $h\geq2$ is an integer. Theorem 6 requires

$$ \beta\geq r-\frac{r-1}{h}. $$

Theorem 1 supplies a compact policy at $\beta=r$.

For every fixed $r\geq2$ and every $0<\varepsilon<r$, choosing

$$ h>\frac{r-1}{\varepsilon} $$

makes the necessary rate strictly larger than $r-\varepsilon$. Thus any universally sufficient rate strictly below $k$ fails for some rational valuation table, even with one type and only goods.

This establishes sharpness as a worst-case supremum. It does not identify the exact threshold of every table in this family.

6. Remaining open question

The new construction proves feasibility throughout $\beta\geq D$, including $\beta\geq k$, with uniform compact integral lifts. The lower bounds exclude additional inputs and show that this sufficient rate is nearly necessary in the worst case.

They do not classify every particular valuation table with $\beta<D$. In that range, homogeneous-prefix conditions can be insufficient, and Theorem 6 supplies only a necessary condition. No polynomial-time general classification or $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction is proved here.
