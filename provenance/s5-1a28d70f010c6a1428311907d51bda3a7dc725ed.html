The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(k\ge3\), rational ideal points \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\in\mathbb{Q}^k\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\epsilon>0\), define a hyperplane as median when each closed side has mass at least \(1/2\), and limiting when it contains positive mass. Let \(r_Y(\mu)\) be the minimum radius of a ball intersecting every median hyperplane, and \(r_{LP}(\mu)\) the minimum radius for every limiting median hyperplane. Assuming \(r_Y(\mu)>0\), decide whether \(r_{LP}(\mu)\le\epsilon r_Y(\mu)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is specified by its support: distinct ideal-point types \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\in\mathbb Q^k\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\) satisfying \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(k\ge3\) is part of the input, \(m:=k\), and \(L\) is the total binary input length.
For any set \(A\), write \(\mu(A)=\sum_{i:p_i\in A}\mu_i\). A median hyperplane has mass at least \(1/2\) in each closed halfspace, with boundary mass counted in both. A limiting median hyperplane is median and contains \(k\) affinely independent support points.
Let \(r_Y(\mu)\) and \(r_{LP}(\mu)\) be the minimum radii of closed Euclidean balls intersecting, respectively, every median hyperplane and every limiting median hyperplane. Centers range independently over \(\mathbb R^k\), radii are nonnegative, and an empty limiting family has radius \(0\). The input includes binary-rational \(\epsilon>0\) and promises \(r_Y(\mu)>0\); no general-position assumption is imposed.

Is deciding whether \(r_{LP}(\mu)\le\epsilon r_Y(\mu)\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite unweighted societies, the LP-yolk radius can be arbitrarily small relative to the positive yolk radius when \(k\ge3\). [Hu and Bailey](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10788v1)
Computing the discrete yolk radius is NP-hard in variable dimension; this does not settle the promised ratio decision problem. [Recognizing Majority-Rule Equilibrium](https://www2.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/papers/medians.pdf)

Result

The promised ratio decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds even when the support consists of the vertices of a full-dimensional regular simplex and all masses and the threshold are dyadic rationals.

The proof reduces PARTITION to the ratio decision problem. The simplex keeps the LP-yolk radius fixed. Its yolk radius attains a particular upper bound exactly when a half-mass subset exists. A quantitative gap permits a rational threshold.

1. A rational regular simplex

Let \(n\ge 4\) be a power of four, and set \(k=n-1\). Write \(B=\sqrt n\), which is an integer, and let \(\mathbf 1\in\mathbb R^{n-1}\) be the all-ones vector. Define

\( \beta=\frac{1}{B(B-1)},\qquad v_i=e_i-\beta\mathbf 1\quad(1\le i<n),\qquad v_n=\frac1B\mathbf 1. \)

These points have rational coordinates of binary length \(O(\log n)\). Direct calculation gives

\( v_i\cdot v_j=\delta_{ij}-\frac1n,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n v_i=0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n v_iv_i^\top=I_{n-1}. \)

For example, the coefficient needed for both the first and third identities is

\( -2\beta+(n-1)\beta^2=-\frac1n. \)

The points are affinely independent: if \(\sum_i t_i=0\), then their Gram matrix gives

\( \left\|\sum_i t_i v_i\right\|^2 =\sum_i t_i^2-\frac1n\left(\sum_i t_i\right)^2 =\sum_i t_i^2. \)

Thus an affine dependence must have every coefficient zero.

Put

\( s=\frac n2,\qquad \lambda=\frac1{\sqrt n},\qquad \ell=\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}},\qquad \rho=\frac1{\sqrt{n(n-1)}}. \)

Every vertex has norm \(\ell\). For a unit vector \(u\), its projections \(z_i=u\cdot v_i\) satisfy

\( \sum_i z_i=0,\qquad \sum_i z_i^2=1. \)

Throughout the proof, \(\mu(S)\) denotes the total mass of the vertices indexed by \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\).

2. The LP-yolk radius is fixed

Assume that every vertex mass satisfies \(\mu_i<1/2\).

Because the support is an affinely independent set of \(n=k+1\) points, its limiting median hyperplanes are precisely its \(n\) facet hyperplanes. The facet opposite \(v_i\) is

\( F_i=\{x:v_i\cdot x=-1/n\}. \)

It is median: the closed halfspace on the side excluding \(v_i\) contains boundary mass \(1-\mu_i>1/2\), while the other closed halfspace contains the entire support.

The origin has distance \(\rho\) from every facet, so \(r_{LP}\le\rho\). Conversely, if a ball with center \(c\) and radius \(r\) intersects every facet, then

\( |v_i\cdot c+1/n|\le r\ell \)

for each \(i\). Summing and using \(\sum_i v_i=0\) yields

\( nr\ell \ge\sum_i|v_i\cdot c+1/n| \ge\left|\sum_i(v_i\cdot c+1/n)\right| =1. \)

Consequently,

\( \boxed{r_{LP}=\rho}. \)

In particular, \(r_Y\ge r_{LP}=\rho>0\). Every society constructed below therefore satisfies the promise.

3. The yolk detects a half-mass subset

Assume additionally that

\[ |S|<s\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu(S)<1/2. \tag{1} \]

3.1. A universal upper bound

Represent a median hyperplane as

\( H(u,b)=\{x:u\cdot x=b\},\qquad \|u\|=1. \)

Changing both signs if necessary, assume \(b\ge0\). Its closed halfspace \(\{x:u\cdot x\ge b\}\) has mass at least \(1/2\). By (1), at least \(s\) vertices have projections at least \(b\).

Choose \(s\) such vertices, indexed by \(S\), and put \(Z=\sum_{i\in S}z_i\). Then \(Z\ge sb\), and the complementary projections sum to \(-Z\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\( 1=\sum_i z_i^2 \ge \frac{Z^2}{s}+\frac{Z^2}{s} \ge nb^2. \)

Hence \(b\le\lambda\). The ball centered at the origin with radius \(\lambda\) intersects every median hyperplane, proving

\[ r_Y\le\lambda. \tag{2} \]

3.2. A half-mass subset forces equality

Suppose \(\mu(S)=1/2\). Condition (1), applied also to the complement, implies \(|S|=s\).

Define

\( u_S=\frac2{\sqrt n}\sum_{i\in S}v_i. \)

The simplex identities imply

\( \|u_S\|=1,\qquad u_S\cdot v_j= \begin{cases} \lambda,&j\in S,\\ -\lambda,&j\notin S. \end{cases} \)

Both \(H(u_S,\lambda)\) and \(H(u_S,-\lambda)\) are median, because the two groups each have mass \(1/2\). For every center \(c\),

\( \max\{|u_S\cdot c-\lambda|,\ |u_S\cdot c+\lambda|\}\ge\lambda. \)

Thus every ball intersecting these two hyperplanes has radius at least \(\lambda\). Together with (2), this proves

\[ \boxed{\mu(S)=1/2\text{ for some }S\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_Y=\lambda}. \tag{3} \]

3.3. No half-mass subset gives a quantitative gap

Suppose no subset has mass \(1/2\), and suppose a known number \(0<\eta\le1/2\) satisfies

\[ |\mu(S)-1/2|\ge\eta \quad\text{for every }S. \tag{4} \]

We will prove

\[ \boxed{r_Y\le\lambda(1-\gamma)},\qquad \gamma=\frac{\eta^3}{16n^2}. \tag{5} \]

Define

\( w=\sum_i\mu_i v_i,\qquad h=\frac{\eta^2}{8n},\qquad t=\frac{\lambda h}{2},\qquad c=tw. \)

Since \(w\) is a convex combination of vertices, \(\|w\|\le\ell<1\).

Consider any median \(H(u,b)\), oriented so that \(b\ge0\). We already know \(b\le\lambda\).

Case 1: \(b<\lambda(1-h)\). Its distance from \(c\) is bounded by

\( |u\cdot c-b| \le t\|w\|+b \le\lambda(1-h/2). \)

Because

\( \frac h2=\frac{\eta^2}{16n}\ge\frac{\eta^3}{16n^2}=\gamma, \)

this distance is at most \(\lambda(1-\gamma)\).

Case 2: \(b\ge\lambda(1-h)\). First, exactly \(s\) vertices have projections at least \(b\).

There are at least \(s\) by (1). If there were at least \(s+1\), choose \(s+1\) of them. Applying Cauchy–Schwarz separately to these projections and their complement gives

\( 1\ge\frac{n(s+1)}{s-1}b^2, \)

and therefore

\( \frac b\lambda\le\sqrt{\frac{s-1}{s+1}} =\sqrt{1-\frac4{n+2}} \le1-\frac2{n+2} \le1-\frac1n. \)

This contradicts \(b/\lambda\ge1-h\), since \(h<1/n\).

Let \(S\) index the exactly \(s\) vertices whose projections are at least \(b\). Medianity and (4) imply

\( \mu(S)\ge1/2+\eta. \)

For the unit vector \(u_S\) defined above,

\( u\cdot u_S =\frac2{\sqrt n}\sum_{i\in S}z_i \ge\frac b\lambda \ge1-h. \)

Consequently,

\( \|u-u_S\|\le\sqrt{2h}=\frac{\lambda\eta}{2}. \)

Also, the simplex inner products give

\( u_S\cdot w =2\lambda\bigl(\mu(S)-1/2\bigr) \ge2\lambda\eta. \)

It follows that

\( u\cdot w \ge u_S\cdot w-\|u-u_S\|\|w\| \ge\frac32\lambda\eta \ge\lambda\eta. \)

Moreover,

\( b-tu\cdot w \ge b-t\|w\| \ge\lambda(1-3h/2)>0. \)

Thus the distance from \(c\) to this median is

\( |u\cdot c-b| =b-tu\cdot w \le\lambda-t\lambda\eta =\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda h\eta}{2}\right). \)

Finally,

\( \frac{\lambda h\eta}{2} =\frac{\eta^3}{16n^{3/2}} \ge\frac{\eta^3}{16n^2} =\gamma. \)

The distance is again at most \(\lambda(1-\gamma)\). Both cases hold for every median, proving (5).

4. Encoding PARTITION in the masses

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete PARTITION problem: given positive binary-encoded integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_q\), decide whether some subset sums to half their total. Its completeness is a classical result of [Karp](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let

\( W=\sum_{i=1}^q a_i. \)

Choose the smallest power of four satisfying \(n\ge\max\{4,2q\}\). Thus \(n=O(q)\). Pad the integer list to length \(n\) by setting

\( c_i=a_i\quad(i\le q),\qquad c_i=0\quad(i>q). \)

Choose the smallest power of two \(D\) satisfying

\( D>8n^2W. \)

Assign vertex \(v_i\) the mass

\[ \mu_i=\frac1n+\frac{nc_i-W}{D}. \tag{6} \]

These are dyadic rationals because \(n\) divides \(D\). Their sum is one:

\( \sum_i\mu_i =1+\frac{n\sum_i c_i-nW}{D} =1. \)

Since \(|nc_i-W|\le nW\),

\( \left|\mu_i-\frac1n\right|<\frac1{8n}. \)

Thus every mass is positive and less than \(1/2\).

Furthermore,

\( \sum_i\left|\mu_i-\frac1n\right| \le\frac{2nW}{D} <\frac1{4n}. \)

For any subset with fewer than \(s=n/2\) vertices,

\( \mu(S) \le\frac{s-1}{n}+\frac1{4n} =\frac12-\frac3{4n} <\frac12. \)

This verifies (1). By taking complements, any subset with more than \(s\) vertices has mass greater than \(1/2\).

For a subset of exactly \(s\) vertices, (6) gives

\( \mu(S)=\frac12+\frac{n\sum_{i\in S}c_i-sW}{D}. \)

Consequently,

\[ \mu(S)=1/2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i\in S}c_i=W/2. \tag{7} \]

A solution of the original PARTITION instance uses at most \(q\le s\) entries and can be padded to exactly \(s\) entries using the zero entries. Conversely, removing zero entries from a subset satisfying (7) gives a solution of PARTITION. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{PARTITION is yes}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{some support subset has mass }1/2}. \tag{8} \]

If PARTITION is no, all subset masses have denominator dividing \(D\), and \(D\) is even. Hence (4) holds with \(\eta=1/D\). Formula (5) then holds with

\( \gamma=\frac1{16n^2D^3}. \)

5. An exactly computable dyadic threshold

The ratio in the half-mass case is

\( \alpha=\frac{\rho}{\lambda}=\frac1{\sqrt{n-1}}. \)

We need a rational threshold slightly above \(\alpha\), but below \(\alpha/(1-\gamma)\).

Set

\( M=128n^3D^3, \)

which is a power of two. Let \(j\) be the smallest nonnegative integer satisfying

\( (n-1)j^2\ge M^2, \)

and output

\( \epsilon=\frac jM. \)

This positive dyadic rational can be computed exactly by integer binary search. By the definition of \(j\),

\( \alpha\le\epsilon<\alpha+\frac1M =\alpha+\frac{\gamma}{8n}. \)

Since \(\alpha>1/n\) and \(0<\gamma<1\),

\( \frac{\alpha}{1-\gamma}-\alpha =\frac{\alpha\gamma}{1-\gamma} >\frac{\gamma}{n}. \)

Therefore the constructed threshold satisfies

\[ \boxed{\alpha\le\epsilon<\frac{\alpha}{1-\gamma}}. \tag{9} \]

6. Correctness and polynomial size

If PARTITION is yes, (3) and (8) give \(r_Y=\lambda\). Section 2 gives \(r_{LP}=\rho\), so (9) implies

\( \frac{r_{LP}}{r_Y}=\alpha\le\epsilon. \)

The ratio instance is yes.

If PARTITION is no, the quantitative gap gives \(r_Y\le\lambda(1-\gamma)\). Since \(r_Y\ge\rho>0\), division is legitimate, and

\( \frac{r_{LP}}{r_Y} \ge\frac{\rho}{\lambda(1-\gamma)} =\frac{\alpha}{1-\gamma} >\epsilon. \)

The ratio instance is no.

The construction has \(\tau=n=O(q)\) distinct support points and \(m=k=n-1\). Its coordinate lengths are \(O(\log n)\), while

\( \log D=O(\log n+\log W),\qquad \log M=O(\log n+\log D). \)

All masses, coordinates, and the threshold therefore have polynomial total binary length, and all construction steps take polynomial time. No expansion into individual voters is used.

Every output satisfies \(k\ge3\) and the promise \(r_Y>0\). Thus this is a polynomial-time many-one reduction to the exact promised decision problem, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
