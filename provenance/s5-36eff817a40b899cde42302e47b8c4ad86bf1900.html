The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given indivisible projects \(C\) with sizes \(s_j\), budget \(b\), and \(\tau\) complete voter types \(\theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), where each monotone submodular utility \(u_\theta:2^C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) has a specified finite representation supporting value queries, compute an integral committee \(O\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{j\in O}s_j\le b\) such that no nonzero mass vector \(0\le\lambda\le\mu\) and integral committee \(T\) with \(\sum_{j\in T}s_j\le b\sum_\theta\lambda_\theta\) satisfy \(u_\theta(T)>67.37\,u_\theta(O\cup\{q\})\) for every \(\theta\) with \(\lambda_\theta>0\) and every \(q\in C\); determine whether this is computable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length \(L\).

Result

The search problem is solvable by a randomized algorithm in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The algorithm always returns a feasible integral committee, and that committee is stable with probability at least \(2/3\).

The proof uses the fractional-welfare and iterative-rounding approach of the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12499). The additional argument below establishes that polynomial precision suffices for binary rational masses, without expanding types into voters or depending on the reciprocal of the smallest mass.

1. Normalization and coalition saturation

Divide every cost by \(b\), so that the budget becomes \(1\). Write \(c(T)=\sum_{j\in T}s_j\) for the normalized cost.

For a set \(W\) of types, let \(M(W)=\sum_{\theta\in W}\mu_\theta\). Set \(\alpha=67.37\). Given an integral committee \(O\), define

\(E_O(T)=\{\theta:u_\theta(T)>\alpha u_\theta(O\cup\{q\})\text{ for every }q\in C\}\).

A blocking deviation exists precisely when some nonempty integral \(T\) satisfies

\(c(T)\le M(E_O(T))\).

Indeed, every blocking coalition uses only types in \(E_O(T)\), and its mass is at most \(M(E_O(T))\). Conversely, if this inequality holds, take the full mass of every type in \(E_O(T)\). Since \(T\) is nonempty and all costs are positive, this coalition has positive mass. The empty committee cannot block because its utility is zero.

We may discard types whose utilities are identically zero: such types never belong to \(E_O(T)\). For every remaining type, divide its utility by \(\max_{j\in C}u_\theta(\{j\})\). This maximum is positive: submodularity and normalization imply

\(u_\theta(T)\le\sum_{j\in T}u_\theta(\{j\})\).

These operations preserve every multiplicative comparison in the stability definition. Henceforth,

\(\max_j u_\theta(\{j\})=1,\qquad 0\le u_\theta(T)\le m\).

Every marginal utility lies in \([0,1]\).

If all types were discarded, return the empty committee. If \(c(C)\le1\), return \(C\), which is stable by monotonicity. Assume otherwise.

Fix the rational constants

\(\varepsilon=10^{-7},\quad \kappa=0.21,\quad \omega=0.23,\quad \gamma=7.435,\quad \Gamma=7.435001,\quad \rho=0.053548.\)

Let

\(d=\varepsilon/m,\qquad C_s=\{j:s_j\le d\},\qquad C_\ell=C\setminus C_s.\)

Include \(C_s\) in the output from the outset. Its cost is at most \(\varepsilon\). A blocking committee must contain an item of \(C_\ell\): otherwise it is a subset of the output and cannot strictly improve any type's utility. Consequently, every possible blocking committee has cost strictly greater than \(d\).

This cost bound is what permits polynomial precision despite arbitrarily small type masses.

2. A polynomial-time weighted fractional subroutine

For \(x\in[0,1]^{C_\ell}\), define

\(F_\theta(x)=\mathbb E[u_\theta(C_s\cup R_x)]\),

where \(R_x\) includes each item \(j\in C_\ell\) independently with probability \(x_j\).

We will call the subroutine with a nonempty active set \(W\) and a fractional budget \(B\) satisfying

\(\kappa\omega d\le B<1-\varepsilon.\)

Write \(S_\ell=\sum_{j\in C_\ell}s_j\). Since \(c(C)>1\), we have \(S_\ell>1-\varepsilon>B\). Define

\(\eta=\varepsilon B/S_\ell,\qquad P_B=\{x:\eta\le x_j\le1,\ \sum_{j\in C_\ell}s_jx_j\le B\}.\)

The lower-bound vector has cost \(\varepsilon B\), so \(P_B\) contains points of cost exactly \(B\).

Normalize the active masses by setting \(w_\theta=\mu_\theta/M(W)\), and consider

\(\Phi_W(x)=\sum_{\theta\in W}w_\theta\log F_\theta(x).\)

The subroutine produces a point \(x\in P_B\) of cost exactly \(B\) satisfying the approximate first-order condition

\(\max_{z\in P_B}\nabla\Phi_W(x)\cdot(z-x)\le\zeta,\qquad \zeta=\varepsilon d.\) (1)

Uniform bounds

Let

\(a=\kappa\omega\varepsilon^2/m^2.\)

Because \(S_\ell\le m\), we have \(\eta\ge a\). If a maximum-utility singleton belongs to \(C_s\), then \(F_\theta(x)\ge1\). Otherwise, that singleton is selected with probability at least \(\eta\). Thus throughout \(P_B\),

\(a\le F_\theta(x)\le m.\) (2)

The derivative formula is

\(\partial_jF_\theta(x)=\mathbb E[u_\theta(C_s\cup R\cup\{j\})-u_\theta(C_s\cup R)]\),

where \(R\) samples the other items. Therefore \(0\le\partial_jF_\theta\le1\). Mixed second derivatives are expected differences of two marginals; submodularity gives their values in \([-1,0]\), and diagonal second derivatives are zero. Consequently,

\(\left|\partial_{jk}\log F_\theta(x)\right|\le a^{-1}+a^{-2}\le2a^{-2}.\)

The same bound holds for each Hessian entry of \(\Phi_W\), since its weights sum to \(1\).

In particular, these bounds do not involve the smallest mass.

Obtaining condition (1)

Let \(H=2m^2/a^2\). Start at any point of cost exactly \(B\). At each iteration estimate the gradient by \(\widehat g\), with coordinate error at most \(\zeta/(8m)\). Find a maximizer \(z\) of \(\widehat g\cdot z\) subject to the coordinate bounds and cost exactly \(B\). This is a fractional-knapsack computation: start at the lower bounds and allocate the remaining expenditure in decreasing order of \(\widehat g_j/s_j\).

Set \(\widehat G=\widehat g\cdot(z-x)\). If \(\widehat G\le\zeta/2\), stop.

The true gradient is nonnegative. Hence its maximum over \(P_B\) can be attained at a point of cost exactly \(B\): any unused budget can be filled without decreasing the objective. For every feasible direction, the error in its estimated inner product is at most \(\zeta/8\), because its coordinatewise absolute sum is at most \(m\). At stopping, therefore, the true gap is at most \(5\zeta/8\), establishing (1).

Otherwise, \(\nabla\Phi_W(x)\cdot(z-x)>3\zeta/8\). Replace \(x\) by

\(x+h(z-x),\qquad h=\zeta/(4H).\)

The Hessian bound and \(\sum_j|z_j-x_j|\le m\) give

\(\Phi_W(x+h(z-x))\ge\Phi_W(x)+h\nabla\Phi_W(x)\cdot(z-x)-Hh^2/2.\)

Thus every such iteration increases the objective by more than \(\zeta^2/(16H)\). By (2), its entire range has length at most \(\log(m/a)\). The number of iterations is consequently polynomial in \(m\), since \(a\) and \(\zeta\) are inverse polynomials. For a concrete predetermined cap, one can use

\(\left\lceil16Hm/(a\zeta^2)\right\rceil+2,\)

using \(\log(m/a)\le m/a\).

Estimating the gradient

Estimate \(F_\theta(x)\) by sampling utilities in \([0,m]\), and estimate each derivative by sampling marginals in \([0,1]\). If both errors are at most \(\Delta\le a/2\), then

\(\left|\frac{\widehat{\partial_jF_\theta}}{\widehat F_\theta}-\frac{\partial_jF_\theta}{F_\theta}\right|\le4\Delta/a^2.\)

It suffices to choose

\(\Delta\le\min\{a/2,\ \zeta a^2/(32m)\}.\)

Taking the weighted sum preserves the coordinate error bound because the weights sum to \(1\). Hoeffding's inequality gives error probability at most \(2\exp(-2N\Delta^2/m^2)\) for an average of \(N\) independent observations in \([0,m]\). Thus polynomially many samples suffice, including a union bound over all estimates made by the algorithm.

The logarithmic objective is used only in the analysis; the algorithm does not evaluate logarithms.

3. The weighted deviation bound

Suppose the preceding subroutine returns \(x\), and \(T\) is an integral committee with cost \(c\in[d,1]\). Let \(A\subseteq W\) satisfy

\(u_\theta(T)>rF_\theta(x)\quad\text{for every }\theta\in A,\qquad r>1.\)

Then

\(M(A)<\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\frac{M(W)c}{B(r-1)}.\) (3)

Here is the proof.

Let \(g=\nabla\Phi_W(x)\), \(p=(\mathbf1_{T\cap C_\ell}-x)_+\), and \(v=x-\eta\mathbf1\). Submodularity implies that \(F_\theta\) is concave along every nonnegative direction: its mixed second derivatives are nonpositive. Applying this along \(p\), and using monotonicity, yields

\(\nabla F_\theta(x)\cdot p\ge F_\theta(x+p)-F_\theta(x)\ge u_\theta(T)-F_\theta(x).\)

All gradient coordinates are nonnegative. After division by \(F_\theta(x)\), weighted summation over \(A\), and inclusion of the other nonnegative terms, we obtain

\(g\cdot p>(r-1)M(A)/M(W).\) (4)

Concavity along the ray from \(0\) to \(x\), with \(C_s\) fixed, also gives

\(\nabla F_\theta(x)\cdot x\le F_\theta(x)-F_\theta(0)\le F_\theta(x).\)

Since \(0\le v\le x\), this implies \(g\cdot v\le1\).

Write \(B'=(1-\varepsilon)B\), and let \(t=\min\{1,B'/c\}\). The point

\(z=x+t\left(p-\frac{c}{B'}v\right)\)

belongs to \(P_B\). Indeed:

  • Its coordinates remain at least \(\eta\), because \(tc/B'\le1\).
  • They remain at most \(1\), because \(x+tp\le x+p\le1\).
  • Its cost is at most \(B\), because the cost of \(p\) is at most \(c\), whereas the cost of \(v\) is exactly \(B'\).

Condition (1) therefore implies

\(g\cdot p\le c/B'+\zeta/t.\)

Since \(c\ge d\), \(B'\le1\), \(d\le1\), and \(\zeta=\varepsilon d\), both cases \(c\le B'\) and \(c>B'\) give

\(\zeta/t\le\varepsilon c/B'.\)

Combining this inequality with (4) proves (3).

The proof allows \(M(A)\) to be arbitrarily small. No precision requirement proportional to that mass appears.

4. Rounding while controlling unsatisfied mass

Suppose the fractional point \(x\) has cost \(B=\kappa D\). Call a type \(\gamma\)-satisfied by an integral set \(Q\) if

\(\max_{q\in C}u_\theta(Q\cup\{q\})\ge F_\theta(x)/\gamma.\)

A rounding trial takes \(C_s\), independently includes each item with \(s_j\le B\) according to \(x_j\), and excludes items with \(s_j>B\). Reject the trial if its selected items in \(C_\ell\) have cost exceeding \(D\).

Define

\(\beta=(\kappa e^{1-\kappa})^{1/\kappa}+(\gamma-1)e^{2-\gamma}.\)

For each type, the probability that a trial is either rejected or fails to \(\gamma\)-satisfy that type is at most \(\beta\).

Proof of the rounding bound

Let \(J_1=\{j\in C_\ell:s_j>B\}\) and \(J_2=C\setminus J_1\). Write \(G_1,G_2\) for the expected utilities restricted to these respective sets, with the probabilities supplied by \(x\) and with \(C_s\) included deterministically in \(J_2\).

Subadditivity gives \(F_\theta(x)\le G_1+G_2\). Moreover,

\(G_1\le\sum_{j\in J_1}x_ju_\theta(\{j\})\le\max_{j\in J_1}u_\theta(\{j\}),\)

because \(\sum_{j\in J_1}x_j\le1\).

If \(G_1\ge F_\theta(x)/\gamma\), a singleton additament satisfies the type in every feasible trial. Otherwise,

\(G_2>(1-1/\gamma)F_\theta(x).\)

If some singleton in \(J_2\) has utility at least \(G_2/(\gamma-1)\), that singleton again suffices. In the remaining case, let

\(h_\theta=\max_{j\in J_2}u_\theta(\{j\})<G_2/(\gamma-1).\)

Here \(h_\theta>0\), and every marginal of the restricted utility divided by \(h_\theta\) lies in \([0,1]\).

We use the following known concentration theorem: for a nonnegative monotone submodular function with marginals at most \(1\), evaluated on independent Bernoulli variables, its value \(Z\) satisfies

\(\log\mathbb E[e^{tZ}]\le(e^t-1)\mathbb E[Z]\quad\text{for every real }t.\)

This follows from the self-bounding property and Theorem 3.1 of [Vondrák's concentration note](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.2791). The restricted, rescaled utility has precisely these hypotheses; deterministic inclusions are permitted as Bernoulli variables of probability \(1\).

Applying Markov's inequality with \(t=-\log r\) gives the lower-tail bound

\(\Pr[Z\le\mathbb E[Z]/r]\le(re^{1-r})^{\mathbb E[Z]/r}.\)

Use \(r=\gamma-1\) and \(\mathbb E[Z]=G_2/h_\theta>\gamma-1\). The probability that the trial utility is below \(G_2/(\gamma-1)\) is at most \((\gamma-1)e^{2-\gamma}\). Outside this event, its utility exceeds \(F_\theta(x)/\gamma\).

For the budget bound, divide each randomly selected item's cost by \(B\). Each resulting coefficient is at most \(1\), and the expected total is at most \(1\). For \(t>0\), independence and the inequality \(e^{ta}\le1+a(e^t-1)\) for \(a\in[0,1]\) bound its moment-generating function by \(\exp(e^t-1)\). Taking \(t=\log(1/\kappa)\) bounds the probability of cost exceeding \(D=B/\kappa\) by \((\kappa e^{1-\kappa})^{1/\kappa}\).

The union bound proves the claim.

Computable satisfaction certificates

We must test satisfaction without knowing \(F_\theta(x)\) exactly. Estimate it and construct a rational upper bound \(A_\theta\) satisfying

\(F_\theta(x)\le A_\theta\le(\Gamma/\gamma)F_\theta(x).\) (5)

This requires only inverse-polynomial additive accuracy. For example, estimate with error at most

\(r_0=a(\Gamma/\gamma-1)/2\)

and set \(A_\theta=\widehat F_\theta+r_0\). Inequality (2) then proves (5).

Certify a type as satisfied when the exact circuit evaluations show

\(\max_q u_\theta(Q\cup\{q\})\ge A_\theta/\Gamma.\)

Every certified type is truly \(\Gamma\)-satisfied. Every truly \(\gamma\)-satisfied type is certified.

For a trial, let \(Y=1\) if it is infeasible; otherwise let \(Y\) be the fraction of active mass that is uncertified. Conditional on valid estimates, the preceding per-type bound and linearity of expectation give \(\mathbb E[Y]\le\beta\). Thus

\(\Pr[Y\le\rho]\ge1-\beta/\rho.\)

The constant verification in Section 8 shows \(\beta<0.05354737<\rho\), and hence this probability exceeds \(10^{-5}\).

Repeated trials therefore find, with arbitrarily high prescribed confidence in polynomial time, a feasible \(Q\) whose uncertified mass is at most \(\rho M(W)\). This bound also has no dependence on the smallest mass.

5. The complete algorithm

Let \(W_0\) contain the nonzero types, initialize \(O=C_s\), and set

\(D_0=(1-\varepsilon)(1-\omega),\qquad D_t=D_0\omega^t.\)

While \(W_t\) is nonempty and \(D_t\ge d\):

  • Apply the fractional subroutine with active types \(W_t\) and budget \(B_t=\kappa D_t\).
  • Obtain the upper bounds (5).
  • Repeat rounding trials until one has selected cost at most \(D_t\) and uncertified mass at most \(\rho M(W_t)\).
  • Add its selected projects to \(O\), remove its certified types, and proceed to \(t+1\).

Consequently,

\(M(W_t)\le\rho^tM(W_0)\le\rho^t.\) (6)

The number of stages before \(D_t<d\) is \(O(\log(m/\varepsilon))\).

The terminal stage

If active types remain at the first stage \(t\) with \(D_t<d\), compute one additional fractional point with budget \(B_t=\kappa D_t\). This call is valid because \(D_t\ge\omega d\).

Since every item in \(C_\ell\) has cost greater than \(d\),

\(\sum_{j\in C_\ell}x_j<B_t/d<1.\)

Submodularity gives, for every remaining type,

\(F_\theta(x)\le u_\theta(C_s)+\sum_{j\in C_\ell}x_j\bigl(u_\theta(C_s\cup\{j\})-u_\theta(C_s)\bigr)\le\max_{q\in C}u_\theta(C_s\cup\{q\}).\)

Thus \(C_s\) alone, together with an additament, satisfies every remaining type even with factor \(1\). Declare these types satisfied and terminate.

Every nonzero type has now been assigned to a stage where it is \(\Gamma\)-satisfied. The terminal fractional point will be included in the stability analysis.

Feasibility

The union of selected projects has cost at most

\(c(O)\le\varepsilon+\sum_{t\ge0}D_t=\varepsilon+D_0/(1-\omega)=1.\)

Overlaps only decrease the union's cost. The terminal stage adds no projects outside \(C_s\).

6. Proof of stability

Assume, for contradiction, that a nonempty integral \(T\) blocks the output. Set

\(S=E_O(T),\qquad c=c(T).\)

By Section 1, \(d<c\le M(S)\le1\).

Partition \(S\) according to the stages at which its types were satisfied. Let \(S_t\) be the part satisfied at stage \(t\), and let \(x^t\) be that stage's fractional point.

For every \(\theta\in S_t\), some additament \(q\) satisfies

\(u_\theta(Q_t\cup\{q\})\ge F_\theta(x^t)/\Gamma,\)

where \(Q_t\subseteq O\). At the terminal stage, take \(Q_t=C_s\). Monotonicity and the definition of \(S\) imply

\(u_\theta(T)>\alpha u_\theta(O\cup\{q\})\ge(\alpha/\Gamma)F_\theta(x^t).\)

Apply (3) with \(r=\alpha/\Gamma\) and \(B_t=\kappa D_t\). Summing over the partition yields

\(M(S)\le\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\frac{c}{\alpha/\Gamma-1}\sum_t\frac{M(W_t)}{\kappa D_t}.\)

Using (6) and \(\rho<\omega\),

\(\sum_t\frac{M(W_t)}{\kappa D_t}\le\frac{1}{\kappa D_0}\sum_{t\ge0}(\rho/\omega)^t=\frac{\omega}{\kappa(1-\varepsilon)(1-\omega)(\omega-\rho)}.\)

Therefore,

\(M(S)\le c\frac{K}{\alpha/\Gamma-1},\qquad K=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\omega}{\kappa(1-\varepsilon)^2(1-\omega)(\omega-\rho)}.\)

Section 8 verifies the strict inequality

\(\Gamma(1+K)<67.369<67.37=\alpha.\)

It follows that \(K/(\alpha/\Gamma-1)<1\), and hence \(M(S)<c\). This contradicts the blocking-budget inequality \(c\le M(S)\).

Thus the output is stable whenever all required estimates and stage computations succeed.

7. Bit complexity and bounded error

All mass sums and normalized weights are computed exactly as rationals. Adding the input rationals has polynomial bit complexity: a common denominator can be their product, whose bit length is at most the sum of their denominator bit lengths. Dividing by the active mass \(M(W)\) likewise yields rational weights of polynomial bit length, even when that mass is very small.

Each circuit evaluation takes polynomial time in \(L\), and its rational output has polynomial bit length. Utility normalization uses exact rational arithmetic.

The fractional subroutine has a polynomial iteration cap. Its step size is rational, and its linear-oracle outputs have polynomial bit length. After polynomially many affine updates, the iterates still have polynomial bit length. The required sampling accuracies are inverse polynomials in \(m\). Sample averages, gradient ratios, satisfaction thresholds, cost comparisons, and mass comparisons therefore have polynomial bit complexity.

Rational Bernoulli variables can be generated by rejection sampling from binary integers. Each attempt succeeds with probability at least \(1/2\); capping the number of attempts per draw at a logarithmic bound makes the total probability of a sampling abort arbitrarily small while keeping the worst-case running time polynomial.

Finally, use predetermined caps for iterations, samples, and rounding trials. Allocate failure probability at most \(1/12\) each to:

  • all inaccurate utility and derivative estimates;
  • failure to find an acceptable rounding trial at any stage;
  • capped Bernoulli sampling.

Fresh samples and conditional Hoeffding bounds justify the union bound even though queries are adaptive. If any cap is reached, return \(C_s\), which is feasible. Outside these failure events, the preceding proof establishes stability. The success probability is at least \(3/4\), and therefore at least \(2/3\).

This proves the claimed bounded-error polynomial-time algorithm.

8. Exact verification of the constants

The numerical inequalities used above admit rational certificates.

Let \(E=2.203396427\), \(r_1=0.02548282\), and \(r_2=0.02806455\). For \(x=0.79\), the exponential series gives

\(e^x\le\sum_{n=0}^{20}\frac{x^n}{n!}+\frac{x^{21}}{21!}\frac{1}{1-x/22}<E.\)

The last inequality is a rational comparison. Another rational comparison gives

\((0.21E)^{100}<r_1^{21}.\)

Since \(1/\kappa=100/21\), these inequalities prove

\((\kappa e^{1-\kappa})^{1/\kappa}<r_1.\)

For the second term, the rational comparison

\(\sum_{n=0}^{50}\frac{(5.435)^n}{n!}>\frac{6.435}{r_2}\)

implies \(6.435e^{-5.435}<r_2\). Consequently,

\(\beta<r_1+r_2=0.05354737.\)

In particular,

\(1-\beta/\rho>\frac{0.00000063}{0.053548}>10^{-5}.\)

Finally, substitution of the stated rational constants into \(K\) gives the exact rational inequality

\(\Gamma(1+K)=7.435001\left(1+\frac{(1+10^{-7})\,0.23}{0.21(1-10^{-7})^2\,0.77(0.23-0.053548)}\right)<67.369.\)

Thus neither the success-probability bound nor the stability constant relies on floating-point rounding.
