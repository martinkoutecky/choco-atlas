The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), chair order \(\sigma\), distinguished candidate \(d\), and \(q\) ordered cohorts with binary-rational masses \(\alpha_i>0\), \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\), and allowed rankings \(\Omega_i\), each cohort's common ranking is revealed on arrival. Does there exist a policy that, after observing each current ranking, assigns mass \(x_i\in[0,\alpha_i]\) to the left side and the rest to the right, such that for every allowed future ranking sequence the ties-promote plurality partition runoff succeeds? In constructive mode, success means some runoff winner \(w\) satisfies \(w\succeq_\sigma d\); in destructive mode, it means no runoff winner \(w\) satisfies \(w\preceq_\sigma d\). All population mass votes in the runoff for its highest-ranked finalist.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) candidates, let \(\sigma\) be a strict chair order, and let \(d\in C\). Write \(a\succeq_\sigma b\) when the chair ranks \(a\) at least as highly as \(b\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses. Here types include cohort identity; \(\tau\) counts listed positive-mass cohorts, not possible rankings. Total society mass is \(1\).
The input includes a possibly empty history of cohorts with known strict rankings and fixed binary-rational left/right mass allocations, followed by \(q\geq1\) ordered cohorts of masses \(\alpha_i>0\). History mass plus \(\sum_{i=1}^q\alpha_i\) equals \(1\).
For each remaining cohort, \(\Omega_i\) is a nonempty explicitly listed set of strict rankings of \(C\); \(\Omega_1\) is a singleton giving the already revealed current ranking. Each later ranking is chosen arbitrarily from its own \(\Omega_i\) and revealed upon arrival.
After observing ranking \(i\), a deterministic policy irrevocably chooses a real \(x_i\in[0,\alpha_i]\), assigning that mass left and the remainder right. Its choice may depend on the revealed history but not future rankings.
Run mass-weighted plurality on \(C\) separately on each side. Ties promote: the union of both winner sets forms the finalists. A zero-mass side promotes every candidate. All society mass, including the history, votes in the plurality runoff for its highest-ranked finalist; all tied runoff winners belong to \(W\).
Constructive success means \(W\cap\{c\in C:c\succeq_\sigma d\}\neq\varnothing\); destructive success means \(W\cap\{c\in C:d\succeq_\sigma c\}=\varnothing\). The decision problem asks whether a policy guarantees the specified success for every allowed ranking sequence.

For each mode, is this continuous online plurality partition problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(q\), and total input bit length \(L\).

Discrete online plurality partition with ties promoting is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hard in both modes and belongs to \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\); discrete offline partition is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete in both nonunique-winner modes. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/1203.0411v2). No classification of this precise continuous model was located.

Classification

Both constructive and destructive continuous online plurality partition are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Hardness holds even when every \(\Omega_i\) is a singleton, so all rankings are known in advance. This singleton restriction is, in fact, \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

The proof below allows every split to be an arbitrary real number and uses dyadic rational input masses.

1. A reduction for both modes

We reduce from perfect three-dimensional matching: given disjoint sets \(X,Y,Z\), each of cardinality \(k\), and triples \(S_1,\ldots,S_t\), does some collection of \(k\) triples cover \(U=X\cup Y\cup Z\)? This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. We use nonempty instances with \(k,t\geq1\); excluding the trivial empty instances preserves hardness. [Karp’s original paper](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

We regard each \(S_j\) as a three-element subset of \(U\). Define
\[ \]
\(D=2k+2,\qquad B=3D+2t+4,\qquad P=B+2k+1,\qquad A=P-4-2t=3D+2k+1.\)

We initially describe unnormalized masses. Section 2 converts them to dyadic masses summing to one.

The candidates are \(p,b,s_1,\ldots,s_t\), together with auxiliary candidates introduced below. Any displayed ranking is completed to a strict ranking by appending all unmentioned candidates in a fixed order.

Auxiliary cohorts

An auxiliary cohort of mass \(r\), where \(0<r\leq1\), receives its own fresh candidate \(z\), ranks \(z\) first, and has its entire mass fixed on the left. Every such candidate is used first by exactly one cohort.

Consequently, every auxiliary candidate has first-round left score at most \(1\) and right score \(0\).

Fixed history and remaining cohorts

Construct the following history:

  • One cohort of mass \(4\), ranking \(p>b>\cdots\), with left/right allocations \((2,2)\).
  • One cohort of mass \(4\), ranking \(b>p>\cdots\), with allocations \((2,2)\).
  • For each \(j\), one cohort of mass \(1\), ranking \(s_j>p>b>\cdots\), entirely on the left.
  • \(A\) auxiliary cohorts of mass \(1\), each ranking its own auxiliary candidate first, followed by \(p>b>\cdots\).
  • \(B-4\) auxiliary cohorts of mass \(1\), each ranking its own auxiliary candidate first, followed by \(b>p>\cdots\).
  • For each element \(e\in U\), \(D\) auxiliary cohorts of mass \(1\). Each ranks its own auxiliary candidate first, then all \(s_j\) with \(e\in S_j\), in increasing index order, then \(b>p>\cdots\).

There are \(q=t\) remaining cohorts. Cohort \(j\) has mass \(1\) and the single allowed ranking \(s_j>p>b>\cdots\).

Let \(y_j\in[0,1]\) denote its unnormalized mass assigned left.

2. Dyadic normalization and input size

The total mass constructed so far is
\[ \]
\(N=8+2t+A+(B-4)+3kD=P+B+3kD.\)

Let \(T\) be the smallest power of two with \(T\geq N\), and put \(K=(T-N)/2\).

Add auxiliary cohorts contributing total mass \(K\) to \(p\) in the runoff and another total mass \(K\) to \(b\). Specifically, for each of these two totals, use \(\lfloor K\rfloor\) unit auxiliary cohorts and, if necessary, one auxiliary cohort of mass \(K-\lfloor K\rfloor\). Their rankings begin respectively with \(z>p>b\) and \(z>b>p\).

The resulting total mass is \(T\). Divide every cohort mass and fixed allocation by \(T\). Because \(T\) is a power of two and \(K\) is an integer or half-integer, all resulting masses and allocations are dyadic rationals.

All subsequent score calculations use unnormalized units; actual scores are divided by \(T\). A legal actual split \(x_j\) corresponds exactly to \(y_j=Tx_j\in[0,1]\).

Since \(N=O(t+k^2)\) and \(T<2N\), the construction has
\[ \]
\(m=O(t+k^2),\qquad \tau=O(t+k^2),\qquad q=t.\)

Every cohort, including every auxiliary cohort, is explicitly listed. With full strict rankings, the output bit length and construction time are both
\[ \]
\(O\!\left((t+k^2)^2\log(t+k+2)\right).\)

Thus this is a polynomial-time reduction under the specified support representation.

3. First-round finalists: real splits select a subset

Both \(p\) and \(b\) have score \(2\) on each side.

For every selectable candidate \(s_j\), its left and right scores are respectively
\[ \]
\(1+y_j\quad\text{and}\quad1-y_j.\)

Every auxiliary candidate has left score at most \(1\) and right score \(0\). Hence the maximum score on each side is exactly \(2\), for every legal real-valued allocation.

The right winner set is therefore exactly \(\{p,b\}\). The left winner set is
\[ \]
\(\{p,b\}\cup\{s_j:y_j=1\}.\)

Writing \(J=\{j:y_j=1\}\), the finalist set is exactly
\[ \]
\(F=\{p,b\}\cup\{s_j:j\in J\}.\)

Every subset \(J\) is achievable: choose \(y_j=1\) for \(j\in J\) and \(y_j=0\) otherwise. Conversely, a fractional choice with \(y_j<1\) does not promote \(s_j\). This identifies the finalists for every real allocation.

Both sides contain positive anchor mass, so the zero-mass convention is respected automatically.

4. Runoff scores and correctness

Let \(u(J)\) be the number of elements of \(U\) uncovered by the triples indexed by \(J\).

Candidate \(p\) receives:

  • Its own anchor mass \(4\).
  • Mass \(2\) from the history and remaining cohorts of each unselected \(s_j\).
  • Its auxiliary support \(A+K\).

Thus
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{score}(p)=4+2(t-|J|)+A+K=P+K-2|J|.\)

Candidate \(b\) receives its anchor mass \(4\), its auxiliary support \(B-4+K\), and mass \(D\) for every uncovered element. Hence
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{score}(b)=B+K+D\,u(J).\)

Each selected candidate \(s_j\) receives its own mass \(2\) and possibly element-cohort mass. An element cohort can vote for \(s_j\) only if its element belongs to \(S_j\). Since \(|S_j|=3\),
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{score}(s_j)\leq2+3D<B\leq\operatorname{score}(b).\)

Therefore every runoff winner belongs to \(\{p,b\}\). Their score difference is
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{score}(p)-\operatorname{score}(b)=2k+1-2|J|-D\,u(J).\)

If \(u(J)\geq1\), then, using \(D=2k+2\),
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{score}(p)-\operatorname{score}(b)\leq2k+1-D=-1.\)

Thus \(b\) is the unique winner whenever some element is uncovered.

If \(u(J)=0\), the difference is positive exactly when \(|J|\leq k\). But covering \(3k\) elements with three-element sets requires \(|J|\geq k\). Consequently, \(p\) is the unique winner exactly when \(J\) consists of \(k\) triples covering \(U\).

Such a cover is exact: its triples have total cardinality \(3k\), equal to the cardinality of their union, so they are pairwise disjoint.

We have proved:
\[ \]
\(W=\{p\}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ J\text{ is a perfect three-dimensional matching}.\)

For every other allocation, \(W=\{b\}\).

Constructive mode

Put \(p\) first in the chair order and choose \(d=p\). The acceptable set is exactly \(\{p\}\). Constructive success is therefore equivalent to existence of a perfect matching.

Destructive mode

Put \(p\) first and \(b\) second in the chair order, and choose \(d=b\). The forbidden set is exactly \(C\setminus\{p\}\). Destructive success is therefore equivalent to \(W=\{p\}\), again equivalent to existence of a perfect matching.

All ranking sets are singletons. Any split vector can be implemented by a deterministic policy, and every policy induces a split vector on the unique ranking sequence. The reduction thus proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for both specified online problems.

5. Algorithms and running times

All rankings known: NP-completeness

When every \(\Omega_i\) is a singleton, enumerate the two nonempty first-round winner sets \(H_\ell,H_r\). There are fewer than \(2^{2m}\) pairs.

For each pair, its finalist set and runoff scores are fixed by the input rankings. Check whether those runoff scores satisfy the requested success condition.

If they do, test whether the guessed first-round winner sets are realizable. Let \(s_{a,h}(x)\) be candidate \(a\)'s first-round score on side \(h\). These are rational affine functions of the split variables. Introduce thresholds \(M_h\) and require
\[ \]
\(s_{a,h}(x)=M_h\quad(a\in H_h),\qquad s_{a,h}(x)<M_h\quad(a\notin H_h),\)
together with \(0\leq x_i\leq\alpha_i\).

These constraints characterize the winner sets exactly. They also handle zero-mass sides: all scores are then zero, so only the full candidate set can satisfy the constraints.

Strict inequalities can be checked by replacing each with
\[ \]
\(s_{a,h}(x)\leq M_h-\varepsilon,\qquad0\leq\varepsilon\leq1,\)
and maximizing \(\varepsilon\). The original system is feasible exactly when the optimum is positive: finitely many satisfied strict inequalities have a positive minimum slack. All variables can be bounded, and all coefficients are rational, so rational linear programming applies in polynomial bit complexity. [Aspvall and Stone, *Khachiyan’s Linear Programming Algorithm*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0196677480900024).

This gives deterministic time
\[ \]
\(2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,q,L).\)

Alternatively, guessing the two winner sets and performing this polynomial-time verification proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with the reduction, the singleton restriction is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete in both modes.

General online instances

Put
\[ \]
\(R=\prod_{i=2}^{q}|\Omega_i|,\)
with the empty product equal to \(1\). This is the number of allowed complete ranking sequences.

Expand the tree of revealed ranking prefixes. It has at most \(qR\) decision nodes. Assign one split variable to each node, shared by all descendant leaves. This enforces the information restriction exactly: a decision depends only on already revealed rankings.

For a deterministic policy, previous allocations are themselves determined by the ranking prefix, so this tree representation loses no permitted dependence on revealed history. Conversely, any assignment of legal splits to its nodes defines a deterministic policy.

Enumerate two nonempty winner sets at every leaf. There are at most \(2^{2mR}\) such assignments. At each leaf, compute the runoff outcome and reject the assignment if success fails there. For surviving assignments, impose the winner-set constraints from above simultaneously at all leaves, using the shared node variables. One rational linear program tests feasibility.

The resulting deterministic running time is
\[ \]
\(2^{O(mR)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,q,R,L).\)

Since the ranking sets are explicitly listed, \(|\Omega_i|\leq L\), giving the bound solely in the requested parameters
\[ \]
\(2^{O(mL^{q-1})}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,q,L^{q-1},L).\)

One remaining cohort

For \(q=1\), both modes admit a polynomial-time algorithm.

Let \(a\) be the current cohort's top candidate. Only its scores vary:
\[ \]
\(s_{a,\ell}(x)=\ell_a+x,\qquad s_{a,r}(x)=r_a+\alpha_1-x.\)

All other first-round scores are fixed. Winner sets can change only at the at most \(2(m-1)\) values where one of these expressions equals another candidate's score. Test every breakpoint in \([0,\alpha_1]\), both endpoints, and one rational midpoint of each intervening interval. The winner sets are constant on each such open interval, so these tests are exhaustive.

There are \(O(m)\) tests, each requiring \(O(m\tau)\) rational operations to compute the runoff. The bit running time is
\[ \]
\(O\!\left(m^2\tau\,\operatorname{poly}(L)\right).\)
