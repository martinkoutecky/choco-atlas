The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete or truncated rankings of \(C\) candidates, a seat count \(m\), and a fixed tie-breaking rule, use WIGM with normalized quota \(q=1/(m+1)\). What is the minimum \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_r|\mu(r)-\nu(r)|\) over ranking distributions \(\nu\) whose WIGM winner set differs from that of \(\mu\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\mathcal C\) contain \(C\geq2\) candidates, let \(1\leq m<C\) be the seat count, and fix a total candidate order for breaking all ties. Let \(R\) consist of all repetition-free candidate lists, including complete, truncated, and empty rankings.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. The input distribution \(\mu\) has total mass \(1\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Define WIGM with fixed quota \(q=1/(m+1)\). Initially all candidates are hopeful; each ranking's current weight is its mass and counts toward its highest-ranked hopeful candidate, or is exhausted if none remains.
At each round, stop if all seats are filled. Otherwise seat simultaneously all hopeful candidates with tally at least \(q\), ordered by decreasing tally and then the fixed order; if they exceed the remaining vacancies, seat only the first candidates up to that number and stop.
For each simultaneously seated candidate with pre-round tally \(T\), multiply the weights currently assigned to that candidate by \((T-q)/T\); remove all seated candidates and reassign the resulting weights to the highest-ranked remaining hopeful candidates.
If no candidate reaches quota, seat all hopeful candidates and stop when their number equals the remaining vacancies; otherwise eliminate a minimum-tally candidate using the fixed order and transfer its assigned weights without scaling. Use exact arithmetic, and write \(W(\eta)\) for the unordered winner set.
Allow \(\nu\) to be any distribution on \(R\) with nonnegative real masses summing to \(1\), including types absent from the input. Define \(\rho(\mu)=\inf\{\tfrac12\sum_{r\in R}|\mu(r)-\nu(r)|:W(\nu)\neq W(\mu)\}\); attainment is not assumed.

Given also a binary rational \(b\in[0,1]\), is deciding whether \(\rho(\mu)\leq b\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(C,m,\tau,L\).

Known discrete results: audit-graph certificates satisfying coherence and weak security certify a ballot-replacement margin greater than \(M/2\) when \(M\leq q\); this is a conditional lower bound, not an exact complexity classification ([Heitzmann](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.10887v1)).
For discrete single-winner STV/IRV, deciding whether one ballot replacement can change the winner is NP-complete for any fixed tie-breaking mechanism ([Xia](https://econcs.seas.harvard.edu/resource/xiaec12pdf)). No result settling the continuous problem defined here was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified total mass one for both distributions and allowed arbitrary nonnegative real masses in the alternative distribution.
  • Allowed alternative distributions to introduce any ranking type, matching unrestricted ballot replacement; included empty rankings as explicitly permitted by the discrete definition.
  • Restricted the seat count to at least one and fewer than the candidate count, excluding the case where every candidate necessarily wins.
  • Instantiated the fixed tie-breaking rule as a total candidate order used for elimination and equal seating tallies.
  • Preserved simultaneous quota seating and pre-round transfer values, exhaustion, and the final seating of all remaining hopeful candidates.
  • Capped simultaneous seating at the remaining vacancies: the normalized quota permits excess qualifying candidates at equality, whereas the discrete Droop quota prevents this case.
  • Replaced minimum by infimum because fixed tie-breaking can prevent attainment, and formulated its rational-threshold decision problem to give an exact complexity target.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already for \(m=1\) and strictly positive \(b\). The reduction below uses dyadic input masses, polynomial support size, and the prescribed fixed tie-breaking order.

For \(m=1\), the problem is also in \(\mathrm{NP}\), hence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. An exact algorithm for that case runs in \(2^{O(C\log C)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) time.

1. The source problem

We use the following known theorem: given an explicitly listed profile of complete rankings and a designated candidate \(c\), deciding whether some sequence of minimum-tally tie-breaking choices leaves \(c\) as the last remaining candidate is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is the PUT-STV winner problem, stated as Theorem 4 by [Conitzer, Rognlie, and Xia](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/preferenceIJCAI09.pdf).

Here the source procedure eliminates a minimum-tally candidate until one remains; it does not stop at a quota.

Let a source instance have candidate set \(A\), with \(|A|=k\geq2\), and \(N\geq1\) ballots. Write \(P\) for its profile.

First we ensure that \(c\) has positive first-preference mass without changing whether it is a possible winner.

Choose a power of two \(D>N(N+1)\), and set
\(K=\lfloor(D-1)/N\rfloor\) and \(s=D-KN\).
Then \(1\leq s\leq N<K\). Construct an integer-weighted profile \(Q\) by multiplying every ballot weight in \(P\) by \(K\), and adding weight \(s\) to an arbitrary complete ranking that starts with \(c\). Its total weight is \(D\).

Lemma 1. Candidate \(c\) is a PUT-STV winner of \(P\) if and only if it is a PUT-STV winner of \(Q\).

Proof. Fix a hopeful set \(H\subseteq A\) containing \(c\). Let \(p_a(H)\) be the integer tally of \(a\) in \(P\). The corresponding tally in \(Q\) is
\(Kp_a(H)+s\,\mathbf 1_{\{a=c\}}\).

For any \(e\in H\setminus\{c\}\), being a minimum-tally candidate in \(P\) implies being one in \(Q\): all comparisons with other candidates are multiplied by \(K\), while \(c\)'s tally receives an additional positive amount.

Conversely, if \(e\neq c\) is a minimum-tally candidate in \(Q\), comparisons with candidates other than \(c\) give \(p_e(H)\leq p_a(H)\). Its comparison with \(c\) gives
\(Kp_e(H)\leq Kp_c(H)+s\).
Since the tallies are integers and \(s<K\), this implies \(p_e(H)\leq p_c(H)\).

Thus the permitted eliminations that preserve \(c\) are identical in the two profiles, at every hopeful set containing \(c\). Their complete elimination sequences ending in \(c\) are consequently identical. \(\square\)

2. Construction of the continuous instance

Introduce one additional candidate \(d\), assigning it the first position in the prescribed candidate order. This is possible by assigning candidate names to the fixed ordered positions.

Set \(m=1\), so \(q=1/2\). For every ranking in \(Q\), insert \(d\) immediately after \(c\). Give the resulting ranking its weight in \(Q\), divided by \(2D\). Finally give the singleton ranking \((d)\) mass \(1/2\). Call this distribution \(\mu\).

All masses are dyadic because \(D\) is a power of two. Let \(\gamma\) denote the mass of the transformed rankings that start with \(c\). The added ranking ensures
\(\gamma\geq s/(2D)\geq1/(2D)>0\).

Initially \(d\)'s tally is \(1/2\). Every other candidate has tally at most \(1/2\). If another candidate also reaches \(1/2\), \(d\) wins the seating tie. Therefore
\(W(\mu)=\{d\}\).

We will prove the following gap:

  • If \(c\) is a PUT-STV winner of \(Q\), then \(\rho(\mu)=0\).
  • Otherwise, \(\rho(\mu)\geq1/(8D)\).

The reduction outputs the strictly positive threshold \(b=1/(16D)\).

3. Bounds valid for arbitrary perturbations

For a nonempty hopeful set \(H\), define \(t_a(H,\eta)\) to be the mass of rankings whose highest-ranked member of \(H\) is \(a\). These are the actual tallies before the first seating when \(m=1\), since eliminations do not scale weights.

If \(\delta=\tfrac12\sum_{r\in R}|\mu(r)-\nu(r)|\), then, for every \(H\) and \(a\in H\),
\(|t_a(H,\nu)-t_a(H,\mu)|\leq\delta\).
Indeed, each tally is the probability of a fixed subset of rankings, and differences of event probabilities are bounded by total variation.

Suppose \(c,d\in H\). In the constructed distribution:

  • \(t_d(H,\mu)=1/2\). Every transformed \(Q\)-ranking contains hopeful \(c\) before \(d\), so none contributes to \(d\).
  • The original candidates' tallies sum to \(1/2\).
  • \(t_c(H,\mu)\geq\gamma\), hence \(t_a(H,\mu)\leq1/2-\gamma\) for every \(a\in H\setminus\{c,d\}\).
  • Every original candidate's tally is an integer multiple of \(g=1/(2D)\).

Now assume \(\delta<\delta_0=1/(8D)\).

No candidate outside \(\{c,d\}\) can reach quota while \(c,d\) remain hopeful. Its tally is at most \(1/2-\gamma+\delta<1/2\).

Candidate \(d\) cannot be eliminated while at least two original candidates remain. Some original candidate has baseline tally at most \(1/4\). Its perturbed tally is at most \(1/4+\delta\), whereas \(d\)'s is at least \(1/2-\delta\). The latter is strictly larger because \(\delta<1/8\).

Eliminating \(c\) forces \(d\) to win in the next round. At least mass \(\gamma\) is assigned to \(c\) in the baseline distribution, and every such ranking places \(d\) immediately after \(c\). After removing \(c\), \(d\)'s baseline tally is therefore at least \(1/2+\gamma\); its perturbed tally exceeds \(1/2+\gamma-\delta>1/2\). No other candidate can simultaneously have tally at least \(1/2\), since total current weight is at most one.

These statements hold for arbitrary real perturbations on all of \(R\), including new, truncated, and empty rankings.

4. Proof of the gap

A possible source winner gives radius zero

Suppose \(Q\) has a permitted elimination sequence
\(e_1,\ldots,e_{k-1}\)
ending in \(c\).

Define a distribution \(\sigma\) supported on singleton rankings by
\(\sigma((c))=3/4\) and
\(\sigma((e_i))=i/[2k(k-1)]\).
These masses sum to one. They strictly increase along the chosen elimination sequence, with \(c\)'s mass larger than all the others.

For \(0<\varepsilon<1/(8D)\), put
\(\nu_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon\sigma\).
Then \(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu_\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon\).

Consider a round along the chosen sequence while \(c,d\) remain hopeful. Every hopeful original candidate \(a\) has tally
\((1-\varepsilon)t_a(H,\mu)+\varepsilon\sigma((a))\).
The tally of \(d\) is \((1-\varepsilon)/2<1/2\).

At the next prescribed elimination \(e_i\):

  • If another hopeful original candidate has the same baseline tally, its singleton mass is strictly larger, so its perturbed tally is strictly larger.
  • If it has a larger baseline tally, the difference is at least \(g=1/(2D)\). Its perturbed tally difference is at least \((1-\varepsilon)g-\varepsilon>0\), because \(\varepsilon<g/4\).

Thus \(e_i\) is the unique minimum among original candidates. The bounds from Section 3 ensure that \(d\) is not a minimum while at least two original candidates remain, and that no original candidate other than \(c\) reaches quota.

Consequently, either \(c\) seats earlier, or the chosen eliminations proceed until only \(c,d\) remain. In the latter case every transformed \(Q\)-ranking is assigned to \(c\), giving it tally
\((1-\varepsilon)/2+3\varepsilon/4=1/2+\varepsilon/4>q\).
Hence \(W(\nu_\varepsilon)=\{c\}\).

Since \(\varepsilon\) can be arbitrarily small, \(\rho(\mu)=0\). This uses an infimum; it does not assert attainment.

A sufficiently close changed winner gives a source winner

Suppose \(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)<1/(8D)\) and \(W(\nu)\neq\{d\}\).

Section 3 shows that \(c\) cannot be eliminated, and that no candidate other than \(c,d\) can seat while both remain hopeful. Also, \(d\) cannot be eliminated until only one original candidate remains. Therefore the changed winner must be \(c\).

Consider any original candidate \(e\neq c\) eliminated before \(c\) wins. For every other hopeful original candidate \(a\), its actual elimination implies
\(t_e(H,\nu)\leq t_a(H,\nu)\).
The total-variation bound gives
\(t_e(H,\mu)\leq t_a(H,\mu)+2\delta\).

Both baseline tallies are multiples of \(g=1/(2D)\), and \(2\delta<g\). Therefore
\(t_e(H,\mu)\leq t_a(H,\mu)\).

While \(c,d\) remain hopeful, these baseline original tallies are exactly the corresponding tallies in \(Q\), divided by \(2D\). Thus every such elimination is a permitted minimum-tally elimination in \(Q\).

There are two ways the perturbed election can finish with \(c\).

  • Candidate \(d\) is eliminated. By Section 3, \(c\) was already the only remaining original candidate. The preceding eliminations therefore form a complete PUT-STV sequence in \(Q\) ending in \(c\).
  • Candidate \(c\) reaches quota while \(d\) remains hopeful. Then \(t_c(H,\mu)\geq1/2-\delta\). This tally is at most \(1/2\) and lies on the grid of spacing \(g>\delta\), so it equals \(1/2\). Every other hopeful original candidate consequently has baseline tally zero. Eliminate those candidates in any order. Their zero tallies remain zero: no baseline ballot is assigned to them, so their elimination transfers no baseline weight. This extends the preceding eliminations to a PUT-STV sequence in \(Q\) ending in \(c\).

Thus any changed winner within distance \(1/(8D)\) implies that \(c\) is a PUT-STV winner of \(Q\). Contrapositively, a no-instance has \(\rho(\mu)\geq1/(8D)\). The gap is proved.

5. Reduction size and hardness conclusion

Choose \(D\) to be the least power of two exceeding \(N(N+1)\). Then \(D=O(N^2)\), and its binary length is \(O(\log(N+1))\).

The constructed instance has:

  • \(C=k+1\) and \(m=1\);
  • support size \(\tau\leq\tau_P+2\leq N+2\), after merging identical rankings;
  • masses and threshold with \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits;
  • total input length \(L=O((N+2)(k\log(k+1)+\log(N+1)))\).

The construction takes polynomial time in the source input length and never enumerates \(R\). Lemma 1 and the gap establish
\(\rho(\mu)\leq1/(16D)\)
if and only if the source instance is a yes-instance.

Therefore the requested problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even for \(m=1\), dyadic masses, and strictly positive \(b\). Unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), it has no algorithm polynomial in \(C,m,\tau,L\).

6. Exact complexity for one seat

For completeness, the one-seat case admits an exact algorithm handling the infimum explicitly.

A trace specifies an ordered sequence of eliminations and a terminal winner. For a fixed trace, all hopeful sets are known. Before its terminal seating, weights are unscaled, so all tallies are linear functions of the distribution variables \(x_r\), for \(r\in R\).

The conditions for realizing the trace are linear:

  • Every elimination round requires all tallies to be strictly below \(1/2\).
  • The eliminated candidate must have minimum tally, with strict comparisons against candidates that would beat it in an elimination tie.
  • If more than one candidate remains at termination, the winner must have tally at least \(1/2\) and beat every other hopeful candidate under the prescribed tally and tie order.
  • If only one remains, it wins without further conditions.

Thus each trace defines a bounded region with weak and strict rational linear inequalities.

First test whether that region is nonempty. This is a rational linear program: introduce a common slack \(z\in[0,1]\) into every strict inequality and maximize \(z\). If strict inequalities occur, the region is nonempty exactly when the optimum is positive. With none, ordinary feasibility suffices.

For a nonempty trace region, replacing all strict inequalities by weak ones gives its closure. To verify this, interpolate any point of the weak relaxation with a point satisfying the original strict inequalities. Every positive interpolation amount satisfies the strict inequalities and converges to the chosen relaxed point.

Let the input support be \(r_1,\ldots,r_\tau\), with masses \(\mu_i\). Introduce variables \(h_i\) satisfying
\(0\leq h_i\leq\mu_i\) and \(h_i\leq x_{r_i}\).
Since both distributions have mass one,
\(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,x)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\min\{\mu_i,x_{r_i}\}\).
Minimizing \(1-\sum_i h_i\) over the trace closure therefore gives the exact infimum distance to that trace.

There are finitely many traces. Taking the minimum over the nonempty traces whose winner differs from \(W(\mu)\) gives exactly \(\rho(\mu)\).

The number of rankings is
\(|R|=\sum_{j=0}^{C}C!/(C-j)!<eC!\),
and the number of traces is also less than \(eC!\). Each linear program has polynomial size in \(|R|,C,\tau,L\). Hence this algorithm runs in
\(2^{O(C\log C)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) time.

Finally, this argument gives membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). A certificate consists of:

  • a trace with a different winner;
  • a rational point satisfying its original strict conditions;
  • a rational point in its weak relaxation with distance objective at most \(b\).

Polynomial-size certificates exist. Apart from coordinate nonnegativity, these linear programs have only \(O(C^2+\tau)\) constraints. A vertex therefore has at most \(O(C^2+\tau)\) nonzero ranking masses. Its rational entries have polynomial bit length by Cramer's rule applied to the corresponding polynomial-size basis matrix. An optimal positive-slack vertex supplies the second item, and an optimal closure vertex supplies the third. Each ranking has length at most \(C\), so the complete certificate is polynomial in \(C,\tau,L\), and verification is polynomial as well.

The two-point certificate is essential: the closure point need not itself change the winner, but the strict point proves that it is approachable within the specified trace. This accounts for nonattainment.

Together with the reduction, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for \(m=1\). The unrestricted WIGM problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; no membership claim for its nonlinear multi-seat case is needed for the requested classification.
