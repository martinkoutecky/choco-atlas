Theorem 4.1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), partitioned into \(k\) equal-mass groups, a population scale \(H\gg\tau\), and small-good classes \(g\) with mass supply \(\lambda_g\) and additive per-copy values \(v_t(g)\) satisfying \(\sum_g H\lambda_g v_t(g)\le 1+\varepsilon\) for every type, choose masses \(y_{t,z,r}\) over types, integral small-good bundles \(z\), and large-good status \(r\in\{0,1\}\). Require type-mass conservation, aggregate use at most \(\lambda_g\) of each small-good class, and group-specific large-good supply \(2/(3k)\) valued at \(1/\varepsilon\) by eligible types. Distinguish whether there is a feasible allocation with mass \(2/3\) receiving large goods and every person without one receiving utility at least \(3(1-\varepsilon)/H\), or whether every such allocation has \(H\sum_{t,z} y_{t,z,0}\sum_g v_t(g)z_g\le 65/81+3\varepsilon\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a rational constant \(0<\varepsilon<4/81\). A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Each type belongs to one specified group \(T_j\), where \(k\ge1\) and \(\sum_{t\in T_j}\mu_t=1/k\).
The input includes a binary-encoded integer population scale \(H\ge100\tau\), divisible by \(3k\), and \(m\ge1\) small-good classes with positive binary-rational supplies \(\lambda_g\), integral inventories \(H\lambda_g\), and nonnegative binary-rational additive per-copy values \(v_t(g)\), satisfying \(\sum_g H\lambda_g v_t(g)\le1+\varepsilon\) for every type. All non-large goods, including any dummy goods, belong to these classes.
Each group has large-good mass supply \(2/(3k)\), valued at \(1/\varepsilon\) by its types and zero by other types. A person may receive at most one large good, from their own group, and may also receive small goods. A small-good bundle is \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\) with \(z_g\le H\lambda_g\); write \(u_t(z)=\sum_g v_t(g)z_g\).
An allocation consists of nonnegative real masses \(y_{t,z,r}\), where \(r\in\{0,1\}\) indicates large-good receipt, satisfying \(\sum_{z,r}y_{t,z,r}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,z,r}y_{t,z,r}z_g\le\lambda_g\), and \(\sum_{t\in T_j,z}y_{t,z,1}\le2/(3k)\). Goods may remain unused; type masses may split arbitrarily, without requiring \(Hy_{t,z,r}\) to be integral.
Let \(\mathcal F\) be these allocations with \(\sum_{t,z}y_{t,z,1}=2/3\). The input is promised to satisfy either YES: some \(y\in\mathcal F\) has \(u_t(z)\ge3(1-\varepsilon)/H\) whenever \(y_{t,z,0}>0\); or NO: every \(y\in\mathcal F\) satisfies \(H\sum_{t,z}y_{t,z,0}u_t(z)\le65/81+3\varepsilon\).

Is distinguishing YES from NO solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,k,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length, including the encoding of \(H\), for each fixed \(\varepsilon\).

The finite-agent counterpart is NP-hard to distinguish under the Unique Games Conjecture, with the same utility thresholds and group-specific large goods. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27098v2#S4)
No complexity classification for exactly this continuous promise problem was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the fixed rational epsilon to 0<epsilon<4/81 so that the YES and NO promises are disjoint; the stated interval (0,1) otherwise permits overlap.
  • Made the informal scale condition H much greater than tau precise as H>=100 tau, with H binary encoded; required divisibility by 3k to preserve integral finite-population large-good counts.
  • Interpreted H lambda_g as the number of available indivisible copies in class g, required these inventories to be integral, and bounded each person's bundle by the available inventory.
  • Specified nonnegative rational per-copy values and positive rational supplies, following the additive nonnegative valuation domain.
  • Made group membership part of each type, restricted large-good receipt to the recipient's group, and allowed small goods alongside a large good.
  • Included dummy goods among the non-large classes, retained permission to leave goods unused, and specified arbitrary real allocation masses without population-count integrality.
  • Interpreted the NO quantifier as ranging over all feasible allocations with large-good recipient mass 2/3, without imposing the YES utility threshold.

Result

The continuous promise problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time. For each fixed \(\varepsilon\), the running time is
\[ (m\tau+\tau+m+k+L)^{O(1)} \]
bit operations. In particular, the algorithm has no polynomial dependence on the numerical value of \(H\).

The key is that the maximum welfare appearing in the NO condition admits an exact polynomial-size linear program.

1. Large-good constraints determine each group’s remaining mass

Write \(N_g=H\lambda_g\), which is a positive integer by hypothesis. Define
\[ W(y)=H\sum_{t,z}y_{t,z,0}u_t(z). \]

For \(y\in\mathcal F\), let
\[ b_j=\sum_{t\in T_j,z}y_{t,z,1}. \]
Each \(b_j\le 2/(3k)\), while \(\sum_jb_j=2/3\). The upper bounds also sum to \(2/3\), so every one is attained:
\[ b_j=\frac{2}{3k}. \]
Since group \(j\) has total mass \(1/k\), its mass receiving no large good is exactly \(1/(3k)\).

2. An exact linear program for maximum welfare

Introduce real variables \(a_t\) and \(x_{tg}\). Their intended meanings are the mass of type \(t\) receiving no large good and the total quantity of class \(g\) allocated to that mass.

Consider the following linear program:
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize}\quad& H\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{g=1}^{m}v_t(g)x_{tg}\\ \text{subject to}\quad& 0\le a_t\le\mu_t &&\text{for every }t,\\ &\sum_{t\in T_j}a_t=\frac{1}{3k} &&\text{for every }j,\\ &0\le x_{tg}\le N_ga_t &&\text{for every }t,g,\\ &\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{tg}\le\lambda_g &&\text{for every }g. \end{aligned} \]
Denote its optimum by \(W^*\). It is feasible: take \(a_t=\mu_t/3\) and \(x_{tg}=0\). Its feasible set is closed and bounded, so its optimum exists.

Claim. \(W^*=\max_{y\in\mathcal F}W(y)\).

Every allocation yields a feasible LP point

Given \(y\in\mathcal F\), set
\[ a_t=\sum_z y_{t,z,0}, \qquad x_{tg}=\sum_z y_{t,z,0}z_g. \]
The first two LP constraints follow from type-mass conservation and Section 1. The bundle bound \(z_g\le N_g\) gives \(x_{tg}\le N_ga_t\). Finally,
\[ \sum_t x_{tg} \le \sum_{t,z,r}y_{t,z,r}z_g \le\lambda_g. \]
Additivity gives equality of objectives:
\[ W(y)=H\sum_{t,g}v_t(g)x_{tg}. \]

Every feasible LP point yields an allocation

Fix a feasible LP point \((a,x)\). For a type with \(a_t>0\), define
\[ p_{tg}=\frac{x_{tg}}{a_tN_g}\in[0,1]. \]
Let \(U\) be uniform on \([0,1]\), and define an integer bundle by
\[ z_g(U)= \begin{cases} N_g,&U<p_{tg},\\ 0,&U\ge p_{tg}. \end{cases} \]
Every such bundle satisfies the prescribed inventory bounds, and
\[ \mathbb E[z_g(U)]=N_gp_{tg}=\frac{x_{tg}}{a_t}. \]

This distribution uses at most \(m+1\) bundles: its bundle changes only at the at most \(m\) thresholds \(p_{tg}\). Allocate mass \(a_t\) according to this distribution, with \(r=0\). Allocate the remaining mass \(\mu_t-a_t\) the empty small-good bundle with \(r=1\).

If \(a_t=0\), the LP forces \(x_{tg}=0\); allocate all type-\(t\) mass the empty bundle with \(r=1\).

These assignments conserve each type’s mass. Small-good usage in class \(g\) is exactly \(\sum_t x_{tg}\le\lambda_g\). Large-good usage in group \(j\) is
\[ \sum_{t\in T_j}(\mu_t-a_t) =\frac1k-\frac1{3k} =\frac2{3k}. \]
Thus the constructed allocation belongs to \(\mathcal F\), and its welfare equals the LP objective. This proves the claim.

3. The promise gap gives a decision algorithm

In a YES instance, the promised allocation has total mass \(1/3\) receiving no large good, each with utility at least \(3(1-\varepsilon)/H\). Therefore
\[ W^*\ge H\cdot\frac13\cdot\frac{3(1-\varepsilon)}H =1-\varepsilon. \]

In a NO instance, the exact LP characterization gives
\[ W^*\le\frac{65}{81}+3\varepsilon. \]
The thresholds are strictly separated because
\[ (1-\varepsilon)-\left(\frac{65}{81}+3\varepsilon\right) =\frac{16}{81}-4\varepsilon>0. \]

Their midpoint is \(c=73/81+\varepsilon\). The algorithm tests feasibility of the LP constraints together with
\[ H\sum_{t,g}v_t(g)x_{tg}\ge c. \]
It outputs YES if feasible and NO otherwise. In a promised YES instance this system is feasible; in a promised NO instance it is infeasible. Hence the algorithm correctly distinguishes the two cases.

This argument uses the promise: feasibility of this welfare threshold need not establish the pointwise YES condition on an arbitrary unpromised input.

4. Bit complexity and the finite-agent distinction

The feasibility system has \(\tau(m+1)\) variables and \(O(\tau m+\tau+m+k)\) constraints. Every coefficient is rational. Computing \(N_g=H\lambda_g\) and the objective coefficients \(Hv_t(g)\) requires polynomial-time binary arithmetic, and their bit lengths are \(O(L)\) for fixed \(\varepsilon\).

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming decides feasibility in time polynomial in the number of variables, constraints, and their total binary encoding length. All its hypotheses hold here. Consequently, the running time is
\[ (m\tau+\tau+m+k+L)^{O(1)}. \]
No enumeration of inventories or integer bundles is required.

The cited finite-agent theorem concerns allocations to indivisible agents. Its hardness statement therefore does not supply a hardness result for arbitrary splitting of type masses. [Viswanathan, Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27098v2#S4) Here, that splitting permits the exact realization of every feasible LP point proved above, which yields the polynomial-time classification.
