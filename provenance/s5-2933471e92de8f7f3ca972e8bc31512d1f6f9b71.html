Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given three good categories, a finite support of complete types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), incomes \(b_t\), and rankings over bundles \(S\subseteq\{1,2,3\}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\), decide whether a CE exists and output one if it does. Find positive prices \(p_j\) and nonnegative assignment masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\) such that \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}\lambda_{t,S}=q_j\), and positive assignment to \(S\) is allowed only when \(S\) is a most-preferred bundle of type \(t\) among those affordable at prices \(p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=3\) good categories. Each type specifies a positive binary rational income \(b_t\) and an explicit complete, transitive weak ranking \(\succeq_t\) over all bundles \(S\subseteq\{1,2,3\}\), satisfying \(S\supseteq R\Rightarrow S\succeq_t R\). Ties are allowed.
Each individual receives at most one unit of each category. Supplies are binary rationals \(q_j\in[0,1]\) per unit of population; all supply must be allocated.
A competitive equilibrium consists of prices \(p_j>0\) and assignment masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\geq0\), with \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}\lambda_{t,S}=q_j\).
Write \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\). If \(\lambda_{t,S}>0\), require \(p(S)\leq b_t\) and \(S\succeq_t R\) for every \(R\) with \(p(R)\leq b_t\). Any tied optimum may receive mass; affordability applies to each assigned bundle, not to average expenditure.
Incomes need not be exhausted or distinct. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is deciding whether such a competitive equilibrium exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can an exact equilibrium also be computed whenever one exists, with running time polynomial in \(m=3\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?

For three discrete goods and monotonically increasing preferences, a CE exists and can be constructed for almost all positive income vectors. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.04212v6#S5)
No result settling the complexity of this exact mass-based problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to one, consistent with a distribution over types and per-capita supplies.
  • Required positive rational incomes and complete, transitive, weakly monotonically increasing bundle rankings, following the discrete goods domain.
  • Allowed indifference and arbitrary splitting among tied affordable optima, without imposed tie-breaking.
  • Interpreted bundles as containing at most one unit per category and imposed affordability on each assigned bundle.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Whenever an equilibrium exists, an exact rational equilibrium can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), with \(m=3\) fixed.

The proof enumerates polynomially many affordability patterns, including patterns on price boundaries, and solves one rational allocation feasibility problem for each pattern. It applies to all the stated inputs, including tied preferences, equal incomes, and zero supplies.

1. Affordability patterns determine demand

Let \(\mathcal B=2^{\{1,2,3\}}\) be the eight bundles. For every type \(t\) and nonempty bundle \(S\), define the affine function

\(\displaystyle f_{t,S}(p)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j-b_t.\)

There are \(N=7\tau\) such functions. Fix an ordering \(f_1,\ldots,f_N\), retaining duplicates if necessary.

A positive price vector determines a sign vector \(\sigma\in\{-,0,+\}^N\). The associated region is

\(\displaystyle C_\sigma=\{p\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3:\operatorname{sign}(f_i(p))=\sigma_i\text{ for every }i\}.\)

For every nonempty bundle, affordability means that its sign is either \(-\) or \(0\). The empty bundle is always affordable because incomes are positive. Consequently, throughout a nonempty region \(C_\sigma\), each type has the same affordable set \(A_t(\sigma)\).

Define its demanded bundles by

\(\displaystyle D_t(\sigma)=\{S\in A_t(\sigma):S\succeq_t R\text{ for every }R\in A_t(\sigma)\}.\)

Because the ranking is complete and transitive and \(A_t(\sigma)\) is finite and nonempty, \(D_t(\sigma)\) is nonempty. It can be computed directly from the explicitly supplied ranking.

Thus each type’s demand correspondence is constant throughout every sign region, including regions where bundle costs equal incomes.

2. There are only polynomially many sign regions

We prove a bound that includes regions of every dimension.

First, an arrangement of \(n\) affine hyperplanes in a space of dimension \(d\) has at most

\(\displaystyle R(n,d)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom ni\)

full-dimensional chambers. To justify this, adding the last hyperplane leaves old chambers intact or splits them in two. The number split is at most the number of chambers induced on that hyperplane by the previous hyperplanes. Hence

\(\displaystyle R(n,d)\leq R(n-1,d)+R(n-1,d-1).\)

Together with \(R(0,d)=1\) and \(R(n,0)=1\), induction and Pascal’s identity give the bound. Coincident or parallel hyperplanes can only decrease the number of splits.

Now consider any realizable sign vector \(\sigma\), initially allowing prices anywhere in \(\mathbb R^3\). Let \(Z\) be its set of zero coordinates, and let \(k\leq3\) be the rank of the normals of those hyperplanes.

Select \(k\) hyperplanes in \(Z\) whose normals form a basis. Their common intersection \(E\) is exactly the common intersection of all hyperplanes in \(Z\), and has dimension \(3-k\). Indeed, every other zero normal is a linear combination of the selected normals; because these equations have a common solution, their constant terms satisfy the same linear combination.

Fix a deterministic rule for selecting this basis. There are at most \(\binom Nk\) possible selected subsets. For any such subset, restrict the remaining hyperplanes to \(E\). Hyperplanes containing \(E\) have forced zero signs; hyperplanes disjoint from \(E\) have forced nonzero signs. The other restrictions form an arrangement in dimension \(3-k\).

A sign region with equality flat \(E\) lies in a chamber of this restricted arrangement. Distinct sign vectors give distinct chambers. Therefore the total number of realizable sign vectors is at most

\(\displaystyle F(N)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{3}\binom Nk\sum_{i=0}^{3-k}\binom Ni=O((N+1)^3).\)

Restricting to positive prices cannot increase this count. The bound permits duplicate hyperplanes and all degeneracies.

3. Exact feasibility and enumeration of price regions

A harmless price bound

Put \(M=1+\max_t b_t\). Every realizable sign vector has a realization with

\(\displaystyle 0<p_j\leq M\quad(j=1,2,3).\)

To prove this, start with any positive realization \(p\) and replace each coordinate by \(p'_j=\min\{p_j,M\}\).

A bundle containing a changed coordinate costs at least \(M>\max_t b_t\) after the change and was also unaffordable for every type before the change. Bundles containing no changed coordinate retain their costs. Thus every sign is preserved.

Testing a prescribed sign vector

For a prescribed sign vector on the first \(n\) affine functions, solve the following rational linear program in the four variables \(p_1,p_2,p_3,\varepsilon\):

  • Maximize \(\varepsilon\).
  • Impose \(0\leq\varepsilon\leq1\) and \(\varepsilon\leq p_j\leq M\) for each \(j\).
  • Impose \(f_i(p)\leq-\varepsilon\) whenever \(\sigma_i=-\).
  • Impose \(f_i(p)=0\) whenever \(\sigma_i=0\).
  • Impose \(f_i(p)\geq\varepsilon\) whenever \(\sigma_i=+\).

The prescribed sign region is nonempty exactly when this program is feasible and its optimal value is positive.

For the forward implication, take a bounded positive realization. Each required strict inequality has a positive margin. The minimum of these finitely many margins, the three prices, and \(1\) is positive and supplies a feasible \(\varepsilon\).

For the reverse implication, any feasible solution with \(\varepsilon>0\) has positive prices and exactly the prescribed signs.

The feasible set is closed and bounded, so a nonempty feasible set has an attained optimum. We use the standard exact rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and bounded optimization with rational input can be solved in polynomial bit time, returning an exact rational solution of polynomial encoding length. This is the polynomial-time linear-programming theorem originating with [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319). All its hypotheses hold here.

Enumerating all realizable patterns

Begin with the empty sign vector. At step \(n\), extend each retained prefix by each of the three possible signs for \(f_n\), and retain precisely the extensions passing the preceding feasibility test.

Inductively, the retained list consists of exactly the realizable prefixes: every realizable extension has a realizable parent, and the test retains exactly the nonempty extensions.

At each step, Section 2 bounds the number retained by \(O((N+1)^3)\). There are \(N\) steps and three tests per retained prefix. Thus enumeration uses \(O((N+1)^4)\) linear-programming calls. Each call has four variables, \(O(N)\) constraints, and rational input of encoding length polynomial in \(L\).

The enumeration therefore runs in polynomial time. It also returns an exact rational representative price vector for every retained full sign pattern.

4. Exact market clearing within a region

Fix a retained sign pattern \(\sigma\). Introduce nonnegative variables \(\lambda_{t,S}\) only for \(S\in D_t(\sigma)\), and solve the rational feasibility system

\(\displaystyle \sum_{S\in D_t(\sigma)}\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\quad\text{for every type }t,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{\substack{S\in D_t(\sigma)\\j\in S}}\lambda_{t,S}=q_j\quad\text{for }j=1,2,3,\)

\(\displaystyle \lambda_{t,S}\geq0.\)

There are at most \(8\tau\) variables and \(\tau+3\) equality constraints. All coefficients and right-hand sides are rational and have polynomial total encoding length. Exact rational linear programming therefore decides feasibility and, when feasible, returns an exact rational allocation in polynomial time.

This system expresses individual affordability correctly: every bundle receiving positive mass belongs to \(D_t(\sigma)\), so that bundle itself is affordable and optimal. No average-expenditure condition is substituted for individual affordability.

Moreover, every variable is bounded above by its type mass \(\mu_t\). Thus the feasible allocation set is bounded, and the returned allocation has polynomial encoding length.

5. Correctness and total running time

The complete algorithm enumerates all retained sign patterns and tests the allocation system for each one. It returns the first feasible allocation together with that pattern’s representative prices, or reports nonexistence if none is feasible.

Soundness. Suppose the algorithm returns \((p,\lambda)\). The allocation equations assign each type its full mass and exhaust every supply. Every bundle receiving positive mass is in \(D_t(\sigma)\), hence is affordable and weakly preferred to every affordable bundle at the returned prices. The price feasibility test guarantees \(p_j>0\). Therefore \((p,\lambda)\) is an equilibrium.

Completeness. Suppose an equilibrium \((p^*,\lambda^*)\) exists, allowing initially arbitrary real prices and masses. Its sign pattern \(\sigma^*\) is retained by the enumeration. Every positively assigned bundle belongs to \(D_t(\sigma^*)\), so \(\lambda^*\) proves feasibility of that pattern’s allocation system. Exact rational linear programming therefore returns a rational allocation. Pairing it with the rational representative of \(\sigma^*\) preserves all affordable sets and demanded bundles, and consequently gives an exact rational equilibrium.

There are \(O((N+1)^3)\) final allocation tests, and the price enumeration uses \(O((N+1)^4)\) tests. Every test has polynomial encoding length and polynomial bit complexity. Since \(N=7\tau\), the total running time and output length are polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\) for \(m=3\).

The proof does not require monotonicity; complete, transitive rankings over the eight bundles suffice.

6. Relation to the cited existence theorem

The cited paper studies allocations of individual indivisible items among finitely many agents, and its three-good theorem establishes existence for almost all income vectors in that setting. [Source, Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.04212v6#S5)

Existence is not automatic in the present mass-based model, even with strictly increasing preferences and positive supplies. For example, take one type with mass \(1\), any positive income, preferences ordered by bundle cardinality, and supplies \(q_1=q_2=q_3=1/6\).

At any prices, all demanded bundles have one common cardinality \(k\in\{0,1,2,3\}\): they attain the maximum affordable cardinality. Any allocation supported on those bundles therefore satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^3q_j=\sum_S|S|\lambda_S=k\sum_S\lambda_S=k.\)

But the specified supplies sum to \(1/2\), a contradiction. Thus this instance has no equilibrium. The algorithm above decides such failures as well as constructing equilibria when they exist.
