The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates at rational positions \(x_c\), a target \(c^\star\), a movement amount \(\delta\), and voter types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), positions \(x_t\), and directed contact intensities \(\lambda_{tu}\), choose seed masses \(s_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t s_t\le\beta\). Define limiting activation as the least fixed point reached from \(s\) of \(a_u=\mu_u-(\mu_u-s_u)\exp(-\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_{tu}a_t)\). Activated mass of type \(t\) moves toward \(x_{c^\star}\) by at most \(\delta\); compute the resulting plurality shares \(q_c(s)\) and return feasible seed masses whose increase in margin, \(q_{c^\star}(s)-\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c(s)-(q_{c^\star}^0-\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c^0)\), is within \(\varepsilon\) of its supremum.

Result

The requested guarantee is achievable by a randomized polynomial-time algorithm. In fact, the algorithm below returns feasible rational seeds satisfying

\(\displaystyle D(s)+X(s)\ge \frac34(1-e^{-1})\,\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon\)

with probability at least \(\frac23\). Since \(\frac34(1-e^{-1})>\alpha\), this settles the stated approximation question.

The proof has three ingredients: diminishing returns of the activation map, polynomial-time fixed-point evaluation after positive seed padding, and a simultaneous continuous greedy procedure for all rivals.

1. The rival-gain correction gives a monotone surrogate

First, moving toward the target cannot make an existing target supporter abandon it. Indeed, the target’s nearest-candidate region is an interval, with endpoints included or excluded according to the fixed tie order. If this region is nonempty, it contains \(x_{c^\star}\); consequently, it contains the segment between any of its points and \(x_{c^\star}\). This also handles coincident candidate positions: a target excluded everywhere by an earlier coincident candidate has no supporters to lose.

Define the target gain and each rival’s losses and arrivals by

\(\displaystyle G(s)=\sum_{t:b(x_t)\ne c^\star,\ b(y_t)=c^\star}a_t(s), \)

\(\displaystyle L_c(s)=\sum_{t:b(x_t)=c,\ b(y_t)\ne c}a_t(s), \qquad I_c(s)=\sum_{t:b(x_t)\ne c,\ b(y_t)=c}a_t(s). \)

Thus \(q_{c^\star}(s)=q_{c^\star}^0+G(s)\), \(q_c(s)=q_c^0-L_c(s)+I_c(s)\), and \(X(s)=\max_{c\ne c^\star}I_c(s)\).

Write

\(\displaystyle R_0=\max_{c\ne c^\star}q_c^0,\qquad d_c=R_0-q_c^0\ge0,\qquad F_c(s)=G(s)+L_c(s). \)

Consider

\(\displaystyle H(s)=G(s)+R_0-\max_{c\ne c^\star}\bigl(q_c^0-L_c(s)\bigr) =\min_{c\ne c^\star}\bigl(F_c(s)+d_c\bigr). \)

Because rival arrivals are nonnegative,

\(\displaystyle D(s)\le H(s).\)

Also, \(I_c(s)\le X(s)\) for every rival, so

\(\displaystyle \max_{c\ne c^\star}\bigl(q_c^0-L_c(s)+I_c(s)\bigr) \le \max_{c\ne c^\star}\bigl(q_c^0-L_c(s)\bigr)+X(s). \)

Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \boxed{D(s)+X(s)\ge H(s)\ge D(s).}\)

Each \(F_c\) is a weighted activation objective:

\(\displaystyle F_c(s)=\sum_t w_{ct}a_t(s),\qquad w_{ct}\in\{0,1,2\}.\)

In particular, \(0\le F_c(s)\le2\). These weights and the constants \(d_c\) are computable exactly from the rational positions and the tie order.

2. Activation has diminishing returns

For vectors, inequalities below are coordinatewise. A function \(f\) has diminishing returns if, whenever \(u\le v\) and the indicated increments are within its domain,

\(\displaystyle f(u+h e_j)-f(u)\ge f(v+h e_j)-f(v) \quad\text{for every }h\ge0. \)

We prove this property for every activation coordinate and every nonnegative weighted activation objective.

Let

\(\displaystyle \Phi_u(s,a)=\mu_u-(\mu_u-s_u) \exp\!\left(-\sum_t\lambda_{tu}a_t\right). \)

On the product box \(0\le s,a\le\mu\), all first partial derivatives of \(\Phi_u\) are nonnegative, and all second partial derivatives are nonpositive. More explicitly, putting \(h_u=\sum_t\lambda_{tu}a_t\),

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial\Phi_u}{\partial s_u}=e^{-h_u}, \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi_u}{\partial a_t} =(\mu_u-s_u)\lambda_{tu}e^{-h_u}, \)

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\Phi_u}{\partial a_t\partial a_v} =-(\mu_u-s_u)\lambda_{tu}\lambda_{vu}e^{-h_u}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\Phi_u}{\partial s_u\partial a_t} =-\lambda_{tu}e^{-h_u}. \)

The remaining second derivatives involving seed coordinates are zero.

Starting with \(a^{(0)}(s)=s\), induction and the second-derivative chain rule show that every \(a_u^{(k)}(s)\) has nonnegative first partial derivatives and nonpositive second partial derivatives. Specifically, in a composition, each second derivative is a sum of:

  • a nonnegative first derivative of the outer function times a nonpositive second derivative of an inner function;
  • a nonpositive second derivative of the outer function times nonnegative first derivatives of inner functions.

Every term is therefore nonpositive.

The iteration is increasing, because \(\Phi(s,s)\ge s\), and remains bounded by \(\mu\). Its limit exists and is a fixed point by continuity of \(\Phi\). Passing to the limit in the finite-increment inequalities proves diminishing returns for \(a_u(s)\). Monotonicity passes to the limit as well.

These conclusions hold on the entire seed box, without imposing the budget constraint. Hence every \(F_c\) is monotone and has diminishing returns.

The same derivative argument gives concavity along every nonnegative line direction. Passing to the limit preserves that concavity. Since \(a(0)=0\), we obtain the useful scaling inequality

\(\displaystyle \boxed{F_c(\theta s)\ge\theta F_c(s) \quad\text{for }0\le\theta\le1.} \)

This argument does not assume continuity at zero.

3. Polynomial-time evaluation with strictly positive seeds

We next establish the numerical oracle needed by the algorithm.

Evaluation lemma. Given rational seeds with \(0<s_t\le\mu_t\) for every type, and a rational accuracy \(0<\xi<1\), all the values \(F_c(s)\) can be approximated to absolute error \(\xi\) in time polynomial in the seed and instance encoding lengths, \(m,\tau\), and \(\log(1/\xi)\).

3.1 Positive seeds make the fixed point unique

For fixed \(s>0\), the map \(a\mapsto\Phi(s,a)\) is coordinatewise monotone and coordinatewise concave. Moreover, \(\Phi(s,0)=s>0\).

Suppose \(a,b\) are fixed points and \(a\not\ge b\). Both vectors are strictly positive. Set

\(\displaystyle \gamma=\min_u\frac{a_u}{b_u}<1.\)

Then \(a\ge\gamma b\). Monotonicity and concavity imply

\(\displaystyle a=\Phi(s,a)\ge\Phi(s,\gamma b) \ge\gamma\Phi(s,b)+(1-\gamma)\Phi(s,0) >\gamma b. \)

This contradicts equality in a coordinate attaining the minimum ratio. Thus \(a\ge b\); interchanging the two fixed points proves uniqueness.

Furthermore, any vector satisfying \(0\le a\le\mu\) and \(a\le\Phi(s,a)\) lies below this unique fixed point: iterating from that vector gives an increasing bounded sequence converging to a fixed point.

3.2 A bounded convex formulation

Let \(J=\{t:s_t=\mu_t\}\). Its activation coordinates are exactly \(\mu_t\), so eliminate them. If no coordinates remain, evaluation is immediate.

For a remaining coordinate, write \(a_u=\mu_u b_u\). The condition \(a\le\Phi(s,a)\) is equivalent to

\(\displaystyle \kappa_u(b):= \log(1-b_u)-\log(1-s_u/\mu_u) +\sum_{t\notin J}\lambda_{tu}\mu_t b_t +\sum_{t\in J}\lambda_{tu}\mu_t \ge0. \)

Fix a rational cutoff \(0<\eta<\frac12\), and define the convex compact set

\(\displaystyle K_\eta=\{b:0\le b_u\le1-\eta,\ \kappa_u(b)\ge0\text{ for every remaining }u\}. \)

Convexity follows because each \(\kappa_u\) is concave.

Maximize \(\sum_{u\notin J}\mu_u b_u\) over \(K_\eta\). Every feasible activation vector lies below the true fixed point \(a(s)\). Conversely, multiplying the remaining coordinates of \(a(s)\) by \(1-\eta\), while retaining the forced coordinates in \(J\), gives a feasible point. To see this, apply concavity to the reduced fixed-point map:

\(\displaystyle \Phi_{\mathrm{red}}\bigl((1-\eta)a_{\mathrm{red}}(s)\bigr) \ge (1-\eta)a_{\mathrm{red}}(s)+\eta\Phi_{\mathrm{red}}(0) \ge (1-\eta)a_{\mathrm{red}}(s). \)

Consequently, a feasible solution whose linear objective is within \(\zeta\) of the convex optimum satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_u|a_u(s)-\bar a_u|\le\eta+\zeta. \)

Every weighted objective with weights at most \(2\) is therefore approximated within \(2(\eta+\zeta)\).

3.3 The convex program has polynomial bit complexity

Here are the conditioning and oracle details.

Put

\(\displaystyle \rho=\min_{u\notin J}s_u/\mu_u>0,\qquad b^0=(\rho/8)\mathbf1. \)

Using \(\log(1-z)\ge-2z\) for \(0\le z\le\frac12\), and \(-\log(1-z)\ge z\), gives

\(\displaystyle \kappa_u(b^0)\ge\rho-\rho/4=3\rho/4.\)

On the cutoff box, the gradient of each constraint has norm bounded by

\(\displaystyle M_0=\eta^{-1}+\max_{u\notin J}\sum_{t\notin J}\lambda_{tu}\mu_t. \)

Thus \(K_\eta\) contains a ball centered at \(b^0\) with radius

\(\displaystyle r_0=\min\{\rho/16,\ \rho/(8M_0),\ 1/8\}, \)

and is contained in a ball of radius \(\tau\). Both radii have polynomial-length binary encodings.

Constraint values can be approximated to arbitrary binary precision in polynomial time, and their gradients are rational and computable exactly. For completeness, logarithms of positive rationals can be evaluated by binary range reduction followed by

\(\displaystyle \log r=2\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad z=\frac{r-1}{r+1}, \)

where the reduced argument lies in \([1,2]\), so \(|z|\le\frac13\).

These calculations furnish weak separation. A certified violated concave constraint gives a valid central separating cut through its rational gradient. If every constraint is violated by at most a small tolerance, mixing the query point with \(b^0\) makes it feasible with a controlled displacement. Explicitly, if \(\kappa_u(b)\ge-v\) for every \(u\), then

\(\displaystyle b'=(1-\gamma)b+\gamma b^0,\qquad \gamma=\frac{v}{v+\rho/2}, \)

belongs to \(K_\eta\), by concavity.

We use the standard rational ellipsoid theorem: weak linear optimization over a convex body is polynomial-time given weak separation, known inner and outer radii, and their binary encodings. Its precision dependence is logarithmic. These hypotheses have all been verified here. See [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, *Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4).

To ensure an exactly feasible rational output, project the weak optimization output onto the cutoff box and mix it with \(b^0\) as above. If the weak output is within distance \(\omega\) of \(K_\eta\), its constraint violations after projection are at most \(M_0\omega\). The repair loses at most \(2M_0\omega/\rho\) in the linear objective, besides the weak optimization error and projection error. Choosing \(\omega\) sufficiently small requires only polynomially many precision bits.

Taking \(\eta,\zeta\le\xi/8\), then rounding the resulting weighted values with the remaining error allowance, proves the evaluation lemma.

This also proves continuity of activation at every strictly positive seed vector: any subsequential limit along seeds converging to such a vector is a fixed point there, and uniqueness identifies it.

4. Padding and smoothing with polynomial bounds

Set

\(\displaystyle h=\frac1{8\tau},\qquad \theta=\frac34,\qquad P=\left\{v\ge0:\ v_t\le\frac{\mu_t}{\beta},\ \sum_t v_t\le\theta\right\}. \)

For a rival \(c\), define on the nonnegative orthant

\(\displaystyle g_c(v)=F_c\!\left(\min\{\mu,\beta(v+h\mathbf1)\}\right), \)

where the minimum is coordinatewise.

Every seed coordinate used here is positive. Clipping at the upper capacities preserves monotonicity and diminishing returns: at a smaller starting coordinate, clipping permits at least as much of an increment, and the same permitted increment has at least as large a marginal value.

Let \(Z\) have independent uniform coordinates in \([0,h]\), and define

\(\displaystyle \varphi_c(v)=\mathbb E[g_c(v+Z)].\)

These functions are monotone, have diminishing returns, and take values in \([0,2]\).

They have polynomial smoothness bounds. The function \(g_c\) is defined and continuous even for \(v_t\ge-h/2\), since all resulting seeds remain positive. Concavity in each individual coordinate yields, for \(v\ge0\) and \(u>0\),

\(\displaystyle 0\le\frac{g_c(v+u e_j)-g_c(v)}u \le \frac{g_c(v)-g_c(v-(h/2)e_j)}{h/2} \le\frac4h. \)

Changing coordinates one at a time therefore gives

\(\displaystyle |g_c(v)-g_c(w)|\le\frac4h\|v-w\|_1. \)

Integration over coordinate \(j\) gives the derivative formula

\(\displaystyle \partial_j\varphi_c(v) = \mathbb E_{Z_{-j}} \left[ \frac{g_c(v+Z_{-j}+h e_j)-g_c(v+Z_{-j})}{h} \right], \)

where \(Z_{-j}\) has zero in coordinate \(j\). Continuity of \(g_c\) justifies this formula and continuity of the derivative. It implies

\(\displaystyle 0\le\partial_j\varphi_c(v)\le2/h, \qquad \|\nabla\varphi_c(v)-\nabla\varphi_c(w)\|_\infty \le M\|v-w\|_1, \qquad M=8/h^2. \)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \varphi_c(v+\Delta)\ge \varphi_c(v)+\nabla\varphi_c(v)\cdot\Delta -\frac M2\|\Delta\|_1^2. \)

Finally, for any \(v\in P\), the rational seed vector

\(\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{out}}(v)=\min\{\mu,\beta(v+2h\mathbf1)\}\)

is feasible, because

\(\displaystyle \sum_t s_t^{\mathrm{out}}(v) \le\beta(\theta+2\tau h)=\beta. \)

Monotonicity gives

\(\displaystyle \boxed{F_c(s^{\mathrm{out}}(v))\ge\varphi_c(v).}\)

5. Simultaneous continuous greedy

Suppose nonnegative thresholds \(T_c\) have a common witness \(v^\star\in P\) satisfying \(\varphi_c(v^\star)\ge T_c\) for all rivals.

At any \(v\ge0\), diminishing returns and monotonicity give

\(\displaystyle \nabla\varphi_c(v)\cdot v^\star \ge \varphi_c(v\vee v^\star)-\varphi_c(v) \ge T_c-\varphi_c(v). \)

For the first inequality, integrate from \(v\) to \(v\vee v^\star\). Each gradient coordinate along that increasing segment is at most its value at \(v\); also, \((v^\star-v)_+\le v^\star\).

Thus all the required direction inequalities have a common solution in \(P\).

We implement this observation with rational arithmetic. Put

\(\displaystyle r=\varepsilon/32,\qquad N=\left\lceil M/(2r)\right\rceil.\)

At an iteration point \(v\), obtain estimates with

\(\displaystyle |\widehat\varphi_c-\varphi_c(v)|\le r,\qquad \|\widehat g_c-\nabla\varphi_c(v)\|_\infty\le r. \)

Solve the rational linear feasibility problem

\(\displaystyle d\in P,\qquad \widehat g_c\cdot d\ge T_c-\widehat\varphi_c-2r \quad\text{for every rival }c. \)

Whenever a common witness exists and the estimates are accurate, this linear program is feasible: substituting \(v^\star\), whose coordinate sum is at most \(1\), incurs at most \(r\) in each gradient inner product and at most \(r\) in each value.

Choose a positive dyadic grid spacing \(\rho_{\mathrm{grid}}\le rh/(8\tau)\), and round each coordinate of \(d\) downward to this grid, obtaining \(\bar d\in P\). Since \(\|\widehat g_c\|_\infty\le2/h+r\le3/h\), rounding loses at most \(r\) in an estimated inner product. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \nabla\varphi_c(v)\cdot\bar d \ge T_c-\varphi_c(v)-5r. \)

Update \(v\leftarrow v+\bar d/N\), starting at zero and taking \(N\) steps. Every intermediate point belongs to \(P\), as it is an average of at most \(N\) vectors in \(P\), completed with zero vectors.

The smoothness bound gives the recurrence

\(\displaystyle \varphi_c(v_{k+1}) \ge (1-1/N)\varphi_c(v_k)+T_c/N-6r/N. \)

Here the Taylor loss is at most \(M/(2N^2)\le r/N\). Iterating and using \(\varphi_c(0)\ge0\) yields

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\varphi_c(v_N)\ge(1-e^{-1})T_c-6r.} \)

Indeed, \(1-(1-1/N)^N\ge1-e^{-1}\).

6. Threshold guesses and the approximation bound

Try all guesses

\(\displaystyle z\in\{0,r,2r,\ldots,\lfloor2/r\rfloor r\},\)

using thresholds

\(\displaystyle T_c(z)=\max\{0,\theta z-d_c\}.\)

A run whose direction linear program is infeasible may be discarded.

Consider a guess admitting a feasible seed vector \(s^\star\) with \(D(s^\star)\ge z\). Section 1 gives

\(\displaystyle F_c(s^\star)+d_c\ge z\quad\text{for every rival }c.\)

Set \(v^\star=\theta s^\star/\beta\). Then \(v^\star\in P\). Padding, monotonicity, and the scaling inequality imply

\(\displaystyle \varphi_c(v^\star) \ge F_c(\theta s^\star) \ge\theta F_c(s^\star) \ge\theta z-\theta d_c \ge\theta z-d_c. \)

Nonnegativity supplies the threshold when the last expression is negative. Thus these thresholds have a common witness.

Writing \(C=1-e^{-1}\), the completed run produces seeds satisfying

\(\displaystyle H(s^{\mathrm{out}}(v_N)) \ge \min_{c\ne c^\star}\bigl(C\,T_c(z)+d_c\bigr)-6r \ge C\theta z-6r. \)

The last inequality follows separately when \(d_c\ge\theta z\), and when \(d_c<\theta z\); in the latter case,

\(\displaystyle C\,T_c(z)+d_c=C\theta z+(1-C)d_c\ge C\theta z.\)

Evaluate the outputs’ \(H\)-values to error at most \(r\), and return the output with largest estimated value. Include the zero seed vector, whose \(H\)-value is exactly zero. Selection costs at most another \(2r\).

Since \(D(0)=0\), we have \(0\le\mathrm{OPT}\le2\). There is a guessed \(z\ge\mathrm{OPT}-r\) for which the required witness exists:

  • If \(\mathrm{OPT}\le r\), use \(z=0\) and \(s^\star=0\).
  • Otherwise, choose a grid value strictly below \(\mathrm{OPT}\), at distance at most \(r\). The definition of supremum supplies a feasible \(s^\star\) with \(D(s^\star)>z\).

No attainment assumption is needed.

On the event that all estimates are accurate, the returned seeds consequently satisfy

\(\displaystyle D(s)+X(s)\ge H(s) \ge C\theta\,\mathrm{OPT}-(C\theta+8)r \ge \frac34(1-e^{-1})\,\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon, \)

because \(C\theta+8<9\) and \(r=\varepsilon/32\).

7. Sampling, probability, and running time

It remains to implement the smoothed estimates with finite random bits.

Take a power of two \(K\ge64\tau/(hr)\). Replace each continuous uniform coordinate by a uniform choice from

\(\displaystyle \{0,h/K,\ldots,(K-1)h/K\}.\)

Coupling the two distributions coordinatewise gives an \(\ell_1\) displacement at most \(\tau h/K\). The bounds from Section 4 imply that this replacement changes a function expectation by at most \(4\tau/K\), and a derivative expectation by at most \(8\tau/(hK)\).

For each sampled function value, use the evaluation lemma with accuracy \(\xi=rh/64\). For derivative estimates, evaluate the two endpoints in the derivative formula. Their induced error is at most \(2\xi/h\). Round final estimates to a fixed dyadic precision with error at most \(r/16\).

The remaining sampling error can be at most \(r/2\). Both function samples and derivative samples have range bounded by \(2/h\). If \(Q\) bounds the total number of scalar estimates, Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound show that

\(\displaystyle S=\left\lceil\frac{32}{h^2r^2}\log(6Q)\right\rceil \)

samples per estimate suffice for simultaneous accuracy with probability at least \(\frac23\). The same argument applies to adaptively chosen iteration points, by conditioning on previous randomness.

There are \(O(1/r)\) guesses, \(N=O(\tau^2/r)\) steps per guess, and at most \((m-1)(\tau+1)\) scalar estimates per step. Hence \(Q\), \(S\), and the number of evaluation calls are polynomial in \(m,\tau,1/\varepsilon\).

Each direction is rounded to the fixed dyadic grid before updating. Therefore every iteration coordinate is an integer multiple of \(\rho_{\mathrm{grid}}/N\), preventing accumulation of large rational denominators. Estimates likewise use fixed precision. All linear programs and fixed-point oracle calls consequently have polynomial input encoding lengths.

The evaluation lemma depends on contact intensities and tiny seed masses through their binary encodings and logarithmic conditioning bounds. It does not require a polynomial convergence rate for the original fixed-point iteration.

Thus the total running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\), and the output is always a feasible rational seed vector.
