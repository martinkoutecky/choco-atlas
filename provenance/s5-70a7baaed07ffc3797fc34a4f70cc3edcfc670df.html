The continuous mirror question, as graded

For a fixed finite type set \(T\) with known masses \(\mu_t\), an unknown binary prior, and unknown type-specific signal channels \(P_t(s\mid\omega)\), suppose each task reveals the exact population report measure \(\nu\) induced by conditionally independent signals and the realized \(\omega\). What is the minimax sample complexity \(N(\varepsilon,\delta)\) for using \(N\) i.i.d. task samples to return \(F\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), \(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(F(\nu)-f^*(\nu))^2]\le\varepsilon\), where \(f^*(\nu)=P(\omega=1\mid\nu)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a known list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the bit length of this description.
Each task draws \(\omega\in\{0,1\}\) with unknown prior \(p\in(0,1)\). Type \(t\) has a known finite signal alphabet \(S_t\), with \(m=\max_t|S_t|\geq2\), and an unknown channel \(P_t(\cdot\mid\omega)\), an arbitrary probability distribution on \(S_t\). These parameters remain fixed across tasks.
An individual receiving \(s\in S_t\) truthfully reports \(r_t(s)=\Pr(\omega=1\mid s,t)\); reports for signals of zero unconditional probability may be assigned arbitrarily. Individual signals are independent conditional on \(\omega\).
The exact population observation in state \(w\) is defined as the unlabelled report measure \(\nu_w=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{s\in S_t}P_t(s\mid w)\delta_{r_t(s)}\), where \(\delta_x\) is unit mass at \(x\). No type labels, signals, or finite-population sampling noise are observed.
Training consists of \(N\) independent pairs \((\nu_{\omega},\omega)\). A learner returns a measurable \(F:\mathcal P([0,1])\to[0,1]\); define \(f^*(\nu)=\Pr(\omega=1\mid\nu)\) almost surely. For \(\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)\), let \(N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)\) be the least sample count permitting one learner to guarantee \(\mathbb E_\nu[(F(\nu)-f^*(\nu))^2]\leq\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), uniformly over all admissible priors and channels, including learner randomness.

What is the minimax sample complexity \(N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)\), including its dependence on \(\varepsilon,\delta\) and the known society and signal alphabets?

For a finite list of \(n\geq2\) conditionally independent experts, the verified bounds are \(\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\) and \(O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/(\varepsilon\delta)))\), independently of \(n\) and signal-alphabet size ([Lin–Chen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13126v4)). No result settling the exact population-measure model above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational type masses to total mass one and defined L as the known society description's bit length.
  • Specified known finite signal alphabets, following the underlying discrete model, and allowed arbitrary real channel probabilities; the anchor also permits infinite signal spaces.
  • Restricted the unknown prior to (0,1), as in the anchor, and fixed the prior and channels across tasks.
  • Defined exact population reports as the mixture of conditional report laws, eliminating ambiguity about aggregation of infinitely many independent signals.
  • Interpreted the observed report measure as unlabelled, merging equal report values across types.
  • Made minimax quantification uniform over priors and channels for each fixed known society and specified the learner's output domain and probability guarantee.
  • Allowed one supported type because a type denotes a positive population mass rather than a single expert; the discrete restriction of at least two experts does not imply two types.

Status and proved results

The full minimax function is not settled here. The results below substantially sharpen the bounds in the notes: they determine every positive sample count when all alphabets have at least two signals, and determine the full minimax function in that case throughout $\varepsilon<1/8$, including all confidence levels.

Write
$$ \alpha=\sum_{\{t:\,|S_t|=1\}}\mu_t, \qquad h=\sqrt{\varepsilon}, \qquad h_0=\frac{\sqrt2-1}{2}, \qquad \varepsilon_0=h_0^2=\frac{3-2\sqrt2}{4}. $$
For a real number $z$, let $[z]_+=\max\{z,0\}$.

The principal conclusions are:

  • Exact criterion for positive sample counts when $\alpha=0$. If $h\geq1/4$, every positive sample count is sufficient. If $0<h<1/4$, put

$$ a_h=\frac{1+2h-\sqrt{1-4h}}2. $$
A positive integer $N$ is sufficient if and only if
$$ \bigl[(1-a_h)^N-\delta\bigr]_+ + \mathbf1_{\{h<h_0\}}\bigl[a_h^N-\delta\bigr]_+ \leq\delta. $$
Let $N_+(\varepsilon,\delta)$ denote the least positive integer satisfying this criterion, with $N_+=1$ when $h\geq1/4$. It is independent of the support masses, support size, description length, and alphabet sizes.

  • Full minimax function when $\alpha=0$ and $\varepsilon<1/8$. Define

$$ d_*(\varepsilon)= \begin{cases} 2/3,&0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0,\\ 1/2,&\varepsilon_0\leq\varepsilon<1/8. \end{cases} $$
Then
$$ N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)= \begin{cases} 0,&\delta\geq d_*(\varepsilon),\\ N_+(\varepsilon,\delta),&\delta<d_*(\varepsilon). \end{cases} $$

  • When $\alpha>0$, one sample gives zero error. Moreover, zero samples give zero error with probability at least $1/2$, uniformly over all models.
  • The case $\alpha=1/2$ is completely resolved:

$$ N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)= \begin{cases} 0,&\varepsilon\geq1/8\ \text{or}\ \delta\geq1/2,\\ 1,&\varepsilon<1/8\ \text{and}\ \delta<1/2. \end{cases} $$
If $\alpha>1/2$, zero samples give zero error.

  • An additional mass-dependent case. If $\alpha>0$ and exactly one type has more than one signal, and that type has exactly two signals, then zero samples give zero error whenever $\alpha\neq1/2$.

The remaining gaps concern zero-sample feasibility in the larger-error region for $\alpha=0$, and general societies with $0<\alpha<1/2$.

1. Calibration determines the possible targets

Let
$$ Q=(1-p)\nu_0+p\nu_1 $$
be the unconditional population report distribution.

For each type and signal, Bayes’ rule gives
$$ r_t(s)\bigl((1-p)P_t(s\mid0)+pP_t(s\mid1)\bigr) =pP_t(s\mid1). $$
If the unconditional signal probability is zero, both conditional probabilities are zero because $p\in(0,1)$. Thus its arbitrary report contributes nothing.

Summing over types and signals yields
$$ xQ(dx)=p\nu_1(dx), \qquad (1-x)Q(dx)=(1-p)\nu_0(dx). $$
In particular,
$$ \int x\,Q(dx)=p. $$

Put
$$ v=\int(x-p)^2Q(dx). $$
Since $\int x^2Q(dx)=p^2+v$, the state-specific means are
$$ \int x\,\nu_1(dx)=p+\frac vp, \qquad \int x\,\nu_0(dx)=p-\frac v{1-p}. $$

If $v=0$, then $Q=\delta_p$. Since both mixture coefficients are positive,
$$ \nu_0=\nu_1=\delta_p, \qquad f^*(\delta_p)=p. $$
If $v>0$, the two means differ. Hence
$$ \nu_0\neq\nu_1, \qquad f^*(\nu_0)=0, \qquad f^*(\nu_1)=1. $$

Thus every model is either uninformative, with one Dirac observation, or informative, with two observations that identify their states exactly.

Two further consequences will be used repeatedly:
$$ \nu_0(\{1\})=0, \qquad \nu_1(\{0\})=0. $$
On the interior of the report interval, define
$$ \ell(x)=\frac{x}{1-x}. $$
Dividing the calibration identities gives
$$ \nu_1(dx)=\frac{1-p}{p}\ell(x)\nu_0(dx), \qquad x\in(0,1). $$
In particular, both observations have the same interior support.

If an informative model has $\nu_0=\delta_x$ with $0<x<1$, then its mean implies $p>x$, and calibration gives
$$ \nu_1= \frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(p)}\delta_x+ \left(1-\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(p)}\right)\delta_1. $$
The coefficient at $1$ is positive. The analogous statement holds when $\nu_1=\delta_x$: then $p<x$, and $\nu_0$ has positive mass at $0$.

Consequently, if both informative observations are Dirac measures, they must be $\delta_0$ and $\delta_1$. In every other informative model, at most one observation is Dirac.

These conclusions do not require conditional independence.

2. Exact minimax criterion for every positive sample count

Assume throughout this section that $\alpha=0$, so every type has at least two signals.

2.1 Admissible Dirac alternatives

For every $0<x<p<1$, all types can use identical two-signal channels
$$ P_t(A\mid0)=1, \qquad P_t(A\mid1)=\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(p)}, \qquad P_t(B\mid1)=1-\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(p)}. $$
The remaining conditional probabilities are zero. The reports are $x$ at $A$ and $1$ at $B$. Therefore
$$ \nu_0=\delta_x, \qquad \nu_1= \frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(p)}\delta_x+ \left(1-\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(p)}\right)\delta_1. $$
All probabilities are valid because $\ell(x)<\ell(p)$.

Similarly, for every $0<p<x<1$, there is an informative model with $\nu_1=\delta_x$.

Unused signals receive zero probability. These channels can be realized independently conditional on the state. Because all types use identical channels, the constructions work for every choice of masses.

2.2 Necessary conditions

Fix a positive integer $N$ and a learner satisfying the uniform guarantee.

Consider the uninformative model with prior $x\in(0,1)$ and observation $\delta_x$. Conditional on the training data consisting entirely of $(\delta_x,0)$, write
$$ Z_0(x)=F(\delta_x), \qquad r_0(x)=\Pr\bigl(|Z_0(x)-x|\leq h\bigr). $$
Here the probability is over learner randomness. Define $Z_1(x)$ and $r_1(x)$ analogously for training data entirely equal to $(\delta_x,1)$.

The two homogeneous-label training events are disjoint. Their contributions to failure in the uninformative model imply
$$ (1-x)^N(1-r_0(x))+x^N(1-r_1(x))\leq\delta. $$

Suppose
$$ x>h, \qquad (1-x)(x-h)^2>h^2. $$
Use an informative alternative from Section 2.1 with prior $p>x$ sufficiently close to $x$ that
$$ (1-p)(x-h)^2>\varepsilon. $$
On the common training data entirely equal to $(\delta_x,0)$, success for the uninformative model requires
$$ Z_0(x)\geq x-h. $$
This entails failure for the informative alternative, whose risk is at least
$$ (1-p)Z_0(x)^2>\varepsilon. $$
Thus
$$ (1-p)^N r_0(x)\leq\delta. $$
Letting $p$ decrease to $x$ gives
$$ r_0(x)\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{(1-x)^N}\right\}. $$

The corresponding argument with a state-$1$ Dirac alternative gives
$$ r_1(x)\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{x^N}\right\} $$
whenever
$$ 1-x>h, \qquad x(1-x-h)^2>h^2. $$

Define the two incompatibility conditions
$$ B_0(x): \quad x>h\ \text{and}\ (1-x)(x-h)^2>h^2, $$
$$ B_1(x): \quad 1-x>h\ \text{and}\ x(1-x-h)^2>h^2. $$
A necessary condition for learning with $N$ samples is therefore
$$ G_N(x):= \mathbf1_{B_0(x)}\bigl[(1-x)^N-\delta\bigr]_+ + \mathbf1_{B_1(x)}\bigl[x^N-\delta\bigr]_+ \leq\delta $$
for every $x\in(0,1)$.

2.3 Sufficiency of the same conditions

Suppose $G_N(x)\leq\delta$ for every $x$.

If training contains two distinct observation measures, memorize their labels. If the sole observed measure is non-Dirac, assign its observed label there and the opposite label everywhere else. Both rules have zero risk on admissible models.

If the sole observed measure is $\delta_x$ and both labels occur, the model is uninformative; predict $x$ there.

It remains to handle one Dirac observation and one label. Put
$$ a(x)=[x-h]_+, \qquad b(x)=\min\{1,x+h\}. $$

After training data entirely equal to $(\delta_x,0)$:

  • If $B_0(x)$ is false, predict $a(x)$ at $\delta_x$.
  • If $B_0(x)$ is true, predict $a(x)$ with probability

$$ \rho_0(x)=\min\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{(1-x)^N}\right\}, $$
and predict $0$ otherwise.

  • Predict $1$ at every other observation.

After training data entirely equal to $(\delta_x,1)$, use the symmetric rule: predict $b(x)$ always if $B_1(x)$ is false, and otherwise predict $b(x)$ with probability
$$ \rho_1(x)=\min\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{x^N}\right\}, $$
predicting $1$ otherwise. Assign $0$ elsewhere. At $x=0,1$, use the correct endpoint prediction.

In an uninformative model, the predictions $a(x)$ and $b(x)$ are within distance $h$ of $x$. Its failure probability is exactly $G_N(x)$.

In an informative model with $\nu_0=\delta_x$, write $q=1-p$. Then $q<1-x$. If $B_0(x)$ is false, the risk is at most
$$ q\,a(x)^2\leq(1-x)a(x)^2\leq\varepsilon. $$
If $B_0(x)$ is true, failure can occur only when all samples have state $0$ and the learner selects $a(x)$. Its probability is at most
$$ q^N\rho_0(x) \leq (1-x)^N\rho_0(x) \leq\delta. $$
The state-$1$ calculation is identical.

An informative model cannot have two problematic Dirac observations: Section 1 showed that two distinct Dirac observations must be $\delta_0,\delta_1$, which this learner predicts correctly.

This proves sufficiency.

2.4 Evaluating the condition explicitly

The polynomial governing $B_0$ factors as
$$ (1-x)(x-h)^2-h^2 = x\bigl(-x^2+(1+2h)x-(2h+h^2)\bigr). $$

If $h\geq1/4$, the quadratic discriminant $1-4h$ is nonpositive, so $B_0$ and $B_1$ are both empty. Hence one sample suffices for every confidence level.

If $0<h<1/4$, define
$$ a_h=\frac{1+2h-\sqrt{1-4h}}2, \qquad b_h=\frac{1+2h+\sqrt{1-4h}}2. $$
Then
$$ B_0(x)\iff a_h<x<b_h, \qquad B_1(x)\iff1-b_h<x<1-a_h. $$
Also $a_h+b_h=1+2h>1$, and
$$ a_h<1/2\iff h<h_0. $$

When $h\geq h_0$, the two bad intervals are disjoint. Taking the supremum over either interval gives
$$ \sup_xG_N(x)=\bigl[(1-a_h)^N-\delta\bigr]_+. $$

When $h<h_0$, their intersection is $(a_h,1-a_h)$. On that interval,
$$ G_N(x)=\bigl[(1-x)^N-\delta\bigr]_+ +\bigl[x^N-\delta\bigr]_+. $$
This function is convex: $x^N$ and $(1-x)^N$ are convex for positive integers $N$, and the maximum of a convex function and zero is convex. Its supremum is consequently its common limiting value at the two endpoints. Outside the intersection, the remaining single term is no larger. Thus
$$ \sup_xG_N(x)= \bigl[(1-a_h)^N-\delta\bigr]_+ +\bigl[a_h^N-\delta\bigr]_+. $$

This proves the positive-sample criterion stated at the start.

In particular, since $a_h=2h+O(h^2)$, it recovers
$$ N_+(\varepsilon,\delta) =\Theta\!\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\log(1/\delta)\right) $$
in the small-error, high-confidence regime. For each fixed $\delta<1/2$, sufficiently small $\varepsilon$ actually gives
$$ N_+(\varepsilon,\delta) = \left\lceil \frac{\log(1/(2\delta))}{-\log(1-a_h)} \right\rceil. $$
Indeed, this integer tends to infinity, so its $a_h^N$ term eventually lies below $\delta$.

3. Zero-sample randomization for non-Dirac observations

We next construct a measurable rule that correctly predicts both observations of every informative model with probability at least $1/2$, except for the separate Dirac ambiguity.

First, any observation containing $0$ must be a state-$0$ observation; any observation containing $1$ must be a state-$1$ observation. Predict those labels directly. A realizable observation cannot contain both endpoints.

Consider a finite non-Dirac measure $u$ supported entirely in $(0,1)$. Let its two smallest atom locations be $x_1<x_2$, with masses $a_1,a_2>0$. Define
$$ D(u)= \frac{\log(a_2/a_1)} {\log(\ell(x_2)/\ell(x_1))}, \qquad C(u)=\lfloor D(u)\rfloor\pmod2. $$
The denominator is positive.

If both observations of an informative model are supported entirely in $(0,1)$, calibration gives
$$ \nu_1(dx)=\frac{\ell(x)\nu_0(dx)} {\int\ell(x)\nu_0(dx)}. $$
The atom locations are unchanged, while the ratio of the first two atom masses is multiplied by $\ell(x_2)/\ell(x_1)$. Consequently,
$$ D(\nu_1)=D(\nu_0)+1, \qquad C(\nu_1)=1-C(\nu_0). $$

Choose one fair random bit $B$. On interior non-Dirac observations, predict $C(u)$ if $B=0$, and $1-C(u)$ if $B=1$.

If both observations are interior and non-Dirac, exactly one choice of $B$ predicts both labels correctly. If only one is interior and non-Dirac, the endpoint observation is already correct and the interior prediction is correct with probability $1/2$.

For completeness, all these rules admit measurable extensions to $\mathcal P([0,1])$. Let
$$ M=\sum_t|S_t|. $$
All realizable observations have at most $M$ atoms. The set of measures with at most $M$ atoms is closed: it is the continuous image of the compact parameter space of $M$ locations and their simplex of weights. On finite-atom strata, sorted locations and their masses are Borel functions, obtained from distribution-function evaluations and shrinking intervals around the recovered locations. Thus $D$ and $C$ are Borel on their domains. Assign, for example, $1/2$ outside the specified domains.

4. The exact zero-sample thresholds below error $1/8$

Assume $\alpha=0$.

4.1 A universal confidence of $1/3$ with zero error

Use the non-Dirac rule from Section 3. Predict $0$ at $\delta_0$ and $1$ at $\delta_1$. At each interior Dirac observation $\delta_x$, use one global random choice among the three predictions
$$ 0,\qquad x,\qquad1, $$
with equal probabilities.

In an uninformative model, the prediction equals $p$ with probability $1/3$.

If an informative model has an interior Dirac observation, its companion contains the appropriate endpoint by Section 1 and is classified correctly. The Dirac prediction is correct with probability $1/3$.

All other informative models are classified correctly with probability at least $1/2$. Therefore zero samples give zero risk with probability at least $1/3$ uniformly:
$$ \delta\geq2/3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)=0. $$

4.2 Necessity of $\delta\geq2/3$ when $\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$

Suppose a zero-sample learner works uniformly, and let
$$ Z=F(\delta_{1/2}). $$
The uninformative prior-$1/2$ model requires
$$ \Pr\left(Z\in[1/2-h,1/2+h]\right)\geq1-\delta. $$

Use informative state-$0$ Dirac alternatives with priors decreasing to $1/2$. Their necessary success conditions, followed by continuity of probability for decreasing closed intervals, give
$$ \Pr(Z\leq\sqrt2\,h)\geq1-\delta. $$
Symmetric state-$1$ alternatives give
$$ \Pr(Z\geq1-\sqrt2\,h)\geq1-\delta. $$

For $h<h_0$, the three intervals
$$ [0,\sqrt2\,h], \qquad [1/2-h,1/2+h], \qquad [1-\sqrt2\,h,1] $$
are pairwise disjoint. Hence
$$ 3(1-\delta)\leq1, $$
which requires $\delta\geq2/3$.

4.3 Confidence $1/2$ is achievable when $\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_0$

Use Section 3 on non-Dirac measures. At an interior Dirac measure $\delta_x$, use a fair choice between two predictions.

If $B_0(x)$ is false, choose between
$$ [x-h]_+ \quad\text{and}\quad1. $$
If $B_0(x)$ is true, choose between
$$ 0 \quad\text{and}\quad\min\{1,x+h\}. $$

When $h\geq h_0$, $B_0$ and $B_1$ cannot both hold. Thus in the second case $B_1(x)$ is false.

The first prediction always has squared loss at most $\varepsilon$ in every state-$0$ Dirac model: this follows either from $B_0$ being false or from predicting $0$. The second prediction always has squared loss at most $\varepsilon$ in every state-$1$ Dirac model. At least one of the predictions is within distance $h$ of $x$, so the uninformative model succeeds with probability at least $1/2$.

An informative model involving an interior Dirac observation has an endpoint-containing companion, classified correctly. Therefore the complete risk guarantee holds with probability at least $1/2$:
$$ \varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_0,\quad\delta\geq1/2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)=0. $$

4.4 Necessity of $\delta\geq1/2$ when $\varepsilon<1/8$

Choose
$$ a=1/2-d,\qquad b=1/2+d, \qquad u=\tfrac12\delta_a+\tfrac12\delta_b, $$
where $d>0$ will be small. Put
$$ R=\frac{\ell(a)+\ell(b)}2, \qquad p_0=\frac R{1+R}, \qquad p_1=\frac1{1+R}. $$

There is an admissible model with prior $p_0$ and $\nu_0=u$: for every type, assign state-$0$ probabilities $1/2,1/2$, and state-$1$ probabilities
$$ \frac{\ell(a)}{2R}, \qquad \frac{\ell(b)}{2R}. $$
They sum to one, and Bayes’ rule gives reports $a,b$.

Because $b=1-a$, an analogous model with prior $p_1$ has $\nu_1=u$, using state-$1$ probabilities $1/2,1/2$ and state-$0$ probabilities
$$ \frac{1}{2R\ell(a)}, \qquad \frac{1}{2R\ell(b)}. $$
These also sum to one.

The occurrence probability of $u$ in both models is
$$ q=1-p_0=p_1, $$
and $q$ tends to $1/2$ as $d$ tends to zero.

Success in the first model necessarily requires
$$ F(u)\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon/q}, $$
while success in the second requires
$$ F(u)\geq1-\sqrt{\varepsilon/q}. $$
For $\varepsilon<1/8$, choose $d$ sufficiently small that $\varepsilon<q/4$. These conditions are disjoint, so their success probabilities sum to at most one. Therefore $\delta\geq1/2$ is necessary.

Combining Sections 2 and 4 proves the full formula for $\alpha=0$, $\varepsilon<1/8$ stated at the start.

5. Further control of the larger-error region

For every society, the constant predictor $1/2$ has risk at most $1/4$, so
$$ \varepsilon\geq1/4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)=0. $$

For $\alpha=0$ and $\varepsilon\geq1/16$, Section 2 gives $N_+=1$. Thus the remaining larger-error question is a zero-versus-one-sample question.

The following lower bound settles part of that region:
$$ \alpha=0,\quad\varepsilon<1/4,\quad \delta< \frac{1-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{T,\mu,S}(\varepsilon,\delta)\neq0. $$

To prove it, choose $a=1/2-d$, $b=1/2+d$, with $d$ small. Starting from any probability measure $u_0$ with positive masses at $a,b$, recursively define
$$ u_{j+1}(dx)= \frac{\ell(x)u_j(dx)}{\int\ell(x)u_j(dx)}. $$
Each pair $(u_j,u_{j+1})$ is realizable as $(\nu_0,\nu_1)$ using identical binary channels across types, with prior
$$ p_j= \frac{\int\ell(x)u_j(dx)} {1+\int\ell(x)u_j(dx)}. $$
Since the average odds lie strictly between $\ell(a)$ and $\ell(b)$,
$$ a<p_j<b. $$

For a randomized zero-sample learner, put $Z_j=F(u_j)$. Its risk in the $j$th model is
$$ R_j=(1-p_j)Z_j^2+p_j(1-Z_{j+1})^2. $$
Since both coefficients exceed $1/2-d$, Cauchy’s inequality gives
$$ R_j\geq \frac{1/2-d}{2} \bigl(1-(Z_{j+1}-Z_j)\bigr)^2. $$
Choose $d$ so small that
$$ c_d=1-\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{1/2-d}}>0. $$
On success, $Z_{j+1}-Z_j\geq c_d$. Always, $Z_{j+1}-Z_j\geq-1$. A uniform success probability of at least $1-\delta$ therefore implies
$$ \mathbb E[Z_{j+1}-Z_j]\geq c_d(1-\delta)-\delta. $$
Summing over $k$ successive models and using $Z_k-Z_0\leq1$ yields
$$ k\bigl(c_d(1-\delta)-\delta\bigr)\leq1. $$
Letting $k$ tend to infinity gives $\delta\geq c_d/(1+c_d)$. Finally let $d$ decrease to zero.

This lower bound does not determine the remaining zero-sample boundary throughout $[1/8,1/4)$.

6. Single-signal types: one sample and two sharp mass cases

6.1 One sample gives zero error whenever $\alpha>0$

Every single-signal type reports $p$ in both states. Consequently,
$$ \nu_w(\{p\})\geq\alpha. $$
If $\nu_w=\delta_x$, then $x=p$. The state-specific mean formula from Section 1 now forces $v=0$, so both observations equal $\delta_p$.

One sample therefore suffices:

  • If its observation is $\delta_x$, predict $x$ there.
  • Otherwise, predict its label there and the opposite label everywhere else.

The first case is necessarily uninformative; the second is necessarily informative. The risk is zero for every training realization.

With zero samples, use the non-Dirac rule from Section 3 and predict $x$ at every $\delta_x$. Informative Dirac observations are impossible here, so this gives zero risk with probability at least $1/2$ uniformly.

6.2 If $\alpha>1/2$, zero samples give zero error

The prior $p$ is the unique atom of mass greater than $1/2$. Recover it from the observation $u$, and let
$$ b(u)=\int x\,u(dx). $$
Predict $0$ if $b(u)<p$, $1$ if $b(u)>p$, and $p$ if $b(u)=p$.

The mean identities prove correctness. To define the rule on the whole observation space, assign $1/2$ to measures outside the finite-atom domain or without a unique atom of mass greater than $1/2$. This is a Borel extension.

6.3 Exact minimax function when $\alpha=1/2$

Consider a non-Dirac observation. If it has a unique atom of mass at least $1/2$, that atom identifies $p$, and comparison with its mean gives the correct label.

The only alternative is
$$ u=\tfrac12\delta_a+\tfrac12\delta_b, \qquad a<b. $$
Its prior must be $a$ or $b$. If $a=0$ or $b=1$, the permissible state is determined by the endpoint restrictions. For $0<a<b<1$, it can be state $0$ with prior $b$, or state $1$ with prior $a$.

For such an interior two-atom measure, predict
$$ z(a,b)=\frac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt a+\sqrt{1-b}}. $$
Its risk contribution in either interpretation is
$$ \kappa(a,b)= \frac{a(1-b)}{(\sqrt a+\sqrt{1-b})^2}. $$

$$ \kappa(a,b) \leq\frac{\sqrt{q_0q_1}}4 \leq\frac{q_0+q_1}{8} <\frac18. $$

$$ \nu_1(\{b\})=\tfrac12, \qquad \nu_1(\{a\})=\tfrac12\frac{\ell(a)}{\ell(b)}, \qquad \nu_1(\{1\})=\tfrac12\left(1-\frac{\ell(a)}{\ell(b)}\right). $$
The last two masses are positive and below $1/2$, so $b$ is its unique atom of mass at least $1/2$. The state-$1$ interpretation is symmetric.

Thus a model has at most one ambiguous observation of this interior two-atom form, and its total risk is below $1/8$. Predict $x$ at $\delta_x$ and assign $1/2$ on the remaining, unrealizable cases. The resulting rule is measurable by the finite-atom construction already given. This proves zero-sample sufficiency for $\varepsilon\geq1/8$.

For necessity below $1/8$, take
$$ a=1/2-d,\qquad b=1/2+d. $$
Single-signal types have total mass $1/2$, as do the remaining types. With prior $b$, make the latter types report $a$ deterministically in state $0$, using a second signal exclusive to state $1$. With prior $a$, use the symmetric construction with report $b$ in state $1$.

in opposite states, with occurrence probability $q=1/2-d$. For $\varepsilon<1/8$, choose $d$ so small that $\varepsilon<q/4$. The necessary success intervals for $F(u)$ are disjoint. Hence zero samples are impossible if $\delta<1/2$.

7. Exactly one binary type alongside single-signal types

Suppose $\alpha>0$, exactly one type has more than one signal, and it has two signals. Its mass is $\beta=1-\alpha$.

We prove that if $\alpha\neq1/2$, no non-Dirac measure can arise in opposite states in two admissible models.

First, in a non-Dirac observation, the prior atom has mass exactly $\alpha$. To see this, consider the binary type. If both signals have positive state-$0$ probability, normalization of the state-$1$ channel gives
$$ \sum_s P(s\mid0)\ell(r(s))=\ell(p). $$
If one positive-probability report equals $p$, the other must also equal $p$. If only one signal has positive state-$0$ probability and reports $p$, the observation is again Dirac. Thus the binary type cannot contribute positive mass at $p$ in a non-Dirac state-$0$ observation. The state-$1$ proof uses reciprocal odds and is identical.

Suppose a non-Dirac measure $u$ arises in state $0$ with prior $p_0$ and in state $1$ with prior $p_1$. Its mean gives
$$ p_1<\int x\,u(dx)<p_0. $$
Hence $p_0,p_1$ are distinct atoms of $u$, each of mass exactly $\alpha$.

The observation has at most three atoms.

If it has two atoms, their masses sum to $2\alpha=1$, so $\alpha=1/2$.

If it has three atoms, write the third location as $z$. Its mass is $1-2\alpha>0$. In the state-$0$ model, the binary type’s two positive-probability reports are $p_1,z$. Their odds average equals $\ell(p_0)$. Since $p_1<p_0$, necessarily
$$ z>p_0. $$
In the state-$1$ model, its reports are $p_0,z$. Their reciprocal odds average equals $1/\ell(p_1)$. Since $p_0>p_1$, necessarily
$$ z<p_1. $$
These inequalities are incompatible.

Therefore opposite-state overlap is impossible when $\alpha\neq1/2$.

A measurable zero-error classifier follows by testing the finitely many possible prior atoms of mass $\alpha$. For state $0$, remove the singleton contribution and normalize:
$$ v=\frac{u-\alpha\delta_p}{\beta}. $$
The binary residual is feasible precisely in either of the following cases:

  • $v=\delta_z$ with $z<p$;
  • $v$ has two atoms below $1$ and $\int\ell(x)v(dx)=\ell(p)$.

The first case is realized by a second signal exclusive to state $1$; the second follows by setting the other channel probabilities proportional to odds. State-$1$ feasibility uses the symmetric conditions with reciprocal odds. These are finite Borel tests, and the preceding argument shows that their state-$0$ and state-$1$ acceptance sets are disjoint. Dirac measures are assigned their atom location.

Thus zero samples give zero error for this entire class of societies when $\alpha\neq1/2$.

8. What remains open

The results determine the full minimax function for $\alpha=0$ below error $1/8$, and determine every positive sample count for $\alpha=0$ at arbitrary error and confidence. Above error $1/8$, the remaining uncertainty is whether zero samples suffice in part of the region
