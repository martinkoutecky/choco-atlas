The anchor (named result in the paper)

Proposition 5 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) alternatives, rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(\tau\) complete preference types, and rational piecewise-linear utilities \(u_t(S,q)\) that increase with community share \(q\), decide whether there is an assignment \(x_{t,S}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(q_S=\sum_t x_{t,S}\), such that no positive-mass coalition can profit by moving together to any alternative.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) be a set of \(m\geq2\) alternatives. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has utilities \(u_t(S,q)\in\mathbb R\), defined for every \(S\in M\) and \(q\in[0,1]\), continuous and strictly increasing in \(q\), and piecewise linear with explicitly listed binary-rational breakpoints and values. Utilities induce complete preferences; equality means indifference, with no tie-breaking. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An assignment consists of real masses \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\in M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\); write \(q_S=\sum_t x_{t,S}\). Each alternative has one community, possibly empty; there is no outside option, and type masses are divisible.
A blocking move to \(R\in M\) selects real masses \(0\leq y_{t,S}\leq x_{t,S}\) for \(S\neq R\), with \(\delta=\sum_t\sum_{S\neq R}y_{t,S}>0\), such that \(u_t(R,q_R+\delta)>u_t(S,q_S)\) whenever \(y_{t,S}>0\). Movers join the existing community at \(R\); everyone else stays, and incumbent consent is unnecessary. An assignment is stable if it has no blocking move.

Is deciding whether a stable assignment exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents with strict preferences increasing in community size, stable-assignment existence is NP-complete when the number of alternatives is unrestricted; checking a given assignment is polynomial-time, and two alternatives always admit a polynomial-time computable stable assignment. [Source](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/202200570/2021_Book_AlgorithmicDecisionTheory.pdf)
No result settling the exact divisible-mass, piecewise-linear-utility problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize total mass to one so community share has domain [0,1]; require positive masses and nonempty support.
  • Interpret increasing utilities as strictly increasing, matching strict community-size monotonicity; specify continuous piecewise-linear functions by explicit rational breakpoints and values, with unrestricted signs.
  • Allow utility equality to mean indifference, and require strict improvement for every positive portion of movers. Global strict ordering over all alternative-share pairs is not imposed on the continuous utility model.
  • Allow arbitrary real assignment and coalition masses, including splitting a type across communities.
  • Specify one community per alternative, no outside option, and moves that join existing destination mass without incumbent consent.
  • Require strictly positive moving mass, excluding the vacuous unchanged assignment admitted by a literal reading of the supplied stability definition.
  • Take the number of alternatives as unrestricted with at least two, and include all utility data in the total input bit length L.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Deciding whether a stable assignment exists is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

The decisive observation is that strict increase in community size forces every stable assignment to place each type’s entire mass at one alternative. Fractional blocking moves from such assignments can then be enlarged to moves of entire types.

1. Every stable assignment is pure

Call an assignment pure if, for every type \(t\), there is an alternative \(a(t)\) with \(x_{t,a(t)}=\mu_t\).

Lemma 1. Every stable assignment is pure.

Proof. Suppose type \(t\) has positive mass at two distinct alternatives \(S\) and \(R\). Interchanging their names if necessary, assume

\(u_t(R,q_R)\geq u_t(S,q_S).\)

Choose \(0<\varepsilon\leq x_{t,S}\), and move mass \(\varepsilon\) of this type from \(S\) to \(R\). This is feasible, and strict increase gives

\(u_t(R,q_R+\varepsilon)>u_t(R,q_R)\geq u_t(S,q_S).\)

Thus the move blocks the assignment. Consequently, a stable assignment cannot split any type. \(\square\)

This argument also handles indifference: equality between the two current utilities still produces a strictly improving move.

2. Fractional blocking is equivalent to blocking by entire types

Fix a pure assignment \(a\), and write

\(q_S=\sum_{t:a(t)=S}\mu_t,\qquad v_t=u_t(a(t),q_{a(t)}).\)

Lemma 2. There is a blocking move to \(R\) if and only if there is a nonempty set \(C\subseteq\{t:a(t)\neq R\}\) such that

\(u_t\left(R,q_R+\sum_{j\in C}\mu_j\right)>v_t\qquad\text{for every }t\in C.\)

Proof. Such a set directly supplies a blocking move: move every member type’s entire mass to \(R\).

Conversely, suppose a fractional blocking move to \(R\) has total mass \(\delta>0\). Let \(C\) consist of the types contributing positive mass. Because the assignment is pure, each contributing type moves from its unique assigned alternative. Put

\(D=\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t.\)

Then \(D\geq\delta\) and \(q_R+D\leq1\). For every \(t\in C\), monotonicity and the blocking condition imply

\(u_t(R,q_R+D)\geq u_t(R,q_R+\delta)>v_t.\)

Hence moving the entire mass of every type in \(C\) also blocks. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial-time verification and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate specifies one alternative \(a(t)\) for each type. Its length is \(O(\tau\log m)\). Lemma 1 ensures that every yes-instance has such a certificate.

To verify it, compute the community masses and current utilities. For each destination \(R\), apply the following elimination procedure:

  • Initialize \(C=\{t:a(t)\neq R\}\).
  • Compute \(W(C)=\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t\).
  • Remove every type \(t\in C\) satisfying

\(u_t(R,q_R+W(C))\leq v_t.\)

  • Repeat until no type is removed.

If the final set is nonempty, every remaining type strictly benefits when all remaining types move. Lemma 2 therefore gives a blocking move.

If the final set is empty, no blocking set exists. Indeed, suppose \(K\) were a blocking set. Initially \(K\subseteq C\). Whenever \(K\subseteq C\), every \(t\in K\) satisfies

\(u_t(R,q_R+W(C))\geq u_t(R,q_R+W(K))>v_t.\)

Thus no member of \(K\) is removed, so \(K\) survives every iteration. This contradicts an empty final set.

There are at most \(\tau\) removal rounds per destination, giving \(O(m\tau^2)\) utility evaluations. Community masses, evaluation points, and evaluated utilities are rational numbers of polynomial bit length: sums and evaluations of explicitly represented rational piecewise-linear functions require only polynomially many rational arithmetic operations. All comparisons, including equality, are exact.

Therefore verification takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), and the existence problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. Polynomial reduction from the finite-agent problem

We use the finite-agent hardness result stated in the question: stable-assignment existence for unrestricted alternatives and strict preferences increasing in community size is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is Proposition 5 of Abramowitz, Elkind, Grossi, Shapiro, and Talmon, *Democratic Forking: Choosing Sides with Social Choice*. [Source, printed p. 354](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/202200570/2021_Book_AlgorithmicDecisionTheory.pdf#page=364).

The source permits deviations involving several destinations. Under increasing preferences, existence of such a deviation is equivalent to existence of a deviation to one destination: select all movers entering any one destination, and keep everyone else fixed. Their destination community is at least as large as in the original deviation, since its outgoing movers now stay. Thus every selected mover still strictly improves. This also matches the observation used in Proposition 4 of the source.

Let a source instance have \(n\) agents, alternatives \(M_0\), and \(m_0=|M_0|\). Agent \(i\) has an explicitly listed strict order over outcomes \((S,k)\), where \(S\in M_0\) and \(1\leq k\leq n\). Choose integer ranks

\(r_i(S,k)\in\{1,\ldots,m_0n\},\)

with larger ranks representing preferred outcomes. Increasing preferences imply

\(r_i(S,k+1)>r_i(S,k).\)

The construction below uses only dyadic rational masses and breakpoints, so it also satisfies the stricter interpretation of “binary-rational.”

Construction

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two strictly greater than \(n\). Then \(n<N\leq2n\).

Create:

  • One type for each original agent \(i\), with mass \(\mu_i=1/N\).
  • One additional type \(d\), with mass \(\mu_d=(N-n)/N\).
  • One additional alternative \(D\), so \(M=M_0\cup\{D\}\).

The masses are positive and sum to one.

For each original type \(i\) and alternative \(S\in M_0\), define its utility by linear interpolation through the points

\(u_i(S,0)=r_i(S,1)-1,\)

\(u_i(S,k/N)=r_i(S,k)\qquad(1\leq k\leq n),\)

\(u_i(S,1)=r_i(S,n)+1.\)

Every successive listed value strictly increases. Thus each function is continuous, piecewise linear, and strictly increasing throughout its domain.

For original types at the additional alternative, set

\(u_i(D,q)=-2+q.\)

For the additional type, set

\(u_d(D,q)=2+q,\qquad u_d(S,q)=q\quad(S\in M_0).\)

All these functions also satisfy the required continuity and strict increase.

If distinct utility functions are required for distinct listed types, add the constant \(i(m_0n+5)\) to every utility of original type \(i\). This makes the functions distinct without changing any comparison for that type or any argument below.

There are \(O(m_0n^2)\) listed interpolation points, each with polynomial bit length. The construction therefore has polynomial size and is computable in polynomial time.

Forced locations of the additional mass

In every stable assignment, type \(d\) must be entirely at \(D\). Otherwise, some positive mass of this type receives utility at most \(1\) at an original alternative and can strictly improve by moving to \(D\), where utility is at least \(2\).

Every original type must be entirely outside \(D\). Before any optional additive shift, its utility at \(D\) is at most \(-1\), whereas its utility at every original alternative is at least \(0\). Any positive mass assigned to \(D\) can therefore strictly improve by leaving it. Additive shifts preserve these inequalities.

Together with Lemma 1, this shows that every stable assignment consists of the additional type at \(D\) and one original alternative for each original type.

Correspondence of blocking moves

Let \(a:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to M_0\) be a finite-agent assignment. Construct the corresponding pure mass assignment and place type \(d\) at \(D\).

If alternative \(S\) has \(k_S\) original agents, its mass is \(k_S/N\). An original type assigned there consequently receives utility \(r_i(S,k_S)\), up to its irrelevant additive constant.

Consider a nonempty coalition \(C\) of original agents outside destination \(R\in M_0\). Moving their entire corresponding types produces destination mass

\((k_R+|C|)/N.\)

Every participating type strictly improves exactly when

\(r_i(R,k_R+|C|)>r_i(a(i),k_{a(i)})\qquad(i\in C),\)

which is precisely the finite-agent blocking condition.

No original type can benefit by moving to \(D\), and type \(d\), already at \(D\), cannot benefit by moving to an original alternative. Therefore Lemma 2 shows that the constructed mass assignment is stable exactly when the finite-agent assignment is stable.

Conversely, every stable mass assignment has the forced form proved above and hence determines a finite-agent assignment. A finite blocking coalition would give a blocking move of entire types, so this finite assignment must be stable.

We have therefore proved the equivalence:

\(\text{The finite instance has a stable assignment}\iff\text{the constructed divisible-mass instance has a stable assignment}.\)

This establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Conclusion

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with dyadic rational masses and breakpoints. In particular, it has no polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
