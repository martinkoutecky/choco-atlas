The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\), rational masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict rankings of opposite-side types, decide whether there is a coupling \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with row sums \(p_i\) and column sums \(q_j\) such that, on the positive cells \((i,j)\), neither side’s directed envy graph has a cycle. In the \(I\)-side graph, an arc from \((i,j)\) to \((i',j')\) means \(j'\succ_i j\); in the \(J\)-side graph, it means \(i'\succ_j i\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

The input consists of disjoint, nonempty finite type sets \(I,J\), positive binary rational masses \(p_i,q_j\) with \(\sum_{i\in I}p_i=\sum_{j\in J}q_j=1\), and a complete strict ranking of opposite-side types for every type. All opposite-side types are acceptable.
A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass and their binary rational masses. Let \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) be the total support size, not the number of possible preference types; let \(m=\max\{|I|,|J|\}\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A coupling is a real matrix \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{I\times J}\) satisfying \(\sum_{j\in J}x_{ij}=p_i\) and \(\sum_{i\in I}x_{ij}=q_j\); mass may be split among partners.
Both directed envy graphs have vertex set \(S(x)=\{(i,j):x_{ij}>0\}\). For distinct vertices \((i,j),(i',j')\), the first graph has an arc from the former to the latter exactly when \(j'\succ_i j\); the second has that arc exactly when \(i'\succ_j i\). Vertices sharing a type are included in these comparisons.
A coupling is coalitionally exchange-stable when both graphs are acyclic.

Is deciding whether a coalitionally exchange-stable coupling exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies with equally many agents on each side, existence of a coalitionally exchange-stable matching is NP-complete even with complete strict preferences; such a matching necessarily matches everyone. [Chen, Chmurovic, Jogl, and Sorge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.05725v2).
No result settling the exact coupling problem defined above was identified in the literature search.

  • Made the rankings complete and all opposite-side types acceptable, as required by the anchor's complete-preference domain.
  • Required nonempty supports and strictly positive listed masses, following the support-input convention.
  • Specified real coupling entries and permitted mass splitting; the graded question did not explicitly specify the entries' number domain.
  • Explicitly included comparisons between distinct positive cells sharing a type, as prescribed by the graded arc definitions.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(|I|=|J|=m\) and every mass equals \(1/m\).

The key fact is that acyclicity is preserved when support vertices are deleted. For equal masses, every coupling has a perfect matching inside its support, so allowing mass splitting does not change existence.

1. Stability is inherited by smaller supports

For any set \(T\subseteq I\times J\), define its two envy graphs by the comparisons in the question.

Lemma 1. If both envy graphs on \(T\) are acyclic, then both envy graphs on every subset \(T'\subseteq T\) are acyclic.

Proof. Whether an arc exists between two vertices depends only on those vertices and the input preferences. Consequently, each graph on \(T'\) is the induced subgraph of the corresponding graph on \(T\). A directed cycle in an induced subgraph would also be a directed cycle in the original graph. This applies equally to vertices sharing a type. \(\square\)

In particular, if \(x\) is stable and \(y\) is any coupling with \(S(y)\subseteq S(x)\), then \(y\) is stable.

2. Equal masses permit extraction of a stable matching

Lemma 2. Suppose \(|I|=|J|=n\) and \(p_i=q_j=1/n\) for all types. Every coupling \(x\) has a perfect matching contained in \(S(x)\).

Proof. Form the undirected bipartite graph \(H\) with parts \(I,J\) and edge set \(S(x)\). For \(A\subseteq I\), write \(N_H(A)\) for its neighborhood. Since positive entries from rows in \(A\) can occur only in columns in \(N_H(A)\),

\(\displaystyle \frac{|A|}{n} =\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in N_H(A)}x_{ij} \leq \sum_{j\in N_H(A)}\sum_{i\in I}x_{ij} =\frac{|N_H(A)|}{n}.\)

Thus \(|N_H(A)|\geq |A|\) for every \(A\subseteq I\). Hall’s marriage theorem states that this condition is sufficient for a finite bipartite graph to have a matching covering \(I\). Because \(|I|=|J|\), that matching is perfect. \(\square\)

Corollary. For equal masses, a stable coupling exists if and only if a coalitionally exchange-stable perfect matching exists with the same preferences.

Proof. Let \(x\) be a stable coupling. By Lemma 2, choose a perfect matching \(M\subseteq S(x)\). Give each edge of \(M\) mass \(1/n\), obtaining a coupling \(y\). Lemma 1 proves that both envy graphs of \(y\) are acyclic.

To identify these graphs with matching envy, write the matching as a bijection \(\pi:I\to J\). The map \(i\mapsto(i,\pi(i))\) identifies the first support graph with the graph on first-side agents whose arcs satisfy \(\pi(i')\succ_i\pi(i)\). Likewise, \(j\mapsto(\pi^{-1}(j),j)\) identifies the second support graph with the second-side matching envy graph.

In a perfect bipartite matching, envy arcs cannot cross between sides: the partner of an agent on the other side lies on the evaluating agent’s own side and is unacceptable. Hence an exchange-blocking coalition is precisely a directed cycle in one of these two graphs. Their acyclicity proves that \(M\) is coalitionally exchange-stable.

Conversely, assigning mass \(1/n\) to each edge of a coalitionally exchange-stable perfect matching gives a coupling with these same two acyclic graphs. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial reduction and hardness

Theorem 2 of Chen, Chmurovic, Jogl, and Sorge establishes that deciding existence of a coalitionally exchange-stable marriage matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for complete strict preferences. Their marriage instances have equally sized sides. [Source: Theorem 2 and the definitions in Section 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.05725v2).

Take such an instance with \(n\) agents on each side. Create one distinct type for each agent, retain all preference lists, and assign every type mass \(1/n\). This satisfies every premise of the coupling problem: both supports are nonempty and disjoint, masses are positive binary rationals summing to one on each side, and preferences are complete and strict.

The source matching problem requires no additional perfectness restriction. If a matching were imperfect, equal side sizes would leave an unmatched agent on each side. These two agents would each prefer the other’s unmatched “partner”—the other agent itself—to remaining unmatched, forming an exchange-blocking pair. Thus every stable source matching is perfect.

The corollary therefore proves both directions of the reduction.

For the resulting coupling instance, \(m=n\) and \(\tau=2n\). Under ordinary binary index encoding, its length is \(L=O(m\tau\log(m+1))\); constructing it takes \(O(m\tau\log(m+1))\) bit operations by copying the rankings and writing the masses. No enumeration of possible preference types occurs.

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Polynomial certificates for arbitrary rational masses

Real-valued couplings do not obstruct membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). We prove a stronger certificate property.

Lemma 3. If a stable coupling exists, then a stable rational coupling exists with at most \(\tau-1\) positive entries, each of binary encoding length \(O(L)\).

Proof. Start with any stable coupling \(x\). Regard its support as an undirected bipartite graph on \(I\cup J\).

If this graph contains a cycle, that cycle has even length. Partition its edges into alternating sets \(E_-\) and \(E_+\), and let \(\delta=\min_{e\in E_-}x_e>0\). Subtract \(\delta\) on \(E_-\) and add \(\delta\) on \(E_+\).

Each cycle vertex has one incident edge of each sign, so all row and column sums remain unchanged. Entries remain nonnegative; at least one becomes zero. Every increased entry was already positive, so the support strictly decreases. Stability is preserved by Lemma 1.

Repeating this operation finitely many times produces a coupling \(y\) whose undirected support is a forest. A forest on \(\tau\) vertices has at most \(\tau-1\) edges.

It remains to bound the entries’ encoding lengths. Write the input masses as \(p_i=a_i/b_i\) and \(q_j=c_j/d_j\), with positive integer denominators, and define \(D=(\prod_{i\in I}b_i)(\prod_{j\in J}d_j)\). The binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), and all scaled marginals \(Dp_i,Dq_j\) are integers.

Consider the edge weights \(Dy_e\) on the support forest. In any component containing an edge, a leaf’s unique incident edge has weight equal to that leaf’s residual marginal. Initially these residual marginals are integers. Remove the leaf and its edge, subtracting that integer weight from the neighbor’s residual marginal. The remaining residual marginals are still integers.

Successive leaf removal exhausts the forest, proving that every \(Dy_e\) is an integer. Since \(0<y_e\leq 1\), every positive entry has the form \(k_e/D\), where \(1\leq k_e\leq D\). Both numerator and denominator have \(O(L)\) bits. \(\square\)

A certificate lists at most \(\tau-1\) type pairs and their positive integer numerators \(k_e\), using the common denominator \(D\) computed from the input. Its length is \(O(\tau L)\).

A verifier checks that the pairs are distinct, checks positivity and all scaled marginal equations, and constructs both envy graphs on the listed pairs. It checks every ordered pair of distinct support vertices, including pairs sharing a type, and tests both graphs for directed cycles.

There are \(O(m\tau)\) ranking entries and \(O(\tau^2)\) possible envy arcs. Using elementary binary arithmetic, a conservative verification bound is \(O((m\tau+\tau^2)L^2)\) bit operations. Lemma 3 supplies an accepted certificate for every yes-instance; an accepted certificate directly defines a stable coupling. Therefore the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

5. Conclusion

The exact coupling problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The hardness already holds for uniform masses, where support restriction and Hall’s theorem rigorously eliminate the apparent advantage of splitting mass.
