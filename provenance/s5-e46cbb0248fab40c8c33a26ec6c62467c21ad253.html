The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two nonatomic populations partitioned into finitely many types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), acceptable type pairs, strict type rankings, and costs \(c_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\), find a minimum-cost measurable one-to-one matching plan with no Pareto-improving alternative. Compare the same individuals across plans; an alternative is a Pareto improvement if almost every individual weakly prefers their new assignment and a positive mass strictly prefers it.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are two populations with type sets \(I\) and \(J\). A society is given by its support: the list of its types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Write \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) for the total support size, not the number of possible types. Masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j>0\) need not sum to one or have equal totals across populations.
Realize each type as a disjoint interval of its stated length with Lebesgue measure. Acceptability is specified by \(E\subseteq I\times J\), with \(m=|E|\); each type strictly ranks its acceptable opposite-side types. Individuals of the same partner type are indifferent, and every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched.
A matching plan \(M\) is a measurable, measure-preserving bijection, with measurable inverse, between measurable subsets of the two populations, using only acceptable type pairs; individuals outside these subsets are unmatched. Plans are considered up to null sets.
Let \(x_{ij}(M)\) be the mass of matched pairs of types \(i,j\). Given \(c_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\), the cost is \(C(M)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}c_{ij}x_{ij}(M)\), counting each pair once.
An alternative plan Pareto-improves \(M\) if, comparing the same individuals across plans, almost every individual on both sides weakly prefers the alternative assignment and a positive total mass strictly prefers it. A plan is Pareto-optimal if no such alternative exists; alternatives range over all feasible plans, without a cardinality or cost restriction.
Given a binary rational threshold \(K\geq0\), consider whether there exists a Pareto-optimal plan \(M\) with \(C(M)\leq K\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite bipartite populations with strict preferences and costs in \(\{0,1\}\), minimum-cost Pareto-optimal matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even for deciding whether cost zero is attainable; multiplicative approximation is also hard. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.03434)
No complexity result for exactly the continuous problem defined above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The mass normalization was unspecified; allow arbitrary positive rational masses and unequal population totals, consistent with arbitrary finite population sizes.
  • Realize the unspecified nonatomic spaces as disjoint Lebesgue intervals and require measurable, measure-preserving partial bijections with measurable inverses.
  • Strict type rankings induce indifference between individuals of the same partner type; no identity-based tie-breaking is added.
  • Carry over the preference convention that every acceptable partner is strictly better than being unmatched, and allow unmatched individuals.
  • Define total cost as the integral of pair costs, counting each matched pair once.
  • Pareto improvements may use any feasible matching plan, regardless of its cost or matched mass, as in the discrete Pareto-optimal matching definition.
  • Use a nonnegative binary rational cost threshold to state the exact optimization question as a decision problem; define m as the number of acceptable type pairs and L as total input length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when every type has mass \(1\) and the threshold is \(K=0\).

The essential point is that, for unit type masses, allowing divisible populations does not reduce the minimum cost of a Pareto-optimal matching. We prove this by establishing both directions of a correspondence with finite matchings.

1. Two preliminary facts

Let \(G=(I\cup J,E)\) be a finite bipartite graph. Write \(\delta(v)\) for the edges incident to a vertex \(v\), and define

\(\displaystyle P(G)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}:\sum_{e\in\delta(v)}x_e\leq 1\text{ for every }v\in I\cup J\right\}.\)

Fact 1: matching decomposition. Every \(x\in P(G)\) has a finite decomposition

\(\displaystyle x=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\alpha_r\chi^{Q_r},\qquad \alpha_r>0,\qquad \sum_{r=1}^{q}\alpha_r=1,\)

where each \(Q_r\) is a finite matching and \(\chi^{Q_r}\) is its incidence vector.

Here is a justification of the integrality needed for this fact. Multiply the rows indexed by \(J\) of the vertex-edge incidence matrix by \(-1\). Each column then has one entry \(1\) and one entry \(-1\). Every square submatrix has determinant in \(\{0,1,-1\}\): if a column has at most one nonzero entry, expand along that column and use induction; otherwise every column has both entries, and the sum of the rows is zero. Adding the rows for nonnegativity preserves this determinant property, by the same expansion argument. Thus the constraint matrix is totally unimodular. Since the right-hand sides are integral, Cramer's rule shows that every vertex of \(P(G)\) is integral. Its integral points are precisely matching incidence vectors. Finally, the bounded polytope \(P(G)\) is the convex hull of its vertices.

Fact 2: measurable cohorts can be rearranged. A measurable subset of a bounded interval of mass \(s\) contains a measurable subset of any prescribed mass \(t\in[0,s]\). Two such sets of equal positive mass admit a measure-preserving measurable bijection with measurable inverse, after discarding null sets.

For completeness, these statements follow from cumulative mass parametrization. For a measurable set \(S\), put

\(\displaystyle F_S(a)=\lambda\bigl(S\cap(-\infty,a]\bigr).\)

This function is continuous, so its values give subsets of every intermediate mass. If \(s=\lambda(S)>0\), its generalized inverse

\(\displaystyle q_S(t)=\inf\{a:F_S(a)\geq t\},\qquad 0<t<s,\)

pushes Lebesgue measure on \((0,s)\) to Lebesgue measure restricted to \(S\). Indeed, the mass of parameters satisfying \(q_S(t)\leq a\) is \(F_S(a)\). Continuity gives \(F_S(q_S(t))=t\); the reverse identity holds for almost every point of \(S\), since failures occur only along flat intervals of \(F_S\) and their endpoints, which have zero \(S\)-measure. Consequently these maps are measurable inverses modulo null sets. Composing the parametrizations for two sets proves the assertion.

These facts apply to real masses as well as rational masses.

2. Every component of a continuous Pareto-optimal plan is finitely Pareto-optimal

Give every type mass \(1\), and let \(M\) be any feasible continuous plan. Its mass vector \(x=x(M)\) lies in \(P(G)\), so take a decomposition as in Fact 1.

Lemma. If \(M\) is Pareto-optimal, then every matching \(Q_r\) in this decomposition is Pareto-optimal in the finite instance.

Proof. Fix a component \(Q=Q_r\), and put \(\varepsilon=\alpha_r>0\). For every edge \(e\in Q\), the decomposition implies \(x_e(M)\geq\varepsilon\).

For a type \(v\), let \(u_v(M)\) denote its unmatched mass. The decomposition also gives

\(\displaystyle u_v(M)=1-\sum_{e\in\delta(v)}x_e(M)=\sum_{s=1}^{q}\alpha_s\left(1-\sum_{e\in\delta(v)}\chi^{Q_s}_e\right).\)

In particular, if \(v\) is unmatched in \(Q\), then \(u_v(M)\geq\varepsilon\).

We can therefore select one cohort of mass \(\varepsilon\) from each type as follows.

  • For every \((i,j)\in Q\), select mass \(\varepsilon\) of the type-\(i\) individuals whom \(M\) pairs with type \(j\), and select their actual partners under \(M\) as the type-\(j\) cohort.
  • For every vertex unmatched in \(Q\), select mass \(\varepsilon\) from that type's unmatched individuals.

Fact 2 supplies the required subsets. Since \(Q\) is a matching, these selections do not conflict. Moreover, no pair of \(M\) has exactly one selected endpoint: selected matched cohorts were chosen together with their actual partners.

Suppose \(Q\) were not finitely Pareto-optimal. Then some finite matching \(R\) would weakly improve every vertex and strictly improve at least one vertex relative to \(Q\).

On the selected cohorts, replace the assignments prescribed by \(Q\) with those prescribed by \(R\). For each edge of \(R\), pair the corresponding cohorts by a measure-preserving bijection supplied by Fact 2. Leave the rest of \(M\) unchanged. This is a feasible measurable plan: the selected cohorts have equal mass, and the original pairs outside them remain intact.

Every selected individual had precisely the partner type, or unmatched status, prescribed by \(Q\). Thus every such individual weakly improves. If \(h\) finite vertices strictly improve under \(R\), then the strictly improving individuals have total mass \(h\varepsilon>0\). Everyone outside the cohorts keeps the same assignment.

This contradicts Pareto-optimality of \(M\), proving the lemma. \(\square\)

Cost consequence. For every continuous Pareto-optimal plan \(M\), the decomposition satisfies

\(\displaystyle C(M)=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\alpha_r c(Q_r),\qquad c(Q_r)=\sum_{e\in Q_r}c_e.\)

Hence at least one finitely Pareto-optimal component has cost at most \(C(M)\). In particular, a continuous Pareto-optimal plan of cost zero implies a finite Pareto-optimal matching of cost zero.

3. A finite Pareto-optimal matching remains optimal against continuous alternatives

Conversely, let \(Q\) be a Pareto-optimal matching in the finite instance. Define its continuous realization \(M_Q\) by pairing the entire unit intervals for every edge of \(Q\), using translations, and leaving the other type intervals unmatched.

Lemma. The plan \(M_Q\) is Pareto-optimal among all continuous feasible plans.

Proof. For each vertex \(v\), let \(b_v\) be its partner under \(Q\), with \(b_v=\bot\) if it is unmatched. Here \(\bot\) denotes the unmatched outcome, ranked below every acceptable partner.

Suppose a continuous plan \(N\) Pareto-improves \(M_Q\). Every individual of type \(v\) has the same baseline outcome \(b_v\). Define the allowed edge set

\(\displaystyle H=\{(i,j)\in E:j\succeq_i b_i\text{ and }i\succeq_j b_j\}.\)

Because almost every individual weakly improves, \(N\) uses edges outside \(H\) only on null sets. Its mass vector \(y=x(N)\), restricted to \(H\), belongs to \(P(H)\).

Also, if \(v\) is matched in \(Q\), almost none of its individuals can become unmatched: every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to \(\bot\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \sum_{e\in\delta_H(v)}y_e=1\qquad\text{for every vertex }v\text{ matched in }Q.\)

For an edge \((i,j)\in H\), define

\(\displaystyle w_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{j\succ_i b_i\}+\mathbf{1}\{i\succ_j b_j\}.\)

Since the baseline outcome is constant within each type, the total mass of strictly improving individuals under \(N\) is exactly

\(\displaystyle \sum_{(i,j)\in H}w_{ij}y_{ij}>0.\)

Apply Fact 1 to \(y\), obtaining

\(\displaystyle y=\sum_{s=1}^{p}\beta_s\chi^{R_s},\qquad \beta_s>0,\qquad \sum_{s=1}^{p}\beta_s=1,\)

where each \(R_s\) is a finite matching using only edges of \(H\).

Every \(R_s\) must match every vertex matched in \(Q\). Indeed, at such a vertex the displayed saturation equality expresses \(1\) as a positive convex combination of matching degrees in \(\{0,1\}\), so every component degree is \(1\).

Furthermore,

\(\displaystyle 0<\sum_{(i,j)\in H}w_{ij}y_{ij}=\sum_{s=1}^{p}\beta_s\sum_{(i,j)\in R_s}w_{ij}.\)

Thus some \(R_s\) has strictly positive inner sum. That matching weakly improves every finite vertex:

  • formerly matched vertices remain matched, to partners weakly preferred to their baselines;
  • formerly unmatched vertices either remain unmatched or receive acceptable partners.

Its positive inner sum means that at least one vertex strictly improves. Hence it Pareto-improves \(Q\), contradicting the finite Pareto-optimality of \(Q\). \(\square\)

The continuous realization also has exactly the same cost:

\(\displaystyle C(M_Q)=c(Q),\)

because each selected edge carries mass \(1\), and each pair is counted once.

4. Polynomial reduction and conclusion

The finite hardness premise in the question is supported by Kavitha's *Maximum Matchings and Popularity*: Theorem 11 establishes the satisfiability equivalence for cost zero, with strict preferences and edge costs in \(\{0,1\}\); Theorem 12 states the resulting hardness. [Source, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.03434)

Transform a finite instance into a continuous instance by making each finite vertex a distinct type of mass \(1\), copying its acceptable edges and strict preference list, copying the edge costs, and setting \(K=0\).

Section 3 shows that a finite Pareto-optimal matching of cost zero gives a continuous Pareto-optimal plan of cost zero. Section 2 proves the converse, including for continuous plans that split type masses among several partner types.

Thus the transformation preserves the answer. It adds only one constant-size mass encoding per vertex, and leaves the graph and preference lists unchanged. Its output length is polynomial in the finite input length, with \(\tau\) equal to the number of finite vertices and \(m\) equal to the number of finite edges.

Therefore the continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard already for unit masses and \(K=0\). A running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would consequently imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Step-by-step verification

1. Matching decomposition

The definition of \(P(G)\) correctly describes nonnegative edge masses with unit capacity at every vertex.

The total-unimodularity argument is valid:

  • Bipartiteness allows the row sign changes to give each edge column one positive and one negative entry.
  • In any square submatrix, a column with no nonzero entries gives determinant zero; a column with one nonzero entry permits induction by expansion.
  • Otherwise, each column contains both entries, so the row sum is zero and the determinant vanishes.
  • Appending the nonnegativity rows preserves this property by expansion along those rows. Reversing the earlier row sign changes also preserves it.

At a vertex, a full-rank collection of active constraints therefore has an integral right-hand side and determinant of absolute value \(1\). The stated use of Cramer’s rule correctly establishes integrality. Integral feasible vectors are precisely matching incidence vectors.

The polytope is nonempty and bounded: the zero vector is feasible, and each edge coordinate is at most \(1\). Consequently, its representation as the convex hull of its vertices applies. Removing any zero coefficients produces exactly the finite decomposition stated in Fact 1.

No rationality assumption on the vector being decomposed is needed. In particular, continuous plans with irrational edge masses are covered.

2. Measurable rearrangement

Fact 2 supplies both properties required later: selecting cohorts of a specified mass and matching equal-mass cohorts.

For a measurable set \(S\) in a bounded interval, its cumulative mass function is continuous and ranges from zero to its total mass. Thus intersections with initial intervals give every intermediate mass, including the endpoint cases.

For positive total mass, the generalized inverse is measurable. The stated identity relating the mass of parameters satisfying \(q_S(t)\leq a\) to \(F_S(a)\) correctly identifies its pushforward measure as Lebesgue measure restricted to \(S\).

The inverse identities also hold modulo null sets. Continuity gives \(F_S(q_S(t))=t\) for interior parameters. Failure of the reverse identity can occur in flat portions and their endpoints; the flat portions have zero \(S\)-measure, and the relevant endpoints form a countable set. Portions where the cumulative mass is zero or its full value likewise contribute no exceptional positive mass.

Consequently, after discarding null sets, the parametrizations yield the claimed measure-preserving measurable bijections with measurable inverses. This is sufficient because the task identifies plans up to null sets.

3. Continuous Pareto-optimality forces finite Pareto-optimality of every component

The mass vector of a unit-mass continuous plan belongs to \(P(G)\). For any positive-weight component \(Q_r\), putting \(\varepsilon=\alpha_r\) gives both necessary availability bounds:

  • Every edge of \(Q_r\) carries at least \(\varepsilon\) mass in the continuous plan.
  • Every vertex unmatched in \(Q_r\) has at least \(\varepsilon\) unmatched mass.

The unmatched-mass identity follows directly from the convex decomposition and the fact that its coefficients sum to \(1\).

The cohort construction is valid. Each vertex occurs in at most one edge of \(Q_r\), so selections cannot conflict within a type. Selecting actual partners under the original plan ensures that no original pair has exactly one selected endpoint. The selected partner sets are measurable because the original plan has a measurable inverse, and their masses agree by measure preservation.

If a finite matching \(R\) Pareto-improves \(Q_r\), replacing assignments on these cohorts is feasible. Every cohort has the same mass, Fact 2 supplies each required pairing, and the unchanged remainder consists of intact original pairs. The resulting finite union of restricted and replacement maps has the required measurability and measure-preservation properties.

Every selected individual has the baseline outcome prescribed by \(Q_r\). Therefore finite weak improvement translates into individual weak improvement. If \(h\geq1\) finite vertices strictly improve, the strictly improving individuals have total mass \(h\varepsilon>0\), counting individuals on both sides as required.

This contradicts continuous Pareto-optimality. The lemma correctly applies to every positive-weight component of any such decomposition.

4. Cost consequence

The cost identity follows by linearity, with each edge counted once.

Since the coefficients form a positive convex combination, some component has cost at most the continuous plan’s cost. By the preceding lemma, that component is finitely Pareto-optimal.

For cost zero, nonnegativity of the edge costs guarantees a finitely Pareto-optimal matching of cost zero. The converse implication needed for the reduction is therefore established without assuming an integral continuous plan.

5. A finite Pareto-optimal matching withstands continuous alternatives

The continuous realization \(M_Q\) is feasible: translations pair whole unit intervals, and unmatched types remain unmatched. Every individual of a type has the same baseline outcome.

The allowed edge set \(H\) correctly imposes weak improvement at both endpoints. Any positive mass assigned outside \(H\) would make a positive mass of individuals worse off. Thus a purported Pareto improvement uses such edges only on null sets.

For every vertex matched in \(Q\), its assigned mass under the alternative must remain \(1\). Otherwise, a positive mass of formerly matched individuals would become unmatched, contrary to weak improvement. This establishes the stated saturation equalities.

The weights \(w_{ij}\) correctly count strictly improving endpoints. Because baseline outcomes are constant within each type, the weighted sum is exactly the total mass of strictly improving individuals. Unmatched individuals contribute no omitted strict improvement.

Applying Fact 1 to \(P(H)\) is legitimate. At a saturated vertex, a positive convex combination of component degrees in \(\{0,1\}\) equals \(1\) only if every component degree equals \(1\). Hence every component retains all formerly matched vertices.

The positive weighted sum guarantees a component with a strictly improving endpoint. That component weakly improves every finite vertex:

  • Formerly matched vertices remain matched along allowed edges.
  • Formerly unmatched vertices remain unmatched or receive acceptable partners.

It therefore Pareto-improves \(Q\), giving the required contradiction. This argument covers all continuous alternatives, including those splitting types among several partners.

The cost equality for \(M_Q\) is also correct: every selected edge carries exactly unit mass.
