Corollary 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an ordered finite support of \(\tau\) single-peaked types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and a unique median type \(r\), determine the least interaction time \(s\) at which the triadic mass process places at least \(1-\epsilon\) of its mass on \(r\), and provide a certified bound on that time. The process starts at \(\nu(0)=\mu\); at each infinitesimal interaction, three types are drawn independently from \(\nu\), and their mass is relabeled to the median type. Establish how this accuracy guarantee relates to the finite-population process as population size grows.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: an ordered list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). The support size \(\tau\) counts present types, not possible types. Types and candidates coincide, so \(m=\tau\); type \(i\) has strict single-peaked preferences with peak \(i\).
Assume a unique median type \(r\), meaning \(\sum_{i<r}\mu_i<1/2<\sum_{i\leq r}\mu_i\). Every interaction relabels all three sampled types to their median, including repeated types.
Define the deterministic mass process by \(\nu(0)=\mu\) and \(\dot\nu_i(s)=\mathbb E[3\mathbf1\{\operatorname{med}(X_1,X_2,X_3)=i\}-\sum_{j=1}^3\mathbf1\{X_j=i\}]\), where the samples are independent with distribution \(\nu(s)\).
Given binary rationals \(\epsilon,\eta,\delta\in(0,1)\), put \(s_\epsilon=\inf\{s\geq0:\nu_r(s)\geq1-\epsilon\}\), with \(\inf\varnothing=\infty\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For comparison, initialize an urn with \(N\mu_i\) balls of type \(i\), taking \(N\) through common denominator multiples. Each step samples three balls uniformly with replacement and relabels each distinct sampled ball to the median sampled type. Let \(\nu^{(N)}(s)\) be its empirical distribution after \(\lfloor Ns\rfloor\) steps.

Can a certified additive-\(\eta\) approximation to \(s_\epsilon\), or detection that it is infinite, be computed in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\eta)\), or is this approximation problem NP-hard?
What quantitative guarantees with confidence \(1-\delta\) relate this mass accuracy to \(\nu^{(N)}\) and the eventual winning type as \(N\to\infty\)?

Discrete: with \(n\) ordered participants and \(k=O(a^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) copies per participant, the winner has rank error \(O(a\sqrt n)\) from the median with probability at least \(1-\delta\); expected total comparisons are \(O(kn\log^2(kn))\). [Goel–Lee](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.0664)
Continuous: for a uniform distribution on \([0,1]\), the median of one sampled triad has density \(6x(1-x)\); this concerns one interaction, not the evolving mass process. No result settling the stated computational task was located. [Goel–Lee](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.0664)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive masses to sum to one.
  • Identified each ordered type with its own candidate and peak, so m equals tau; preferences are strict and single-peaked.
  • Specified unique median by strict inequalities on the cumulative masses surrounding its atom.
  • Interpreted infinitesimal interactions as deterministic expected relabeling drift, with time s equal to the number of finite-urn interactions divided by N.
  • Included repeated types in median selection and relabeled each distinct sampled ball only once in the finite process.
  • Introduced binary-rational additive precision eta because the least interaction time need not have a finite rational representation; allowed infinity and initial attainment at time zero.
  • Fixed finite populations to have exact counts N mu_i along common denominator multiples, and introduced delta for their probabilistic accuracy guarantees.

1. Result

The stated approximation problem is polynomial-time solvable. Under the strict median hypothesis, \(s_\epsilon\) is always finite. A deterministic algorithm can output rational endpoints \(s_-\) and \(s_+\) satisfying

\[ 0\leq s_-\leq s_\epsilon\leq s_+, \qquad s_+-s_-\leq\eta, \]

in time polynomial in \(\tau,L,\log(1/\eta)\). Since \(m=\tau\), this gives the requested complexity bound.

The finite urn admits both an explicit finite-time concentration bound and an exact formula for its eventual winning type. The population size needed for these guarantees depends on the initial median gap and need not be polynomial in the input bit length.

2. Exact deterministic dynamics

Define the cumulative masses

\[ F_i(s)=\sum_{j\leq i}\nu_j(s), \qquad F_0(s)=0,\qquad F_\tau(s)=1. \]

A sampled median is at most \(i\) exactly when at least two sampled types are at most \(i\). Independence therefore gives

\[ \Pr(\operatorname{med}(X_1,X_2,X_3)\leq i) =3F_i^2(1-F_i)+F_i^3. \]

Summing the prescribed mass derivatives over types at most \(i\) yields

\[ \dot F_i =3(3F_i^2-2F_i^3-F_i) =f(F_i), \qquad f(x)=3x(1-x)(2x-1). \]

Thus the cumulative masses satisfy independent scalar differential equations.

The scalar flow preserves its initial order: two solutions cannot cross, by uniqueness for the polynomial vector field \(f\). Since \(0\) and \(1\) are stationary solutions, the resulting cumulative masses remain ordered in \([0,1]\). Their successive differences consequently define a distribution solving the original system. This also establishes global existence and uniqueness.

Explicit solution below one half

For \(0\leq q<1/2\), let \(Q(s;q)\) solve \(\dot Q=f(Q)\) with \(Q(0;q)=q\). Set \(y=1-2Q\). Direct substitution gives

\[ \dot y=\frac32y(1-y^2). \]

Consequently, \(w=y^{-2}-1\) satisfies \(\dot w=-3w\). Therefore

\[ Q(s;q) =\frac12\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt{1+C(q)e^{-3s}}}\right), \qquad C(q)=\frac{4q(1-q)}{(1-2q)^2}. \]

This formula includes \(q=0\), for which \(C(q)=0\) and \(Q(s;0)=0\). Also, \(f(1-x)=-f(x)\), so the complementary mass of a cumulative solution starting above one half follows this same formula.

Write

\[ a=F_{r-1}(0),\qquad b=1-F_r(0), \qquad A=C(a),\qquad B=C(b). \]

The strict median assumption gives \(a,b<1/2\). Hence

\[ \nu_r(s) =1-Q(s;a)-Q(s;b) =\frac12\left( \frac1{\sqrt{1+Ae^{-3s}}} +\frac1{\sqrt{1+Be^{-3s}}} \right). \]

If \(\tau=1\), then \(\nu_r(s)=1\) and \(s_\epsilon=0\). Otherwise, \(A+B>0\), so \(\nu_r(s)\) is strictly increasing and tends to \(1\). Since \(\epsilon>0\), the hitting time is finite on every promised input.

3. Certified polynomial-time computation

First test the rational inequality \(\mu_r\geq1-\epsilon\). If it holds, output \(s_-=s_+=0\).

Otherwise, put \(z=e^{-3s}\) and define

\[ G(z)=\frac12\left( (1+Az)^{-1/2}+(1+Bz)^{-1/2} \right). \]

There is a unique \(z_*\in(0,1)\) satisfying \(G(z_*)=1-\epsilon\), and

\[ s_\epsilon=-\frac13\log z_*. \]

We can bracket \(z_*\) by exact rational comparisons, without numerically comparing nearly equal transcendental quantities.

Exact comparison oracle

For a rational test point \(z>0\), set

\[ u=\frac1{1+Az},\qquad v=\frac1{1+Bz},\qquad c=2(1-\epsilon),\qquad D=c^2-u-v. \]

Comparing \(G(z)\) with \(1-\epsilon\) is equivalent to comparing \(\sqrt u+\sqrt v\) with \(c\).

If \(D<0\), then \((\sqrt u+\sqrt v)^2>c^2\), so \(G(z)>1-\epsilon\). If \(D\geq0\), squaring quantities whose signs are now known gives

\[ \operatorname{sign}(G(z)-(1-\epsilon)) =\operatorname{sign}(4uv-D^2). \]

Thus each comparison, including equality detection, uses exact rational arithmetic.

A bracket bounded away from zero

For \(x\geq0\),

\[ 1-(1+x)^{-1/2}\leq x/2, \]

because the derivative of the left-hand side is at most \(1/2\). It follows that

\[ 1-G(z)\leq\frac{(A+B)z}{4}. \]

Set \(h=2\epsilon/(A+B)\). Our initial test failed, so

\[ \epsilon<1-G(1)\leq(A+B)/4. \]

Therefore \(0<h<1/2\), and \(1-G(h)\leq\epsilon/2\). In particular,

\[ h<z_*<1. \]

Bisect this bracket until its rational endpoints \(z_-\leq z_*\leq z_+\) satisfy \(z_+-z_-\leq h\eta\). The resulting exact time bracket has width

\[ \frac13\log\frac{z_+}{z_-} \leq\frac{z_+-z_-}{3z_-} \leq\frac{\eta}{3}. \]

Compute outward rational enclosures of its two logarithmic endpoints, each of width at most \(\eta/6\). Taking the outer endpoints, and replacing a negative lower endpoint by zero, produces a certified time interval of width at most \(\eta\).

Bit complexity

The cumulative sums, \(A,B,h\), and all initial rational quantities have bit length \(O(L)\). In particular, \(\log(1/h)=O(L)\). The number of bisections is therefore \(O(L+\log(1/\eta))\), and the rational operands retain polynomial bit length.

For completeness, certified logarithms can be computed by binary range reduction and the series

\[ \log u =2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad w=\frac{u-1}{u+1}, \qquad 1\leq u\leq2. \]

Here \(0\leq w\leq1/3\). After retaining terms through \(j=K-1\), the remaining positive tail is at most

\[ \frac{2w^{2K+1}}{(2K+1)(1-w^2)}. \]

The same series computes \(\log2\). Range reduction introduces only \(O(L)\) multiples of \(\log2\), so polynomially many terms suffice for the required certified precision.

This proves the claimed polynomial-time algorithm. Detection of infinity is unnecessary on valid inputs, because Section 2 excludes it.

4. Quantitative finite-time urn approximation

Let

\[ F_i^{(N)}(s)=\sum_{j\leq i}\nu_j^{(N)}(s). \]

Assume \(\tau\geq2\), \(N\) is an allowed denominator multiple, \(T>0\), and \(0<\delta_0<1\). Define

\[ E_N(T,\delta_0) =e^{3T}\left( \sqrt{\frac{8T}{N}\log\frac{2(\tau-1)}{\delta_0}} +\frac{9T}{8N} \right) +\frac{3}{4N}. \]

Then, with probability at least \(1-\delta_0\),

\[ \sup_{0\leq s\leq T} \max_{1\leq i<\tau} |F_i^{(N)}(s)-F_i(s)| \leq E_N(T,\delta_0). \]

Consequently, on the same event,

\[ \sup_{0\leq s\leq T} \max_i|\nu_i^{(N)}(s)-\nu_i(s)| \leq2E_N(T,\delta_0), \]

and

\[ \sup_{0\leq s\leq T} \|\nu^{(N)}(s)-\nu(s)\|_1 \leq2\tau E_N(T,\delta_0). \]

For \(\tau=1\), both processes are identical.

Proof, including repeated sampled balls

Fix a cutoff \(i\), and let \(K_j\) be the number of balls with type at most \(i\) after \(j\) steps. Conditional on \(K_j=k\), put \(p=k/N\).

The median lies on the majority side of the cutoff. Thus

\[ \Pr(K_{j+1}=k+1\mid K_j=k)=3p^2(1-p), \]

\[ \Pr(K_{j+1}=k-1\mid K_j=k)=3p(1-p)^2. \]

These formulas remain exact when a ball is sampled repeatedly. In a sample split between the two sides, the minority side occupies exactly one sampled position, so exactly one ball changes sides. Repeating a majority-side ball does not change that conclusion.

Writing \(Z_j=K_j/N\), we obtain

\[ \mathbb E[Z_{j+1}-Z_j\mid\mathcal F_j]=f(Z_j)/N. \]

The centered increments have absolute value at most \(2/N\). Maximal Azuma–Hoeffding therefore gives, for \(J=\lfloor NT\rfloor\),

\[ \Pr\left(\max_{j\leq J}|M_j|>u\right) \leq2\exp\left(-\frac{N^2u^2}{8J}\right), \]

where \(M_j\) is the cumulative centered increment. For \(J=0\), this martingale is identically zero. A union bound over the \(\tau-1\) cutoffs gives the square-root term in \(E_N\).

On \([0,1]\),

\[ |f(x)|\leq3/4,\qquad |f'(x)|=|-18x^2+18x-3|\leq3. \]

Consequently, each exact ODE step has Euler remainder at most

\[ \left| F_i((j+1)/N)-F_i(j/N)-f(F_i(j/N))/N \right| \leq\frac{9}{8N^2}. \]

Indeed, the integrand changes by at most \(3(3/4)t\) over an interval of length \(1/N\). Summing these remainders and applying discrete Grönwall gives the factor \(e^{3T}\) and the term \(9T/(8N)\). Between grid points, the deterministic cumulative mass changes by at most \(3/(4N)\), while the urn process is constant. This proves the stated bound.

Consequences for mass and hitting time

Use the certified upper endpoint \(s_+\) from Section 3. Taking \(T\geq s_+\), the preceding event implies

\[ \nu_r^{(N)}(s_+) \geq1-\epsilon-2E_N(T,\delta_0). \]

Thus the computed time comes with an explicit finite-population mass guarantee.

There is also a quantitative guarantee for the urn hitting time

\[ S_\epsilon^{(N)} =\inf\{s\geq0:\nu_r^{(N)}(s)\geq1-\epsilon\}. \]

Let

\[ \gamma=\min\{1/2-a,\;1/2-b\}>0, \qquad \kappa=2E_N(T,\delta_0). \]

If \(\kappa<\min\{\epsilon,1-\epsilon\}\) and \(T\geq s_{\epsilon-\kappa}\), then, on the same event,

\[ s_{\epsilon+\kappa} \leq S_\epsilon^{(N)} \leq s_{\epsilon-\kappa}. \]

Before the lower endpoint, the deterministic median mass plus its error allowance is below the target. At the upper endpoint, the deterministic mass minus that allowance reaches the target. This argument does not require the urn mass to be monotone.

To convert this bracket into a time error, let \(D(s)=1-\nu_r(s)\). Each outside tail \(q\) is nonincreasing and satisfies

\[ -\dot q=3q(1-q)(1-2q)\geq3\gamma q, \]

since \(1-q\geq1/2\) and \(1-2q\geq2\gamma\). Hence \(-\dot D\geq3\gamma D\). Integrating between successive target levels gives

\[ |S_\epsilon^{(N)}-s_\epsilon| \leq\frac1{3\gamma} \log\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon-\kappa}. \]

If \(s_\epsilon=0\), the urn also starts above the target and its hitting time is exactly zero.

For fixed input and fixed \(T\), \(E_N(T,\delta_0)\) tends to zero along the allowed denominator multiples.

5. Exact winner law and confidence bounds

The cutoff reduction and binomial absorption law appear in [Goel–Lee, Sections 3.1–3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.0664). Here they apply to arbitrary initial masses, as the following direct argument verifies.

Let \(W_N\) denote the eventual winning type, and set

\[ k_i=N F_i(0),\qquad k_0=0,\qquad k_\tau=N. \]

Define

\[ H_N(k) =2^{-(N-1)} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{N-1}{j}, \qquad H_N(0)=0. \]

Then

\[ \Pr(W_N\leq i)=H_N(k_i), \qquad \Pr(W_N=i)=H_N(k_i)-H_N(k_{i-1}). \]

Equivalently, if \(B_N\) has distribution \(\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,1/2)\), the winner has the type containing initial ball rank \(B_N+1\).

Proof of absorption and the formula

For a cutoff count, both transition probabilities are positive at every interior state. From every such state, consecutive upward moves can reach \(N\). Since there are finitely many states, there is a uniform positive probability of reaching a boundary within \(N\) steps. Iterating this bound proves almost-sure absorption.

Every cutoff therefore absorbs almost surely. There are finitely many cutoffs, and absorbed cutoffs remain absorbed. Once all have absorbed, all balls have one type.

Let \(h(k)\) be the probability that a cutoff absorbs at \(N\). Its harmonic equation gives

\[ p_+(k)(h(k+1)-h(k)) =p_-(k)(h(k)-h(k-1)). \]

Writing \(d_k=h(k)-h(k-1)\), we obtain

\[ d_{k+1}=\frac{N-k}{k}d_k. \]

Thus \(d_k=d_1\binom{N-1}{k-1}\). The boundary conditions \(h(0)=0\), \(h(N)=1\) imply \(d_1=2^{-(N-1)}\), proving \(h=H_N\). Applying this to each cutoff proves the winner law.

Exponential concentration on the median type

The two ways to miss \(r\) are

\[ W_N<r \iff B_N\leq Na-1, \]

\[ W_N>r \iff B_N\geq N(1-b), \]

where these equivalences describe the binomial representation of the winner law.

The binomial mean is \((N-1)/2\). The corresponding distances from that mean are

\[ N(1/2-a)+1/2, \qquad N(1/2-b)+1/2. \]

Both are at least \(N\gamma\). Hoeffding’s inequality for the \(N-1\) independent Bernoulli variables therefore yields

\[ \Pr(W_N\neq r) \leq2\exp\left(-\frac{2N^2\gamma^2}{N-1}\right) \leq2e^{-2N\gamma^2}. \]

In particular,

\[ N\geq\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2\gamma^2} \]

suffices for \(r\) to win with probability at least \(1-\delta\). Consequently, \(\Pr(W_N=r)\) tends to \(1\) as \(N\) tends to infinity.

Joint guarantee with confidence \(1-\delta\)

Use \(\delta_0=\delta/2\) in the finite-time mass bound, and require

\[ N\geq\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2\gamma^2}. \]

A union bound then shows that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), both of the following hold:

  • The cumulative and type-mass approximation bounds hold throughout \([0,T]\), including the guarantee at the computed time \(s_+\leq T\).
  • The eventual winning type is \(r\).

There is also a guarantee obtained directly from the mass at \(s_+\). Put \(c=\epsilon+2E_N(T,\delta_0)\). If \(c<1/2\), then on the mass-accuracy event each outside tail at \(s_+\) is at most \(c\). Applying the absorption bound conditionally on that urn state gives

\[ \Pr(W_N\neq r) \leq\delta_0+ 2\exp\left(-2N(1/2-c)^2\right). \]

This explicitly connects certified near-consensus mass to the eventual winner.

6. Scope of the conclusion

The deterministic hitting-time computation is polynomial in the encoded input and requested precision. The finite-population requirements have different scaling: the displayed trajectory estimate contains \(e^{3T}\), and winner concentration requires a population proportional to \(\gamma^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\). Since the median gap can be exponentially small in \(L\), these population requirements can be exponential in the input length.

All guarantees above concern the specified strict-median, finite-support model, with replacement sampling and relabeling of each distinct sampled ball. No interchange of the infinite-time and large-population limits is needed: the trajectory estimate handles fixed time horizons, and the exact absorption law handles the eventual winner separately.
