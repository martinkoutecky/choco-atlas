Result

The assertion fails for some admissible sequence. In fact, there is a sequence with \(k(n)=0\) and \(d(n)\to\infty\), \(d(n)=o((\log n)^2)\), along which the probability of the simultaneous event tends to \(0\).

The obstruction comes from the unmatched-mass bound. We prove a sufficiently strong upper bound on its expectation along a slowly growing degree sequence. The relevant coefficient is

\(c=\sqrt{\frac{3+\sqrt5}{4}}>1.\)

The proof treats each type’s mass and preference list together. Thus it does not apply the discrete-agent theorem to clones with correlated preferences; the cited [discrete theorem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.14653) does not justify such an application.

1. A deterministic bound for every stable allocation

Let \(G\) be a finite bipartite compatibility graph, with capacities \(b_v>0\) and strict neighbor rankings. In this section, capacities need not be integers.

For each directed compatible pair \(v\to w\), define the operator

\((Tq)_{v\to w}=\left[b_v-\sum_{z\succ_v w}q_{z\to v}\right]_+,\)

where \(z\succ_v w\) means that \(v\) strictly prefers \(z\) to \(w\), and \([s]_+=\max\{s,0\}\). The operator reverses coordinatewise inequalities.

We first establish its connection with stability.

Lemma 1. For any stable allocation \(x\), the quantities

\(q^*_{v\to w}=b_v-\sum_{z\succ_v w}x_{vz}\)

satisfy \(q^*=Tq^*\). Moreover,

\(x_{vw}=\min\{q^*_{v\to w},q^*_{w\to v}\}\)

and

\(u_v=\left[b_v-\sum_{w\sim v}q^*_{w\to v}\right]_+.\)

Proof. Feasibility gives \(q^*_{v\to w}\ge x_{vw}\). The difference \(q^*_{v\to w}-x_{vw}\) is precisely the mass at \(v\) that is unmatched or assigned to types worse than \(w\). Hence stability says that these differences cannot be positive at both endpoints. Consequently, \(x_{vw}\) equals the smaller of the two directed quantities.

List \(v\)’s neighbors in preference order as \(w_1,\ldots,w_\ell\). The preceding identity gives

\(x_{vw_j}=\min\left\{b_v-\sum_{a<j}x_{vw_a},q^*_{w_j\to v}\right\}.\)

Induction on \(j\) therefore yields

\(\sum_{a\le j}x_{vw_a}=\min\left\{b_v,\sum_{a\le j}q^*_{w_a\to v}\right\}.\)

Taking the prefix before \(w_j\) proves \(q^*=Tq^*\); taking the entire list proves the unmatched-mass formula. \(\square\)

The converse also holds: if \(q=Tq\), then defining \(x_{vw}=\min\{q_{v\to w},q_{w\to v}\}\) gives a feasible stable allocation. Indeed, the same prefix induction proves feasibility and identifies \(q_{v\to w}\) with the residual capacity before \(w\). At every edge, at least one endpoint’s residual capacity equals \(x_{vw}\), so that edge cannot block. This argument also applies to any locally finite infinite graph.

Now initialize \(q^{(0)}=0\) and iterate \(q^{(h+1)}=Tq^{(h)}\). Antitonicity gives

\(q^{(0)}\le q^{(2)}\le\cdots\le q^*\le\cdots\le q^{(3)}\le q^{(1)}.\)

It follows that, for every stable allocation,

\[u_v\le g_t(v):=\left[b_v-\sum_{w\sim v}q^{(2t)}_{w\to v}\right]_+.\tag{1}\]

The bound \(g_t(v)\) depends only on a finite neighborhood of \(v\), of radius at most \(2t+1\). For a fixed neighborhood and rankings, it is continuous in the capacities: its defining operations are finite sums, subtraction, and positive parts.

These facts will let us transfer an infinite-tree calculation to the normalized finite markets.

2. The fixed-degree limiting trees

Fix an integer \(d\ge2\), and temporarily consider balanced markets with \(n\) types on each side and unnormalized capacities \(b_v\), independently uniform on \(\{1,2\}\).

The neighborhood of a uniformly chosen male type converges, at every fixed radius as \(n\to\infty\), to the following random bipartite tree:

  • A male root has exactly \(d\) female neighbors.
  • A male reached through an edge has \(d-1\) other female neighbors.
  • A female root has a \(\operatorname{Poisson}(d)\) number of male neighbors.
  • A female reached through an edge has a \(\operatorname{Poisson}(d)\) number of other male neighbors.

All capacities are independent and uniform on \(\{1,2\}\). Rankings are independent and uniform.

Here is a direct justification of the local convergence. A fixed number of male choices collide with probability \(O(n^{-1})\). For a fixed collection of exposed women, an unexposed man chooses two or more of them with probability \(O(n^{-2})\); the probability that any of the \(n\) unexposed men does this is \(O(n^{-1})\). Counts of men adjacent to the exposed women converge jointly to independent Poisson variables: their joint probability generating function has the form

\(\left(1+\frac d n\sum_a(z_a-1)+O(n^{-2})\right)^{n-O(1)} \longrightarrow \exp\left(d\sum_a(z_a-1)\right).\)

This argument applies successively during exploration. For a fixed radius, the explored size is tight because its expectation is bounded by a constant depending only on \(d\) and the radius. Truncating the exploration at a fixed size and then removing that truncation proves the stated convergence. The independent weights and rankings carry over as well.

We now run the message iterations on these trees. All iterates take values in \(\{0,1,2\}\). Their even and odd subsequences have pointwise limits \(l\) and \(h\), respectively. Finite vertex degrees allow passage through \(T\), giving

\(l=T(h),\qquad h=T(l).\)

Choose the mixed message configuration

\(q_{v\to w}=h_{v\to w}\quad\text{if }v\in M,\qquad q_{v\to w}=l_{v\to w}\quad\text{if }v\in W.\)

Because the graph is bipartite, this configuration satisfies \(q=Tq\). It therefore defines a stable allocation by Lemma 1 and its converse.

For a male root, the bound \(g_t\) in (1) decreases to the unmatched capacity in this mixed allocation. Write its expectation as \(U_M\); write the expected unmatched capacity at a female root in the same allocation as \(U_W\).

No uniqueness assertion for infinite-tree stable allocations is needed.

3. Exact equations for the tree messages

Let \(Q_M,Q_W\) denote the directed messages at the two endpoints of a distinguished edge, directed toward each other. Each message depends only on the component on its own side after that edge is removed, together with its endpoint’s ranking of the distinguished edge. Thus \(Q_M\) and \(Q_W\) are independent.

Put

\(p_{S,1}=\Pr(Q_S=1),\qquad p_{S,2}=\Pr(Q_S=2),\qquad p_S=p_{S,1}+p_{S,2},\qquad r_S=\frac{p_{S,1}}{p_S}.\)

The probabilities \(p_M,p_W\) are positive. For example, a capacity-\(2\) endpoint sends message \(2\) whenever no preferred neighbor sends a positive message, an event of positive probability for both cavity-degree distributions.

We derive the message probabilities. Suppose an endpoint has \(N\) neighbors preferred to the distinguished edge, and their independent incoming messages have probabilities \(p_1,p_2\), with \(p=p_1+p_2\). Define

\(A(p)=\mathbb E(1-p)^N,\qquad B(p)=\mathbb E\bigl[N(1-p)^{N-1}\bigr].\)

A message equals \(2\) precisely when the endpoint has capacity \(2\) and receives no positive preferred message. A message equals \(1\) when either:

  • its capacity is \(1\) and it receives no positive preferred message; or
  • its capacity is \(2\) and it receives exactly one message of size \(1\), with all other preferred messages zero.

Consequently,

\[\Pr(Q=2)=\frac12 A(p),\qquad \Pr(Q=1)=\frac12 A(p)+\frac12 p_1B(p).\tag{2}\]

In particular, \(1/2\le r_S\le1\).

At a male endpoint, \(N\) is uniform on \(\{0,\ldots,d-1\}\). Thus

\(A_D(p)=\frac{1-(1-p)^d}{dp},\qquad B_D(p)=\frac{1-(1-p)^d-dp(1-p)^{d-1}}{dp^2}.\)

At a female endpoint, assign independent uniform marks in \([0,1]\) to rank neighbors. Conditional on the distinguished edge’s mark \(a\), the number of preferred other neighbors is \(\operatorname{Poisson}(da)\). Integrating over \(a\) gives

\(A_P(p)=\frac{1-e^{-dp}}{dp},\qquad B_P(p)=\frac{1-(1+dp)e^{-dp}}{dp^2}.\)

Equation (2) therefore implies

\[p_M=A_D(p_W)+\frac12p_{W,1}B_D(p_W),\qquad p_W=A_P(p_M)+\frac12p_{M,1}B_P(p_M).\tag{3}\]

Set

\(\lambda_M=dp_M,\qquad \lambda_W=dp_W,\qquad T=dp_Mp_W=\frac{\lambda_M\lambda_W}{d},\)

and define

\(H_W=1-(1-p_W)^d,\qquad J_W=H_W-\lambda_W(1-p_W)^{d-1},\)

\(H_M=1-e^{-\lambda_M},\qquad J_M=H_M-\lambda_Me^{-\lambda_M}.\)

Multiplying (3) by the appropriate denominators yields

\[T=H_W+\frac12r_WJ_W =H_M+\frac12r_MJ_M.\tag{4}\]

The size-\(2\) message probabilities in (2) also give

\[r_M=1-\frac{H_W}{2T},\qquad r_W=1-\frac{H_M}{2T}.\tag{5}\]

Finally, applying the same residual-capacity calculation to an entire root neighborhood gives

\[U_M=\frac32(1-p_W)^d +\frac12r_W\lambda_W(1-p_W)^{d-1},\tag{6}\]

\[U_W=e^{-\lambda_M} \left(\frac32+\frac12r_M\lambda_M\right).\tag{7}\]

4. Balance of the two tree unmatched expectations

We need the exact identity

\[U_M=U_W.\tag{8}\]

Here is its justification, including the distinction between root and cavity degrees.

At a male root, each of its \(d\) incident edges has the distinguished-edge law used above. Its expected matched capacity is therefore

\(d\,\mathbb E\min\{Q_M,Q_W\}.\)

At a female root, summing over its Poisson number of incident edges gives the same expression. To see this, the elementary Poisson identity

\(\mathbb E[D f(D-1)]=d\,\mathbb E[f(D)],\qquad D\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(d),\)

shows that selecting an incident edge in this sum leaves a \(\operatorname{Poisson}(d)\) number of other female neighbors. At its male endpoint there are \(d-1\) other neighbors. Thus the edge law is exactly the same distinguished-edge law as for the male-root calculation.

Both root capacities have expectation \(3/2\). Subtracting their equal expected matched capacities proves (8).

5. The unmatched exponent is strictly larger than \(\sqrt d\)

We prove

\[\lambda_M=c\sqrt d+o(\sqrt d),\qquad \lambda_W=c\sqrt d+o(\sqrt d),\tag{9}\]

and consequently

\[\log U_M=\log U_W=-c\sqrt d+o(\sqrt d).\tag{10}\]

All statements concern the mixed tree allocation constructed above as \(d\to\infty\).

5.1 Both message intensities diverge

Equation (4) gives

\[T\le\frac32,\qquad T\ge H_M,\qquad T\ge H_W.\tag{11}\]

From (6)–(7),

\[U_M\le e\left(\frac32+\frac12\lambda_W\right)e^{-\lambda_W},\qquad U_W\le\left(\frac32+\frac12\lambda_M\right)e^{-\lambda_M}.\tag{12}\]

For the first bound, use
\((1-p_W)^d\le e^{-\lambda_W}\) and
\((1-p_W)^{d-1}\le e^{-\lambda_W+p_W}\le e\,e^{-\lambda_W}\).

Suppose \(\lambda_M\) stays bounded along a subsequence. Equation (7) bounds \(U_W\) away from zero. By (8) and (12), \(\lambda_W\) must also stay bounded. Conversely, bounded \(\lambda_W\) bounds \(U_M\) away from zero, because

\(U_M\ge\frac32(1-\lambda_W/d)^d.\)

Thus either intensity staying bounded forces both to stay bounded along that subsequence.

Then \(T=\lambda_M\lambda_W/d\to0\). From (11), and from \(H_W\ge1-e^{-\lambda_W}\), both intensities must actually tend to zero. For sufficiently small \(\lambda_M>0\),

\(T\ge1-e^{-\lambda_M}\ge\frac12\lambda_M.\)

Dividing by \(\lambda_M\) gives \(\lambda_W/d\ge1/2\), contradicting bounded \(\lambda_W\). Hence

\(\lambda_M\to\infty,\qquad\lambda_W\to\infty.\)

5.2 Their product has a non-unit limiting coefficient

The divergence just proved implies

\(H_M,H_W,J_M,J_W\to1.\)

For \(J_W\), the additional term vanishes because

\(\lambda_W(1-p_W)^{d-1}\le e\,\lambda_We^{-\lambda_W}\to0.\)

Equations (4)–(5) now give

\(T=1+\frac12r_W+o(1) =1+\frac12r_M+o(1),\)

and

\(r_M=1-\frac1{2T}+o(1),\qquad r_W=1-\frac1{2T}+o(1).\)

Thus

\(T=\frac32-\frac1{4T}+o(1).\)

By (11), \(T\ge1-o(1)\) and \(T\le3/2\). Every subsequential limit must therefore be the larger root of

\(T^2-\frac32T+\frac14=0.\)

It follows that

\[T\longrightarrow\frac{3+\sqrt5}{4}=c^2.\tag{13}\]

5.3 Balance identifies the individual intensities

Using (7), (8), and (12),

\(\frac32e^{-\lambda_M} \le U_W=U_M \le e\left(\frac32+\frac12\lambda_W\right)e^{-\lambda_W}.\)

Since \(\lambda_W\le d\), this implies

\[\lambda_W\le\lambda_M+O(\log d).\tag{14}\]

Together with \(\lambda_M\lambda_W\le3d/2\), it implies \(\lambda_W=O(\sqrt d)\): if \(\lambda_W\le\lambda_M\), use the product bound directly; otherwise use (14) and the product bound.

Hence \(p_W=O(d^{-1/2})\), and

\(d\log(1-p_W)=-\lambda_W+O(1).\)

Taking logarithms in (6) gives

\(\log U_M=-\lambda_W+O(\log d).\)

Equation (7) similarly gives

\(\log U_W=-\lambda_M+O(\log d).\)

Their equality proves
\(\lambda_M-\lambda_W=O(\log d)\).
Combining this with (13) proves (9), and then (10).

In particular, for every fixed \(\beta<c\), all sufficiently large \(d\) satisfy

\[U_M<e^{-\beta\sqrt d}.\tag{15}\]

6. Transfer to the normalized finite markets

Return to the prescribed random family, choosing \(k=0\). Let

\(B_S=\sum_{v\in S}b_v,\qquad \mu_v=b_v/B_S.\)

Scale every mass by \(3n/2\), obtaining capacities

\(\widehat b_v=\frac{3n}{2}\mu_v =b_v\frac{3n/2}{B_S}.\)

Scaling preserves stability. Since the weights have mean \(3/2\), the law of large numbers gives

\(\frac{3n/2}{B_M}\to1,\qquad \frac{3n/2}{B_W}\to1\)

in probability. Also, \(0<\widehat b_v\le3\) deterministically.

Let \(I\) be a uniformly chosen male type, independently of the instance. Apply the bound (1), with these scaled capacities, to the selected stable allocation. Its scaled unmatched masses satisfy

\(\Delta_M =\frac{2}{3n}\sum_{i\in M}\widehat u_i \le\frac{2}{3n}\sum_{i\in M}g_t(i).\)

Therefore,

\[\mathbb E\Delta_M\le\frac23\,\mathbb E g_t(I).\tag{16}\]

For fixed \(d,t\), the local convergence in Section 2, the convergence of the two normalization factors, and continuity of \(g_t\) imply

\[\mathbb E g_t(I)\longrightarrow \mathbb E g_t(\text{male tree root}).\tag{17}\]

For completeness, the expectation convergence follows by truncating neighborhood size, applying convergence on each finite neighborhood, and then removing the truncation. The uniform bound \(0\le g_t\le3\) controls the discarded part. Dependence between the normalization factors and local weights causes no difficulty: the factors converge in probability to constants.

On the tree, \(g_t\) decreases to the male-root unmatched capacity of the mixed allocation. Since \(0\le g_t\le2\), bounded convergence gives

\[\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb E g_t(\text{male tree root})=U_M.\tag{18}\]

Fix constants \(1<\alpha<\beta<c\). By (10), for every sufficiently large fixed \(d\), we can choose a finite \(t=t(d)\) such that

\(\mathbb E g_{t(d)}(\text{male tree root}) \le e^{-\beta\sqrt d}.\)

Equations (16)–(17) then show that there is a threshold \(N_d\) such that, for every \(n\ge N_d\), the normalized finite market of degree \(d\) satisfies

\[\mathbb E\Delta_M\le e^{-\alpha\sqrt d}.\tag{19}\]

This conclusion applies to the men-optimal stable allocation because the deterministic bound applies to every stable allocation.

7. An admissible sequence whose success probability tends to zero

Increase the thresholds \(N_d\), if necessary, so that they are strictly increasing and

\(N_d\ge\lceil e^{d^2}\rceil.\)

For sufficiently large \(n\), define

\(d(n)=\max\{d:N_d\le n\},\qquad k(n)=0.\)

For the finitely many earlier indices, take \(d(n)=1\).

Every threshold is finite, so \(d(n)\to\infty\). Moreover,

\(d(n)\le\sqrt{\log n},\)

which implies \(d(n)=o((\log n)^2)\). The constraints \(d(n)\le n\) and \(|k(n)|\le n^{1-\epsilon}\) also hold. Since every male list has length \(d(n)\), the mass-weighted quantity called \(d\) in the task is exactly \(d(n)\).

Let \(E_n\) be the simultaneous event asked for in the question. Its male unmatched-mass condition requires

\(\Delta_M\ge e^{-\sqrt{d(n)}-d(n)^{0.3}}.\)

Markov’s inequality and (19) therefore give

\(\Pr(E_n) \le e^{\sqrt{d(n)}+d(n)^{0.3}}\mathbb E\Delta_M \le \exp\left(-(\alpha-1)\sqrt{d(n)}+d(n)^{0.3}\right) \longrightarrow0.\)

The last limit holds because \(d^{0.3}=o(\sqrt d)\).

Thus the proposed unmatched-mass estimate already fails along this admissible sequence, regardless of the rank estimates.

8. Computational parameters

The proof uses no expansion into individuals. For a support graph with \(e\) compatible pairs, one message iteration can be evaluated by scanning each preference list once, using \(O(e+\tau)\) arithmetic operations and storage. Hence \(h\) iterations cost \(O(h(e+\tau))\) arithmetic operations, where \(e\le |M||W|\le m^2\).

For binary-encoded rational capacities, putting all capacities over a common denominator gives a sufficient bit-time bound

\(O\!\left(L^3+h(e+\tau)(L+\log\tau)\right).\)

The threshold construction above is qualitative: it establishes an admissible sequence without asserting a quantitative bound on \(t(d)\) or \(N_d\).
