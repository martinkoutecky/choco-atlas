The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(m\) item classes with rational supply \(\sigma_j\) per unit of population, and consistent utility functions \(u_{tj}(p_j)\), decide whether a competitive equilibrium exists and, if so, compute a minimum equilibrium price vector and supporting assignment. Each individual remains unit-demand and receives at most one indivisible item; assignment variables \(y_{tj}\) may be fractional because they represent the mass of type \(t\) assigned to class \(j\). Require \(\sum_j y_{tj}+z_t=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{tj}\leq\sigma_j\), and nonnegative assigned and unassigned masses; assigned mass must receive a utility-maximizing item, unassigned mass must have no positive-utility item available, and unsold supply must have price zero. Interpret a reported \(p_j^+\) by equilibria with the same assignment at prices arbitrarily close above \(p_j\); measure complexity in \(m\), \(|T|\), and input bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) buyer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq1\) item classes with positive binary-rational supplies \(\sigma_j\) per unit of population. Each individual receives at most one indivisible item.
Utilities \(u_{tj}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R\) have budgets \(b_{tj}\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\cup\{\infty\}\), are continuous and strictly decreasing on \([0,b_{tj}]\), and equal \(-1\) above the budget; equality with the budget is affordable. Utilities may be negative; receiving nothing gives utility \(0\).
Utility descriptions belong to a fixed encoding with uniform polynomial-time exact evaluation and inversion on rational arguments, returning rationals or \(\infty\) when an inverse on the affordable branch does not exist. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including these descriptions.
Consistency is promised: a path \(P=(j_1,t_1,j_2,\ldots,t_{\ell-1},j_\ell)\), allowing repetitions, is transitive at \(r\) if \(u_{t_kj_k}(r_{j_k})=u_{t_kj_{k+1}}(r_{j_{k+1}})\geq0\) for every \(k\). Whenever \(P\) is transitive at nonnegative price vectors \(p,q\) with \(q_j>p_j\) for every \(j\), and every path incidence is affordable at \(q\), any type \(t\) with \(q_{j_1}\leq b_{tj_1}\) and \(u_{tj_1}(q_{j_1})\geq0\) satisfies \(u_{tj_1}(p_{j_1})\geq u_{tj_\ell}(p_{j_\ell})\Rightarrow u_{tj_1}(q_{j_1})\geq u_{tj_\ell}(q_{j_\ell})\).
An equilibrium consists of prices \(p\geq0\) and real masses \(y_{tj},z_t\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_jy_{tj}+z_t=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_ty_{tj}\leq\sigma_j\). Define \(U_t(p)=\max\{0,\max_j u_{tj}(p_j)\}\).
Require \(y_{tj}>0\Rightarrow u_{tj}(p_j)=U_t(p)\), \(z_t>0\Rightarrow U_t(p)=0\), and \(\sum_ty_{tj}<\sigma_j\Rightarrow p_j=0\). Comparisons include every class regardless of its allocation; ties may be split freely.
If equilibria exist, let \(a_j\) be the infimum of their prices for class \(j\), and let \(A\) contain the coordinates whose infima are attained in some equilibrium. A minimum equilibrium consists of one fixed supporting assignment \((y,z)\) and entries \(a_j\) for \(j\in A\), \(a_j^+\) otherwise: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), that assignment supports an equilibrium at prices \(q_j=a_j\) for \(j\in A\) and \(a_j<q_j\leq a_j+\varepsilon\) otherwise.
Is deciding equilibrium existence and, whenever one exists, computing a minimum equilibrium with a supporting assignment solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the continuous problem NP-hard?
For finitely listed unit-demand buyers and one unit per item, existence and minimum-equilibrium computation have a strongly polynomial-time algorithm under consistency and polynomial-time utility evaluation and inversion. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1004.2565)
For a different divisible-goods model with linear valuations, quasi-linear utility and expenditure budgets, equilibrium prices are unique and equilibrium outcomes maximize revenue. [Source](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/TE5913)

Status and new partial results

The general complexity question remains unresolved here. The following results advance it:

  • For one buyer type, with arbitrarily many item classes and the general utilities in the question, equilibrium existence and minimum-equilibrium computation are polynomial-time solvable.
  • For budgeted quasi-linear utilities, equilibrium existence and minimum-equilibrium computation are polynomial-time solvable when the number of classes is fixed.
  • For budgeted quasi-linear utilities and arbitrary numbers of classes and types, equilibrium existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$. An exact finite algorithm computes the requested minimum equilibrium, although its running time can be exponential.
  • For quasi-linear utilities without budgets, both problems are polynomial-time solvable for arbitrary numbers of classes and types.

All statements concern the mass-assignment model in the question. In particular, none requires explicitly replicating the population.

1. Feasibility at fixed rational prices

We first prove the fixed-price result needed below.

For a price vector $p$, define
$$ D_t(p)=\{j:u_{tj}(p_j)=U_t(p)\}, \qquad U_t(p)=\max\{0,\max_j u_{tj}(p_j)\}. $$

Proposition 1. At specified rational prices $p$, equilibrium feasibility and a supporting assignment can be computed in time polynomial in the input length and the encoding length of $p$.

Proof. Use a circulation network with a source $s$, one vertex for each type, one vertex for each class, and a sink $v$. Its edges have the following lower and upper capacities:

  • $s\to t$ has both capacities equal to $\mu_t$.
  • $t\to j$ exists precisely when $j\in D_t(p)$ and has capacities $0$ and $\mu_t$.
  • $t\to v$ exists precisely when $U_t(p)=0$ and has capacities $0$ and $\mu_t$.
  • $j\to v$ has upper capacity $\sigma_j$. Its lower capacity is $\sigma_j$ when $p_j>0$, and $0$ when $p_j=0$.
  • $v\to s$ has both capacities equal to $1$.

The flows on $t\to j$ and $t\to v$ represent $y_{tj}$ and $z_t$, respectively. Conservation at type vertices gives the type mass equations. Conservation at class vertices and their capacities give the supply constraints and clearing of positive-price classes. The available type edges enforce utility maximization and outside-option eligibility.

Thus feasible circulations correspond exactly to supporting assignments.

The network has polynomial size. Exact evaluation at rational prices determines its edges in polynomial time. Rational lower-bound circulation feasibility reduces to rational maximum flow and is polynomial-time solvable.

For the encoding bound, let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all masses and supplies. Then $\log D=O(L)$. Scaling capacities by $D$ gives an integral-capacity network, which has an integral feasible circulation whenever it has a feasible circulation. Consequently, a supporting assignment can be chosen with entries in $D^{-1}\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$. Each assignment entry is at most $1$, so its binary encoding length is $O(L)$. $\square$

2. One buyer type: a complete polynomial-time algorithm

Assume $\tau=1$. Its mass is necessarily $1$. Write $u_j$ for its utility for class $j$, and put
$$ v_j=u_j(0). $$

Only the positive values $v_j$ enter the following cutoff.

If
$$ \sum_{j:v_j>0}\sigma_j<1, $$
define $\lambda=0$. Otherwise, define
$$ \lambda= \max\left\{ c\in\{v_j:v_j>0\}: \sum_{j:v_j\geq c}\sigma_j\geq1 \right\}. $$

This maximum exists in the second case: at the smallest positive $v_j$, the cumulative supply is at least $1$.

Define
$$ J_{>}=\{j:v_j>\lambda\}, \qquad J_{=}=\{j:v_j=\lambda\}, \qquad h=\sum_{j\in J_{>}}\sigma_j. $$

In both cases,
$$ h<1. $$
For $\lambda=0$, this is its defining inequality. For $\lambda>0$, if $h\geq1$, the smallest value strictly above $\lambda$ would satisfy the cutoff condition, contradicting maximality of $\lambda$. Also, when $\lambda>0$,
$$ h+\sum_{j\in J_{=}}\sigma_j\geq1. $$

Theorem 2. An equilibrium exists if and only if, for every $j\in J_{>}$, the affordable branch of $u_j$ takes the value $\lambda$.

When these inverses exist, let $c_j$ be the unique solution of $u_j(c_j)=\lambda$ on the affordable branch. Then the attained minimum prices are
$$ p_j^*= \begin{cases} c_j,&j\in J_{>},\\ 0,&j\notin J_{>}. \end{cases} $$
These prices and a supporting assignment are computable in polynomial time.

2.1 An upper bound on equilibrium utility

Let $q$ be any equilibrium, and let $U=U(q)$.

We claim that
$$ U\leq\lambda. $$

Suppose instead that $U>\lambda$. Then $U>0$, so the type must receive its entire mass $1$ in items. Every allocated class $j$ gives nonnegative utility $U$, and therefore is affordable. Since its affordable utility decreases with price,
$$ v_j=u_j(0)\geq u_j(q_j)=U. $$

The total supply of classes satisfying $v_j\geq U$ is less than $1$:

  • If $\lambda=0$, even the total supply of all classes with positive $v_j$ is less than $1$.
  • If $\lambda>0$ and the set $\{j:v_j\geq U\}$ is nonempty, its smallest initial utility is a value $c>\lambda$. By maximality of $\lambda$, the cumulative supply at $c$ is less than $1$.

In either case, these classes cannot supply mass $1$. This contradiction proves the claim.

2.2 Necessity of the inverse condition

Fix $j\in J_{>}$. At an equilibrium $q$,
$$ u_j(0)=v_j>\lambda\geq U. $$
Thus $q_j$ cannot be zero. Because $\sigma_j>0$, clearing of this positive-price class gives
$$ y_j=\sigma_j>0. $$
Utility maximization consequently implies
$$ u_j(q_j)=U\geq0. $$
In particular, $q_j$ is affordable.

Continuity on $[0,q_j]$, together with
$$ u_j(0)>\lambda\geq u_j(q_j), $$
shows that the affordable branch takes the value $\lambda$. Strict decrease makes its inverse unique.

Therefore failure of any required inverse certifies nonexistence.

2.3 Construction and minimality

Suppose all required inverses exist. Since $v_j>\lambda$, each $c_j$ is strictly positive.

Give the type the full supply $\sigma_j$ of every class in $J_{>}$. This uses mass $h<1$.

If $\lambda>0$, distribute the remaining mass $1-h$ among classes in $J_{=}$, respecting their supplies. Their total available supply is sufficient. Set $z=0$.

If $\lambda=0$, give the type nothing further and set $z=1-h$.

At $p^*$:

  • Each class in $J_{>}$ gives utility $\lambda$.
  • Each class outside $J_{>}$ has price zero and gives utility $v_j\leq\lambda$.
  • Every positive-price class receives its full supply.
  • Outside-option mass is used only when $\lambda=0$.

Thus the constructed assignment supports an equilibrium.

Finally, let $q$ be any equilibrium. For $j\in J_{>}$, the preceding necessity argument gives
$$ u_j(q_j)=U\leq\lambda=u_j(c_j), $$
with both $q_j$ and $c_j$ affordable. Strict decrease implies $q_j\geq c_j$. For other classes, $q_j\geq0=p_j^*$. Hence
$$ q\geq p^* $$
coordinatewise.

Therefore $p^*$ is the attained minimum equilibrium price vector.

Computing $v_j$, sorting the positive values, summing rational supplies, and evaluating the required inverses all take polynomial time. The inversion arguments have polynomial encoding length, so the promised inversion routine returns polynomial-length rational roots. Constructing the residual assignment uses only rational arithmetic. $\square$

For one type, the consistency promise is automatic: transitivity makes that type’s utilities at the two endpoints of a path equal. Thus this theorem resolves a subcase of the promised problem.

3. Budgeted quasi-linear utilities

For the remainder of this section and Sections 4–5, assume
$$ u_{tj}(x)= \begin{cases} v_{tj}-x,&0\leq x\leq b_{tj},\\ -1,&x>b_{tj}, \end{cases} $$
where every $v_{tj}$ is rational. Values may be negative, and budgets may be infinite.

3.1 Verification of consistency

Suppose a path is transitive at $p$ and $q$ as in the promise. For consecutive classes on the path, transitivity gives
$$ v_{t_kj_k}-p_{j_k} = v_{t_kj_{k+1}}-p_{j_{k+1}}, $$
and the corresponding equality at $q$. Subtraction yields
$$ q_{j_k}-p_{j_k} = q_{j_{k+1}}-p_{j_{k+1}}. $$
Hence the price increment is the same at the path’s two endpoints.

Consider an external type satisfying the promise’s conditions at the first endpoint. If the last endpoint is unaffordable at $q$, its utility there is $-1$, while its utility at the first endpoint is nonnegative, so the required conclusion holds.

Otherwise both endpoints are affordable at $q$, and consequently at $p$. Their quasi-linear utilities decrease by the same amount from $p$ to $q$. The comparison at $p$ is therefore preserved at $q$.

Thus this entire subclass satisfies the stated consistency promise.

3.2 A finite arrangement with constant demand graphs

Consider the following rational hyperplanes in price space:
$$ p_j=0, $$
$$ p_j=b_{tj}\qquad\text{for finite budgets}, $$
$$ p_j=v_{tj}, $$
and
$$ p_j-p_k=v_{tj}-v_{tk}\qquad(j<k). $$

Let $H$ be their number, retaining duplicates if desired. Then
$$ H\leq m+2\tau m+\tau\binom m2. $$

A cell is a nonempty set obtained by specifying, for each hyperplane, whether its defining affine expression is negative, zero, or positive. Retain only cells contained in the nonnegative orthant. Such cells partition that orthant, including its boundaries.

Within a cell:

  • Each incidence’s affordability is constant.
  • The sign of each affordable utility is constant.
  • Comparisons between affordable utilities are constant.
  • Each coordinate’s zero or positive status is constant.

These facts imply that $D_t(p)$ and outside-option eligibility are constant throughout the cell. Unaffordable utilities are negative and never maximize a utility that includes the outside option.

It follows from Proposition 1 that a cell either consists entirely of equilibrium prices or contains no equilibrium prices. Moreover, in a feasible cell, any assignment obtained at one representative supports every price in that cell.

3.3 Exact cell enumeration

Each cell has a description by rational affine equalities and strict inequalities. To test its nonemptiness, orient its strict inequalities as
$$ a_i\cdot p<c_i. $$
Introduce $\delta$ and solve the rational linear program
$$ a_i\cdot p\leq c_i-\delta, \qquad 0\leq\delta\leq1, $$
together with the cell’s equalities, maximizing $\delta$.

The cell is nonempty exactly when the optimum is positive. Indeed, any point satisfying finitely many strict inequalities has a positive minimum slack. Conversely, a solution with positive $\delta$ satisfies all the original strict inequalities.

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility and finite linear optimization are solvable in polynomial time, and feasible instances admit rational solutions of polynomial encoding length. Here the constraints and objective are rational, their number is polynomial in $H$, and the objective $\delta$ is bounded above by $1$.

Starting with all of price space, insert hyperplanes one at a time. Replace each current cell by its nonempty negative, zero, and positive intersections with the new hyperplane. The linear program above performs each nonemptiness test and supplies a rational representative.

For arbitrary $m$, there are at most $3^H$ sign patterns. For fixed $m$, the number of cells is polynomial in $H$. Here is a proof of the latter assertion.

A cell’s affine hull has codimension $r$ and is defined by $r$ independent hyperplanes from the arrangement. For a fixed such hull, its dimension is $m-r$, and cells having that hull are full-dimensional regions of the induced arrangement there.

An arrangement of $n$ hyperplanes in dimension $d$ has at most
$$ \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom ni $$
full-dimensional regions. This follows inductively: adding one hyperplane creates at most as many new regions as the induced arrangement on that hyperplane has regions.

Consequently, the total number of cells is at most
$$ \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom Hr \sum_{i=0}^{m-r}\binom Hi. $$
Every summand has degree at most $m$ in $H$. Thus the bound is polynomial for fixed $m$. The incremental enumeration therefore uses polynomially many rational linear programs when $m$ is fixed.

4. Computing infima, attainment, and one fixed assignment

Finding feasible cells already decides existence. Computing the precise minimum object requires additional care because a feasible cell can approach a budget boundary without including it.

We first establish the order property needed for simultaneous approximation.

4.1 Equilibrium prices are closed under coordinatewise minimum

Lemma 3. Under the general utility hypotheses of the question, if $p$ and $q$ are equilibrium prices, then their coordinatewise minimum is also an equilibrium price vector.

Proof. Define
$$ w_{tj}(x)=\max\{0,u_{tj}(x)\}. $$
Each $w_{tj}$ is nonincreasing: this holds on the affordable branch, and above budget it is zero. Also,
$$ x<y,\quad u_{tj}(y)\geq0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{tj}(x)>u_{tj}(y), $$
because nonnegative utility at $y$ implies that both prices are affordable.

Choose supporting assignments at $p$ and $q$, and put
$$ S=\{j:p_j<q_j\}, \qquad T=\{t:U_t(p)>U_t(q)\}. $$

A type in $T$ has positive utility at $p$ and therefore allocates all its mass to items. Every item it receives at $p$ belongs to $S$: otherwise $q_j\leq p_j$ would imply
$$ U_t(q)\geq w_{tj}(q_j)\geq w_{tj}(p_j)=U_t(p), $$
a contradiction.

Conversely, if a type receives a class $j\in S$ at $q$, then
$$ U_t(p)\geq u_{tj}(p_j)>u_{tj}(q_j)=U_t(q), $$
so that type belongs to $T$.

Every class in $S$ has positive price at $q$ and is therefore full. The preceding two facts give
$$ \sum_{j\in S}\sigma_j = \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in S}y^q_{tj} \leq \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t = \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in S}y^p_{tj} \leq \sum_{j\in S}\sigma_j. $$
All inequalities are equalities. Thus both assignments allocate exactly the types in $T$, with their entire masses, to $S$; every class in $S$ is full in both assignments.

Let $r=\min\{p,q\}$. Use the assignment at $p$ for types in $T$, and the assignment at $q$ for the other types. Classes in $S$ remain full. Outside $S$, loads and prices are those at $q$, so supply feasibility and clearing hold.

Moreover,
$$ U_t(r)=\max\{U_t(p),U_t(q)\}, $$
because for each class the smaller price gives the larger truncated utility. Types in $T$ retain their optimal allocations from $p$ on $S$. Other types retain their optimal allocations from $q$ outside $S$. If their outside-option mass is positive, $U_t(q)=0$ and $U_t(p)\leq U_t(q)$, so outside-option eligibility also persists.

This constructs a supporting assignment at $r$. $\square$

4.2 Computing each coordinate infimum

Return to the quasi-linear subclass. Let $\mathcal C$ be the finite collection of feasible cells. If $\mathcal C$ is empty, there is no equilibrium. Otherwise,
$$ \mathcal E=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal C}C $$
is exactly the equilibrium price set.

For a nonempty cell $C$, its closure $\overline C$ is obtained by replacing its strict inequalities by weak inequalities while retaining its equalities.

To justify this precisely, take $x\in C$ and any point $a$ satisfying those weak inequalities and equalities. For every $0<\theta\leq1$,
$$ (1-\theta)a+\theta x\in C: $$
each strict inequality has positive slack contributed by $x$, and equalities are preserved. These points converge to $a$.

For each $C\in\mathcal C$ and coordinate $j$, compute
$$ c_{Cj}=\min_{p\in\overline C}p_j. $$
This is a feasible rational linear program bounded below by zero. Its optimum is attained, and equals $\inf_{p\in C}p_j$. Therefore
$$ a_j=\min_{C\in\mathcal C}c_{Cj}. $$

Each $a_j$ is rational with polynomial encoding length: it is one of the optima of rational linear programs whose descriptions have polynomial length in the original input.

The coordinate infimum $a_j$ is attained precisely when at least one feasible cell has a nonempty intersection with
$$ \{p:p_j=a_j\}. $$
The strict-feasibility linear program tests these intersections exactly, thereby computing $A$.

Closure is used only to compute infima. An attainment test uses the actual cell, preserving strict budget inequalities.

4.3 Simultaneous approximation

For every positive integer $n$, choose an equilibrium $p^{(n,j)}$ with
$$ p^{(n,j)}_j<a_j+\frac1n $$
for each coordinate $j$. For each $j\in A$, choose an equilibrium $h^{(j)}$ satisfying $h^{(j)}_j=a_j$.

Take the coordinatewise minimum of these finitely many equilibria. By Lemma 3, the resulting $q^{(n)}$ is an equilibrium, and it satisfies
$$ q^{(n)}_j=a_j\qquad(j\in A), $$
$$ a_j<q^{(n)}_j<a_j+\frac1n\qquad(j\notin A). $$

Only finitely many feasible cells exist, so some cell $C$ contains infinitely many of these vectors.

For each feasible cell, define
$$ R_C= C\cap \{p_j=a_j:j\in A\} \cap \{p_j>a_j:j\notin A\}. $$
For the cell just identified, $R_C$ is nonempty and
$$ a\in\overline{R_C}. $$

These two properties are algorithmically testable. Nonemptiness is a strict-feasibility linear program. For a nonempty $R_C$, its closure is again obtained by weakening its strict inequalities, by the interpolation argument above; membership of $a$ can then be checked directly.

4.4 The supporting assignment

Find a feasible cell $C$ for which $R_C$ is nonempty and $a\in\overline{R_C}$. Choose a rational point $x\in R_C$, and compute a supporting assignment $(y,z)$ at $x$ using Proposition 1.

This assignment supports every price in $C$. In particular, for every $0<\theta\leq1$,
$$ q(\theta)=(1-\theta)a+\theta x $$
belongs to $R_C$ and supports that same assignment.

For $j\in A$, $q_j(\theta)=a_j$. For $j\notin A$,
$$ q_j(\theta)-a_j=\theta(x_j-a_j)>0. $$
Given any $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\theta>0$ sufficiently small that
$$ \theta(x_j-a_j)\leq\varepsilon \qquad\text{for every }j\notin A. $$

Thus the fixed assignment has exactly the quantifier order required in the question: one assignment supports an equilibrium within every positive tolerance, with attained coordinates fixed at their infima.

5. Complexity consequences for budgeted quasi-linear utilities

Theorem 4. For budgeted quasi-linear utilities:

  • If $m$ is fixed, equilibrium existence and minimum-equilibrium computation with a supporting assignment are polynomial-time solvable.
  • For arbitrary $m$, the same construction is an exact finite algorithm, with at most exponentially many cells.
  • For arbitrary $m$, equilibrium existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

Proof. Cell enumeration, fixed-price flow tests, coordinate optimization, attainment tests, and selection of $R_C$ require polynomially many operations per enumerated cell. All linear programs and networks have polynomial-size rational descriptions. The number of cells is polynomial when $m$ is fixed.

For membership in $\mathrm{NP}$, any nonempty equilibrium set contains a feasible cell. A representative of that cell has polynomial-length rational coordinates by the strict-feasibility linear program. Proposition 1 supplies a polynomial-length rational supporting assignment. These prices and masses form a certificate verifiable by exact rational comparisons and the equilibrium equations in polynomial time. $\square$

The theorem does not give a polynomial-time algorithm when both $m$ and $\tau$ vary. Its cell enumeration can be exponential.

6. No budgets: a polynomial-time algorithm in all dimensions

Now assume quasi-linear utilities with $b_{tj}=\infty$ for every incidence.

Consider the rational linear program
$$ \max \sum_{t,j}v_{tj}y_{tj} $$
subject to
$$ y_{tj}\geq0, \qquad \sum_jy_{tj}\leq\mu_t, \qquad \sum_ty_{tj}\leq\sigma_j. $$

It is feasible at $y=0$ and bounded, since every $y_{tj}\leq\mu_t$. Its dual is
$$ \min \sum_t\mu_t U_t+\sum_j\sigma_j p_j $$
subject to
$$ U_t\geq0,\qquad p_j\geq0,\qquad U_t+p_j\geq v_{tj}. $$
The dual is feasible, for example at $p=0$ and $U_t=\max\{0,\max_jv_{tj}\}$.

Let $W$ be their common optimum.

For any primal and dual feasible solutions, their objective gap equals
$$ \sum_t U_t\left(\mu_t-\sum_jy_{tj}\right) + \sum_j p_j\left(\sigma_j-\sum_ty_{tj}\right) + \sum_{t,j}y_{tj}(U_t+p_j-v_{tj}). $$
Every summand is nonnegative. For any optimal primal and optimal dual solutions, the gap is zero, so each summand is zero.

Set
$$ z_t=\mu_t-\sum_jy_{tj}. $$
The zero products give:

  • $y_{tj}>0$ implies $v_{tj}-p_j=U_t$.
  • $z_t>0$ implies $U_t=0$.
  • A class with unused supply has $p_j=0$.

The dual inequalities imply that $U_t$ bounds all item utilities and the outside option. Since $\mu_t>0$, each type either has positive outside-option mass or receives positive item mass. The corresponding equality shows that its bound is attained. Hence $U_t=U_t(p)$, and these optimal solutions form an equilibrium.

Conversely, an equilibrium makes every term in the gap expression zero, so its assignment and prices are primal and dual optimal.

Let $F$ be the dual optimal face:
$$ F=\left\{(U,p): U,p\geq0,\ U_t+p_j\geq v_{tj},\ \sum_t\mu_tU_t+\sum_j\sigma_jp_j=W \right\}. $$
It is nonempty and compact: all coefficients $\mu_t,\sigma_j$ are positive, and its nonnegative coordinates are bounded by the objective equality. Its price projection is exactly the equilibrium price set.

Each coordinate infimum is therefore attained. Choose a minimizing equilibrium for each coordinate and take their coordinatewise minimum using Lemma 3. The resulting equilibrium price vector attains every infimum simultaneously.

Consequently, minimizing
$$ \sum_jp_j $$
over $F$ returns precisely that minimum price vector: every equilibrium coordinate is at least its infimum, and their simultaneous infimum is feasible.

Any optimal primal assignment supports the resulting dual optimum, by the gap calculation. All required linear programs have polynomial-size rational descriptions.

Theorem 5. With quasi-linear utilities and no budgets, equilibrium existence and minimum-equilibrium computation with a supporting assignment are polynomial-time solvable for arbitrary $m$ and $\tau$. Equilibria always exist, and all minimum coordinates are attained.

7. What remains open in this submission

These results do not settle the task for arbitrary consistent utility descriptions and varying $m,\tau$. They also do not settle the varying-dimension case of budgeted quasi-linear utilities.

The cited finite-market algorithm establishes a strongly polynomial result for explicitly listed buyers and individual items. Its running-time proof counts demand-edge events. Transferring that algorithm to weighted type–class graphs requires proving that its price updates, marking rules, and event bound survive compression; the results above do not establish that transfer.
