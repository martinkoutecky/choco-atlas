The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) items, \(\tau\) complete buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), prices \(p\), per-capita supplies \(\beta\), bounded integral bundle domains, and strong-gross-substitutes valuations, let \(B_t(p)\) be the minimal preferred bundles of type \(t\). Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(E=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(m\ge1\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) complete buyer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Each type has a nondecreasing valuation \(v_t:[0,c]_{\mathbb Z}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), where \([0,c]_{\mathbb Z}=\{z\in\mathbb Z^E:0\le z\le c\}\) and \(c\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^E\) is binary encoded. Prices \(p\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^E\) and per-capita supplies \(\beta\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^E\) are binary encoded.
Write \(D_t(q)=\arg\max_{z\in[0,c]_{\mathbb Z}}(v_t(z)-\langle q,z\rangle)\). Strong gross substitutes means that for all \(q,r\in\mathbb R^E\) with \(q\le r\) and every \(x\in D_t(q)\), some \(w\in D_t(r)\) satisfies \(w(e)\ge x(e)\) whenever \(q(e)=r(e)\), and \(w(E)\le x(E)\).
For any vector \(a\), let \(a(S)=\sum_{e\in S}a(e)\). Let \(B_t(p)\) consist of those \(z\in D_t(p)\) for which no distinct \(w\in D_t(p)\) satisfies \(w\le z\); retain all utility ties.
Access to each type is through exact oracles: a demand query returns any \(z\in B_t(p)\), and an exchange query, given \(z\in B_t(p)\) and distinct \(e,f\), returns \(\max\{\alpha\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:z-\alpha\chi_f+\alpha\chi_e\in B_t(p)\}\). Their costs are \(\mathrm{DO}\) and \(\mathrm{ExO}\); \(\chi_e\) is the unit vector at \(e\).
Compute rational vectors \(y_t\in\mu_t\operatorname{conv}(B_t(p))\) and \(s\) maximizing \(\sum_{e\in E}s_e\), subject to \(0\le s_e\le\beta_e\) and \(s_e\le\sum_t y_t(e)\).
Also compute the inclusion-wise minimal maximizer, over every \(S\subseteq E\) including \(\varnothing\), of \(\Delta_p(S)=\sum_t\mu_t\min_{z\in B_t(p)}z(S)-\beta(S)\).
Is this joint exact computation solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,\mathrm{DO},\mathrm{ExO}\), where \(L\) is the total bit length of the numerical input, or is it NP-hard even when both oracles have polynomial-time implementations?
For discrete buyers and integral supplies, polymatroid-sum optimization and the inclusion-wise minimal overdemandedness maximizer can be computed in \(O(n\,\mathrm{DO}+nm^3\,\mathrm{ExO})\) time ([Eickhoff et al.](https://publications.rwth-aachen.de/record/1022051/files/1022051.pdf)).
No result addressing this exact mass formulation was identified in the search.

An information obstruction in the stated oracle model

With access limited to the fixed-price demand and exchange oracles specified in the task, exact joint computation is impossible in general. Two admissible societies can have identical oracle implementations but different required outputs. Both implementations can run in polynomial time.

Thus the proposed dichotomy is not exhaustive: the stated oracles need not supply enough information to determine the answer. This is an oracle impossibility result, not an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.

I assume, as intended, that every supported type satisfies the explicitly stated price-response condition. I do not add concave-extensibility. That additional hypothesis appears in [Eickhoff et al., Definition 2.2](https://publications.rwth-aachen.de/record/1022051/files/1022051.pdf), so their demand-set theorem cannot directly resolve this formulation.

1. Two valuations with the same numerical parameters

Both societies have one supported type, with
$$ E=\{1,2,3\},\qquad \tau=1,\qquad \mu_1=1,\qquad c=(6,6,6), $$
and
$$ p=(1/2,1/2,1/2),\qquad \beta=(1,1,4). $$
Write
$$ x^\circ=(2,2,2). $$

The first valuation is
$$ v^{(0)}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^3\min\{z_i,2\}. $$

For the second valuation, define
$$ \mathcal C= \{(6,0,0),(0,6,0),(0,0,6), (0,3,3),(3,0,3),(3,3,0),(2,2,2)\} $$
and set
$$ v^{(1)}(z)= \begin{cases} 6,&\text{if some }b\in\mathcal C\text{ satisfies }b\le z,\\ \max\{z_1,z_2,z_3\},&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$

Both valuations are nondecreasing and take nonnegative integer values. For $v^{(1)}$, once a bundle dominates an element of $\mathcal C$, every larger bundle does too; otherwise monotonicity follows from that of the coordinate maximum. Both valuations are also directly evaluable in polynomial time.

We next prove that both satisfy the task’s price-response condition, including at negative prices.

2. The first valuation satisfies the condition

More generally, every separable valuation
$$ v(z)=\sum_i u_i(z_i) $$
on a finite integer box satisfies the stated condition.

Indeed, demand separates coordinatewise. Suppose $q\le r$ and $x\in D(q)$. On each unchanged-price coordinate, choose $w_i=x_i$. On a coordinate with $q_i<r_i$, choose any quantity $w_i$ maximizing $u_i(k)-r_i k$.

Optimality at the two prices gives
$$ u_i(x_i)-q_i x_i\ge u_i(w_i)-q_i w_i $$
and
$$ u_i(w_i)-r_i w_i\ge u_i(x_i)-r_i x_i. $$
Adding yields
$$ (r_i-q_i)(x_i-w_i)\ge0, $$
so $w_i\le x_i$ whenever the price increases. Consequently $w\in D(r)$, unchanged coordinates are preserved, and
$$ w(E)\le x(E). $$

This applies to $v^{(0)}$.

3. The second valuation satisfies the condition

3.1 A property of the seven bundles

For every $b\in\mathcal C$ and every nonempty $J\subseteq E$, there exists $d\in\mathcal C$ such that
$$ \operatorname{supp}(d)\subseteq J, \qquad d_i\ge b_i\quad(i\in J), $$
where $\operatorname{supp}(d)=\{i:d_i>0\}$.

To verify this, consider the three forms of $b$.

  • If $b=6\chi_i$, retain $b$ when $i\in J$; otherwise choose $6\chi_j$ for any $j\in J$.
  • If $b$ has two coordinates equal to $3$, retain $b$ when both belong to $J$. If exactly one belongs to $J$, choose the vertex with quantity $6$ on that coordinate. If neither belongs to $J$, choose any vertex supported in $J$.
  • If $b=x^\circ$, choose $x^\circ$ when $|J|=3$, the corresponding bundle with two coordinates equal to $3$ when $|J|=2$, and the corresponding vertex when $|J|=1$.

Every element of $\mathcal C$ has total quantity $6$.

3.2 Complete description of demand

Let $D^{(1)}(q)$ denote demand for $v^{(1)}$.

If some price is negative, demand consists exactly of the bundles satisfying
$$ z_i= \begin{cases} 6,&q_i<0,\\ 0,&q_i>0, \end{cases} $$
with arbitrary quantities on zero-price coordinates.

Indeed, valuation is at most $6$, and utility is at most
$$ 6-6\sum_{i:q_i<0}q_i. $$
The displayed bundles attain this bound: a negative-price coordinate has quantity $6$, so the bundle dominates a vertex of $\mathcal C$ and has valuation $6$. Equality in the bound forces the stated quantities.

If prices are nonnegative and some price is zero, demand consists exactly of the bundles $z$ satisfying
$$ z_i=0\quad(q_i>0), \qquad b\le z\quad\text{for some }b\in\mathcal C. $$
Utility is at most $6$, and a vertex on a zero-price coordinate attains $6$. Equality requires valuation $6$ and zero expenditure. Moreover, valuation equals $6$ exactly when the bundle dominates an element of $\mathcal C$.

If every price is positive, put
$$ a=\min_i q_i,\qquad I=\{i:q_i=a\}. $$
Then:

  • If $a<1$,

$$ D^{(1)}(q)= \{b\in\mathcal C:\operatorname{supp}(b)\subseteq I\}. $$

  • If $a=1$, demand consists of that same collection, together with $0$ and all $k\chi_i$ with $i\in I$ and $1\le k\le6$.
  • If $a>1$, demand is $\{0\}$.

Here is the calculation. If $v^{(1)}(z)=6$, then $z$ dominates a bundle of total $6$, so
$$ 6-\langle q,z\rangle \le6-a z(E)\le6(1-a). $$
Equality holds precisely when $z\in\mathcal C$ and its support lies in $I$.

Otherwise, $v^{(1)}(z)=\max_i z_i$. For $a<1$, utility is at most
$$ (1-a)\max_i z_i<6(1-a). $$
For $a=1$, utility is at most
$$ \max_i z_i-z(E)\le0, $$
with equality precisely for zero or an axis bundle whose occupied coordinate has price $1$. Finally, $v^{(1)}(z)\le z(E)$ for every bundle, so when $a>1$, every nonzero bundle has negative utility.

3.3 Verification for every price increase

Fix arbitrary real vectors $q\le r$ and $x\in D^{(1)}(q)$. We construct the required $w$.

Case 1: $r$ has a negative coordinate.

Then $q$ does too. Choose $w$ with quantity $6$ on negative-price coordinates of $r$, quantity $x_i$ on unchanged zero-price coordinates, and zero elsewhere.

The demand description shows that $w\in D^{(1)}(r)$. Also $w\le x$: each coordinate negative at $r$ was negative at $q$ and therefore already had quantity $6$. Every unchanged coordinate is preserved, and $w(E)\le x(E)$.

Case 2: $r$ is nonnegative and has a zero coordinate.

Let
$$ J=\{i:r_i=0\}, \qquad U=\{i:q_i=r_i=0\}. $$
Since $q\le r$, the vector $q$ either has a negative coordinate or is nonnegative with a zero coordinate. In either situation, the demand description supplies $b\in\mathcal C$ with $b\le x$.

By Section 3.1, choose $d\in\mathcal C$ supported in $J$ and satisfying $d_i\ge b_i$ for $i\in J$. Set
$$ w_i= \begin{cases} \max\{d_i,x_i\},&i\in U,\\ d_i,&i\notin U. \end{cases} $$
This bundle lies in the box, is supported on $J$, and dominates $d$. Thus $w\in D^{(1)}(r)$.

Unchanged zero-price coordinates are preserved. Every unchanged positive-price coordinate has quantity zero in $x$ and in $w$. Finally,
$$ \begin{aligned} w(E) &=6+\sum_{i\in U}\max\{0,x_i-d_i\}\\ &\le6+\sum_{i\in U}(x_i-b_i)\\ &\le b(E)+\sum_{i\in E}(x_i-b_i)\\ &=x(E). \end{aligned} $$

Case 3: every coordinate of $r$ is positive.

First suppose $q$ has a negative or zero coordinate. Then $x(E)\ge6$, and every unchanged coordinate has quantity zero in $x$. If $\min_i r_i<1$, choose a demanded vertex of total $6$. Otherwise choose $w=0$. Both choices satisfy all requirements.

It remains to consider strictly positive $q$.

If $x=0$, then $\min_i q_i\ge1$, hence $0\in D^{(1)}(r)$; choose $w=0$.

If $x=k\chi_i$ with $1\le k<6$, then $q_i=\min_j q_j=1$. Retain $x$ if coordinate $i$ is unchanged, and otherwise choose zero. The demand description verifies both choices.

In the remaining situation, $x\in\mathcal C$, its support lies on the cheapest coordinates of $q$, and
$$ a=\min_i q_i\le1. $$
Let
$$ H=\{i:x_i>0,\ q_i=r_i\}. $$
If $H$ is empty, choose a demanded vertex when $\min_i r_i<1$, and choose zero otherwise.

If $H$ is nonempty, then $r_i=a$ for $i\in H$. Since every price at $r$ is at least $a$, we have $\min_i r_i=a$. Put
$$ J=\{i:r_i=a\}. $$
Section 3.1 supplies $w\in\mathcal C$ supported in $J$ with $w_i\ge x_i$ for $i\in J$. This bundle is demanded at $r$, preserves the required positive quantities, and has
$$ w(E)=6=x(E). $$
Unchanged coordinates with zero quantity in $x$ automatically satisfy the required inequality.

These cases exhaust all real price vectors. Thus $v^{(1)}$ satisfies exactly the task’s strong gross substitutes condition.

4. Identical admissible oracle implementations

At the supplied price $p=(1/2,1/2,1/2)$, the first valuation has unique demand
$$ B^{(0)}(p)=\{x^\circ\}. $$
Indeed, each of its first two units per coordinate contributes positive utility, and every subsequent unit contributes negative utility.

For the second valuation, Section 3.2 gives
$$ D^{(1)}(p)=\mathcal C. $$
All these bundles have total $6$, so no two distinct ones are coordinatewise comparable. Consequently
$$ B^{(1)}(p)=\mathcal C. $$

Crucially, every exchange answer from $x^\circ$ is zero in both societies:
$$ \max\{\alpha\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}: x^\circ-\alpha\chi_f+\alpha\chi_e\in B^{(j)}(p)\}=0 $$
for $j\in\{0,1\}$ and distinct $e,f$.

For $j=1$, a pairwise exchange leaves the third coordinate equal to $2$. Every member of $\mathcal C$ other than $x^\circ$ has all coordinates in $\{0,3,6\}$. Thus no positive exchange is possible.

Now give both societies the following identical implementations:

  • The demand oracle always returns $x^\circ$.
  • The exchange oracle, on a bundle $z\in\mathcal C$, computes the maximum exchange by scanning $\mathcal C$. On any other bundle, it returns zero.

This exchange implementation is correct on every valid query for the second society. It is also correct on every valid query for the first society, whose only valid bundle is $x^\circ$.

The task specifies exchange queries only for $z\in B_t(p)$; it imposes no correctness requirement outside that domain. We have nevertheless made the two implementations identical on *all* arguments. Therefore even queries outside the promised domain cannot distinguish these implementations.

The finite lookup implementations run in polynomial time.

This example also directly defeats the previous rank-evaluation procedure: starting from $x^\circ$, every exchange is zero, although
$$ \max_{z\in B^{(1)}(p)}z_3=6>2=x^\circ_3. $$

5. The required outputs differ

5.1 Optimal sales

For the first society, the only feasible allocation vector is
$$ y=x^\circ. $$
Thus its optimal sales vector and value are
$$ s=(1,1,2), \qquad s(E)=4. $$

For the second society,
$$ \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal C) = \{y\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^3:y(E)=6\}. $$
The inclusion from left to right follows because every listed bundle is nonnegative with total $6$. The reverse inclusion follows because $\mathcal C$ contains all three vertices of this simplex.

In particular,
$$ \beta =\frac16(6,0,0)+\frac16(0,6,0)+\frac46(0,0,6) \in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal C). $$
Choosing $y=s=\beta$ achieves sales $6$. This is optimal because $s\le\beta$ implies $s(E)\le6$.

Hence the optimal values are different:
$$ \operatorname{OPT}^{(0)}=4, \qquad \operatorname{OPT}^{(1)}=6. $$
There is no output allocation that is optimal for both societies.

5.2 Minimal overdemandedness maximizers

For the first society,
$$ \Delta^{(0)}_p(S)=x^\circ(S)-\beta(S). $$
The three coordinate contributions are $1,1,-2$. Therefore its unique maximizer is
$$ S^{(0)}_*=\{1,2\}. $$

For the second society, every proper subset $S\subsetneq E$ has an outside coordinate $i$. The bundle $6\chi_i\in\mathcal C$ then proves
$$ \min_{z\in\mathcal C}z(S)=0. $$
On the whole ground set, this minimum is $6$. Consequently
$$ \Delta^{(1)}_p(S)= \begin{cases} -\beta(S),&S\subsetneq E,\\ 0,&S=E. \end{cases} $$
Since every supply is positive, the maximizers are exactly $\varnothing$ and $E$. Their inclusion-wise minimal member is
$$ S^{(1)}_*=\varnothing. $$

Thus the second required output differs as well.

6. Consequence for exact computation

The numerical inputs are identical, and the permitted oracle implementations are identical. Any algorithm using only those inputs and oracles therefore receives the same information in both societies.

Yet the societies have no common correct joint output. Hence no such algorithm can always perform the requested exact computation, regardless of its running time.

This establishes a stronger obstruction than failure of a polynomial bound: the specified fixed-price oracles do not determine the answer. A conventional $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness classification for a richer representation is a different question; no such hardness claim is needed or proved here.
