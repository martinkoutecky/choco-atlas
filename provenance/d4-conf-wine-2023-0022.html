The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), binary utilities \(p:T\times T\to\{0,1\}\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one, decide whether there is a nonnegative mass distribution \(\rho_{abcd}\) over four consecutive types in a large cycle. Require total mass one, stationarity \(\sum_d\rho_{abcd}=\sum_x\rho_{xabc}\), and type marginals \(\mu_t=\sum_{a,c}Q_{atc}\), where \(Q_{abc}=\sum_d\rho_{abcd}\). For every pair of positive-mass triples \((a,t,c)\) and \((d,u,e)\), require \(p(t,a)+p(t,c)\ge p(t,d)+p(t,e)\); for every positive-mass adjacent window \((a,t,u,e)\), require \(p(t,a)\ge p(t,e)\) and \(p(u,e)\ge p(u,a)\). This asks whether a large-cycle mass society can be envy-free except on a set of vanishing population mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(m=|T|=\tau\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Preferences are an explicitly given, possibly asymmetric function \(p:T\times T\to\{0,1\}\). The value \(p(t,t)\) concerns distinct individuals of type \(t\) and may be either binary value. Utility is additive over the two neighbors; envy means a strict utility increase after exchanging seats, including adjacent seats.
Define a mass arrangement to be real numbers \(\rho_{abcd}\geq0\), indexed by \(T^4\), representing frequencies of four consecutive types, with \(\sum_{a,b,c,d\in T}\rho_{abcd}=1\) and \(\sum_{d\in T}\rho_{abcd}=\sum_{x\in T}\rho_{xabc}\) for every \((a,b,c)\in T^3\).
Put \(Q_{abc}=\sum_{d\in T}\rho_{abcd}\) and require \(\sum_{a,c\in T}Q_{atc}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\).
Require \(p(t,a)+p(t,c)\geq p(t,d)+p(t,e)\) whenever \(Q_{atc}>0\) and \(Q_{due}>0\), for all \(a,t,c,d,u,e\in T\), allowing repeated types and identical triples.
Also require \(p(t,a)\geq p(t,e)\) and \(p(u,e)\geq p(u,a)\) whenever \(\rho_{atue}>0\). These conditions define envy-freeness for this mass problem; equality permits indifference.

Is deciding whether such an envy-free mass arrangement exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies, deciding existence of an envy-free cycle seating is NP-hard with binary preferences; with any fixed number of types it is polynomial-time solvable even for arbitrary preferences. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.09549v2).
No result settling this exact mass feasibility problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Removed zero-mass types so that the supplied type list is precisely the positive-mass support.
  • Specified that preferences may be asymmetric and that diagonal type preferences describe distinct individuals of the same type, rather than self-utility.
  • Specified real nonnegative arrangement masses and allowed repeated types and identical triples in the compatibility conditions.
  • Used the stated stationary-window feasibility conditions as the exact definition of the mass problem, without imposing an additional finite-cycle realization or connectedness requirement.
  • Identified m with the number of supported types because this problem has no separate candidate or object universe; defined L as total input bit length.

Result

The stated mass feasibility problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. In particular, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even when all masses are dyadic rationals.

The proof below uses the specified mass constraints directly.

1. Consequences of the constraints

For an arrangement \(\rho\), retain the definition \(Q_{abc}=\sum_d\rho_{abcd}\).

Common utility lemma. For each type \(t\), there is a number \(k_t\in\{0,1,2\}\) such that every supported triple centered at \(t\) has utility \(k_t\). Moreover, every supported triple \((d,u,e)\) satisfies
\[ p(t,d)+p(t,e)\leq k_t. \]

Indeed, \(\mu_t>0\) guarantees at least one supported triple centered at \(t\). Applying the nonadjacent comparison in both directions to two such triples shows that their utilities are equal. Comparing one of them with any supported triple proves the asserted upper bound.

We will also use neighbor-incidence quantities. Set
\[ R_{uv}=\sum_c Q_{uvc}. \]
The flow equations give
\[ R_{uv}=\sum_{c,x}\rho_{xuvc}=\sum_x Q_{xuv}. \]
Consequently, both the row and column marginals of \(R\) are \(\mu\). Define
\[ H_{uv}=R_{uv}+R_{vu}. \]
Then
\[ H_{uv}=H_{vu}, \qquad \sum_v H_{uv}=2\mu_u, \qquad \sum_v p(u,v)H_{uv}=k_u\mu_u. \]
Thus \(H_{uv}\) counts the mass of neighbor incidences between the two types, counting both incidences when \(u=v\).

If the preference row of \(t\) contains a \(1\), then \(k_t\geq1\). To see this, choose \(s\) with \(p(t,s)=1\). Since \(\mu_s>0\), some supported triple has \(s\) as a neighbor. The common utility lemma then gives \(k_t\geq1\).

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Guess the profile \(k\in\{0,1,2\}^{T}\).

Call a triple \((a,b,c)\) admissible if
\[ p(b,a)+p(b,c)=k_b \quad\text{and}\quad p(t,a)+p(t,c)\leq k_t \quad\text{for every }t\in T. \]

Allow \(\rho_{abcd}\) to be positive only when both \((a,b,c)\) and \((b,c,d)\) are admissible and
\[ p(b,a)\geq p(b,d), \qquad p(c,d)\geq p(c,a). \]
Set every other coordinate to zero, and impose the given nonnegativity, normalization, flow, and mass equations.

This is a rational linear feasibility problem of polynomial encoding length: it has at most \(m^4\) variables, and admissibility can be checked in polynomial time. Rational linear feasibility is decidable in time polynomial in its encoding length.

Every envy-free arrangement yields such a profile by the common utility lemma. Conversely, any solution of this linear program satisfies both kinds of envy constraints. Guessing the profile and checking its linear program therefore establishes membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

3. Reduction from \(\mathrm{3SAT}\)

Take a formula with variables indexed by \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) and nonempty clauses indexed by \(j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\). Each clause has at most three literals and contains each variable at most once.

This restriction preserves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness: starting with ordinary \(\mathrm{3SAT}\), remove tautological clauses and repeated literals. If no clauses remain, output a fixed feasible mass instance. Hence assume \(n,r\geq1\).

Write the literals of clause \(j\) as \(\ell_{j1},\ldots,\ell_{jq_j}\), and put \(q=\sum_j q_j\). Thus \(r\leq q\leq3r\).

For each variable introduce five types
\[ X_i,\ Y_i,\ a_i,\ b_i,\ c_i. \]
The types \(X_i,Y_i\) will monitor its positive and negative literals, respectively.

For each clause introduce types
\[ B_j,\ A_j,\ C_j \]
and one type \(P_{jk}\) for each literal occurrence. Finally introduce two types \(Z,E\).

Define the monitor of a literal by \(M(v_i)=X_i\) and \(M(\neg v_i)=Y_i\).

Masses

Choose positive dyadic numbers \(s,w\) satisfying
\[ s\leq\frac1{64n}, \qquad w\leq\frac{s}{16r}, \]
with denominators of polynomial bit length. For example, take
\[ s=2^{-\lceil\log_2(64n)\rceil}, \qquad w=s\,2^{-\lceil\log_2(16r)\rceil}. \]

Assign masses as follows:

| Types | Mass of each type |
|---|---:|
| \(X_i,Y_i\) | \(2s\) |
| \(a_i,b_i,c_i\) | \(s\) |
| \(B_j,A_j,C_j,P_{jk}\) | \(w\) |
| \(Z\) | \(3/4\) |
| \(E\) | \(1/4-7ns-(3r+q)w\) |

These masses sum to \(1\). Their positivity will also follow from the construction below.

Preferences

All entries not specified here are zero.

  • \(Z\) approves only \(Z\).
  • \(B_j\) approves only \(A_j\).
  • \(A_j\) approves only \(C_j\).
  • \(C_j\) approves \(Z,A_j\), and every \(P_{jk}\) belonging to its clause.
  • \(X_i\) approves \(a_i,b_i\). For each occurrence \(\ell_{jk}=v_i\), it also approves \(A_j,P_{jk}\).
  • \(Y_i\) approves \(b_i,c_i\). For each occurrence \(\ell_{jk}=\neg v_i\), it also approves \(A_j,P_{jk}\).

The rows of \(E,a_i,b_i,c_i,P_{jk}\) are identically zero.

There are \(5n+3r+q+2\) types. All masses are dyadic with polynomial bit length, and the explicit preference matrix has polynomial size.

4. Soundness: feasibility implies satisfiability

Assume the constructed instance has an envy-free mass arrangement.

The type \(Z\) is isolated and attains utility \(2\)

Since \(Z\) approves itself, \(k_Z\geq1\).

If \(k_Z=1\), then \(H_{ZZ}=\mu_Z\), so its neighbor incidences with other types total
\[ \sum_{u\neq Z}H_{Zu}=2\mu_Z-H_{ZZ}=\mu_Z=\frac34. \]
By symmetry and the degree bounds of the other types, this total is at most
\[ \sum_{u\neq Z}2\mu_u=\frac12, \]
a contradiction.

Therefore \(k_Z=2\). Every supported triple centered at \(Z\) is consequently \(ZZZ\), and
\[ H_{Zu}=0\quad(u\neq Z), \qquad Q_{ZZZ}=\mu_Z>0. \]

Every \(C_j\) approves \(Z\). Comparing its own seat with \(ZZZ\) forces \(k_{C_j}=2\).

Each clause forces a literal monitor to attain utility \(2\)

Fix a clause \(j\). Because the rows of \(B_j,A_j\) are nonzero,
\[ H_{B_jA_j}=k_{B_j}w\geq w, \qquad H_{A_jC_j}=k_{A_j}w\geq w. \]
The total neighbor incidence available at \(A_j\) is \(2w\). Hence both inequalities are equalities:
\[ k_{B_j}=k_{A_j}=1, \qquad H_{A_jB_j}=H_{A_jC_j}=w. \]

Since \(B_j\) approves only \(A_j\) and has utility \(1\), the common utility lemma forbids every supported neighborhood having two neighbors of type \(A_j\).

In particular, every supported triple centered at \(C_j\) has at most one \(A_j\) neighbor. But
\[ H_{C_jA_j}=w=\mu_{C_j}. \]
Averaging therefore forces every such triple to have exactly one \(A_j\) neighbor.

Its other neighbor must be some \(P_{jk}\): \(C_j\) attains utility \(2\), and adjacency to \(Z\) is impossible because \(Z\) is isolated.

The monitor \(M(\ell_{jk})\) approves both \(A_j\) and \(P_{jk}\). Comparing against this supported neighborhood forces
\[ k_{M(\ell_{jk})}=2. \]
Thus every clause has at least one literal whose monitor attains utility \(2\).

The two monitors of a variable cannot both attain utility \(2\)

Fix \(i\). Let \(S_i\) be the union of the types approved by \(X_i\) and \(Y_i\).

It contains \(a_i,b_i,c_i\). Each clause contributes at most one \(A_j\) and one occurrence type \(P_{jk}\), because that clause contains variable \(i\) at most once. Hence
\[ \mu(S_i)\leq3s+2rw. \]

If both monitors attained utility \(2\), all their neighbor incidences would be with \(S_i\), giving
\[ 8s =\sum_{v\in S_i}(H_{X_iv}+H_{Y_iv}) \leq\sum_{v\in S_i}2\mu_v \leq6s+4rw \leq\frac{25s}{4} <8s. \]
This contradiction proves that they cannot both attain utility \(2\).

Set variable \(i\) to true exactly when \(k_{X_i}=2\). A positive literal with monitor utility \(2\) is then true. A negative literal with monitor utility \(2\) has \(k_{Y_i}=2\), which forces \(k_{X_i}\neq2\), so it too is true.

Every clause has such a literal. The formula is satisfiable.

5. Completeness: satisfiability implies feasibility

Assume a satisfying assignment is given.

We construct \(\rho\) by adding frequencies from periodic words. A word \(W\) of length \(h\), with a per-position mass \(\gamma\), contributes \(\gamma\) to the quadruple beginning at each of its \(h\) cyclic positions.

Such contributions automatically satisfy the flow equations: cyclic shifting pairs every outgoing occurrence of a triple with an incoming occurrence.

Words and their masses

For a true variable \(i\), use the words
\[ (X_i,a_i,X_i,b_i), \qquad (Y_i,c_i,Y_i,E,E), \]
each with per-position mass \(s\).

For a false variable, use
\[ (Y_i,c_i,Y_i,b_i), \qquad (X_i,a_i,X_i,E,E), \]
again with per-position mass \(s\).

Thus each variable contributes the required masses of its five types and contributes \(2s\) to \(E\).

In each clause \(j\), select one true literal \(\ell_{jk}\). Use
\[ (B_j,A_j,C_j,P_{jk}) \]
with per-position mass \(w\).

For each unselected occurrence \(P_{jh}\), use
\[ (P_{jh},E,E) \]
with per-position mass \(w\). This supplies every occurrence type with its required mass and contributes \(2(q-r)w\) to \(E\).

Finally use the homogeneous word \(Z\) with mass \(3/4\), and the homogeneous word \(E\) with mass
\[ \eta=\frac14-9ns-(r+3q)w. \]
Since \(q\leq3r\),
\[ 9ns+(r+3q)w \leq\frac9{64}+10rw \leq\frac9{64}+\frac{10}{1024} <\frac14. \]
Thus \(\eta>0\). In particular, the input mass of \(E\) is positive.

The resulting mass of \(E\) is exactly
\[ 2ns+2(q-r)w+\eta =\frac14-7ns-(3r+q)w. \]
All prescribed masses and normalization are therefore satisfied.

Nonadjacent envy constraints

Assign the intended utilities as follows:

  • \(Z,C_j\), and the monitor corresponding to each variable’s assigned truth value have utility \(2\).
  • \(A_j,B_j\), and the opposite monitor have utility \(1\).
  • Every zero-row type has utility \(0\).

Every type attains its intended utility in the words above. It remains to check that no supported neighborhood exceeds that utility when evaluated by the type.

For a utility-\(2\) type, this is automatic because preferences are binary. For a zero-row type, it is also automatic.

For \(B_j\), its sole approved type \(A_j\) occurs only once in the clause word. No supported neighborhood has two \(A_j\) neighbors. The same argument applies to \(A_j\), whose sole approved type is \(C_j\).

Consider a utility-\(1\) monitor of variable \(i\).

  • In its own five-letter word, every neighborhood has at most one approved neighbor.
  • In the other monitor’s four-letter word, it approves only the shared resource \(b_i\), so again every neighborhood has score at most \(1\).
  • Other variable words contain no types it approves.
  • In a clause word, the only potentially approved neighbor pair is \(A_j,P_{jk}\). The selected occurrence represents a true literal, so its monitor is the utility-\(2\) monitor. Consequently, the utility-\(1\) monitor does not approve the selected \(P_{jk}\), and the pair has score at most \(1\).
  • In an unselected occurrence word \(P_{jh},E,E\), every neighborhood has at most one approved neighbor.
  • It approves neither \(Z\) nor \(E\).

Thus every supported neighborhood has score at most the type’s attained utility. This proves all nonadjacent envy inequalities.

Adjacent envy constraints

An endpoint whose utility is \(2\) automatically satisfies its adjacent-swap inequality: its outer neighbor is approved, and the proposed new outer neighbor has preference value at most \(1\). A zero-row endpoint also automatically satisfies its inequality.

Only the utility-\(1\) endpoints need checking.

A utility-\(1\) monitor appears in a word
\[ (M,d,M,E,E), \]
where it approves \(d\) and approves neither \(M\) nor \(E\).

When adjacent to \(d\), the old and new outer neighbors are \(E\) and \(M\), both valued \(0\). When adjacent to \(E\), its old outer neighbor is \(d\), valued \(1\), and its new outer neighbor is \(E\), valued \(0\). Both inequalities hold.

In the clause word \(B_j,A_j,C_j,P_{jk}\), the remaining checks are:

| Endpoint and adjacent type | Old outer neighbor’s value | New outer neighbor’s value |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(B_j\) adjacent to \(A_j\) | \(p(B_j,P_{jk})=0\) | \(p(B_j,C_j)=0\) |
| \(B_j\) adjacent to \(P_{jk}\) | \(p(B_j,A_j)=1\) | \(p(B_j,C_j)=0\) |
| \(A_j\) adjacent to \(B_j\) | \(p(A_j,C_j)=1\) | \(p(A_j,P_{jk})=0\) |
| \(A_j\) adjacent to \(C_j\) | \(p(A_j,B_j)=0\) | \(p(A_j,P_{jk})=0\) |

These exhaust the utility-\(1\) endpoints. Therefore every required adjacent-swap inequality holds.

The constructed \(\rho\) is an envy-free mass arrangement.

6. Conclusion

The reduction is polynomial, uses positive dyadic masses, and proves equivalence between satisfiability and the exact mass feasibility conditions in the question. Together with membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), this establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
