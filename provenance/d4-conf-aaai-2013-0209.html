The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), ranking types \(T\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and a rational budget \(b\), decide whether there exist an axis \(A\) and nonnegative transfers \(x_{\pi,\rho}\) such that \(\sum_{\rho}x_{\pi,\rho}=\mu_\pi\), \(x_{\pi,\rho}=0\) whenever \(\rho\notin\operatorname{SP}(A)\), and \(\sum_{\pi,\rho}d_K(\pi,\rho)x_{\pi,\rho}\le b\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, and let \(\mathcal R(C)\) be all strict total rankings of \(C\). An axis \(A\) is a total order on \(C\); \(\operatorname{SP}(A)\) denotes the rankings single-peaked with respect to \(A\). Write \(d_K\) for the unnormalized Kendall tau distance, with unit cost per adjacent swap.
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\subseteq\mathcal R(C)\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\pi\), where \(\sum_{\pi\in T}\mu_\pi=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. The input also includes a binary-rational budget \(b\ge 0\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Decide whether there exist one common axis \(A\) and real transfers \(x_{\pi,\rho}\ge 0\), indexed by \(\pi\in T\) and \(\rho\in\mathcal R(C)\), satisfying \(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R(C)}x_{\pi,\rho}=\mu_\pi\), \(x_{\pi,\rho}=0\) whenever \(\rho\notin\operatorname{SP}(A)\), and \(\sum_{\pi\in T,\rho\in\mathcal R(C)}d_K(\pi,\rho)x_{\pi,\rho}\le b\). Destination rankings need not belong to \(T\); their masses are unconstrained.

Is this decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of voters, Global Swaps Single-Peaked Consistency is NP-complete, even with eight voters; with a supplied axis, evaluation takes \(O(nm^3)\) time for \(n\) voters. [Erdélyi, Lackner, and Pfandler (2017)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.2627).
No result settling this exact mass-transfer formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the distribution normalization explicit: positive support masses sum to one, so the budget measures average swap cost.
  • Specified strict total rankings and a total-order axis, retaining all candidates and allowing arbitrary destination rankings, including those outside the input support.
  • Specified real nonnegative transfers with no prescribed destination masses.
  • Specified unnormalized Kendall tau distance with unit adjacent-swap costs.
  • Allowed a nonnegative rational budget, including zero, and restricted to at least two candidates to exclude the vacuous candidate domain.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(\tau\le 8\) and every mass is an integer multiple of \(1/8\). Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. Fractional transfers do not improve the optimum

For an axis \(A\) and a ranking \(\pi\), define

\(\displaystyle \delta_A(\pi):=\min_{\rho\in\operatorname{SP}(A)}d_K(\pi,\rho).\)

This minimum exists: \(\operatorname{SP}(A)\) is finite and nonempty. Indeed, if \(A\) orders the candidates as \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), the ranking \(a_1\succ\cdots\succ a_m\) is single-peaked on \(A\).

Lemma. For a fixed axis \(A\), the minimum feasible transfer cost equals

\(\displaystyle F(A):=\sum_{\pi\in T}\mu_\pi\delta_A(\pi).\)

Proof. Let \(x\) satisfy the mass and destination constraints for \(A\). For each \(\pi\in T\), only destinations in \(\operatorname{SP}(A)\) can receive positive mass. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R(C)}d_K(\pi,\rho)x_{\pi,\rho} \ge \delta_A(\pi)\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R(C)}x_{\pi,\rho} =\mu_\pi\delta_A(\pi).\)

Summing over \(\pi\) proves that every feasible transfer has cost at least \(F(A)\).

For the reverse inequality, choose a minimizer \(\rho_\pi\in\operatorname{SP}(A)\) for each \(\pi\), and set

\(\displaystyle x_{\pi,\rho}= \begin{cases} \mu_\pi,&\rho=\rho_\pi,\\ 0,&\rho\ne\rho_\pi. \end{cases}\)

These transfers satisfy every mass and destination constraint and have cost exactly \(F(A)\). There are no destination-mass constraints coupling the choices for different types. This proves the lemma.

Thus the original decision question is exactly whether

\(\displaystyle \min_A F(A)\le b.\)

In particular, allowing real transfers and splitting a type among several destinations does not reduce the optimum.

2. Polynomial-time reduction proving hardness

We use the following established result: Global Swaps Single-Peaked Consistency is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even for profiles consisting of eight voters. Here the budget counts adjacent swaps across all voters, and the resulting rankings must be single-peaked on one common axis. This is Theorem 6.9 and Remark 6.10 of [Erdélyi, Lackner, and Pfandler (2017)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.2627).

Take an instance of this restricted problem: a candidate set \(C\), a profile \(P=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_8)\), and a nonnegative integer swap budget \(K\).

Construct the mass-transfer instance as follows. Keep the same candidate set, let \(T\) contain precisely the distinct rankings occurring in \(P\), and define

\(\displaystyle r_\pi:=|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,8\}:\pi_i=\pi\}|,\qquad \mu_\pi:=\frac{r_\pi}{8},\qquad b:=\frac K8.\)

This is a valid instance. Each mass is positive, their sum is one, and \(\tau\le 8\). Both the masses and the budget have finite binary expansions. Aggregating the eight rankings takes polynomial time, and encoding \(K/8\) increases the bit length of \(K\) by at most a constant.

For any fixed axis \(A\), the minimum number of adjacent swaps needed to make the profile single-peaked on \(A\) is

\(\displaystyle S(A):=\sum_{i=1}^{8}\delta_A(\pi_i).\)

To justify this equality, any resulting single-peaked ranking for voter \(i\) requires at least \(\delta_A(\pi_i)\) swaps. Conversely, a minimizing destination for each voter can be reached in exactly its Kendall distance, because \(d_K\) is the minimum number of adjacent swaps. The choices for different voters are independent once \(A\) is fixed.

Grouping identical input rankings gives

\(\displaystyle F(A) =\sum_{\pi\in T}\frac{r_\pi}{8}\delta_A(\pi) =\frac18\sum_{i=1}^{8}\delta_A(\pi_i) =\frac{S(A)}8.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \exists A:\ S(A)\le K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists A:\ F(A)\le K/8 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed mass-transfer instance is feasible}.\)

The last equivalence follows from the lemma. This proves a polynomial-time many-one reduction and hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, including the stated restriction \(\tau\le 8\).

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate consists of one axis \(A\) and one destination ranking \(\rho_\pi\) for every \(\pi\in T\). Its size is \(O((\tau+1)m\log m)\).

The verifier first checks that each supplied order is a permutation of \(C\). It then checks that every \(\rho_\pi\) is single-peaked on \(A\). Explicitly, write \(A=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)\), and let \(a_p\) be the top-ranked candidate of \(\rho_\pi\). Check that

  • \(a_j\succ_{\rho_\pi}a_i\) for every \(i<j\le p\);
  • \(a_i\succ_{\rho_\pi}a_j\) for every \(p\le i<j\).

These are exactly the required monotonicity conditions on the two sides of the peak, and can be checked in \(O(m^2)\) time.

Finally, the verifier computes the Kendall distances by counting candidate pairs ordered differently and checks

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\pi\in T}\mu_\pi d_K(\pi,\rho_\pi)\le b.\)

This rational comparison takes polynomial time in the input bit length: a common denominator obtained by multiplying the encoded denominators has bit length at most their total encoded length.

If the certificate passes, sending all mass \(\mu_\pi\) to \(\rho_\pi\) supplies feasible transfers. Conversely, if feasible real transfers exist, the lemma provides minimizing destinations whose deterministic transfers also satisfy the budget. Hence every yes-instance has such a certificate.

Together with the reduction, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
