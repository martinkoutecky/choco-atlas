The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite alternative set \(C\), a finite set of complete-ranking voter types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a fixed rule for resolving pairwise-majority ties, let \(T_\mu\) be the induced tournament. Starting from any positive integer urn \(n_0\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{C}\), repeatedly draw three alternatives independently from the current urn distribution, compare them sequentially under \(T_\mu\), and add a ball for the resulting winner. Does the urn distribution \(p_\tau\) converge almost surely, and what is its limit? Theorem 4 says it converges to the unique \(p^*_\mu\) satisfying \(g_\mu(p^*_\mu,q)\geq 0\) for every distribution \(q\in\Delta(C)\).

Result

Yes. For every permitted society and every strictly positive initial urn, \(p_k\) converges almost surely to the unique distribution \(p^*\in\Delta(C)\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle g_\mu(p^*,q)\geq 0\qquad\text{for every }q\in\Delta(C).\)

The limit is deterministic and independent of the initial urn. It depends on the society only through the tournament \(T_\mu\), including its specified tie orientations.

Proof by the known convergence theorem

For distinct \(x,y\), define \(s_{xy}=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t\). Because each ranking is strict and complete, every type ranks exactly one of \(x,y\) above the other. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle s_{xy}+s_{yx}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t=1.\)

If \(s_{xy}>1/2\), then \(s_{yx}<1/2\); the reverse holds if \(s_{xy}<1/2\). If both equal \(1/2\), the prescribed rule chooses exactly one orientation. Thus \(T_\mu\) is a fixed finite tournament: each pair of distinct alternatives has exactly one directed edge. Its tournament payoff is precisely \(g_\mu\).

The Laslier–Laslier theorem states that, for any finite tournament and any urn containing a positive integer number of balls of every color, independently sampling three colors from the current proportions and adding one ball of the sequential winner produces proportions converging almost surely to the tournament’s unique optimal distribution. Optimality means nonnegative expected payoff against every distribution. This is Theorem 4, with uniqueness supplied by Proposition 1, in [Laslier and Laslier, *Reinforcement learning from comparisons: Three alternatives is enough, two is not*](https://arxiv.org/html/1301.5734).

Every hypothesis holds here:

  • The alternative set is finite and nonempty, and \(T_\mu\) is a fixed tournament.
  • The initial counts are strictly positive integers. Their binary encoding imposes no restriction on the theorem.
  • Conditional on the current urn, the three draws are independent with distribution \(p_k\).
  • The reinforced color is exactly \(w(w(a,b),c)\), the sequential winner used in the theorem.

The processes therefore have the same transition rule. Applying the theorem gives

\(\displaystyle \mathbb P\!\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}p_k=p^*\right)=1.\)

For \(m=1\), this also follows directly: the sole alternative always has proportion \(1\).

Exact characterization and computation of the limit

The limit can be computed without simulating the urn. Let \(e_y\) denote the distribution concentrated at \(y\). Bilinearity gives

\(\displaystyle g_\mu(p,q)=\sum_{y\in C}q(y)\,g_\mu(p,e_y).\)

Hence nonnegativity against every distribution is equivalent to nonnegativity against every \(e_y\). Therefore \(p^*\) is the unique feasible point of the rational linear system

\(\displaystyle p(x)\geq 0\quad(x\in C),\qquad \sum_{x\in C}p(x)=1,\qquad \sum_{x\in C}p(x)g_\mu(x,y)\geq 0\quad(y\in C).\)

Both directions follow explicitly: feasibility makes the displayed weighted sum nonnegative for every \(q\); conversely, taking \(q=e_y\) yields each inequality. Existence and uniqueness follow from the tournament theorem above.

For computational purposes, write \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\) and take \(D=\prod_t b_t\). The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Each comparison can be decided exactly by comparing \(2\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}a_t(D/b_t)\) with \(D\). Using elementary integer arithmetic, constructing all orientations takes at most \(O(\tau m^2L^2)\) bit operations.

The resulting linear program has \(m\) variables, \(O(m)\) constraints, and coefficients in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Standard exact rational linear programming computes its unique feasible point in \(\operatorname{poly}(m)\) bit operations. Thus the limit is exactly computable in total time \(O(\tau m^2L^2)+\operatorname{poly}(m)\). This computation does not require enumerating ranking types outside the supplied support.
