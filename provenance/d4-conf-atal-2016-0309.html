The continuous mirror question, as graded

The input includes a positive integer horizon \(T\), rational probabilities \(p_{tu}\in[0,1]\), and an explicit rational utility table \(U(0),\ldots,U(T)\) satisfying \(U(w)+U(T-w)=0\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Result

The specified exact SPE computation problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,T,L\). The algorithm computes two rational matrix-game equilibria, fills a table with \(O(T^2)\) entries, and constructs polynomial-size circuits for the behavioral strategies.

The proof accommodates arbitrary utility tables satisfying the stated hypotheses; monotonicity is unnecessary.

1. Two matrix games suffice

Enumerate the supports as \(S_A=\{1,\ldots,a\}\) and \(S_B=\{1,\ldots,b\}\), and write \(P=(p_{ij})\). Let \(\Delta_a,\Delta_b\) denote their probability simplices. For any real \(a\times b\) matrix \(M\), define its zero-sum value, with A maximizing, by

\(v(M)=\max_{x\in\Delta_a}\min_{y\in\Delta_b}x^{\mathsf T}My=\min_{y\in\Delta_b}\max_{x\in\Delta_a}x^{\mathsf T}My.\)

The equality and existence of optimal distributions follow from the finite zero-sum minimax theorem.

Compute optimal distribution pairs \((x^+,y^+)\) for \(P\) and \((x^-,y^-)\) for \(-P\). Define

\(\alpha=v(P),\qquad \beta=-v(-P).\)

Because every entry of \(P\) lies in \([0,1]\), both \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) lie in \([0,1]\).

Let \(J\) be the all-ones \(a\times b\) matrix. For arbitrary real numbers \(c,d\),

\(v(cJ+dP)=\begin{cases}c+d\alpha,&d>0,\\c,&d=0,\\c+d\beta,&d<0.\end{cases}\)

Moreover, an optimal distribution pair is \((x^+,y^+)\) when \(d>0\), \((x^-,y^-)\) when \(d<0\), and any pair when \(d=0\).

Justification. For every pair of distributions, \(x^{\mathsf T}Jy=1\). Adding \(cJ\) therefore adds the constant \(c\) to every expected payoff. Multiplication by a positive scalar preserves optimal distributions and scales the value.

If \(d<0\), then \(dP=(-d)(-P)\), where \(-d>0\). Hence the optimal pair for \(-P\) remains optimal, and

\(v(cJ+dP)=c+(-d)v(-P)=c+d\beta.\)

The negative case is essential because the utility table need not be monotone.

2. Backward recursion

A state is a pair \((k,w)\), where \(k\) rounds have been completed and A has won \(w\) of them. Its domain is \(0\leq w\leq k\leq T\).

At every nonterminal history, every supported type remains available. Indeed, fewer than \(T\) representatives have been used in total, while each supported type supplies at least \(T\). The masses impose no selection restrictions. Thus the available type choices do not depend on earlier choices.

Define numbers \(V_{k,w}\) backward, beginning with

\(V_{T,w}=U(w)\qquad(0\leq w\leq T).\)

Suppose the entries for round count \(k+1\) have been defined. Put

\(c_{k,w}=V_{k+1,w},\qquad d_{k,w}=V_{k+1,w+1}-V_{k+1,w}.\)

If the current choices are \(i,j\), their expected continuation value is

\(M^{k,w}_{ij}=p_{ij}V_{k+1,w+1}+(1-p_{ij})V_{k+1,w}=c_{k,w}+d_{k,w}p_{ij}.\)

Consequently, define

\(V_{k,w}=\begin{cases}c_{k,w}+d_{k,w}\alpha,&d_{k,w}>0,\\c_{k,w},&d_{k,w}=0,\\c_{k,w}+d_{k,w}\beta,&d_{k,w}<0.\end{cases}\)

At every history with state \((k,w)\), prescribe the pair \((x^+,y^+)\) if \(d_{k,w}>0\), the pair \((x^-,y^-)\) if \(d_{k,w}<0\), and a fixed pair of pure supported types if \(d_{k,w}=0\).

These prescriptions define behavioral strategies at every nonterminal public history.

3. Proof of subgame perfection

We prove by backward induction the following stronger assertion: at every history with state \((k,w)\), the prescribed strategy for A guarantees expected payoff at least \(V_{k,w}\) against every behavioral strategy for B, and the prescribed strategy for B guarantees A’s expected payoff at most \(V_{k,w}\) against every behavioral strategy for A.

This assertion quantifies over arbitrary history-dependent deviations.

At \(k=T\), the payoff is \(U(w)=V_{T,w}\), so both guarantees hold.

Now fix a history with state \((k,w)\), assuming both guarantees hold at every successor history.

First, suppose B follows the prescribed strategy and A deviates arbitrarily. Let \(x\) be A’s distribution at the current history, and let \(y^*\) be B’s prescribed distribution.

Conditional on the current choices and outcome, the induction hypothesis bounds A’s continuation payoff above by \(V_{k+1,w+1}\) after an A win and by \(V_{k+1,w}\) after an A loss. Therefore A’s expected payoff is at most

\(x^{\mathsf T}M^{k,w}y^*.\)

By Section 1, \(y^*\) is an optimal minimizing distribution for \(M^{k,w}\). Thus, for every \(x\),

\(x^{\mathsf T}M^{k,w}y^*\leq v(M^{k,w})=V_{k,w}.\)

This proves B’s guarantee.

Conversely, suppose A follows its prescribed strategy and B deviates arbitrarily. Write \(x^*\) for A’s prescribed distribution and \(y\) for B’s current distribution. The continuation guarantees for A give an expected payoff at least

\((x^*)^{\mathsf T}M^{k,w}y.\)

Because \(x^*\) is an optimal maximizing distribution,

\((x^*)^{\mathsf T}M^{k,w}y\geq v(M^{k,w})=V_{k,w}.\)

This proves A’s guarantee and completes the induction.

When both teams follow the prescribed strategies, the two guarantees force expected payoff \(V_{k,w}\). Neither team can profitably deviate in any subgame. Hence the constructed pair is an SPE, and the exact initial value is \(V_{0,0}\).

In particular, histories with equal round count and win count have equal values, regardless of their previous type choices.

4. Exact computation and bit complexity

Computing the two matrix-game equilibria

We use the standard theorem that rational linear programming admits exact optimization in time polynomial in the binary encoding length. Optimal rational solutions can be chosen with polynomial encoding length. These facts follow from polynomial-time LP algorithms and the determinant bounds for basic feasible solutions; see [Goemans’s linear programming notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-854j-advanced-algorithms-fall-2008/908fe54d659623efadda2d033b895caf_notes_lp.pdf).

For completeness, the applicable LPs have especially simple hypotheses. For each \(M\in\{P,-P\}\), solve the maximizing-player LP

\(\max z\quad\text{subject to}\quad x\in\Delta_a,\quad \sum_{i=1}^{a}x_iM_{ij}\geq z\ \text{for every }j,\quad -1\leq z\leq1,\)

and the minimizing-player LP

\(\min z\quad\text{subject to}\quad y\in\Delta_b,\quad \sum_{j=1}^{b}M_{ij}y_j\leq z\ \text{for every }i,\quad -1\leq z\leq1.\)

All coefficients are rational. The feasible regions are nonempty and compact: any simplex distribution with \(z=-1\) is feasible for the maximizing LP, and any simplex distribution with \(z=1\) is feasible for the minimizing LP. Since entries of \(M\) lie in \([-1,1]\), the bounds on \(z\) do not alter the optimal values. Minimax, equivalently LP duality, identifies their common value and optimal distributions.

Each LP has \(O(m)\) variables and \(O(\tau)\) constraints, with polynomial input length. Thus both equilibrium pairs and both values are computable exactly in polynomial time. Let \(B\) be a polynomial bound in \(m,\tau,L\) on the bit length of each resulting rational number.

Controlling all continuation denominators

A polynomial number of rational operations alone would not suffice; their operands must also have polynomial bit length. Here that follows from a common-denominator construction.

Let \(R\) be the product of the positive denominators of the input utilities. Then

\(RU(w)\in\mathbb Z\quad\text{for every }w,\qquad \log_2R=O(L).\)

Choose a common denominator \(Q\) for \(\alpha,\beta\), for example the product of their positive denominators. Write

\(\alpha=A_0/Q,\qquad \beta=B_0/Q,\)

where \(A_0,B_0\) are integers satisfying \(0\leq A_0,B_0\leq Q\), and \(\log_2Q=O(B)\).

We claim that every continuation value has the representation

\(V_{k,w}=\dfrac{N_{k,w}}{RQ^{T-k}},\qquad N_{k,w}\in\mathbb Z.\)

At \(k=T\), set \(N_{T,w}=RU(w)\). Suppose the representation holds at \(k+1\). The two successor values have the same denominator, so the sign of \(d_{k,w}\) is exactly the sign of

\(N_{k+1,w+1}-N_{k+1,w}.\)

Choose \(C=A_0\) for a positive difference, \(C=B_0\) for a negative difference, and \(C=0\) for a zero difference. The recurrence becomes the integer calculation

\(N_{k,w}=(Q-C)N_{k+1,w}+C N_{k+1,w+1}.\)

Dividing by \(RQ^{T-k}\) gives precisely the recurrence in Section 2, proving the claim.

Set \(H=\max_{0\leq w\leq T}|U(w)|\). Every recurrence is a convex combination of its two successor values, because \(0\leq C/Q\leq1\). Inductively,

\(|V_{k,w}|\leq H,\qquad |N_{k,w}|\leq HRQ^{T-k}.\)

The input encoding gives \(\log_2(1+H)=O(L)\). Therefore every numerator used by the algorithm has bit length

\(O\bigl(1+L+TB\bigr).\)

There are \(O(T^2)\) table entries, and each requires a constant number of integer comparisons, additions, and multiplications on polynomial-size integers. The entire table, including \(V_{0,0}\), is therefore computable in polynomial bit time.

5. Polynomial-size strategy circuits

During the backward computation, store the sign of \(d_{k,w}\) for each nonterminal state. This table has \(O(T^2)\) entries.

Use a fixed-length history encoding consisting of its length \(k\) and at most \(T\) padded round records. A circuit computes \(w\) by counting A’s wins among the first \(k\) records. It then looks up the stored sign for \((k,w)\) and outputs:

  • \(x^+\) for A and \(y^+\) for B when the sign is positive;
  • \(x^-\) for A and \(y^-\) for B when the sign is negative;
  • the fixed pure distributions when the sign is zero.

Represent each probability by its binary numerator and positive denominator, padded to a common polynomial width. Counting, table lookup, and selecting among these constant vectors all admit Boolean circuits of polynomial size, constructible in polynomial time.

The circuits therefore evaluate the exact behavioral distributions at every nonterminal history. Their behavior on terminal or malformed encodings can be assigned arbitrarily.

The explicit utility table contains \(T+1\) rational entries, so \(T=O(L)\). Thus the algorithm is polynomial in the full binary input length as well as in the requested parameters.
