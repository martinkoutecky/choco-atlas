Theorem 4.1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), each type has a group \(g(t)\), eligible departments, a strict preference order, and a department-priority profile. Assume the eligible type–department pairs admit a common total order consistent with each type’s preferences and each department’s priorities. Given rational lower and upper quotas for departments and group–department pairs, and assuming a feasible complete allocation exists, does there exist a polynomial-time algorithm in the input bit length and the numbers of types, groups, and departments that finds a rational allocation \(x_{t,d}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{d}x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) satisfying all quotas and limited stability? For the stability test, reset each group–department upper quota to its realized mass \(\bar q_{g,d}=\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}\). A block exists if some positive mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(d_0\) prefers an eligible department \(d\), and a lower-priority type \(t'\) in the same group has positive mass at \(d\), so that for some \(\varepsilon>0\), moving \(\varepsilon\) of \(t\) from \(d_0\) to \(d\) and leaving \(\varepsilon\) of \(t'\) unassigned respects the upper bounds. As in the paper’s blocking test, this check concerns upper-bound feasibility of the resulting partial assignment; the complete allocation itself satisfies the lower bounds.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
There are \(m\) departments and \(r\) groups, each containing positive mass. Every type \(t\) belongs to one group \(g(t)\); all types in group \(g\) have the same nonempty eligible department set \(D_g\).
Each type has a strict preference order on \(D_{g(t)}\), and each department has a strict priority order on its eligible types. Mass within one type has equal priority. Assume the eligible pairs admit a common total order whose restrictions agree with both preferences and priorities.
Department quotas \(p_d,q_d\) and eligible group–department quotas \(p_{g,d},q_{g,d}\) are finite nonnegative binary rationals, with lower bounds at most upper bounds; assume \(\sum_{g:d\in D_g}p_{g,d}\le p_d\) and \(q_d\le\sum_{g:d\in D_g}q_{g,d}\).
A complete allocation is a rational array \(x_{t,d}\ge0\), zero on ineligible pairs, satisfying \(\sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t\), \(p_d\le\sum_t x_{t,d}\le q_d\), and \(p_{g,d}\le\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}\le q_{g,d}\). Promise that a feasible complete allocation exists.
For its stability test, replace each group–department upper bound by \(\bar q_{g,d}=\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}\), retain department upper bounds, and ignore all lower bounds.
A block consists of types \(t,t'\) in the same group and departments \(d_0,d\), with \(x_{t,d_0}>0\), \(x_{t',d}>0\), \(d\succ_t d_0\), and \(t\succ_d t'\), such that some \(0<\varepsilon\le\min\{x_{t,d_0},x_{t',d}\}\) can be moved from \((t,d_0)\) to \((t,d)\) while removing that much mass from \((t',d)\), respecting the upper bounds. Limited stability means absence of blocks; admission without displacement is impossible under the reset bounds.

Is finding a feasible, limited-stable complete allocation on these promised inputs polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,r,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For indivisible students with group-based eligibility, feasible complete assignment and the same common-order condition, Greedy-Alloc finds a limited-stable complete assignment in polynomial time. [Akahori, Sekiguchi and Tamura](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/torsj/60/0/60_50/_pdf)
No result settling this exact rational-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored group-based eligibility: all types in a group share its eligible department set, as required by the discrete model.
  • Specified strict department priorities over distinct eligible types; mass within a type has equal priority and does not strictly outrank itself.
  • Removed zero-mass types from the support and required every listed group to contain positive mass.
  • Specified finite nonnegative rational quotas with ordered endpoints and carried over the discrete model's department versus group-quota consistency conditions.
  • Made explicit that the reset bounds exclude admission without displacement; the replacement test ignores lower bounds and permits the displaced mass to remain unassigned.

Result

Finding a feasible, limited-stable complete allocation is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,r,L\).

The algorithm first fixes feasible group–department totals. It then distributes those totals among types by processing eligible pairs in a common order. Group-based eligibility ensures completeness, and the common-order condition ensures limited stability.

1. Compute feasible group–department totals

Write \(G\) for the set of groups and define each group’s mass by
\(M_g=\sum_{t:g(t)=g}\mu_t\).

Construct a circulation network with vertices \(\sigma,\omega\), one vertex for each group, and one vertex for each department. Its arcs and lower–upper capacity intervals are:

| Arc | Capacity interval |
|---|---|
| \(\sigma\to g\) | \([M_g,M_g]\) |
| \(g\to d\), for \(d\in D_g\) | \([p_{g,d},q_{g,d}]\) |
| \(d\to\omega\) | \([p_d,q_d]\) |
| \(\omega\to\sigma\) | \([1,1]\) |

The feasibility promise implies that this network has a feasible circulation: aggregate any promised feasible allocation by group and department.

Find a feasible rational circulation, and let \(y_{g,d}\) be its flow on \(g\to d\). Flow conservation and the capacity bounds give

\( \displaystyle \sum_{d\in D_g}y_{g,d}=M_g,\qquad p_{g,d}\le y_{g,d}\le q_{g,d},\qquad p_d\le\sum_{g:d\in D_g}y_{g,d}\le q_d. \)

We will construct an allocation whose group–department totals are exactly these \(y_{g,d}\).

2. Construct the common order

Let \(E=\{(t,d):t\in T,\ d\in D_{g(t)}\}\), and let \(K=|E|\le m\tau\).

A common order can be found even if it is not supplied explicitly. Construct a directed graph on \(E\):

  • For each type, add an arc from each eligible pair to the next pair in its preference order, with more preferred departments first.
  • For each department, add an arc from each eligible pair to the next pair in its priority order, with higher-priority types first.

The promised common total order respects every arc, so this graph is acyclic. A topological ordering therefore gives an order
\(e_1,\ldots,e_K\) satisfying

\( \displaystyle d\succ_t d_0\ \Longrightarrow\ (t,d)\text{ precedes }(t,d_0), \)

and

\( \displaystyle t\succ_d t'\ \Longrightarrow\ (t,d)\text{ precedes }(t',d). \)

Adjacent comparisons suffice because the remaining comparisons follow by transitivity.

3. Allocate greedily in that order

Initially set every allocation entry to zero. Maintain residual type masses \(R_t=\mu_t\) and residual group–department capacities \(C_{g,d}=y_{g,d}\).

Process each pair \((t,d)\) once, in the common order. With \(g=g(t)\), set

\( \displaystyle \delta=\min\{R_t,C_{g,d}\},\qquad x_{t,d}=\delta, \)

and update

\( \displaystyle R_t\leftarrow R_t-\delta,\qquad C_{g,d}\leftarrow C_{g,d}-\delta. \)

Ineligible entries remain zero.

All residuals stay nonnegative and never increase. Throughout the algorithm,

\( \displaystyle R_t=\mu_t-\sum_d x_{t,d},\qquad C_{g,d}=y_{g,d}-\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}. \)

Here unprocessed entries are still zero.

4. Proof of completeness and feasibility

First, no eligible pair can have both residuals positive at termination.

Indeed, suppose final residuals satisfy \(R_t>0\) and \(C_{g(t),d}>0\). Since residuals never increase, both were positive when \((t,d)\) was processed. Subtracting their minimum made at least one of them zero. It remained zero thereafter, contradicting the supposed final residuals.

Next, for every group \(g\), the residual identities give

\( \displaystyle \sum_{t:g(t)=g}R_t =M_g-\sum_{t:g(t)=g}\sum_{d\in D_g}x_{t,d} =\sum_{d\in D_g}C_{g,d}. \)

If some final \(R_t\) were positive, this equality would provide a department \(d\in D_{g(t)}\) with final \(C_{g(t),d}>0\). Crucially, group-based eligibility makes \((t,d)\) eligible. This contradicts the preceding paragraph.

Thus every final \(R_t\) is zero. The displayed equality and nonnegativity then imply that every final \(C_{g,d}\) is also zero. Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,d}=y_{g,d}. \)

The computed totals \(y_{g,d}\) satisfy all original group–department and department bounds. Hence \(x\) is a feasible complete allocation.

5. Proof of limited stability

We prove that there cannot exist types \(t,t'\) in one group \(g\) and departments \(d_0,d\) satisfying

\( \displaystyle x_{t,d_0}>0,\qquad x_{t',d}>0,\qquad d\succ_t d_0,\qquad t\succ_d t'. \)

Suppose otherwise. The common order places \((t,d)\) before both \((t,d_0)\) and \((t',d)\).

When \((t,d)\) is processed, the allocation at \((t,d_0)\) has not yet occurred. Since its eventual value is positive and residual type mass only decreases, we have

\( \displaystyle R_t\ge x_{t,d_0}>0. \)

Likewise, the allocation at \((t',d)\) has not yet occurred. Both types belong to \(g\), so that later allocation uses the same residual capacity \(C_{g,d}\). Therefore,

\( \displaystyle C_{g,d}\ge x_{t',d}>0. \)

Processing \((t,d)\) exhausts at least one of these two positive residuals.

  • If it exhausts \(R_t\), every later allocation involving type \(t\) is zero, contradicting \(x_{t,d_0}>0\).
  • If it exhausts \(C_{g,d}\), every later allocation from group \(g\) to department \(d\) is zero, contradicting \(x_{t',d}>0\).

This proves that no such witnesses exist, and therefore no block exists.

For clarity, the upper-bound condition in the specified block test adds no obstacle to witnesses of this form: for any \(0<\varepsilon\le\min\{x_{t,d_0},x_{t',d}\}\), the proposed displacement leaves the group–department and department totals at \(d\) unchanged and decreases those at \(d_0\). It preserves nonnegativity and respects every retained upper bound. Our argument excludes the witnesses themselves.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

The circulation computation and greedy allocation can be performed exactly with polynomially many bits.

Let \(H\) be the product of the positive denominators of all input masses and quotas, as represented in the input. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and multiplying all capacities by \(H\) produces integers of polynomial bit length. In particular, each scaled \(HM_g\) is an integer.

The standard feasible-circulation reduction applies: subtract each arc’s lower bound, compute the resulting vertex imbalances, and introduce a super-source and super-sink. A maximum flow saturating all super-source arcs yields a feasible circulation. All capacities are nonnegative integers after scaling, so an integral maximum-flow algorithm applies. For example, Edmonds–Karp uses polynomially many arithmetic operations independently of capacity magnitudes; the integers manipulated have polynomial bit length.

The original network has \(r+m+2\) vertices and at most \(rm+r+m+1\) arcs. Thus this computation is polynomial in \(m,r,L\), together with the input size needed to aggregate the masses.

After rescaling, every \(y_{g,d}\) is a multiple of \(1/H\). The greedy algorithm uses only minima and subtraction, so every output entry is also a multiple of \(1/H\). Since \(0\le x_{t,d}\le1\), each entry has a representation \(a/H\) with \(0\le a\le H\), using \(O(L)\) bits.

Finally, the precedence graph has \(K\le m\tau\) vertices and \(O(K)\) arcs. Its topological ordering and the \(K\) greedy steps take polynomial time.

Thus the algorithm produces an exactly represented feasible, limited-stable complete allocation in polynomial time.
