The continuous mirror question, as graded

For fixed \(k\), let \(\mu\in\Delta(S_k)\) be a society distribution over ranking types, and let \(\Phi\) be an aggregate IIA rule whose comparison of \(a\) and \(b\) is \(\phi_{ab}(p_{ab}(\mu))\), where \(p_{ab}(\mu)=\sum_{\sigma:a\succ_\sigma b}\mu_\sigma\). Given a specified distribution \(\Pi\) over societies and query access to \(\Phi\), prove or refute that for every \(\epsilon>0\) there is a \(\delta_{k,\Pi}(\epsilon)>0\) such that \(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)>\epsilon\) implies \(\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[\Phi(\mu)\text{ is nontransitive}]\ge\delta_{k,\Pi}(\epsilon)\), where \(\mathcal G_k^\infty\) is the class of aggregate IIA rules transitive for every society.

Fix \(m=k\ge3\) alternatives, and let \(S_k\) be their strict rankings. The society domain is \(\mathcal D_k=\Delta(S_k)\cap\mathbb Q^{S_k}\), so masses are nonnegative and sum to \(1\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) ranking types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not \(k!\); measure any running times in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and other stated parameters.
An aggregate IIA rule has arbitrary functions \(\phi_{ab}:\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\to\{-1,1\}\) for \(a<b\), with \(\Phi_{ab}(\mu)=\phi_{ab}(p_{ab}(\mu))\) and \(p_{ab}(\mu)=\sum_{\sigma:a\succ_\sigma b}\mu_\sigma\). The sign \(1\) means socially preferring \(a\) to \(b\); reverse comparisons have opposite signs, and ties are forbidden.
Let \(\mathcal G_k^\infty\) consist of these rules whose output is transitive for every \(\mu\in\mathcal D_k\). Unanimity is not required.
Let \(\Pi\) be any specified probability measure on the countable domain \(\mathcal D_k\), and suppose queries return \(\Phi(\mu)\). Define \(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)=\inf_{G\in\mathcal G_k^\infty}\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[\Phi(\mu)\ne G(\mu)]\), where disagreement means that at least one pairwise comparison differs.

For every fixed \(k\ge3\) and every \(\Pi\), does there exist, for each \(\epsilon>0\), a number \(\delta_{k,\Pi}(\epsilon)>0\), independent of \(\Phi\), such that \(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)>\epsilon\) implies \(\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[\Phi(\mu)\text{ is nontransitive}]\ge\delta_{k,\Pi}(\epsilon)\)?

For finite electorates with independent uniform rankings, [Mossel](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.2574) proves that paradox probability below \(\exp(-C/\eta^{21})\) implies disagreement probability below \(k^2\eta\) with some everywhere-transitive IIA rule, uniformly in electorate size.
[Keller](https://arxiv.org/html/1003.3956v1) strengthens this to paradox probability at most \(c(\epsilon/k^2)^3\) implying distance at most \(\epsilon\), for an absolute \(c>0\). No result settling the stated mass-domain question for arbitrary \(\Pi\) was located.

Answer

No. For every \(k\ge3\), there is a probability measure \(\Pi\) and an aggregate IIA rule \(\Phi\) with zero paradox probability but
\(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)=1/2\).

The obstruction is that transitive outputs on the support of \(\Pi\) need not extend to an everywhere-transitive IIA rule. After constructing the counterexample, we prove that the requested conclusion does hold whenever \(\Pi\) has full support.

1. A counterexample for every \(k\ge3\)

Label the alternatives \(1,\ldots,k\). Define four rankings, with the final segment omitted when \(k=3\):

  • \(r_0:\ 1\succ2\succ3\succ4\succ\cdots\succ k\).
  • \(r_1:\ 2\succ3\succ1\succ4\succ\cdots\succ k\).
  • \(r_2:\ 3\succ1\succ2\succ4\succ\cdots\succ k\).
  • \(r_-:\ 3\succ2\succ1\succ4\succ\cdots\succ k\).

Write \(\delta_r\) for the society concentrated on ranking \(r\), and set
\(\mu^+=\frac23\delta_{r_0}+\frac13\delta_{r_-}\) and
\(\mu^-=\frac13\delta_{r_0}+\frac23\delta_{r_-}\).

Both societies belong to \(\mathcal D_k\), and each has ranking-support size \(\tau=2\). Define the sampling measure by
\(\Pi(\{\mu^+\})=\Pi(\{\mu^-\})=1/2\).

Let \(\Phi\) be majority rule with a fixed tie convention: for every \(a<b\), let \(\phi_{ab}(t)=1\) when \(t>1/2\), and let \(\phi_{ab}(t)=-1\) when \(t\le1/2\). These functions define an aggregate IIA rule on the entire specified domain.

At \(\mu^+\), the triple \((p_{12},p_{23},p_{13})\) equals \((2/3,2/3,2/3)\); at \(\mu^-\), it equals \((1/3,1/3,1/3)\).

Every other pair has the same orientation in \(r_0\) and \(r_-\), so majority rule preserves that orientation. Consequently,
\(\Phi(\mu^+)=r_0\) and \(\Phi(\mu^-)=r_-\).
Both outputs are transitive. Therefore

2. The distance is exactly \(1/2\)

Suppose an aggregate IIA rule \(G\) agrees with \(\Phi\) at both sampled societies. Writing its pair functions as \(g_{ab}\), agreement at \(\mu^+\) forces
\(g_{12}(2/3)=g_{23}(2/3)=1\).
Agreement at \(\mu^-\) forces \(g_{13}(1/3)=-1\).

Now consider the rational society
\(\nu=\frac13\delta_{r_0}+\frac13\delta_{r_1}+\frac13\delta_{r_2}\), whose ranking-support size is \(\tau=3\).

Directly from the three rankings:

  • \(1\succ2\) occurs in \(r_0,r_2\), so \(p_{12}(\nu)=2/3\).
  • \(2\succ3\) occurs in \(r_0,r_1\), so \(p_{23}(\nu)=2/3\).
  • \(1\succ3\) occurs only in \(r_0\), so \(p_{13}(\nu)=1/3\).

IIA therefore forces \(G(\nu)\) to contain the cycle
\(1\succ_G2\succ_G3\succ_G1\).
Thus \(G\notin\mathcal G_k^\infty\).

It follows that every \(G\in\mathcal G_k^\infty\) must disagree with \(\Phi\) at at least one of \(\mu^+,\mu^-\). Each has probability \(1/2\), giving
\(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)\ge1/2\).

Conversely, the constant rule that always outputs \(r_0\) belongs to \(\mathcal G_k^\infty\). It agrees at \(\mu^+\) and disagrees at \(\mu^-\), giving the reverse inequality. Hence
\(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)=1/2\).

Taking \(\epsilon=1/4\) disproves the requested implication for every positive choice of \(\delta_{k,\Pi}(1/4)\).

3. An exact criterion for a fixed measure

We now characterize when the requested conclusion holds.

Define
\(q_\Pi(\Phi)=\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[\Phi(\mu)\text{ is nontransitive}]\), and define the disagreement pseudometric
\(\rho_\Pi(\Phi,\Psi)=\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[\Phi(\mu)\ne\Psi(\mu)]\).

Proposition. For fixed \(k\) and \(\Pi\), the requested conclusion holds for every \(\epsilon>0\) if and only if
\(q_\Pi(\Phi)=0\) implies \(d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)=0\) for every aggregate IIA rule \(\Phi\).

Moreover, distance zero is equivalent to the existence of a single \(G\in\mathcal G_k^\infty\) agreeing with \(\Phi\) at every society of positive \(\Pi\)-mass.

Compactness argument

The coordinates specifying a rule are indexed by
\(C=\{(a,b,t):1\le a<b\le k,\ t\in\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\}\).
This set is countable, and each coordinate takes one of two values.

Given any sequence of rules, enumerate \(C\) and successively take infinite subsequences constant at each coordinate. The diagonal subsequence converges coordinatewise to an aggregate IIA rule \(\Phi_\infty\). At any fixed society, only finitely many coordinates determine the output, so the outputs eventually agree with \(\Phi_\infty\).

This convergence also holds in \(\rho_\Pi\). Indeed, for any \(\alpha>0\), countability of \(\mathcal D_k\) gives a finite set \(F\) with \(\Pi(F)>1-\alpha\). Eventually, the subsequence agrees with \(\Phi_\infty\) simultaneously at every society in \(F\). Its disagreement probability is then less than \(\alpha\).

Two elementary inequalities give continuity of the quantities we need:
\(\lvert q_\Pi(\Phi)-q_\Pi(\Psi)\rvert\le\rho_\Pi(\Phi,\Psi)\), and
\(\lvert d_\Pi(\Phi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)-d_\Pi(\Psi,\mathcal G_k^\infty)\rvert\le\rho_\Pi(\Phi,\Psi)\).
The first holds because the transitivity indicators can differ only when the outputs differ. The second follows by applying the triangle inequality to disagreement with each \(G\), taking infima, and interchanging \(\Phi,\Psi\).

Coordinatewise limits of everywhere-transitive rules remain everywhere transitive: at each society, the output eventually equals the limiting output. Applying the subsequence argument to a sequence approaching the distance infimum therefore shows that the infimum is attained.

Proof of the criterion

Necessity follows immediately: if \(q_\Pi(\Phi)=0\) but its distance is positive, choose \(\epsilon\) strictly between zero and that distance.

For sufficiency, assume every rule with \(q_\Pi(\Phi)=0\) has distance zero. If the requested conclusion failed at some \(\epsilon>0\), there would be rules \(\Phi_n\) satisfying
\(d_\Pi(\Phi_n,\mathcal G_k^\infty)>\epsilon\) and \(q_\Pi(\Phi_n)<1/n\).

Take a coordinatewise convergent subsequence. The continuity just proved gives
\(q_\Pi(\Phi_\infty)=0\) and
\(d_\Pi(\Phi_\infty,\mathcal G_k^\infty)\ge\epsilon\), contradicting the assumption.

Finally, attainment of the distance infimum shows that distance zero means agreement almost surely with one everywhere-transitive rule. On a countable domain, this means agreement at every atom of positive mass. This proves the proposition.

4. Full support restores the conclusion

Suppose \(\Pi(\{\mu\})>0\) for every \(\mu\in\mathcal D_k\).

If \(q_\Pi(\Phi)=0\), then \(\Phi\) cannot be nontransitive at any society: such a society would contribute positive probability to \(q_\Pi(\Phi)\). Hence \(\Phi\in\mathcal G_k^\infty\), and its distance is zero.

The proposition therefore proves that the requested positive bound exists for every full-support measure, although this argument supplies no explicit quantitative formula.

The counterexample does not contradict the cited finite-electorate results. Keller’s theorem uses the uniform measure on all finite profiles, under which every profile has positive probability; our measure assigns zero probability to the society witnessing the forced cycle.
