The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set \(T\) of top-\(k\) ranking types with mass distribution \(\mu\), finite good classes \(G\) with \(\rho_g\) indivisible copies per unit population, and class-symmetric rankings \(\sigma_t\), decide whether there exist masses \(x_{t,A}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(A\) such that \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,A}A_gx_{t,A}=\rho_g\) for every \(g\), and whenever \(x_{t,A}>0\) and \(x_{s,B}>0\), every additive unit-sum valuation \(v\succeq\sigma_t\) satisfies \(v(A)\ge v(B)\) or \(v(A)\ge v(B\setminus\{g\})\) for some \(g\in B\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a set of \(m\ge1\) good classes, with positive binary-rational supplies \(\rho_g\) of indivisible copies per unit population. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type reports an ordered list \(\sigma_t\) of its top \(k\) distinct classes, with a common \(0\le k\le m\). Copies within a class have equal value. Consistent valuations are vectors \(v\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{G}\) with \(\sum_gv_g=1\): values weakly decrease along the reported list, and every ranked class is weakly preferred to every unranked class. Ties may be ordered arbitrarily; unranked classes have no further constraints.
An integral bundle is \(A\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\), including the empty bundle, with \(v(A)=\sum_g A_gv_g\). There is no individual bound on copies beyond aggregate supply. An allocation consists of nonnegative real masses \(x_{t,A}\), indexed by all integral bundles, satisfying \(\sum_Ax_{t,A}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,A}A_gx_{t,A}=\rho_g\) for every \(g\).
Require robust EF1 across the entire positive support: for every \((t,A),(s,B)\) with \(x_{t,A}x_{s,B}>0\), including \(t=s\), and every valuation \(v\) consistent with \(\sigma_t\), either \(v(A)\ge v(B)\), or there exists \(g\) with \(B_g>0\) such that \(v(A)\ge v(B)-v_g\). The removed copy may depend on \(v\).

Is deciding whether such an allocation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies with \(n\ge2\) agents and \(m\) distinct goods, polynomial-time rules guarantee robust EF1 for every top-\(k\) profile exactly when \(k\ge m-n\) if \(m\bmod n=0\), \(k\ge m-2\) if \(m\bmod n=1\), and \(k\ge m-(m\bmod n)\) otherwise. [Halpern and Shah](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~nisarg/papers/distortion-fair-division.pdf)
No result settling this exact mass feasibility problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to one and took supplies to be positive binary rationals; classes with zero supply are omitted.
  • Interpreted m and top-k rankings at the class level: m is the number of classes, rankings list distinct classes, and every copy of a class has the same value.
  • Normalized per-copy class values by their sum over the class catalogue; consistency uses weak inequalities and arbitrary ordering of ties, with no ordering imposed among unranked classes.
  • Allowed the common ranking length to range from zero to m, retaining the empty-ranking case explicitly considered by the discrete result.
  • Interpreted integral bundles as arbitrary nonnegative integer copy vectors, including empty bundles, without bounding an individual's copies by a per-unit-population supply.
  • Allowed nonnegative real allocation masses over all integral bundles and applied robust EF1 to all positive-support pairs, including different bundles assigned to the same type; removing a good means removing one copy.

Status and new result

This is a partial resolution. The general complexity classification remains open.

I prove a stronger polynomial-time special case than the one in the notes:

If every type reports the same set of ranked classes, feasibility is decidable in polynomial time. The reported orders may differ.

In particular, this resolves every instance with a single type.

The decision in this case depends only on the supplies of the unranked classes and the fractional part of the total ranked supply. Individual ranked supplies need not be integral.

I also give self-contained proofs of the pairwise verification criterion and membership in $\mathrm{NP}$, which are used below.

1. Exact verification of robust EF1

For a bundle $A$ and a set $S\subseteq G$, write
$$ A(S)=\sum_{g\in S}A_g, \qquad |A|=\sum_{g\in G}A_g. $$
For a real number $z$, write $z_+=\max\{z,0\}$.

Fix a type $t$ with report
$$ \sigma_t=(g_{t,1},\ldots,g_{t,k}). $$
Let $R_t$ be its ranked set, $U_t=G\setminus R_t$, and
$$ P_{t,j}=\{g_{t,1},\ldots,g_{t,j}\}. $$

Proposition. Bundle $A$ satisfies robust EF1 toward bundle $B$, under type $t$'s report, exactly when
$$ B(P_{t,j})-A(P_{t,j})\le 1 \qquad (1\le j\le k) $$
and
$$ B(R_t)-A(R_t) +\sum_{g\in U_t}(B_g-A_g)_+ \le 1. $$

Proof. For a consistent valuation $v$, put
$$ M_B(v)=\max\bigl(\{v_g:B_g>0\}\cup\{0\}\bigr). $$
The required EF1 condition is equivalent to
$$ v(B)-v(A)\le M_B(v). $$
Indeed, if envy is positive, removing a copy attaining this maximum eliminates it exactly when the inequality holds. If there is no envy, the inequality holds automatically.

Call a set $S$ upper if membership of a class forces membership of every class constrained to precede it. The nonempty upper sets are the ranked prefixes and the sets $R_t\cup W$, where $W\subseteq U_t$, excluding the empty set.

For every nonempty upper set $S$, the valuation $\mathbf 1_S/|S|$ is consistent. Robust EF1 therefore implies
$$ B(S)-A(S)\le 1. $$
If $B(S)=0$, this inequality follows directly from $A(S)\ge0$.

Conversely, suppose these upper-set inequalities hold. Let the distinct positive coordinate values of a consistent valuation be
$$ 0<a_1<\cdots<a_\ell, \qquad a_0=0, $$
and put $S_i=\{g:v_g\ge a_i\}$. Each $S_i$ is upper, and
$$ v=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(a_i-a_{i-1})\mathbf 1_{S_i}. $$
If $B(S_i)=0$, its contribution to $v(B)-v(A)$ is nonpositive. Otherwise, its contribution is at most $a_i-a_{i-1}$. Consequently,
$$ v(B)-v(A) \le \sum_{i:B(S_i)>0}(a_i-a_{i-1}) =M_B(v). $$
The equality follows because precisely the thresholds at or below $M_B(v)$ intersect $B$.

Finally,
$$ \max_{W\subseteq U_t} \bigl(B(R_t)-A(R_t)+B(W)-A(W)\bigr) = B(R_t)-A(R_t)+\sum_{g\in U_t}(B_g-A_g)_+. $$
This gives the claimed finite test. $\square$

2. Polynomial certificates for the general problem

Let
$$ s=\sum_{g\in G}\rho_g. $$

Proposition. Every supported bundle in a feasible allocation has size $\lfloor s\rfloor$ or $\lceil s\rceil$. Every feasible instance has a certificate with at most $m+\tau$ positive type–bundle pairs, with polynomial-length rational masses. Thus the general problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

Proof. The uniform valuation $v_g=1/m$ is consistent with every report. Applying robust EF1 in both directions gives
$$ \bigl||A|-|B|\bigr|\le1 $$
for any two supported bundles.

The nonempty set of supported bundle sizes has a minimum $q$, since its elements are nonnegative integers. All supported sizes are therefore $q$ or $q+1$. There are only finitely many integral bundles of these sizes, so the allocation has finite support.

The allocation equalities imply
$$ \sum_{t,A}x_{t,A}=1, \qquad \sum_{t,A}|A|x_{t,A}=s. $$
Thus $s$ is their weighted average. If both sizes occur, $q<s<q+1$; otherwise the common size equals $s$. This proves the size restriction.

Associate to a type–bundle pair the integer column
$$ c_{t,A}=(e_t,A)\in\mathbb Z^{m+\tau}. $$
The allocation constraints are
$$ \sum_{t,A}x_{t,A}c_{t,A} = b, \qquad b=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau,\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_m). $$

If the positive-support columns are dependent, choose a nonzero dependence $\sum_i\lambda_i c_i=0$. Summing the type coordinates gives $\sum_i\lambda_i=0$, so the dependence has both positive and negative coefficients. Set
$$ \varepsilon=\min_{\lambda_i>0}\frac{x_i}{\lambda_i}, \qquad x_i'=x_i-\varepsilon\lambda_i. $$
This preserves all equations, keeps all masses nonnegative, and removes at least one support entry. Robust EF1 is preserved because the support only shrinks.

Repeating yields at most $m+\tau$ linearly independent columns. Choose an invertible square row submatrix $C$ of these columns. The remaining masses are uniquely determined by its rational equations and hence are rational.

Every bundle coordinate is at most $\lceil s\rceil$, whose bit length is polynomial in the input length. A common denominator $D$ of $b$ can be obtained by multiplying the input denominators; its bit length is also polynomial. Cramer's rule writes each surviving mass as
$$ x_i=\frac{\det C_i}{D\det C}, $$
where $C_i$ replaces the corresponding column by the selected coordinates of $Db$.

If these matrices have dimension $h$ and entries bounded in absolute value by $H$, their determinants are bounded by $h!H^h$. Here $h\le m+\tau$ and $\log H$ is polynomial in the input length. Thus every mass has polynomial bit length.

A verifier checks the allocation equalities and the criterion from Section 1 for every ordered support pair. All these checks take polynomial time. $\square$

3. Reduction when the ranked set is common

Assume from now on that every type ranks the same set $R$, although its order on $R$ may differ. Write
$$ U=G\setminus R, \qquad z=(\rho_g)_{g\in U}, $$
and
$$ S_R=\sum_{g\in R}\rho_g, \qquad q=\lfloor S_R\rfloor, \qquad r=S_R-q. $$

For a nonnegative vector $w$ on $U$, write $|w|=\sum_{g\in U}w_g$. All vector inequalities below are coordinatewise.

3.1. Necessary restrictions on unranked bundles

The set $R$ is upper for every type. Hence the counts $A(R)$ of supported bundles differ by at most one. Their average is $S_R$.

If $r=0$, every supported bundle has ranked total $q$.

If $0<r<1$, every supported bundle has ranked total $q$ or $q+1$. The total mass with ranked total $q+1$ is exactly $r$, and the remaining mass is $1-r$.

In the latter case, let $\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal H$ be the distinct unranked vectors occurring with ranked totals $q$ and $q+1$, respectively. Both families are nonempty.

Within either family, Section 1 gives
$$ \sum_{g\in U}(B_g-A_g)_+\le1 $$
for every ordered pair.

For $L\in\mathcal L$ and $H\in\mathcal H$, the comparison from the low ranked total to the high ranked total gives
$$ 1+\sum_{g\in U}(H_g-L_g)_+\le1, $$
so $H\le L$. The reverse comparison gives
$$ -1+\sum_{g\in U}(L_g-H_g)_+\le1. $$
Together these are exactly
$$ H\le L, \qquad |L-H|\le2. $$

The supply vector must therefore satisfy
$$ z=(1-r)\,\mathbb E[L]+r\,\mathbb E[H] $$
for distributions on these families.

3.2. These restrictions are sufficient

The ranked coordinates can be supplied without imposing any further restrictions on these unranked distributions.

To prove this, choose a positive integer $N$ divisible by the denominators of all type masses and ranked supplies. Create $N\mu_t$ agents of each type and $N\rho_g$ copies of every ranked class. Allocate these copies by cyclic round robin, with each agent choosing its highest-ranked available class.

Every agent receives $q$ or $q+1$ ranked copies. The proportion receiving $q+1$ is $r$.

The allocation satisfies every ranked-prefix inequality. Indeed, fix agents $i,j$ and a prefix $P$ of $i$'s report. Each pick of $i$ maximizes the indicator value $\mathbf 1_P$ among available copies.

If $i$ precedes $j$ in the cycle, match every pick of $j$ to $i$'s pick in the same cycle. The latter occurs earlier, and $i$ has at least as many picks. Hence
$$ A_j(P)\le A_i(P). $$
If $i$ follows $j$, match every pick of $j$ after its first to $i$'s pick in the preceding cycle. The latter occurs earlier, and the required picks exist. Hence
$$ A_j(P)\le A_i(P)+1. $$
Comparing an agent with itself is automatic.

Aggregate this ranked allocation to masses $y_{t,K}$, where $K$ is a ranked bundle. Given conditional unranked distributions $\lambda_{\mathcal L}$ and $\lambda_{\mathcal H}$, define
$$ x_{t,K+V} = \begin{cases} y_{t,K}\lambda_{\mathcal L}(V),&|K|=q,\\ y_{t,K}\lambda_{\mathcal H}(V),&|K|=q+1. \end{cases} $$
Here $K$ and $V$ have disjoint coordinate sets.

This preserves type masses and ranked supplies. Its unranked supply is the required mixture with weights $1-r$ and $r$. Ranked-prefix inequalities are preserved. The final inequality of Section 1 holds within each level by the within-family condition, and between levels by $H\le L$ and $|L-H|\le2$.

For $r=0$, the same argument works with one unranked distribution whose support satisfies the within-family condition.

The expansion by $N$ proves existence only. The decision algorithm below never performs this expansion.

4. Structure of compatible integral families

The following elementary lemma supplies the needed classification.

4.1. Families with one-copy directed differences

Lemma. Let $\mathcal A$ be a finite nonempty family of nonnegative integral vectors satisfying
$$ \sum_g(B_g-A_g)_+\le1 \qquad (A,B\in\mathcal A). $$
Then $\mathcal A$ is contained in one of the forms
$$ \{C\}\cup\{C+e_g:g\in U\}, $$
or
$$ \{D\}\cup\{D-e_g:g\in U,\ D_g>0\}, $$
for a nonnegative integral center $C$ or $D$.

Proof. Let $c_g=\min_{A\in\mathcal A}A_g$. Each coordinate varies by at most one, so
$$ A=c+\mathbf 1_{S_A}. $$
The resulting sets satisfy
$$ |S_B\setminus S_A|\le1 $$
for every ordered pair. Their sizes consequently differ by at most one.

Let their maximum size be $h$. If $h=0$, the family is a singleton. Otherwise, add a new symbol $*$ to every set of size $h-1$, leaving the sets of size $h$ unchanged. All augmented sets have size $h$ and still have directed differences of size at most one. For sets $S,T$ of sizes $h-1,h$, this follows because
$$ |T\setminus S|-|S\setminus T|=1, \qquad |T\setminus S|\le1, $$
which forces $S\subseteq T$.

An equal-size family with these differences either contains a common $(h-1)$-element subset or lies in a common $(h+1)$-element set. Here is a proof. Choose distinct members
$$ X=I\cup\{a\}, \qquad Y=I\cup\{b\}, $$
where $|I|=h-1$ and $a\ne b$. If all members contain $I$, the first alternative holds. Otherwise, a member omitting $u\in I$ must be
$$ (I\setminus\{u\})\cup\{a,b\}, $$
because it differs from each of $X,Y$ by at most one element. All members omitting part of $I$ therefore lie in $J=I\cup\{a,b\}$. A member containing $I$ has form $I\cup\{z\}$; if $z\notin\{a,b\}$, its difference from the chosen member omitting $u$ contains both $u$ and $z$, a contradiction. Thus all members lie in $J$.

Deleting $*$ from the first alternative yields a common set with at most one element added. Deleting it from the second yields a common set with at most one element removed. Adding back $c$ gives the stated vector forms. $\square$

4.2. Classification of the two ranked-total levels

Lemma. Suppose nonempty finite families $\mathcal L,\mathcal H$ satisfy the within-family condition above and
$$ H\le L, \qquad |L-H|\le2 \qquad (L\in\mathcal L,\ H\in\mathcal H). $$
At least one of the following descriptions holds:

  • There is a nonnegative integral $D$ such that

$$ \mathcal L\subseteq\{D,D+e_g:g\in U\}, \qquad \mathcal H\subseteq\{D,D-e_g:g\in U,\ D_g>0\}. $$

  • The high family is a singleton $\mathcal H=\{C\}$. For some nonnegative integral $E$ with $|E|\le3$,

$$ \mathcal L\subseteq \{C+E\}\cup\{C+E-e_g:E_g>0\}, $$
and every used low vector satisfies $|L-C|\le2$.

  • The low family is a singleton $\mathcal L=\{D\}$. For some nonnegative integral $E\le D$ with $|E|\le3$,

$$ \mathcal H\subseteq \{D-E\}\cup\{D-E+e_g:E_g>0\}, $$
and every used high vector satisfies $|D-H|\le2$.

Proof. Define coordinatewise extrema
$$ C_0=\min_{L\in\mathcal L}L, \qquad D_0=\max_{H\in\mathcal H}H. $$
The cross inequalities imply $D_0\le C_0$. Put
$$ a=\max_{L\in\mathcal L}|L-C_0|, \quad b=\max_{H\in\mathcal H}|D_0-H|, \quad d=|C_0-D_0|. $$
For every cross pair,
$$ |L-H|=|L-C_0|+d+|D_0-H|. $$
Choosing members attaining $a$ and $b$ gives
$$ a+b+d\le2. $$

First suppose $a,b\le1$. Choose an integer $\ell$ satisfying
$$ \max\{0,a+d-1\}\le\ell\le\min\{d,1-b\}. $$
This interval is nonempty: $a\le1$ gives $a+d-1\le d$, and $a+b+d\le2$ gives $a+d-1\le1-b$; also $0\le d$ and $0\le1-b$.

There is an integral vector $T$ between $D_0$ and $C_0$ with $|T-D_0|=\ell$: distribute $\ell$ units among the coordinate capacities $C_0-D_0$. Then
$$ |L-T|\le a+d-\ell\le1, \qquad |T-H|\le b+\ell\le1. $$
Since $H\le T\le L$, these differences are zero or unit vectors. This proves description 1.

If $a=2$, the inequality $a+b+d\le2$ forces $b=d=0$. Hence $\mathcal H=\{C_0\}$. By the preceding lemma, $\mathcal L$ lies in a lower or upper family.

A family lying in $\{Q,Q+e_g\}$ has maximum distance from its coordinatewise minimum at most one. For a singleton this distance is zero; otherwise the minimum is $Q$, since two distinct added coordinates, or the center itself, occur. Thus the lower alternative is impossible when $a=2$.

Consequently, $\mathcal L\subseteq\{Q,Q-e_g\}$. Put $E=Q-C_0\ge0$. Choose $L$ attaining $|L-C_0|=2$. Since $Q-L$ is zero or a unit vector,
$$ |E|=|Q-L|+|L-C_0|\le3. $$
Only coordinates with $E_g>0$ can be deleted while retaining $L\ge C_0$. This proves description 2.

If $b=2$, then $a=d=0$ and $\mathcal L=\{D_0\}$. The preceding lemma applies to $\mathcal H$. A family in an upper form has maximum distance from its coordinatewise maximum at most one, so that alternative is impossible. Thus
$$ \mathcal H\subseteq\{Q,Q+e_g\} $$
for nonnegative integral $Q$. Set $E=D_0-Q\ge0$. Choosing $H$ attaining $|D_0-H|=2$ gives
$$ |E|=|D_0-H|+|H-Q|\le3. $$
Only coordinates with $E_g>0$ can be added while retaining $H\le D_0$. This proves description 3.

These cases exhaust the nonnegative integers satisfying $a+b+d\le2$. $\square$

5. Exact polynomial-time test for a common ranked set

We now turn the structural classification into an algorithm.

5.1. Integral total ranked supply

Suppose $r=0$. Define
$$ F_U=\sum_{g\in U}\bigl(z_g-\lfloor z_g\rfloor\bigr), \qquad H_U=\sum_{g\in U}\bigl(\lceil z_g\rceil-z_g\bigr). $$

Claim. Feasibility is equivalent to
$$ F_U\le1 \quad\text{or}\quad H_U\le1. $$

Proof. All ranked totals equal $q$, so the unranked support satisfies the lemma in Section 4.1.

In its lower form, its expectation satisfies
$$ z_g\ge C_g, \qquad \sum_g(z_g-C_g)\le1. $$
Since integral $C_g\le z_g$ implies $C_g\le\lfloor z_g\rfloor$, this gives $F_U\le1$. The upper form similarly gives $H_U\le1$.

Conversely, if $F_U\le1$, use unranked bundle $\lfloor z\rfloor+e_g$ with probability $z_g-\lfloor z_g\rfloor$, and $\lfloor z\rfloor$ with the remaining probability. Its expectation is $z$, and its support has the required directed differences.

If $H_U\le1$, use $\lceil z\rceil-e_g$ with probability $\lceil z_g\rceil-z_g$, and $\lceil z\rceil$ with the remaining probability. A positively used deletion has $\lceil z_g\rceil\ge1$, so its bundle is nonnegative. Section 3.2 supplies the ranked coordinates.

If $U=\varnothing$, both sums are zero. The unranked distribution consists solely of the empty vector, and the ranked round-robin construction supplies the entire allocation. This explicitly handles the boundary omitted in the notes. $\square$

Notice that this argument requires only $S_R$ to be integral. It does not require the individual ranked supplies to be integral.

5.2. Nonintegral total ranked supply

Suppose $0<r<1$, and put $p=1-r$.

Feasibility is equivalent to passing at least one of the following three tests. All integral vectors below are indexed by $U$.

Test I. There exists a nonnegative integral vector $D$ such that
$$ \sum_g(z_g-D_g)_+\le p, \qquad \sum_g(D_g-z_g)_+\le r. $$

Test II. There exist nonnegative integral vectors $C,E$, with $e=|E|\le3$, such that
$$ h_g=E_g-\frac{z_g-C_g}{p} $$
satisfies
$$ h_g\ge0, \qquad h_g=0\ \text{when }E_g=0, \qquad \sum_g h_g\le1, $$
and, if $e=3$,
$$ \sum_g h_g=1. $$

Test III. There exist nonnegative integral vectors $D,E$, with $E\le D$ and $e=|E|\le3$, such that
$$ a_g=E_g-\frac{D_g-z_g}{r} $$
satisfies
$$ a_g\ge0, \qquad a_g=0\ \text{when }E_g=0, \qquad \sum_g a_g\le1, $$
and, if $e=3$,
$$ \sum_g a_g=1. $$

Proof of equivalence.

In description 1 of Section 4.2, write the conditional low and high expectations as
$$ D+\alpha, \qquad D-\beta, $$
where $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ and $|\alpha|,|\beta|\le1$. Thus
$$ z=D+p\alpha-r\beta. $$
Coordinatewise,
$$ (z_g-D_g)_+\le p\alpha_g, \qquad (D_g-z_g)_+\le r\beta_g, $$
which proves necessity of Test I.

Conversely, when Test I holds, set
$$ \alpha_g=\frac{(z_g-D_g)_+}{p}, \qquad \beta_g=\frac{(D_g-z_g)_+}{r}. $$
Use conditional low probabilities $\alpha_g$ on $D+e_g$, and conditional high probabilities $\beta_g$ on $D-e_g$, putting each remaining probability on $D$. The probability sums are at most one. A positive $\beta_g$ implies $D_g>z_g>0$, so the deletion is valid. These distributions have the required expectation and compatibility.

In description 2, the high vector is $C$. Let $h_g$ be the conditional probability of low vector $C+E-e_g$. The remaining probability is on $C+E$. Deletions can occur only where $E_g>0$. If $|E|=3$, the center itself is forbidden by $|L-C|\le2$, so the deletion probabilities sum to one. The expectation is
$$ z=C+p(E-h), $$
which is precisely Test II.

Conversely, Test II defines these probabilities. Since $h_g\le1$ and positive $h_g$ requires integral $E_g\ge1$, every deletion remains at least $C$. If $e\le2$, every used low vector differs from $C$ by at most two copies. If $e=3$, only deletion vectors are used, and each differs by exactly two copies. Within the low family, directed positive differences are at most one. Thus all required conditions hold.

In description 3, use conditional probabilities $a_g$ on high vectors $D-E+e_g$ and the remaining probability on $D-E$. The center is forbidden when $|E|=3$. Its mixture with the constant low vector $D$ has expectation
$$ z=D-r(E-a). $$
This gives Test III. Conversely, its conditions define valid nonnegative high bundles, all at most $D$, whose distance below $D$ is at most two. Within-family directed differences are at most one.

The structural lemma proves necessity of the union of the tests. The constructions and Section 3.2 prove sufficiency. $\square$

5.3. Implementing the tests in polynomial time

Let $u=|U|$.

For Test I, each coordinate must satisfy
$$ z_g-p\le D_g\le z_g+r. $$
Write $f_g=z_g-\lfloor z_g\rfloor$. Since $p+r=1$:

  • If $f_g<p$, the sole possible integer is $\lfloor z_g\rfloor$.
  • If $f_g>p$, it is $\lceil z_g\rceil$.
  • If $f_g=p$, both are possible.

At an ambiguous coordinate, choosing the floor contributes exactly $p$ to the first budget, while choosing the ceiling contributes exactly $r$ to the second. Therefore at most one ambiguous coordinate can be rounded down and at most one can be rounded up. With more than two ambiguous coordinates, Test I fails. Otherwise there are at most four candidate vectors to check.

For Test II, enumerate all nonnegative integral $E$ with $|E|\le3$. Their number is
$$ \binom{u+3}{3}. $$
Let $J=\{g:E_g>0\}$, so $|J|\le3$.

Outside $J$, the conditions force $C_g=z_g$, so these supplies must be integral. Inside $J$, the conditions imply
$$ 0\le z_g-C_g=p(E_g-h_g)\le3. $$
Here $E_g-h_g\ge0$ because $E_g\ge1$ and $h_g\le1$. Thus enumerate
$$ C_g\in[z_g-3,z_g]\cap\mathbb Z_{\ge0}. $$
There are at most four choices per coordinate and at most $4^3$ candidate vectors for each $E$. Check the displayed Test II conditions exactly.

For Test III, outside $J$ one must have $D_g=z_g$. Inside $J$,
$$ 0\le D_g-z_g=r(E_g-a_g)\le3. $$
Thus enumerate
$$ D_g\in[z_g,z_g+3]\cap\mathbb Z_{\ge0}, $$
again with at most $4^3$ candidates, checking also $D\ge E$.

The tests use $O((u+1)^4)$ rational arithmetic operations. Candidate coordinates have polynomial bit length. Computing $r$, dividing by $r$ or $1-r$, and checking all rational inequalities also require polynomial bit length. The algorithm therefore runs in polynomial time in the binary input size.

This completes the promised polynomial-time result for a common ranked set.

6. Examples showing why all three tests matter

Consider one type reporting only $a$, with
$$ \rho_a=\frac12, \qquad \rho_b=\frac14, \qquad \rho_c=\rho_d=\frac38. $$
Here $r=p=1/2$. Test I fails: every unranked supply is below $p$, so its sole candidate is $D=0$, whose positive-deviation sum is $1>p$.

Nevertheless, Test II succeeds with
$$ C=0, \qquad E=(1,1,1), \qquad h=\left(\frac12,\frac14,\frac14\right). $$
An explicit allocation is
