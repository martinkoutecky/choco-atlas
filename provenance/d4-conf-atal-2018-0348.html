Theorem 5.1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix \(J=\{1,2,3\}\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\in(0,1]\), type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and complete monotonically increasing rankings \(\succ_t\) over \(2^J\). For almost every budget vector \(b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{|T|}\), can one compute positive prices \(p_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{t,S}\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}=q_j\), and \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(S\) is a most-preferred bundle among those satisfying \(\sum_{j\in S}p_j\leq b_t\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(J=\{1,2,3\}\), with \(m=3\), and binary-rational supplies \(q_j\in(0,1]\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Each type has an explicitly listed complete, transitive, weakly monotonically increasing preference relation \(\succeq_t\) over \(2^J\), including the empty bundle; indifferences are allowed.
Every individual of type \(t\) has the same per-capita budget \(b_t>0\). Money has no intrinsic value. Individuals consume subsets of \(J\), with at most one unit of each good.
An equilibrium consists of positive prices \(p_j\) and masses \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq J}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}=q_j\) for every \(j\in J\).
Writing \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\), require \(x_{t,S}>0\) only if \(p(S)\leq b_t\) and \(S\succeq_t R\) for every \(R\subseteq J\) with \(p(R)\leq b_t\). Mass may split among indifferent optimal bundles; no tie-breaking rule is imposed.
For computation, budgets are positive binary rationals and \(L\) is the total input bit length; seek exact prices and assignment masses.

For every fixed society and supply vector, does equilibrium exist for Lebesgue-almost every \(b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\tau}\), or can nonexistence occupy a positive-measure set?
For rational budgets, can equilibrium existence be decided and an equilibrium computed whenever one exists in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is deciding existence NP-hard?

Discrete: with three indivisible goods and any number of agents, equilibrium exists for almost all positive income vectors under monotonically increasing preferences, with an explicit construction ([Segal-Halevi](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.04212v6)).
Continuous nearby result: equilibrium existence is known for continuum economies with indivisible goods and quasilinear utility ([Azevedo, Weyl and White](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/1btvv-6pd54)); that model gives money intrinsic utility. No result settling the exact fixed-budget model above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted complete monotonically increasing rankings as complete, transitive weak orders with weak monotonicity, matching the discrete preference domain; indifferences are allowed without imposed tie-breaking.
  • Specified that budgets are per capita and identical within each type, and that individuals consume subsets rather than fractional goods or lotteries evaluated in expectation.
  • Used real budget vectors for Lebesgue-almost-everywhere existence and binary-rational budgets for exact computation, with total input bit length L.
  • Made the computational question total by allowing an existence decision when no equilibrium exists, while retaining the almost-all-budgets existence question.

Results

Both questions can be settled:

  • Nonexistence can occupy an open, positive-measure set of budgets, even for a society that admits equilibria for other budgets.
  • For fixed \(m=3\), equilibrium existence and exact equilibrium construction are polynomial-time solvable in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

The proofs below allow arbitrary splitting among indifferent optimal bundles.

1. Positive-measure nonexistence

A simple obstruction is that a single type with a strict ranking has a unique optimal bundle, so its aggregate consumption cannot equal fractional supplies. The following example goes further: the same society has equilibrium for an open set of budgets and nonexistence for another open set.

Society and preferences

Take two types, denoted \(A\) and \(B\), with masses and supplies

\(\mu_A=\frac34,\qquad \mu_B=\frac14,\qquad (q_1,q_2,q_3)=\left(\frac12,\frac14,\frac14\right).\)

Type \(A\) ranks bundles solely by cardinality:

\(S\succeq_A R\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |S|\geq |R|.\)

Type \(B\) has the complete ranking

\(\{1,2,3\}\succ_B\{1,2\}\succ_B\{1,3\}\sim_B\{2,3\}\succ_B\{1\}\sim_B\{2\}\sim_B\{3\}\succ_B\varnothing.\)

These are distinct preference relations. Both strictly prefer every bundle to each proper subset, and both strictly prefer every larger-cardinality bundle to every smaller-cardinality bundle. All masses and supplies are positive dyadic rationals.

Lemma: every equilibrium assigns only singletons

Fix positive prices and budgets. For each type \(t\in\{A,B\}\), define

\(k_t=\max\{|S|:S\subseteq J,\ p(S)\leq b_t\}.\)

The empty bundle is affordable, so this maximum exists and belongs to \(\{0,1,2,3\}\).

Every optimal affordable bundle for type \(t\) has cardinality \(k_t\): an affordable bundle of smaller cardinality is strictly worse than an affordable bundle attaining \(k_t\). Consequently, equilibrium assignments would satisfy

\(\sum_{S\subseteq J}|S|x_{t,S}=\mu_t k_t.\)

Summing market-clearing equations over the goods gives

\[ \text{ } \]

\(\frac34k_A+\frac14k_B=\sum_{j=1}^3q_j=1,\)

or equivalently \(3k_A+k_B=4\).

For integers \(k_A,k_B\in\{0,1,2,3\}\), the unique solution is \(k_A=k_B=1\): \(k_A=0\) would require \(k_B=4\), while \(k_A\geq2\) would require \(k_B<0\).

Thus every individual must receive a singleton in any equilibrium.

Proposition: no equilibrium exists when \(b_B\geq2b_A\)

Assume an equilibrium exists. By the lemma, type \(A\)'s entire mass \(\frac34\) receives singletons.

Type \(A\) must receive positive masses of at least two distinct singleton goods. Otherwise, its mass would all consume one good \(j\), implying

\(q_j\geq\frac34,\)

contrary to \(\max_j q_j=\frac12\).

Choose distinct goods \(i,j\) whose singletons are assigned positive mass to type \(A\). Affordability implies

\(p_i\leq b_A,\qquad p_j\leq b_A.\)

Hence, if \(b_B\geq2b_A\),

\(p(\{i,j\})=p_i+p_j\leq2b_A\leq b_B.\)

Type \(B\) can therefore afford a pair, so \(k_B\geq2\). This contradicts the lemma. No equilibrium exists.

In particular, the open set

\(U=\{(b_A,b_B)\in\mathbb R_{>0}^2:b_B>2b_A\}\)

consists entirely of nonexistence instances. It has positive Lebesgue measure; for example, it contains the rectangle \((1,\frac54)\times(3,4)\).

Proposition: the same society has equilibrium when budgets are close

Suppose

\(\max\{b_A,b_B\}<2\min\{b_A,b_B\}.\)

Set \(c=\min\{b_A,b_B\}\) and \(p_1=p_2=p_3=c\). Both types can afford every singleton, and neither can afford any pair. For both types, all singletons are therefore indifferent optimal bundles.

Assign

\(x_{t,\{j\}}=\mu_tq_j\)

for each type and good, and assign zero mass to every other bundle. Since \(\sum_jq_j=1\), each type receives its prescribed mass. Since \(\mu_A+\mu_B=1\), every good clears:

\(\sum_t x_{t,\{j\}}=(\mu_A+\mu_B)q_j=q_j.\)

This is an equilibrium. Thus nonexistence in the example is not an obstruction that persists for every budget vector.

2. Exact polynomial-time computation for three goods

Now consider any input satisfying the task's hypotheses, with rational budgets.

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and optimization for rational linear programs are solvable in polynomial time in their encoding length, with exact rational feasible solutions and finite optima returned with polynomial encoding length. The programs below have explicitly polynomial encoding size.

Step 1: bound equilibrium prices

Let \(B=\max_t b_t\).

In any equilibrium, each good \(j\) has positive supply. Its clearing equation therefore implies that some positively assigned bundle \(S\) contains \(j\). Positivity of prices and affordability yield

\(p_j\leq p(S)\leq b_t\leq B.\)

Thus it suffices to search prices satisfying \(0<p_j\leq B\).

Step 2: enumerate affordability regions

Sort the distinct budget values as

\(0<v_1<\cdots<v_r=B,\qquad r\leq\tau.\)

Partition the positive real line into the following \(2r+1\) regions:

\((0,v_1),\ \{v_1\},\ (v_1,v_2),\ \{v_2\},\ \ldots,\ \{v_r\},\ (v_r,\infty).\)

For each of the seven nonempty bundles \(S\subseteq J\), choose a region for its price \(p(S)\). There are at most

\((2\tau+1)^7\)

such choices.

Each choice determines whether \(p(S)\leq b_t\), for every type and bundle. Indeed, no open region contains a budget value, while singleton regions explicitly record equality. The empty bundle is always affordable.

Step 3: test each price region exactly

For a chosen tuple of regions, introduce variables \(p_1,p_2,p_3,\delta\), and maximize \(\delta\) subject to

\(0\leq\delta\leq1,\qquad \delta\leq p_j\leq B\quad(j\in J).\)

Add the following constraints for each nonempty bundle \(S\):

  • If its region is the point \(\{a\}\), impose \(p(S)=a\).
  • If its region is an open interval \((a,c)\), impose \(p(S)\geq a+\delta\) and \(p(S)\leq c-\delta\).
  • If its region is \((a,\infty)\), impose \(p(S)\geq a+\delta\).

Reject the tuple if this linear program is infeasible or its optimum is zero.

This test is exact. A feasible solution with \(\delta>0\) gives positive prices satisfying every required strict inequality. Conversely, prices in the chosen regions with \(0<p_j\leq B\) have finitely many positive strict-inequality slacks. Choosing \(\delta>0\) no larger than these slacks, the prices, and \(1\), gives a feasible solution with positive objective.

The feasible set is compact because all four variables are bounded. Therefore, whenever feasible, its maximum is attained. Exact rational optimization distinguishes a positive optimum from zero.

Step 4: compute the demand sets and test assignments

For a surviving tuple, let \(\mathcal A_t\) be type \(t\)'s affordable bundles, and define its demand set by

\(\mathcal D_t=\{S\in\mathcal A_t:S\succeq_t R\text{ for every }R\in\mathcal A_t\}.\)

These sets are constant throughout the chosen price region. They can be computed directly from the explicitly listed preferences, using comparisons among only eight bundles.

Solve the rational linear feasibility problem

\(x_{t,S}\geq0\quad(S\in\mathcal D_t),\)

\(\sum_{S\in\mathcal D_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\)

\(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\substack{S\in\mathcal D_t\\j\in S}}x_{t,S}=q_j\quad(j\in J).\)

Set all other assignment variables to zero. This program has at most \(8\tau\) variables and \(\tau+3\) equality constraints.

If feasible, return its assignment together with the prices found in Step 3. If every tuple is rejected or has an infeasible assignment program, report nonexistence.

Correctness

Soundness. Returned prices are positive. The assignment program enforces type masses and market clearing. Every positively assigned bundle belongs to its type's demand set, so it is affordable and weakly preferred to every affordable alternative. Hence the returned objects form an equilibrium.

Completeness. Suppose an equilibrium exists. Its prices satisfy \(p_j\leq B\) by Step 1, and every nonempty bundle price belongs to exactly one enumerated region. That tuple passes Step 3. Its demand sets coincide with the equilibrium's demand sets, so the equilibrium assignment witnesses feasibility in Step 4. The algorithm therefore finds an equilibrium whenever one exists.

Running time and exact output

There are at most \((2\tau+1)^7\) tuples. For each tuple, the price program has four variables and a constant number of constraints; the assignment program has at most \(8\tau\) variables and polynomial encoding size. Sorting, preference comparisons, and exact rational linear programming all take polynomial time.

The total running time is therefore

\((2\tau+1)^7\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L),\)

which is polynomial for the stipulated \(m=3\). Both prices and assignment masses can be returned as exact rationals of polynomial bit length. No polynomial-time claim for unrestricted \(m\) is needed or asserted.

3. Relation to the cited results

The counterexample concerns population masses and per-person bundle constraints. The finite-agent, three-item existence result in [Segal-Halevi](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.04212v6) consequently does not establish existence for this model. Likewise, [Azevedo, Weyl and White](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/1btvv-6pd54) establish existence under quasilinear utility, a different demand model.
