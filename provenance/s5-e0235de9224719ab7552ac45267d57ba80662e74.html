The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over normalized common-slope single-peaked valuation types, and a per-capita cake endowment whose total supply scales with population, can one compute a feasible measurable assignment of integral bundles to residents—allowing each type’s mass to split across bundle patterns—such that the type-mass versions of the mechanisms in Proposition 3 retain their stated Pareto-optimality, envy-freeness, and dominance guarantees?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\ge2\) distinct types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Type \(t\) has density \(v_t(x)=\max\{0,h_t-k|x-p_t|\}\) on \([0,1]\), with rational \(h_t>0\), distinct rational peaks \(p_t\in[0,1]\), common rational \(k>0\), and \(\int_0^1v_t(x)\,dx=1\). Positive supports cover the cake almost everywhere; on some interval of positive length, at least one type has zero density and another has positive density.
The input includes a binary-rational per-capita endowment \(0<b<1\): a population of mass \(M\) has supply \(Mb\,dx\). Normalize population mass to \(1\). Let \(m\) be the number of cells induced by all peaks, positive-support endpoints, and cake endpoints, and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An integral bundle is a finite union of intervals in \([0,1]\). An allocation specifies, for each type, finitely many bundles \(B_{tj}\) and conditional masses \(q_{tj}\ge0\), with \(\sum_jq_{tj}=1\) and \(\sum_{t,j}\mu_tq_{tj}\mathbf1_{B_{tj}}(x)=b\) almost everywhere. Result

All three counterparts can be computed in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\). In fact, the constructions below output rational endpoints and rational conditional masses.

I use “counterpart” in the guarantee-based sense specified in the question. In particular, the leftmost-leaves counterpart need only be Pareto optimal and Pareto dominate the benchmark; it need not reproduce a prescribed execution order.

The main difficulty is obtaining exact Pareto optimality together with the utility floors \(u_t\ge b\). The proof handles this by finding sufficiently accurate *rational welfare weights*, then computing an exact allocation maximizing welfare for those weights.

1. Inclusion profiles and finite-bundle implementation

For an allocation, define its type inclusion profiles by

\(\displaystyle f_t(x)=\sum_j q_{tj}\mathbf 1_{B_{tj}}(x).\)

They satisfy

\(\displaystyle 0\le f_t(x)\le1,\qquad \sum_t\mu_t f_t(x)=b\quad\text{almost everywhere}.\)

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle U_t(A_s)=\int_0^1v_t(x)f_s(x)\,dx,\qquad u_t=\int_0^1v_t(x)f_t(x)\,dx.\)

For intermediate existence arguments, allow arbitrary measurable profiles satisfying these constraints. Let \(\mathcal U\subseteq[0,1]^\tau\) be their attainable utility vectors.

This set is convex and compact. Indeed, the feasible profiles form a weak-star closed subset of the product of the unit balls of \(L^\infty([0,1])\). The bounds and supply equality are weak-star closed, as can be checked against \(L^1\) test functions. Banach–Alaoglu gives compactness, and each utility is weak-star continuous because \(v_t\in L^1\).

All profiles actually output below are rational and piecewise constant. Such profiles have a polynomial-size implementation by integral bundles:

Implementation lemma. Suppose the profiles are constant on a common partition into \(n\) rational intervals. For each type, list its distinct inclusion values, adding both \(0\) and \(1\):

\(\displaystyle 0=r_0<r_1<\cdots<r_d=1.\)

Define

\(\displaystyle q_{tj}=r_j-r_{j-1},\qquad B_{tj}=\{x:f_t(x)\ge r_j\}.\)

Each bundle is a finite union of partition intervals, and

\(\displaystyle \sum_jq_{tj}=1,\qquad \sum_jq_{tj}\mathbf1_{B_{tj}}(x)=f_t(x)\)

almost everywhere. The latter equality follows by telescoping on an interval where \(f_t\) has value \(r_\ell\).

There are at most \(n+1\) bundles per type, with at most \(n\) interval components per bundle. All endpoints and masses are rational. Empty bundles are permitted as empty finite unions.

Thus it suffices to construct polynomial-size rational piecewise constant profiles. Pareto optimality proved against the measurable relaxation implies Pareto optimality against the allocations in the question.

2. An exact weighted-welfare oracle

For positive weights \(w=(w_t)\), write

\(\displaystyle H(w)=\max_{u\in\mathcal U}\sum_t w_tu_t.\)

At position \(x\), put \(a_t(x)=\mu_tf_t(x)\). The pointwise optimization problem is

\(\displaystyle \max\sum_t\frac{w_tv_t(x)}{\mu_t}a_t(x),\qquad 0\le a_t(x)\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_ta_t(x)=b.\)

This is fractional knapsack. Order types by decreasing score \(w_tv_t(x)/\mu_t\), using a fixed rule for ties. Fill capacities \(\mu_t\) in that order until the total reaches \(b\).

To verify optimality, suppose a lower-score type receives positive mass while a higher-score type has unused capacity. Transferring mass from the former to the latter weakly increases the objective, and increases it strictly when the scores differ. Repeating these transfers gives the stated greedy solution.

On each original cell, every density is affine. For rational \(w\), refine the cell at every isolated intersection of two scores. There are at most \(\binom{\tau}{2}\) such intersections per cell. Consequently, the greedy profiles are rational and constant on at most

\(\displaystyle N=m\left(1+\binom{\tau}{2}\right)\)

intervals. Their endpoints, inclusion values, utilities, and \(H(w)\) can all be computed exactly in polynomial time. Their encoding lengths are polynomial in the input length and the encoding length of \(w\).

The construction maximizes the integrand at every position, so it attains \(H(w)\) over the entire measurable relaxation.

Every allocation maximizing a strictly positive weighted sum is Pareto optimal: a Pareto improvement would strictly increase that sum.

The utilitarian counterpart

Set \(w_t=\mu_t\). The pointwise scores become \(v_t(x)\), and the oracle maximizes

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\mu_tu_t.\)

All these weights are positive. The resulting allocation is therefore utilitarian optimal and Pareto optimal, with a polynomial-size rational bundle implementation.

3. The envy-free counterpart

On an original cell \(I\), let

\(\displaystyle S_I=\{t:v_t>0\text{ on the interior of }I\},\qquad \rho_I=\sum_{t\in S_I}\mu_t.\)

Coverage almost everywhere implies \(S_I\ne\varnothing\).

Assign each positive type the inclusion value

\(\displaystyle \alpha_I=\min\{1,b/\rho_I\}.\)

Assign each zero-valued type the inclusion value

\(\displaystyle \beta_I=\begin{cases}0,&b\le\rho_I,\\[2pt](b-\rho_I)/(1-\rho_I),&b>\rho_I.\end{cases}\)

The second case has \(\rho_I<1\). When \(\rho_I=1\), no zero-valued type needs an assignment.

These values lie in \([0,1]\), and direct substitution gives

\(\displaystyle \rho_I\alpha_I+(1-\rho_I)\beta_I=b.\)

Thus supply is respected exactly.

Whenever \(v_t(x)>0\), type \(t\) receives \(\alpha_I\), which is at least every other type’s inclusion value on that cell. Hence, for every pair \(t,s\),

\(\displaystyle v_t(x)f_t(x)\ge v_t(x)f_s(x)\)

almost everywhere. Integrating proves envy-freeness.

Also, \(\alpha_I\ge b\), so normalization gives

\(\displaystyle u_t\ge b\int_0^1v_t(x)\,dx=b.\)

By the mixed-support hypothesis, some cell has \(0<\rho_I<1\). On that cell, \(\alpha_I>b\); every type positive there obtains a strictly positive utility gain over inclusion \(b\). Therefore at least one type satisfies \(u_t>b\).

This allocation is envy-free and Pareto dominates the benchmark. It uses only rational arithmetic on the original cells.

4. A polynomially encoded strict utility margin

The remaining construction needs a strictly feasible utility floor, with a margin whose encoding length is polynomial.

Bisect every original cell, obtaining intervals \(J_1,\ldots,J_{2m}\), and compute

\(\displaystyle V_{tr}=\int_{J_r}v_t(x)\,dx.\)

These are rational numbers of polynomial encoding length. Consider the rational linear program

\(\displaystyle \text{maximize }\delta,\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle 0\le z_{tr}\le1,\qquad \sum_t\mu_tz_{tr}=b\quad\text{for every }r,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_rV_{tr}z_{tr}\ge b+\delta\quad\text{for every }t,\qquad 0\le\delta\le1.\)

The constant profiles \(z_{tr}=b\) show feasibility with \(\delta=0\).

Lemma. The optimal value \(\delta\) is strictly positive.

Proof. Let \(\mathcal U_0\) be the utility polytope of these piecewise constant profiles. Suppose it contains no vector strictly exceeding \(b\mathbf1\) in every coordinate. Separating the convex set \(\mathcal U_0-b\mathbf1\) from the open positive orthant yields a nonzero vector \(c\ge0\) such that

\(\displaystyle c\cdot u\le c\cdot(b\mathbf1)\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathcal U_0.\)

Thus the constant profiles maximize this linear objective.

Fix a subcell \(J_r\) and types \(i,j\). At the constant profiles, the perturbation

\(\displaystyle z_{ir}=b+\varepsilon/\mu_i,\qquad z_{jr}=b-\varepsilon/\mu_j\)

is feasible for sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\) of either sign, because \(0<b<1\). Optimality therefore implies

\(\displaystyle \frac{c_iV_{ir}}{\mu_i}=\frac{c_jV_{jr}}{\mu_j}.\)

If any \(c_i=0\), these equalities, summed over subcells, give \(c_j=0\) for every \(j\), since \(\sum_rV_{jr}=1\). This contradicts \(c\ne0\). Hence every \(c_t>0\).

On each original cell, the affine function

\(\displaystyle \frac{c_i}{\mu_i}v_i(x)-\frac{c_j}{\mu_j}v_j(x)\)

has integral zero on both halves. The average of an affine function on an interval is its value at the midpoint. It therefore vanishes at two distinct points and is identically zero.

It follows that \(c_iv_i/\mu_i=c_jv_j/\mu_j\) almost everywhere on the cake. Integration gives \(c_i/\mu_i=c_j/\mu_j\), and hence \(v_i=v_j\) almost everywhere. Continuity gives equality everywhere. This is impossible: each tent has its unique maximum at its peak, and the peaks are distinct. The contradiction proves the lemma. \(\square\)

Use polynomial-time rational linear programming to compute this positive optimum and a corresponding feasible profile. The LP has polynomially many variables and constraints with polynomially encoded coefficients. Its optimal rational value consequently has polynomial encoding length; this is the standard rational LP guarantee. See, for example, [Theorem 19.5 in these optimization notes](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~15850/notes/lec21.pdf).

We now have a rational \(\delta>0\) and a vector \(u^0\in\mathcal U\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle u_t^0\ge b+\delta\quad\text{for every }t.\)

In particular, \(\delta\le1-b\).

5. Where common slopes are used

We next prove uniqueness and quantitative continuity of the utility vector returned by positive-weight optimization.

5.1 Distinct types cannot be positively proportional on a cell

For peaks \(p<q\), normalization implies

\(\displaystyle |h_p-h_q|<k(q-p).\)

Indeed, if \(h_q\ge h_p+k(q-p)\), the triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle h_q-k|x-q|\ge h_p-k|x-p|\)

for every \(x\). After truncation at zero, \(v_q\ge v_p\) everywhere. The inequality is strict at \(q\), and hence on an interval of positive length by continuity. This contradicts equal integrals. The reversed height inequality is excluded symmetrically.

Consequently, the intercepts \(h_t-kp_t\) are strictly decreasing with peak position, and \(h_t+kp_t\) are strictly increasing.

On any original cell where two densities are positive, their slopes belong to \(\{k,-k\}\). If the slopes agree, positive proportionality would require proportionality factor \(1\), and then equal intercepts, which the preceding inequalities exclude. If the slopes have opposite signs, positive proportionality is impossible.

Thus two positive densities on a cell are never positively proportional.

5.2 Positive-weight optimal utilities are unique

For positive \(w\), two positive scores can tie only at isolated points: otherwise their densities would be positively proportional on a cell. Outside finitely many such points, all positive scores are distinct.

At such a position, the pointwise knapsack solution uniquely determines every positive-valued type’s inclusion. Ties among zero-valued types can change assignments only where their own utility contribution is zero.

Any global weighted maximizer must be pointwise optimal almost everywhere. To see this, subtract its weighted integrand from the greedy integrand. The difference is nonnegative and has integral zero, so it vanishes almost everywhere.

Therefore every maximizer has the same own-utility vector. Denote it by \(u(w)\).

5.3 A quantitative continuity bound

Fix a rational \(W>1\), and restrict weights to

\(\displaystyle Q=[1,W]^\tau.\)

Write the density on cell \(I_c\) as

\(\displaystyle v_t(x)=A_{tc}x+B_{tc}.\)

For every pair positive on that cell, define

\(\displaystyle D_{tsc}=|A_{tc}B_{sc}-A_{sc}B_{tc}|.\)

The preceding nonproportionality result proves \(D_{tsc}>0\). Let \(d_0\) be the minimum of these positive numbers, taking \(d_0=1\) if there are no such pairs. Also put

\(\displaystyle H_0=\max_t h_t,\qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \Lambda=\max\left\{1,\frac{m\binom{\tau}{2}H_0^3(1+W)}{d_0\mu_{\min}}\right\}.\)

These quantities are rational.

Continuity lemma. For \(w,w'\in Q\),

\(\displaystyle \|u(w)-u(w')\|_\infty\le\Lambda\|w-w'\|_\infty.\)

Proof. On a cell where \(v_t,v_s>0\), the ratio \(R=v_t/v_s\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle |R'(x)|=\frac{D_{tsc}}{v_s(x)^2}\ge\frac{d_0}{H_0^2}.\)

The score order between these types is determined by whether

\(\displaystyle R(x)\ge\theta_{ts}(w),\qquad \theta_{ts}(w)=\frac{w_s\mu_t}{w_t\mu_s}.\)

Since all weight coordinates lie in \([1,W]\),

\(\displaystyle |\theta_{ts}(w)-\theta_{ts}(w')|\le\frac{1+W}{\mu_{\min}}\|w-w'\|_\infty.\)

The ratio is strictly monotone. Its derivative bound therefore implies that the length on which this pair changes order is at most

\(\displaystyle \frac{H_0^2(1+W)}{d_0\mu_{\min}}\|w-w'\|_\infty.\)

Orders between positive and zero-valued types cannot change. With a fixed rule for zero-score ties, the greedy profiles can differ only where some positive pair changes order.

Summing the preceding length bound over cells and pairs bounds the measure on which the profiles differ. Each inclusion changes by at most \(1\), and each density is at most \(H_0\). Multiplication by \(H_0\) gives the claimed inequality. \(\square\)

The encoding lengths of \(d_0,H_0,\mu_{\min}\), and \(\Lambda\) are polynomial in the input length and the encoding length of \(W\). Their numerical values may be very large or small; their logarithms have polynomial size.

6. Constructing the Pareto-optimal counterpart

6.1 An auxiliary objective with a strict margin

Using the \(\delta\) from Section 4, set

\(\displaystyle r=b+\delta/2,\qquad C=64\tau/\delta^2,\qquad W=1+2C.\)

Here \(0<r<1\). Define, for \(u\in\mathcal U\),

\(\displaystyle \Phi(u)=\sum_t\left[u_t-C(r-u_t)_+^2\right],\qquad y_+=\max\{y,0\}.\)

This function is concave and continuously differentiable. Compactness gives a maximizer \(u^*\).

The feasible vector \(u^0\) has no penalty, so \(\Phi(u^0)\ge0\). If some coordinate of \(u^*\) were at most \(b+\delta/4\), its penalty would be at least \(C\delta^2/16\). Since every utility is at most \(1\),

\(\displaystyle \Phi(u^*)\le\tau-C\delta^2/16=-3\tau<0,\)

a contradiction. Therefore

\(\displaystyle u_t^*>b+\delta/4\quad\text{for every }t.\)

Its gradient coordinates are

\(\displaystyle w_t^*=1+2C(r-u_t^*)_+.\)

They belong to \([1,W]\). For any \(u\in\mathcal U\), the directional derivative of \(\Phi\) at its maximizer along the segment towards \(u\) is nonpositive. Hence

\(\displaystyle w^*\cdot(u-u^*)\le0.\)

Thus \(u^*\) maximizes welfare for \(w^*\). Section 5.2 gives

\(\displaystyle u(w^*)=u^*.\)

We will not compute \(w^*\) exactly.

6.2 A strongly convex dual objective

For \(w\in Q=[1,W]^\tau\), define

\(\displaystyle F(w)=H(w)+\sum_t\left[\frac{(w_t-1)^2}{4C}-r(w_t-1)\right].\)

For every \(a\in[0,1]\), completing the square gives

\(\displaystyle a-C(r-a)_+^2=\min_{1\le s\le W}\left[sa+\frac{(s-1)^2}{4C}-r(s-1)\right].\)

The minimizer is \(s=1+2C(r-a)_+\), which lies in \([1,W]\).

For any \(w\in Q\), the definition of \(H\) and this identity imply

\(\displaystyle F(w)\ge w\cdot u^*+\sum_t\left[\frac{(w_t-1)^2}{4C}-r(w_t-1)\right]\ge\Phi(u^*).\)

Both inequalities are equalities at \(w^*\). Thus \(w^*\) minimizes \(F\).

The support function \(H\) is convex, while the added quadratic is \(1/(2C)\)-strongly convex. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle F(w)-F(w^*)\ge\frac{\|w-w^*\|_2^2}{4C}\quad\text{for all }w\in Q.\)

This bound also holds when the minimizer is on the boundary of the box: apply strong convexity to the segment from \(w^*\) to \(w\), use minimality at each intermediate point, and let the segment parameter tend to zero.

6.3 Polynomial-time approximate minimization

At every rational \(w\in Q\), the weighted-welfare oracle computes \(H(w)\), \(u(w)\), and therefore \(F(w)\), exactly.

A rational subgradient is

\(\displaystyle g_t(w)=u_t(w)+\frac{w_t-1}{2C}-r.\)

To justify it, for any \(w'\),

\(\displaystyle H(w')\ge w'\cdot u(w)=H(w)+(w'-w)\cdot u(w).\)

Adding the quadratic gradient gives the stated subgradient. Because \(u_t(w)\in[0,1]\), \((w_t-1)/(2C)\in[0,1]\), and \(r\in(0,1)\), every subgradient coordinate has absolute value at most \(2\).

We use the standard bit-complexity ellipsoid theorem for convex minimization: on a rational, full-dimensional box, a polynomial-time rational value/subgradient oracle and a known subgradient bound allow additive-error minimization in time polynomial in the dimension, the encoded bounds, and the encoding length of the requested accuracy. This is the usual weak convex optimization consequence of the ellipsoid method; see [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, Chapters 3–4](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4).

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • The box \(Q\) has rational endpoints, positive inradius \((W-1)/2\), and polynomially encoded radius bounds.
  • Values and subgradients at rational queries are rational and computable in polynomial time.
  • A Euclidean subgradient bound is \(2\sqrt{\tau}\), or the rational upper bound \(2\tau\).
  • All constants defining \(F\) have polynomial encoding length.

Apply the theorem with

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon=\frac{\delta^2}{256C\Lambda^2}.\)

It returns rational \(\widehat w\in Q\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle F(\widehat w)\le F(w^*)+\varepsilon.\)

The encoding length of \(\varepsilon\) is polynomial: \(\delta\), \(C\), \(W\), and \(\Lambda\) all have polynomial encoding length.

Strong convexity yields

\(\displaystyle \|\widehat w-w^*\|_2\le\sqrt{4C\varepsilon}=\frac{\delta}{8\Lambda}.\)

The continuity lemma then gives

\(\displaystyle \|u(\widehat w)-u^*\|_\infty\le\delta/8.\)

Therefore, for every type,

\(\displaystyle u_t(\widehat w)>b+\delta/8>b.\)

Finally, run the weighted-welfare oracle exactly at the rational weights \(\widehat w\). The resulting allocation is exactly Pareto optimal, because every weight is positive. It strictly improves every type over the benchmark.

The approximation is used only to choose weights; the output allocation and its Pareto optimality are exact.

7. Output size and conclusion

Each weighted-welfare allocation uses at most \(N=m(1+\binom{\tau}{2})\) profile intervals. The envy-free allocation uses the original \(m\) cells. The implementation lemma converts these profiles into polynomially many finite integral bundles with polynomially encoded rational endpoints and conditional masses.

Thus the three required outputs are:

  • Utilitarian: the exact welfare optimizer with weights \(w_t=\mu_t\), which is Pareto optimal.
  • Leftmost-leaves counterpart: the exact welfare optimizer with weights \(\widehat w_t>0\), which is Pareto optimal and satisfies \(u_t>b\) for every type.
  • Modified Wang–Wu counterpart: the cellwise positive-support sharing construction, which is envy-free and satisfies \(u_t\ge b\) for every type, with at least one strict inequality.

All steps have polynomial bit complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).
