Theorem 2 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Let \(H\) be a finite set of unit classes and let supported types \(t\) consist of a weak ranking over \(H\) and an endowed class \(e_t\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Set \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\). For an atomless population partitioned into these type cohorts, does there exist a uniform algorithm, polynomial in \(|H|\), \(\tau\), and the mass encoding length \(L\), that specifies a direct exchange rule with a measurable individual assignment \(\alpha(i)\in H\), represented compactly by its mass flows? Each individual must receive one whole unit, and each class must be assigned mass \(q_h\). The rule must be Pareto efficient and individually rational, select a core allocation whenever one exists, and admit no measurable positive-mass coalition whose members can all strictly improve by jointly misreporting or by reallocating their endowed units.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a nonempty finite set of unit classes, with \(m=|H|\). A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \(t=(R_t,e_t)\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(R_t\) is a complete, transitive weak ranking over \(H\), \(e_t\in H\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Represent individuals by the Lebesgue probability space \(I=[0,1]\), partitioned into measurable cohorts \(I_t\) of masses \(\mu_t\). Each individual owns one whole unit of class \(e_t\); units within a class are interchangeable. Set \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\). Endowments are public and fixed; only rankings may be misreported, and there are no transfers.
An allocation is a measurable assignment \(\alpha:I\to H\) satisfying \(\lambda(\alpha^{-1}(h))=q_h\). Its flows are \(x_{th}=\lambda(I_t\cap\alpha^{-1}(h))\). A deterministic direct rule must specify these flows together with a fixed measurable realization convention using public individual labels, so individual outcomes remain identifiable across reports; labels cannot be reset after a deviation.
All welfare comparisons hold almost everywhere. Pareto improvement means weak improvement for almost everyone and strict improvement on a set of positive mass; individual rationality means receiving a class weakly preferred to one's endowment.
A coalition is any measurable \(C\subseteq I\) with \(\lambda(C)>0\), including a proper subset of a cohort. An endowed-unit reallocation \(\beta:C\to H\) must satisfy \(\lambda(C\cap\beta^{-1}(h))=\lambda(\{i\in C:e_i=h\})\) for every \(h\).
The weak core excludes such reallocations strictly improving almost every coalition member. The core excludes such reallocations weakly improving almost every member and strictly improving a positive-mass subset.
The rule must always be Pareto efficient, individually rational, weak-core selecting, and core selecting whenever the core is nonempty. It must also exclude every measurable coalition whose joint ranking misreports, leaving outsiders' reports fixed, strictly improve almost every member relative to truthful reporting under the same rule.
The rule is defined on all measurable ranking-report profiles, including profiles with irrational cohort masses; the computational bound concerns inputs with binary-rational support masses. Computational output consists of exact binary-rational flows and the specified realization convention.

Does a direct exchange rule satisfying all these requirements admit uniform evaluation in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is this rule-implementation problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize total population mass to one and use a Lebesgue atomless probability space; the graded question did not specify total mass.
  • Treat units within each class as interchangeable, retain one whole unit per individual, and exclude monetary transfers.
  • Keep endowments public and fixed under deviations, allowing only ranking reports to change, as in the discrete definition.
  • Require a specified deterministic measurable realization convention with persistent public labels because aggregate flows alone do not determine individual outcomes across reports.
  • Interpret individual welfare conditions almost everywhere, and require strict improvement on positive mass for Pareto and core blocking; null sets cannot create improvements or blockers.
  • Allow coalitions to split type cohorts and require their reallocations to preserve their own endowed class masses.
  • Distinguish the core, which forbids weak improvement with some strict improvement, from the weak core, which forbids strict improvement for all coalition members.
  • Allow arbitrary measurable ranking deviations, including those producing irrational reported masses; impose the bit-complexity requirement only on rational support inputs.
  • Use exact binary-rational mass flows as the computational output representation.

I interpret the question as asking whether there exists a uniformly polynomial-time rule satisfying the entire package.

The general question remains unresolved. The following results advance the incentive part of the problem:

  • All allocations in a nonempty core give each individual the same welfare level.
  • A polynomial-time welfare rule can be constructed that excludes every strictly profitable measurable-coalition deviation from a truthful profile with nonempty core, even when the deviation makes the core empty.
  • For this rule, every possible strictly profitable coalition is confined to a precisely defined terminal market whose core is empty.
  • A four-class example shows why merely selecting welfare-compliant allocations does not establish the incentive requirement.

The construction also specifies a unique flow selection for irrational masses, repairing the deterministic-selection gap identified in the verifier report.

1. Residual markets and feasible sink components

Write $hP_tk$ for strict preference and $hI_tk$ for indifference. Ignore zero-supply classes: feasibility prevents their assignment on positive mass.

For a set $A$ of remaining positive-supply classes, the residual population is
$$ E_A=\{i\in I:e_i\in A\}. $$
Its class supplies remain $q_h$ for $h\in A$. We do not renormalize its mass.

For every positive-mass type endowed in $A$, define
$$ T_t(A)=\{k\in A:kR_tg\text{ for every }g\in A\}. $$
This set is nonempty because $A$ is finite and $R_t$ is complete and transitive.

Define the directed graph $D(A)$ on $A$ by including an edge
$$ e_t\longrightarrow k $$
whenever $k\in T_t(A)$ and $\mu_t>0$. Every vertex has an outgoing edge, so $D(A)$ has a sink strongly connected component.

For a sink component $S$, consider the transportation system
$$ \begin{aligned} y_{tk}&\geq 0,\\ \sum_{k\in S}y_{tk}&=\mu_t &&(e_t\in S),\\ \sum_{t:e_t\in S}y_{tk}&=q_k &&(k\in S),\\ y_{tk}&=0 &&(k\notin T_t(A)). \end{aligned} $$
Call $S$ feasible when this system has a solution.

Sink closure implies
$$ T_t(A)\subseteq S\qquad(e_t\in S). $$
Thus a feasible component can allocate its own endowed supply so that every member receives a top class among all currently available classes.

Fix orders on classes and on all ranking–endowment types. For each feasible transportation system, select its lexicographically least flow vector, using a fixed order on its coordinates.

This selection exists and is unique, including for irrational masses: the feasible set is nonempty and compact, and successive coordinate minimizations leave nonempty compact sets, eventually fixing every coordinate.

2. What the core forces

Lemma 1: forced top assignments

Suppose a residual market on $A$ has a core allocation $\alpha$. If $S$ is a sink component of $D(A)$, then every individual endowed in $S$ receives a member of $T_t(A)$ almost everywhere.

Consequently, $S$ is feasible, its owners consume exactly its entire supply, and restricting $\alpha$ to $E_{A\setminus S}$ gives a core allocation of the remaining market.

Proof

Suppose instead that some type $t$ endowed in $S$ receives a class $h\notin T_t(A)$ on a positive-mass cell. Choose $k\in T_t(A)$. Then
$$ kP_th. $$
Because $S$ is a sink, $k\in S$. Strong connectivity supplies a simple path from $k$ to $e_t$. Together with the edge $e_t\to k$, this produces a directed cycle; if $k=e_t$, use the loop.

Use the dissatisfied cell as the witness for the first edge. Every subsequent edge is witnessed by a positive-mass type whose top set contains that edge’s destination. Choose any positive allocation cell of that type. The destination is weakly preferred to the class received in that cell.

The source vertices of a simple cycle are distinct endowment classes. Hence the chosen witness cells are disjoint. Atomlessness permits selecting an equal positive mass $\varepsilon$ from each cell.

Give each selected subset the endowment class at the next cycle vertex. This balances the coalition’s endowed units class by class. All selected individuals weakly improve, and the first subset strictly improves. This contradicts core membership.

Thus all owners of $S$ receive top classes in $S$. Their total mass is
$$ \lambda(E_S)=\sum_{h\in S}q_h. $$
They therefore exhaust the supply of $S$, and no outsider receives a class in $S$ on positive mass. Their allocation flows solve the transportation system.

The restriction to the remaining population is feasible. Any coalition blocking that restriction would also block $\alpha$, so the restriction belongs to the residual core. $\square$

Feasible-component peeling

Run the following procedure:

  • Construct $D(A)$.
  • Test its sink components for feasibility.
  • If at least one is feasible, select one deterministically, assign its lexicographically selected flows, and remove that component and its endowed population.
  • Repeat until no classes remain or no sink component is feasible.

Crucially, an infeasible sink does not cause immediate termination when another feasible sink is available.

At most $m$ components are removed.

Lemma 2: core nonemptiness and successful peeling

The original core is nonempty exactly when this procedure removes every class. Every allocation from a complete peeling run belongs to the core.

Proof

If a core allocation exists, Lemma 1 shows that every current sink component is feasible. It also shows that, after removing any selected component, the residual market still has a core allocation. Hence the procedure cannot stop before removing every class.

Conversely, suppose the procedure removes all classes, in the order
$$ S_1,\ldots,S_K. $$
Let
$$ A_j=H_+\setminus\bigcup_{\ell<j}S_\ell, $$
where $H_+$ is the set of positive-supply classes.

Suppose a coalition $C$ weakly improves through an endowed-unit reallocation $\beta$. Consider the first component represented in $C$ on positive mass. Earlier coalition endowments have zero mass, so coalition feasibility implies that $\beta$ assigns only classes available at that component’s removal.

Participating owners of $S_j$ already receive top classes in $A_j$. A weak improvement must give them another member of their top set, hence another class in $S_j$. They cannot strictly improve.

Their mass equals the coalition’s endowed supply from $S_j$. They exhaust that supply, so no remaining participant can receive a class in $S_j$. Remove these participants and repeat.

No positive-mass subset can strictly improve, contradicting core blocking. $\square$

In particular, if peeling stops with a nonempty residual market, that residual market has empty core.

Corollary 3: uniqueness of core welfare

If the core is nonempty, then for any two core allocations $\alpha$ and $\beta$,
$$ \alpha(i)I_i\beta(i) $$
almost everywhere.

Proof

Apply Lemma 1 successively along a complete peeling run. Every individual endowed in $S_j$ must receive, under either allocation, a member of $T_t(A_j)$. All members of that set are indifferent for that individual. $\square$

This establishes uniqueness of welfare, rather than uniqueness of assigned classes or flows.

3. A deterministic welfare fallback

When peeling stops with a nonempty residual market, use the following fallback. Its incentive compatibility is not asserted.

3.1 Construct an individually rational weak-core allocation

Maintain residual type cohorts and their masses. Each positive residual type points to:

  • a top class among classes with positive residual endowed supply, using the fixed class order to resolve ties;
  • a positive residual type endowed with that class, using the fixed type order.

The resulting finite functional graph has a directed cycle. Choose one deterministically. If $\delta$ is the smallest residual mass on the cycle, select mass $\delta$ from each cycle cohort, give it the next cohort’s endowment class, and remove the selected individuals and their endowed units.

Each iteration exhausts at least one residual type. There are therefore at most $\tau$ iterations.

Every operation balances removed assignments and endowments class by class. Each selected individual receives a top available class; their own endowment class is available while they remain. The allocation $\alpha^0$ is consequently feasible and individually rational.

To verify weak-core membership, suppose a coalition strictly improves almost every member. Choose the earliest iteration whose selected individuals intersect the coalition in positive mass. Every positive-mass coalition endowment class was available at that iteration: earlier selected coalition members have zero mass. The selected participants already receive top available classes and therefore cannot strictly improve using coalition endowments. This is a contradiction.

3.2 Make a Pareto-efficient individual improvement

Let
$$ B_{th}=I_t\cap(\alpha^0)^{-1}(h), \qquad x^0_{th}=\lambda(B_{th}). $$
Choose integer utilities $u_t$ representing each ranking exactly, with values between $0$ and $m-1$.

Use variables $z_{thk}$ only for $kR_th$, subject to
$$ \begin{aligned} z_{thk}&\geq 0,\\ \sum_k z_{thk}&=x^0_{th},\\ \sum_{t,h}z_{thk}&=q_k. \end{aligned} $$
Maximize
$$ \sum_{t,h,k}u_t(k)z_{thk}, $$
and choose the lexicographically least optimum.

Realize these variables by subdividing each actual cell $B_{th}$. The resulting allocation $\gamma$ weakly improves every individual relative to $\alpha^0$.

It remains individually rational. It also remains in the weak core: a coalition strictly improving upon $\gamma$ would, by transitivity, strictly improve upon $\alpha^0$.

For Pareto efficiency, suppose $\beta$ Pareto improves $\gamma$. It weakly improves $\alpha^0$ by transitivity, so its intersections with the cells $B_{th}$ define a feasible transportation solution. The utility integral increases strictly: every strict improvement raises the corresponding integer utility by at least one, and strict improvement occurs on positive mass. This contradicts optimality.

Thus the fallback satisfies feasibility, individual rationality, Pareto efficiency, and weak-core membership.

4. Fixed labels and polynomial evaluation

For any measurable set $B\subseteq I$, define
$$ F_B(s)=\lambda(B\cap[0,s]). $$
This function is continuous, nondecreasing, and $1$-Lipschitz, with
$$ F_B(0)=0,\qquad F_B(1)=\lambda(B). $$
Therefore, for every $z\in[0,\lambda(B)]$, a prefix cut selects exactly mass $z$. Choose the least cut point when $0<z<\lambda(B)$, and handle the two endpoints by selecting the empty set or all of $B$.

All cohort splitting uses this convention and the original public labels. Successive cuts realize the selected flow vectors. Changing a cohort by a null set changes its selected subsets only by null sets.

Define $F$ to be the rule that first peels every available feasible sink component and then, if necessary, applies the fallback to the terminal population.

Proposition 4: welfare properties of $F$

The rule $F$ is individually rational, Pareto efficient, and weak-core selecting. It selects a core allocation whenever the core is nonempty.

Proof

Individual rationality follows from top assignments during peeling and from the fallback’s individual rationality.

For weak-core membership, a strictly blocking coalition intersecting a peeled component on positive mass has an earliest such component. Its participants there already receive top classes among all positive-mass coalition endowments and cannot strictly improve. A coalition lying entirely in the terminal population cannot block the fallback.

For Pareto efficiency, consider any putative Pareto improvement. Owners of the first peeled component receive top available classes, so weak improvement confines them to that component. Their mass exhausts its supply. No outsider can receive its classes, and these owners cannot strictly improve.

Remove them and repeat for each peeled component. The remaining allocation would Pareto improve the fallback, which is impossible.

If the core is nonempty, Lemma 2 gives a complete peeling run and proves core membership. $\square$

Exactness and running time

On rational inputs, choose a common denominator $D$ for the masses. Its binary length is $O(L)$: the product of the input denominators suffices. For dyadic inputs, take a common power of two.

There are at most $m$ peeling iterations and at most $m$ sink tests per iteration. Each feasibility test is a bipartite maximum-flow problem with $O(\tau+m)$ vertices and $O(\tau m)$ edges.

After scaling by $D$, transportation row and column totals are integers. The constraint matrix is totally unimodular: multiplying the column-sum rows by $-1$ turns it into a directed vertex–edge incidence matrix. Consequently, transportation vertices are integral after scaling.

Successive lexicographic optimization restricts to faces of the original transportation polytope. The final unique point is a vertex, so every selected flow is an integer multiple of $1/D$.

The fallback uses at most $\tau$ cycle operations and a transportation optimization with $O(\tau m^2)$ variables. Its row totals remain multiples of $1/D$. The same integrality argument applies.

Using polynomial-time rational linear programming, the objective optimization and polynomially many coordinate minimizations take time polynomial in $m$, $\tau$, and $L$. All returned flows have polynomial-length exact rational representations.

For arbitrary measurable report profiles, including irrational masses, there are still only finitely many positive ranking–endowment types. All operations remain defined through finite graphs, compact transportation sets, and measurable prefix cuts. No computational bound is claimed for irrational inputs.

5. Commutation of feasible-component removals

The next lemma is the new ingredient needed for incentives.

Lemma 5: persistence and commutation

Fix a report profile and residual market.

  • An available feasible sink component $S$ remains the same feasible sink component until it is removed.
  • Every maximal feasible-component peeling sequence removes $S$.
  • Two simultaneously available feasible sink components can be removed in either order, with identical assignments almost everywhere.
  • All maximal peeling sequences produce the same component assignments and the same terminal residual market.

Here a sequence is maximal when it continues until no feasible sink remains.

Proof

Distinct sink strongly connected components are disjoint.

Suppose $S$ is available and another sink component $B$ is removed. For every type endowed in $S$,
$$ T_t(A)\subseteq S. $$
Removing $B$ therefore leaves its top set unchanged:
$$ T_t(A\setminus B)=T_t(A). $$
Hence the graph within $S$, its sink property, and its transportation system are unchanged. This proves persistence.

Each removal deletes at least one class. A maximal sequence cannot terminate while $S$ remains feasible, so it must eventually remove $S$.

If $S$ and $B$ are simultaneously available, removing either leaves the other’s transportation system unchanged. Their lexicographically selected flows are therefore unchanged. They act on disjoint endowed populations, so their fixed-label realizations also commute.

Finally, compare two maximal sequences. The first component of the first sequence is initially available in the second sequence. By persistence, the second sequence eventually removes it. Commuting it past preceding removals moves it to the front without changing assignments or the final residual market.

The two sequences now have the same first removal. Repeat on the remaining market. This induction proves the last assertion. $\square$

Although the implemented rule can use a fixed component order, its outcome does not depend on that order.

6. Localization of every possible profitable coalition

Theorem 6: coalition localization

At a truthful profile, suppose $F$ peels components
$$ S_1,\ldots,S_K $$
and leaves the terminal class set
$$ A_*=H_+\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^KS_j. $$

If a measurable coalition strictly improves almost every member through joint ranking misreports, then
$$ \lambda(C\setminus E_{A_*})=0. $$

Moreover, its deviation can be analyzed entirely within the terminal population, using the same original labels and the same residual version of $F$.

Proof

First suppose the coalition intersects a peeled population on positive mass. Let $r$ be the least index with
$$ \lambda(C\cap E_{S_r})>0. $$
For every $j<r$, coalition membership in $E_{S_j}$ has zero mass. Rankings of owners of those classes therefore remain unchanged almost everywhere.

At the deviating profile, $S_1$ is still a feasible sink component: its outgoing edges depend only on its owners’ unchanged rankings, its top sets remain inside $S_1$, and its transportation system is unchanged.

By Lemma 5, the deviating peeling sequence removes $S_1$, and that removal can be moved to the beginning without changing its outcome.

After removing $S_1$, the same argument applies to $S_2$. Continuing, the deviating sequence can be reordered to begin with
$$ S_1,\ldots,S_{r-1}. $$
Each of these components consumes only its own supply.

Consequently, almost every individual outside those populations receives, after deviation, a class in
$$ A_r=H_+\setminus\bigcup_{j<r}S_j. $$
This includes almost every coalition member.

But truthful participants in $E_{S_r}$ receive top classes in $A_r$. Their deviating assignments therefore cannot be strictly better. Since their intersection with $C$ has positive mass, the deviation cannot strictly improve almost every coalition member. This is a contradiction.

Thus the coalition must lie almost entirely in the terminal population.

In that case all truthful peeled populations have unchanged reports almost everywhere. The same commutation argument moves all their removals to the beginning of the deviating sequence. The remaining population, supplies, and public labels are exactly those of the truthful terminal market.

Lemma 5 makes the rest of the peeling outcome independent of removal order. The fallback depends only on the resulting residual profile and its existing labels. Therefore the residual outcome is exactly the outcome obtained by running the same construction on that terminal population. $\square$

Corollary 7: incentives from every nonempty-core truthful profile

If the truthful core is nonempty, no measurable coalition can strictly improve almost every member by joint ranking misreports under $F$.

Proof

Lemma 2 gives $A_*=\varnothing$. Theorem 6 would confine any profitable coalition to a null population, contrary to its required positive mass. $\square$

The deviating profile may have empty core. No assumption on its core is used.

Corollary 8: incentive compatibility between core profiles is universal

For any rule selecting core allocations, a coalition cannot strictly improve almost every member when both the truthful and deviating profiles have nonempty cores.

Proof

Take a complete truthful peeling sequence and the first component meeting the coalition on positive mass.

For each preceding component, reports of its owners are unchanged almost everywhere. Lemma 1, applied successively to the deviating core allocation, forces those owners to consume their component’s supply. Thus coalition members cannot receive any earlier component’s classes.

The coalition’s participants in the first represented truthful component already receive top classes among the remaining classes. They cannot strictly improve. $\square$

7. Welfare compliance alone permits a profitable deviation

The following example explains why handling deviations to empty-core profiles requires an additional argument.

Let
$$ H=\{a,b,c,d\}. $$
There are four cohorts:

| Cohort | Mass | Endowment | Truthful ranking |
|---|---:|---|---|
| $U$ | $6/16$ | $a$ | $b\succ c\succ a\succ d$ |
| $V$ | $6/16$ | $b$ | $a\sim b\succ c\succ d$ |
| $W$ | $3/16$ | $c$ | $b\succ c\succ d\succ a$ |
| $Z$ | $1/16$ | $d$ | $c\succ d\succ a\succ b$ |

Truthful core allocation

Initially, $\{a,b\}$ is a sink component. Its transportation system is feasible and forces
$$ U\longmapsto b,\qquad V\longmapsto a. $$
After removing it, the available classes are $\{c,d\}$. Cohort $W$ ranks $c$ first, making $\{c\}$ a feasible sink. Finally, $Z$ receives $d$.

Thus
$$ \alpha(U)=b,\quad \alpha(V)=a,\quad \alpha(W)=c,\quad \alpha(Z)=d $$
belongs to the core by Lemma 2.

A deviation making the core empty

Let the coalition be all of $W$, and let it report
$$ b\succ d\succ c\succ a. $$
The component $\{a,b\}$ is unchanged and remains forced in any putative core allocation.

After its removal, owners of $c$ demand $d$, while owners of $d$ demand $c$. The residual top graph is the strongly connected cycle
$$ c\longrightarrow d\longrightarrow c. $$
Its transportation system would have to give class $d$ to all $3/16$ owners of $c$, although only $1/16$ of $d$ exists. It is infeasible. Lemma 1 therefore proves that the deviating profile has empty core.

A welfare-compliant allocation benefiting the coalition

At the deviating profile, consider $\gamma$ with flows
$$ \begin{aligned} x_{Ub}&=3/16,&x_{Uc}&=3/16,\\ x_{Va}&=6/16,\\ x_{Wb}&=3/16,\\ x_{Zd}&=1/16. \end{aligned} $$
These flows preserve every class supply. They are individually rational under the reported rankings.

They also belong to the weak core. The only individuals capable of strict improvement are the members of $U$ receiving $c$ and the members of $Z$ receiving $d$. The former need $b$; the latter need $c$. A coalition consisting almost everywhere of such individuals owns only $a$ and $d$, so it cannot strictly improve almost every member.

For Pareto efficiency:

  • Current recipients of $b$ strictly rank $b$ first and must retain it in any weak improvement. They exhaust $b$.
  • Cohort $V$ can weakly replace $a$ only by $b$. Since $b$ is exhausted, $V$ retains all of $a$.
  • Members of $U$ receiving $c$ can strictly improve only to $b$, so they retain all of $c$.
  • Cohort $Z$ can strictly improve only to $c$, which is exhausted.

Hence no Pareto improvement exists.

Nevertheless, every member of $W$ receives $b$ under $\gamma$, strictly better in its true ranking than its truthful core assignment $c$.

This demonstrates that welfare compliance at the two profiles does not itself exclude the profitable misreport. The peeling rule $F$ prevents this particular outcome by assigning $\{a,b\}$ to their owners before using any fallback.

8. The remaining problem

The construction has polynomial evaluation and all the required welfare properties. Its incentive proof covers every truthful profile with nonempty core and, more generally, confines any profitable coalition to its terminal residual market.

What remains is to prove the incentive requirement at truthful markets in which every sink component has an infeasible top-assignment transportation system, allowing deviations that may create feasible components.

These residual markets are substantive. For example, take three classes with supplies
$$ q_a=\frac12,\qquad q_b=q_c=\frac14, $$
and homogeneous rankings within each endowment class whose top choices are
$$ a\longrightarrow b,\qquad b\longrightarrow c,\qquad c\longrightarrow a. $$
The entire top graph is one sink component, and its transportation system is infeasible because owners of $a$ require mass $1/2$ of class $b$, whose supply is only $1/4$. No peeling occurs.

The finite-market theorem cited in the question supplies polynomial rules with the full finite-agent property package. [Ahmad’s Theorem 2](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/157074/1/Revised%20Draft%202.pdf) does not by itself provide the needed compressed implementation or fixed-label measurable-coalition proof for these residual markets.

No general incentive theorem for the fallback, hardness reduction, or impossibility proof has been established here. Therefore the requested polynomial-time-versus-$\mathrm{NP}$-hard classification remains open in this write-up.
