The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, a finite set \(T\) of complete ranking types, rational registered masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational reserve masses \(\nu_t\ge0\), a preferred candidate \(p\), and exact replacement mass \(\rho\), decide whether there exists \(x_{t,u}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_u x_{t,u}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,u}\le\nu_u\), and \(\sum_{t,u}x_{t,u}=\rho\), such that \(\mu'_q=\mu_q-\sum_u x_{q,u}+\sum_t x_{t,q}\) and \(\sum_q\mu'_q\mathbf{1}[p\text{ is top-two in }q]\ge\sum_q\mu'_q\mathbf{1}[c\text{ is top-two in }q]\) for every candidate \(c\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge2\) candidates, with preferred candidate \(p\in C\); types are strict complete rankings of \(C\).
A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Let \(T\) be the union of the registered and reserve supports and \(\tau=|T|\), not the number of possible rankings; missing masses are zero.
Registered masses satisfy \(\mu_t\ge0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); reserve masses satisfy \(\nu_t\ge0\) and need not sum to one. The exact replacement mass \(\rho\ge0\) is a binary rational. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A replacement chooses real masses \(x_{t,u}\ge0\), for all \(t,u\in T\), satisfying \(\sum_u x_{t,u}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,u}\le\nu_u\), and \(\sum_{t,u}x_{t,u}=\rho\); replacing by the same ranking is permitted and counts toward \(\rho\).
The resulting masses are \(\mu'_q=\mu_q-\sum_u x_{q,u}+\sum_t x_{t,q}\). Under 2-Approval, success means that \(p\) is a winner, with ties permitted: \(\sum_q\mu'_q\mathbf1[p\text{ is top-two in }q]\ge\sum_q\mu'_q\mathbf1[c\text{ is top-two in }q]\) for every \(c\in C\).

Is deciding whether a successful exact replacement exists solvable in deterministic time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite unweighted voters, Exact Perfect Matching logspace many-one reduces to exact 2-Approval voter replacement; the converse also holds, placing the discrete problem in \(\mathrm{RNC}\subseteq\mathrm{RP}\). [Fitzsimmons–Hemaspaandra](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.12856)
The discrete variant allowing at most the specified number of replacements is in \(\mathrm P\). No result settling the mass problem above was identified in the search. [Full version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.12856)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required at least two candidates so that 2-Approval is defined, and specified that complete rankings are strict total orders.
  • Defined T as the union of the registered and reserve supports, with absent masses zero, so reserve-only types remain available; tau counts this explicit union.
  • Specified rho as a nonnegative binary rational, retaining zero as permitted by the discrete exact-control definition.
  • Specified that reserve masses need not sum to one and use the same mass units as the normalized registered society.
  • Explicitly allowed replacements between distinct registered and reserve voters of the same ranking, counting their mass toward the exact replacement amount, as the discrete definition permits.

Exact mass replacement is in \(\mathrm P\)

Theorem. Under the stated assumptions, successful exact replacement can be decided in deterministic time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, it is equivalent to feasibility of a rational linear program with \(2\tau\) variables.

1. Removed and added masses

For each \(t\in T\), introduce variables \(r_t\) and \(a_t\), representing the registered mass removed from type \(t\) and the reserve mass added of type \(t\). Require

\[ 0\le r_t\le\mu_t,\qquad 0\le a_t\le\nu_t \qquad(t\in T), \]

and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}r_t=\rho,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}a_t=\rho. \]

Here and below, these displayed formulas are linear constraints.

Lemma. These constraints characterize exactly the possible removal and addition marginals of replacements allowed in the question.

Proof. Given an allowed replacement \(x\), define \(r_t=\sum_u x_{t,u}\) and \(a_u=\sum_t x_{t,u}\). The replacement constraints give all the required bounds and equalities.

Conversely, suppose \(r,a\) satisfy those constraints. If \(\rho=0\), nonnegativity forces every \(r_t\) and \(a_t\) to be zero; take every \(x_{t,u}=0\).

If \(\rho>0\), define \(x_{t,u}=r_ta_u/\rho\). These values are nonnegative, and their row and column sums satisfy

\[ \sum_u x_{t,u} =\frac{r_t}{\rho}\sum_u a_u=r_t, \qquad \sum_t x_{t,u} =\frac{a_u}{\rho}\sum_t r_t=a_u. \]

Consequently, they respect the registered and reserve capacities, and their total mass is \(\sum_t r_t=\rho\). All entries, including those with \(t=u\), are permitted by the question. This proves the lemma.

For either construction, the resulting society is \(\mu'_t=\mu_t-r_t+a_t\). It has nonnegative masses because \(r_t\le\mu_t\), and total mass

\[ \sum_t\mu'_t=1-\rho+\rho=1. \]

2. Winner constraints

For candidate \(c\) and type \(t\), let \(b_c(t)\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether \(c\) is among the top two candidates in \(t\).

For every \(c\ne p\), define the integer coefficient \(d_c(t)=b_p(t)-b_c(t)\in\{-1,0,1\}\) and the initial score advantage \(D_c=\sum_t\mu_t d_c(t)\).

After removal and addition, the score advantage of \(p\) over \(c\) is

\[ \sum_t(\mu_t-r_t+a_t)d_c(t) =D_c-\sum_t d_c(t)r_t+\sum_t d_c(t)a_t. \]

Therefore, \(p\) ties or beats \(c\) exactly when

\[ \sum_{t\in T}d_c(t)(r_t-a_t)\le D_c. \]

Add this inequality for each \(c\in C\setminus\{p\}\) to the constraints from Section 1. This completes the linear program.

Its feasibility is equivalent to successful exact replacement: a successful replacement gives feasible marginals by the lemma and satisfies every winner inequality; conversely, feasible marginals can be coupled by the lemma into an allowed replacement, and the winner inequalities ensure that \(p\) is a winner.

3. Encoding and deterministic running time

The linear program has \(2\tau\) variables, \(4\tau\) bound inequalities, two equalities, and \(m-1\) winner inequalities. Replace each equality by two inequalities if needed.

Every variable coefficient belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The right-hand sides are input rationals or the computed advantages \(D_c\).

To verify that these advantages have polynomial encoding length, write each registered mass as \(\mu_t=n_t/q_t\), with positive integer denominator \(q_t\). The product \(Q=\prod_t q_t\) is a common denominator whose binary length is at most the sum of the denominator lengths, hence \(O(L)\). Moreover, \(|D_c|\le\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Thus \(D_c\) can be written with denominator \(Q\) and numerator of absolute value at most \(Q\), using \(O(L)\) bits. These numbers can be computed with polynomially many operations on integers of polynomial bit length.

The coefficients \(d_c(t)\) are obtained directly from the supported rankings. Consequently, the whole rational linear program can be constructed in time and encoding length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). No unsupported rankings need to be enumerated.

The polynomial-time rational linear-programming feasibility theorem states that, for explicitly encoded rational \(A,b\), one can deterministically decide whether there exists a real vector \(z\) satisfying \(Az\le b\), in time polynomial in the encoding length. It applies to equalities represented by paired inequalities and does not require a full-dimensional feasible region. See [Khachiyan, *A polynomial algorithm in linear programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

All its hypotheses hold for the constructed system. Applying that theorem proves the claimed deterministic polynomial-time algorithm.

4. Relation to the discrete problem

The discrete exact-replacement problem concerns indivisible voters, as in [Fitzsimmons–Hemaspaandra](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.12856). The present problem permits arbitrary real replacement masses, so no integrality constraints are needed. Its exactness requirement is enforced by the two linear equalities above. Therefore, the discrete matching equivalence does not obstruct this polynomial-time algorithm.
