The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite projects \(C\), per-capita costs \(q(c)\), budget \(B\), and rational utility types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), compute an integral \(W\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{c\in W}q(c)\leq B\) using mass-weighted PropRank, and verify that every submass \(\sigma\leq\mu\) of mass \(\eta>0\) satisfying \(\sum_{c\in X}q(c)\leq B\eta\) and \(\sum_{c\in X}\min_{t:\sigma_t>0}u_t(c)\geq\alpha\) obeys \(\eta^{-1}\sum_t\sigma_tu_t(W)\geq(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given \(C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), positive binary-rational per-capita costs \(q(c)\), and a positive binary-rational budget \(B\), a society is given by its support: \(\tau\) utility types \(t\in T\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Utilities are additive, with binary-rational \(u_t(c)\geq0\); every project has positive utility for some supported type. Write \(q(X)=\sum_{c\in X}q(c)\) and \(u_{\max}=\max_{t,c}u_t(c)\).
Define mass-weighted PropRank with \(\kappa=1\) as follows. Initially \(W=\varnothing\), time \(h=0\), and per-capita balances and global factors \(p_t=g_t=0\). Every balance earns at unit rate; available projects are \(R=\{c\notin W:q(W)+q(c)\leq B\}\).
At each time, set \(\lambda_t=\max\{g_t,\max_{c\in R}\max_{a\in A_{t,c}}\frac{a}{q(c)}\sum_{s:u_s(c)\geq a}\mu_sp_s\}\), where \(A_{t,c}=\{u_s(c):s\in T,\ 0<u_s(c)\leq u_t(c)\}\), and empty maxima are zero.
Set \(x_t(c)=2p_tu_t(c)/(u_t(c)+\max\{\lambda_t,u_t(c)\})\) when \(u_t(c)>0\), and zero otherwise. Advance to the earliest time at which some available project satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_tx_t(c)\geq q(c)\), recomputing these quantities as time advances.
For each affordable project, let \(\rho_c\geq0\) be the least solution of \(\sum_t\mu_t\min\{x_t(c),\rho_cu_t(c)\}=q(c)\). Buy the project minimizing \(\rho_c\), breaking ties by its input index; subtract \(\min\{x_t(c),\rho_cu_t(c)\}\) from each \(p_t\), set each \(g_t\) to its pre-purchase \(\lambda_t\), and repeat, allowing purchases at the same time, until \(R=\varnothing\).
The condition to check on the resulting integral outcome \(W\) is: for every real submass \(0\leq\sigma_t\leq\mu_t\) with \(\eta=\sum_t\sigma_t>0\), every \(X\subseteq C\), and every \(\alpha\in\mathbb R\), if \(q(X)\leq B\eta\) and \(\sum_{c\in X}\min_{t:\sigma_t>0}u_t(c)\geq\alpha\), then \(\eta^{-1}\sum_t\sigma_tu_t(W)\geq(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\).

Is exact computation of this mass-weighted PropRank outcome, together with deciding whether it satisfies the universal submass condition, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this task NP-hard?

For finite electorates, PropRank guarantees proportionality degree \((\alpha-u_{\max})/2\) under participatory budgeting constraints; exact earliest-purchase calculation is also claimed to take polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08504v1).

Partial result: exact outcome computation contains square-root-sum comparison

The general polynomial-time-versus-NP-hardness question remains unresolved here. I prove a stronger computational obstruction than irrational intermediate balances:

Given positive integers $k_1,\ldots,k_n$ and an integer $K$, exact computation of the specified integral outcome can decide whether
$$ > \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{k_i}\geq K. > $$

The reduction uses polynomially many supported types and projects, with rational input data of polynomial bit length. It handles equality using the specified payment-parameter comparison.

The construction also gives actual purchase times of algebraic degree $2^n$. This rules out a general polynomial-time implementation that explicitly expands minimal polynomials of all purchase times. It does not rule out polynomial-time computation using succinct representations, and it does not establish NP-hardness.

I also prove that the universal submass condition always holds for the specified outcome.

1. Preliminary observations

For every type and project,
$$ 0\leq x_t(c)\leq p_t, $$
because the denominator in the cap formula is at least $2u_t(c)$ when $u_t(c)>0$.

Consequently, payments preserve nonnegative balances. Since balances start at zero and earn at unit rate, at absolute time $h$,
$$ 0\leq p_t\leq h. $$

At an affordability event where
$$ \sum_t\mu_t x_t(c)=q(c), $$
every supporting type must pay its entire cap. Indeed, each payment is at most its cap, all masses are positive, and the weighted payments must sum to the weighted cap sum.

These observations will be used throughout the construction.

2. Proven: the universal submass condition always holds

I use the finite-electorate theorem supplied in the question: under participatory budgeting, PropRank guarantees
$$ \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}u_i(W) \geq \frac{\sum_{c\in X}\min_{i\in S}u_i(c)-u_{\max}}{2} $$
whenever a nonempty coalition $S$ and a project set $X$ satisfy
$$ \operatorname{cost}(X)\leq b\frac{|S|}{n}. $$
This is the proportionality guarantee in [Theorem 4, with Definitions 4 and 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08504v1#S4.Thm4).

2.1. Rational submasses

Fix rational numbers $0\leq\sigma_t\leq\mu_t$, with
$$ \eta=\sum_t\sigma_t>0. $$
Choose a positive integer $N$ such that all $N\mu_t$ and $N\sigma_t$ are integers. Choose a positive integer $s$ clearing the denominators of the costs and budget.

Construct a finite electorate with $N\mu_t$ identical voters of type $t$, project costs
$$ \operatorname{cost}(c)=Ns\,q(c), $$
and budget
$$ b=NsB. $$

There are exactly $N$ voters. Costs are positive integers, utilities are nonnegative, and every project retains a positive-utility supporter. Thus the finite theorem’s hypotheses hold.

The finite run and the weighted run correspond under
$$ h^{\mathrm{fin}}=sh,\qquad p_i^{\mathrm{fin}}=sp_t,\qquad g_i^{\mathrm{fin}}=g_t $$
for every clone $i$ of type $t$.

To verify the correspondence, for any positive utility threshold $a$,
$$ \frac{a}{Nsq(c)} \sum_{i:u_i(c)\geq a}p_i^{\mathrm{fin}} = \frac{a}{q(c)} \sum_{t:u_t(c)\geq a}\mu_t p_t. $$
Nonnegative balances make inclusion of every voter above a fixed threshold optimal in the finite rule’s local maximization. Hence the finite and weighted scaling factors agree.

Their spending caps satisfy
$$ x_i^{\mathrm{fin}}(c)=s x_t(c), $$
so affordability agrees:
$$ \sum_i x_i^{\mathrm{fin}}(c) = Ns\sum_t\mu_t x_t(c). $$

Replacing a weighted payment parameter $\rho_c$ by $s\rho_c$ multiplies both sides of its payment equation by $Ns$. Therefore the least finite payment parameter is $s\rho_c$. Ordering of parameters, equality ties, and input-index tie-breaking agree. Payments and global-factor updates preserve the state correspondence. Feasibility also agrees because all costs and the budget were multiplied by $Ns$.

Induction over purchases proves that the two runs return the same project set.

Now select a finite coalition containing $N\sigma_t$ clones of each type. Its size is $N\eta$, and
$$ q(X)\leq B\eta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{cost}(X)\leq b\frac{N\eta}{N}. $$
The finite theorem gives
$$ \frac{\sum_t\sigma_tu_t(W)}{\eta} \geq \frac{\sum_{c\in X}\min_{t:\sigma_t>0}u_t(c)-u_{\max}}{2}. $$

2.2. Real submasses

Choose a positive integer $D$ with $D\mu_t\in\mathbb Z$ for every type. For an arbitrary real admissible submass vector $\sigma$, define
$$ \sigma_t^{(j)} = \frac{\lceil jD\sigma_t\rceil}{jD}. $$
Because each $\mu_t$ lies on this grid,
$$ \sigma_t\leq\sigma_t^{(j)}\leq\mu_t. $$
Moreover, $\sigma^{(j)}$ has exactly the same positive support as $\sigma$, and
$$ \sigma_t^{(j)}\longrightarrow\sigma_t. $$

Writing $\eta_j=\sum_t\sigma_t^{(j)}$, we have $\eta_j\geq\eta$. Therefore
$$ q(X)\leq B\eta $$
implies $q(X)\leq B\eta_j$, including when the original budget inequality is an equality.

Apply the rational result and take limits. The denominator converges to $\eta>0$, and the common-utility minimum is unchanged because the support is unchanged. Thus
$$ \frac{\sum_t\sigma_tu_t(W)}{\eta} \geq \frac{\sum_{c\in X}\min_{t:\sigma_t>0}u_t(c)-u_{\max}}{2}. $$
If the common-utility sum is at least $\alpha$, this implies the condition in the question.

Consequently, on outcomes of the specified rule, the condition-checking component always answers yes. This conclusion concerns the specified outcome, not verification of an arbitrary supplied project set.

3. Proven: a reduction from square-root-sum comparison

Consider the following exact comparison problem:
$$ k_1,\ldots,k_n\in\mathbb Z,\qquad k_i\geq2, $$
and
$$ K\in\mathbb Z,\qquad 0\leq K\leq S:=\sum_{i=1}^n k_i. $$
The question is whether
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{k_i}\geq K. $$

The restrictions discard only elementary cases: terms equal to $1$ can be removed while adjusting the threshold, and thresholds outside the displayed range are immediately decidable.

3.1. Rational parameters, types, and projects

Define
$$ \delta=\frac{3}{4S}, \qquad a=\frac{\delta(S-n)}{2}, $$
and two rational times
$$ H_0=1+\max_i k_i, \qquad H_1=4H_0+16. $$
Since $S\geq2n$,
$$ \frac{3}{16}\leq a<\frac38. $$

For each index $i$, introduce types $r_i,s_i$ with masses
$$ \mu_{r_i}=\frac{\delta(k_i-1)}2, \qquad \mu_{s_i}=\delta. $$
Introduce two additional types $t_d,t_b$, with masses
$$ \mu_{t_d}=\frac14, \qquad \mu_{t_b}=a. $$
All masses are positive, and their sum is
$$ \sum_i\mu_{r_i}+\sum_i\mu_{s_i}+\mu_{t_d}+\mu_{t_b} = 2a+n\delta+\frac14 = \delta S+\frac14 = 1. $$

Introduce projects $e_i,v_i$ for every index, and projects $d,b,f$. Define costs
$$ q(e_i)=\delta k_i, $$
$$ Q_d:=q(d)=\frac{H_0}{4}, \qquad Q_b:=q(b)=aH_1, $$
$$ Q_f:=q(f)=aH_1-\delta(S-K), $$
and
$$ Q_v:=q(v_i)=aH_1+\frac{H_0}{8} $$
for every $i$. Set the budget to
$$ B=\frac34+Q_d+Q_b. $$

The positive utilities are exactly the following; every other utility is zero.

| Project | Supporting type | Utility |
|---|---|---|
| $e_i$ | $r_i$ | $1$ |
| $e_i$ | $s_i$ | $k_i$ |
| $v_i$ | $r_i$ | $Q_v/\mu_{r_i}$ |
| $f$ | every $r_i$ | $H_0$ |
| $d$ | $t_d$ | $1$ |
| $b$ | $t_b$ | $1$ |

All inputs are rational and satisfy the task’s positivity assumptions.

We will prove
$$ f\in W \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_i\sqrt{k_i}\geq K. $$

3.2. Feasibility inequalities

First,
$$ \sum_iq(e_i)=\delta S=\frac34. $$
Also,
$$ 0\leq\delta(S-K)\leq\frac34, $$
so
$$ Q_b-\frac34\leq Q_f\leq Q_b. $$
In particular,
$$ Q_f-aH_0 = a(H_1-H_0)-\delta(S-K) \geq \frac{3}{16}(3H_0+16)-\frac34 >0. $$
Thus
$$ Q_f>aH_0>0. $$

Until $d$ is purchased, if only projects among the $e_i$ have been purchased, the remaining budget is at least
$$ Q_d+Q_b. $$
Every unpurchased project remains feasible, because
$$ q(e_i)\leq\frac34<Q_b, \qquad Q_f\leq Q_b, $$
and
$$ Q_v=Q_b+\frac{H_0}{8}<Q_b+Q_d. $$

These inequalities justify the available-project sets used below.

3.3. Each project $e_i$ is purchased at time $\sqrt{k_i}$

We prove this while also showing that no other project is purchased before $H_0$.

Consider a state at time $h\leq H_0$ in which only projects among the $e_i$ have been purchased. Write
$$ P(h)=\sum_i\mu_{r_i}p_{r_i}. $$
The preliminary balance bound gives
$$ P(h)\leq ah. $$

For type $r_i$, its rival project $v_i$ contributes the local expression
$$ \frac{Q_v/\mu_{r_i}}{Q_v}\mu_{r_i}p_{r_i} = p_{r_i}\leq h. $$
Project $f$ contributes
$$ \frac{H_0}{Q_f}P(h) \leq \frac{aH_0}{Q_f}h <h. $$

If $e_i$ is unpurchased, neither $r_i$ nor $s_i$ has paid anything, so both balances equal $h$. Its utility-$1$ threshold contributes
$$ \frac{(\mu_{r_i}+\delta)h}{\delta k_i} = \frac{k_i+1}{2k_i}h <h. $$
For $s_i$, its utility-$k_i$ threshold contributes
$$ \frac{k_i\delta h}{\delta k_i}=h. $$

These are all relevant local expressions. Inductively, the global factors of the $r_i,s_i$ are at most $h$: initially they are zero, waiting increases $h$, and each purchase updates them to local maxima bounded by the current $h$.

For an unpurchased $e_i$, it follows that
$$ \lambda_{r_i}=\lambda_{s_i}=h. $$

If $0\leq h\leq1$, both caps equal $h$, and their weighted sum is
$$ \frac{\delta(k_i+1)}2h < \delta k_i=q(e_i). $$

For $1\leq h\leq\sqrt{k_i}$, the caps are
$$ x_{r_i}(e_i)=\frac{2h}{1+h}, \qquad x_{s_i}(e_i)=h. $$
The second equality holds because $h\leq\sqrt{k_i}<k_i$. Hence the weighted cap sum is
$$ F_i(h) = \delta h+\frac{\delta(k_i-1)h}{1+h}. $$
Subtracting the cost gives the exact identity
$$ F_i(h)-q(e_i) = \frac{\delta(h^2-k_i)}{1+h}. $$
Therefore $e_i$ first becomes affordable at
$$ h_i=\sqrt{k_i}. $$

No other project interferes before $H_0$:

  • A rival project has total offered payment at most

$$ \mu_{r_i}p_{r_i}\leq aH_0<Q_b<Q_v. $$

  • Project $f$ has total offered payment at most

$$ P(h)\leq aH_0<Q_f. $$

  • Type $t_d$ supports only $d$. Its local factor is $h/H_0$, and its cap is $h$ until $H_0$. Thus $d$ first becomes affordable at $H_0$.
  • Similarly, $b$ first becomes affordable at $H_1$.

Since every $\sqrt{k_i}<H_0$, induction over the purchase events proves that all the $e_i$ are purchased at their asserted times. If several times coincide, all corresponding projects are purchased at that time: purchasing one does not change the balances of another’s supporters, and all remaining projects remain feasible.

At the purchase of $e_i$, the weighted cap sum equals its cost. Thus both supporters pay their entire caps. Type $r_i$ pays the per-capita amount
$$ D_i=\frac{2\sqrt{k_i}}{1+\sqrt{k_i}}. $$
Its weighted payment is
$$ \mu_{r_i}D_i = \frac{\delta(k_i-1)}2 \frac{2\sqrt{k_i}}{1+\sqrt{k_i}} = \delta(k_i-\sqrt{k_i}). $$

After all these purchases, the total weighted payment by the $r_i$ types is therefore
$$ D=\delta\sum_i(k_i-\sqrt{k_i}). $$

3.4. Project $d$ removes all rivals at time $H_0$

After all the $e_i$ have been purchased, the remaining budget is
$$ Q_d+Q_b. $$
As shown above, $d$ first becomes affordable at $H_0$, while every rival and $f$ remains unaffordable there, and $b$ remains unaffordable until $H_1$.

Thus $d$ is purchased at $H_0$. The remaining budget becomes exactly $Q_b$.

Every rival is now infeasible because
$$ Q_v>Q_b. $$
The available set is precisely
$$ R=\{f,b\}. $$

Immediately before purchasing $d$, every rich type’s scaling factor is at most $H_0$, by the bounds in Section 3.3. Consequently, after the purchase,
$$ g_{r_i}\leq H_0 $$
for every $i$.

Until either $f$ or $b$ is purchased, the rich balances and their weighted sum are
$$ p_{r_i}(h)=h-D_i, \qquad P(h)=ah-D. $$

3.5. Comparing the final two purchase times

For every rich type, the only remaining project with positive utility is $f$, whose utility is $H_0$. Its local expression is common to all rich types:
$$ \frac{H_0P(h)}{Q_f}. $$

If $P(h)\leq Q_f$, then this expression and every rich global factor are at most $H_0$. Hence every rich cap for $f$ equals its entire balance, and the total offered payment is $P(h)$.

If $P(h)\geq Q_f$, the common local expression dominates both $H_0$ and all global factors. The total offered payment is then
$$ \frac{2P(h)}{1+P(h)/Q_f}. $$

Thus the affordability sum for $f$ is exactly
$$ F_f(h)= \begin{cases} P(h),&P(h)\leq Q_f,\\[2mm] \dfrac{2P(h)}{1+P(h)/Q_f},&P(h)\geq Q_f. \end{cases} $$
In both cases,
$$ F_f(h)\geq Q_f \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(h)\geq Q_f. $$

Since $P(h)=ah-D$ is strictly increasing, $f$ first becomes affordable at
$$ h_f=\frac{Q_f+D}{a}. $$
Substituting the definitions,
$$ h_f = H_1+\frac{\delta}{a} \left(K-\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}\right). $$
Also $h_f>H_0$, because $P(H_0)\leq aH_0<Q_f$.

Project $b$ first becomes affordable at $H_1$. Therefore:

  • If $\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}>K$, then $h_f<H_1$, and $f$ is purchased.
  • If $\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}<K$, then $H_1<h_f$, and $b$ is purchased.

It remains to check equality.

If $\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}=K$, both projects first become affordable at $H_1$. Their cap sums equal their costs, so purchasing either requires exhausting all its supporters’ caps.

For $b$, the least payment parameter is
$$ \rho_b=H_1. $$
For $f$, it is
$$ \rho_f = \max_i\frac{H_1-D_i}{H_0} < \frac{H_1}{H_0} < H_1. $$
Here $D_i>0$ and $H_0>1$. Consequently, $f$ wins the equality case by a strict payment-parameter comparison.

After either project is purchased, the other is infeasible: before this purchase the remaining budget is $Q_b$, whereas
$$ Q_b+Q_f>Q_b. $$
All other projects have already been purchased or removed.

We have therefore proved the exact equivalence
$$ \boxed{ f\in W \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_i\sqrt{k_i}\geq K. } $$

3.6. Encoding complexity

The construction has
$$ \tau=2n+2, \qquad m=2n+3. $$
All input data use rational sums, products, and quotients of the source integers and the displayed constants. Their bit lengths are polynomial in the source input length. The utility matrix has polynomially many entries.

Thus this is a polynomial-time reduction to the decision question “does the specified outcome contain $f$?” Exact computation of the whole outcome, and hence the combined task in the question, solves that comparison problem.

4. Proven: actual purchase times can have exponential algebraic degree

In the construction, choose $K=0$ and choose $k_1,\ldots,k_n$ to be distinct primes. Project $f$ is then purchased, and its actual purchase time is
$$ h_f = H_1-\frac{\delta}{a}\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}. $$
I prove that this number has degree $2^n$ over $\mathbb Q$.

4.1. The multiquadratic field has degree $2^n$

Let
$$ E_j=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{k_1},\ldots,\sqrt{k_j}). $$
We use the following elementary observation. If $c$ is not a square in a field $F$ of characteristic zero, and $b\in F$ is a square in $F(\sqrt c)$, write
$$ b=(x+y\sqrt c)^2. $$
Comparison of coefficients gives $2xy=0$. Thus either $b$ is a square in $F$, or $b/c$ is a square in $F$.

Inductively, this observation proves that the rational numbers becoming squares in $E_j$ have square classes generated by $k_1,\ldots,k_j$. No new prime $k_{j+1}$ has such a square class: its prime valuation is odd, while every product of previous primes has valuation zero at $k_{j+1}$.

Therefore $k_{j+1}$ is not a square in $E_j$, and
$$ [E_{j+1}:E_j]=2. $$
It follows that
$$ [E_n:\mathbb Q]=2^n. $$

The products
$$ \prod_{i\in I}\sqrt{k_i}, \qquad I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}, $$
form a basis. In particular, the individual $\sqrt{k_i}$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$.

4.2. The sum itself has degree $2^n$

Each independent choice of signs on the square roots defines a field automorphism. The resulting conjugates of their sum are
$$ \sum_i\varepsilon_i\sqrt{k_i}, \qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}. $$
These $2^n$ values are distinct: equality between two of them would give a nonzero rational linear relation among the individual square roots.

Consequently,
$$ \left[\mathbb Q\left(\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}\right):\mathbb Q\right] = 2^n. $$
The affine transformation defining $h_f$ has nonzero rational coefficient, so
$$ \mathbb Q(h_f) = \mathbb Q\left(\sum_i\sqrt{k_i}\right). $$
Hence
$$ \boxed{[\mathbb Q(h_f):\mathbb Q]=2^n.} $$

Taking the first $n$ primes gives source and constructed input lengths polynomial in $n$: Bertrand’s postulate bounds the $n$th prime by $2^n$.

Therefore a representation that explicitly lists a minimal polynomial’s coefficients has superpolynomial size on this family. This establishes a representation obstruction, rather than a complexity lower bound for the integral outcome.

5. Proven: the future-affordability predicate need not be monotone

A separate obstacle affects an uncomplicated use of binary search.

Take two types of mass $1/2$, budget $B=3$, and the following projects:

| Project | Cost | First type’s utility | Second type’s utility |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $e$ | $1/2$ | $0$ | $2$ |
| $c$ | $4/25$ | $1/4$ | $0$ |
| $d$ | $2$ | $4$ | $4$ |

Before the first purchase, both scaling factors equal $2h$. Project $e$ first becomes affordable at $h=1$. Project $d$ is unaffordable before then, and the offered payment for $c$ is at most $1/9<4/25$ up to that time. Thus $e$ is uniquely purchased first.

Immediately afterward,
$$ p_1=1,\qquad p_2=0,\qquad g_1=g_2=2, $$
and both $c,d$ remain available.

Now consider the hypothetical continuation with no intervening purchase, writing $z$ for additional waiting time. Direct evaluation of the local expressions gives
$$ \lambda_1=\lambda_2=\max\{2,1+2z\}. $$
The local expression from $c$ is
$$ \frac{25}{32}(1+z), $$
which never exceeds that maximum.

For $0\leq z\leq3/2$, the total offered payment for $d$ is
$$ F_d(z)=\frac12+z. $$
For $c$ it is
$$ F_c(z)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{1+z}{9},&0\leq z\leq1/2,\\[2mm] \dfrac{1+z}{5+8z},&z\geq1/2. \end{cases} $$
Solving $F_c(z)\geq4/25$ gives exactly
$$ \frac{11}{25}\leq z\leq\frac57. $$

Thus, on this continuation, some project is affordable at $z=1/2$, none is affordable at $z=1$, and $d$ becomes affordable at $z=3/2$.

The actual rule purchases $c$ at the earliest event, $z=11/25$. The counterexample concerns the predicate evaluated at hypothetical future waiting times from that reachable state. That predicate is not monotone, so binary search on it requires an additional argument or a different predicate.

6. What remains open

The universal submass condition is settled affirmatively for the specified outcome.

For exact outcome computation, the reduction above demonstrates that comparisons involving sums of independently generated square roots affect the integral selection itself. The exponential-degree example also shows why explicitly expanding algebraic states cannot supply a general polynomial-time proof.

Neither result establishes NP-hardness. A general polynomial-time algorithm using succinct exact representations, or a valid NP-hardness reduction, remains missing.
