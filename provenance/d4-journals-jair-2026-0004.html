The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and strictly positive normalized rational piecewise-constant densities \(f_{tj}\) on common cake cells \(I_j\) of lengths \(d_j\), decide whether there are masses \(r_{tj}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_j r_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t r_{tj}>0\), and \(\sum_t r_{tj}/f_{tj}<d_j\) for every cell \(j\). This asks whether a unit-mass population of repeated types can receive connected infinitesimal shares whose utility, scaled by population size, is strictly greater than the equal per-capita entitlement.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq 1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The cake \([0,1]\) is partitioned into \(m\geq 1\) consecutive cells \(I_j\) of positive binary-rational lengths \(d_j\), with \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_j=1\). Type \(t\) has constant binary-rational density \(f_{tj}>0\) on cell \(I_j\), normalized by \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_jf_{tj}=1\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
The decision problem asks whether real masses \(r_{tj}\geq 0\) exist satisfying \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{tj}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), and both \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}r_{tj}>0\) and \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}r_{tj}/f_{tj}<d_j\) for every cell \(j\). Types may split their mass among cells.

Is this mass feasibility problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many agents with normalized valuations positive on every interval of positive length and equal entitlements, deciding whether a partition into one interval per agent gives each agent value strictly greater than \(1/n\), and computing such a partition when it exists, require \(\Theta(n^2)\) Robertson–Webb queries. This finite-agent query bound does not classify the mass feasibility problem above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified that the common cells partition the unit interval and have positive binary-rational lengths summing to one, following the discrete cake domain.
  • Made density normalization explicit: each type values the whole cake at one.
  • Specified that the unknown allocation masses are real and that a type may split its mass among cells; retained both strict cell conditions from the graded question.
  • Defined m as the number of cells and L as the total binary input length, and required positive support masses.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, it has the following exact characterization:

Theorem. A feasible allocation exists if and only if the supported types do not all have the same density vector.

Thus feasibility can be decided by comparing every density vector with that of the first type. The proof also gives a rational feasible allocation whenever one exists.

Identical density vectors imply infeasibility

Suppose \(f_{tj}=f_j\) for every type \(t\) and cell \(j\). If a feasible allocation existed, the strict cell inequalities would give

\( \sum_t r_{tj}<d_jf_j \)

for every \(j\), since \(f_j>0\). Summing these finitely many strict inequalities yields

\( 1=\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_j\sum_t r_{tj}<\sum_jd_jf_j=1, \)

a contradiction.

In particular, instances with \(\tau=1\) or \(m=1\) are infeasible: when \(m=1\), normalization forces every density to equal \(1\).

Different density vectors imply feasibility

Choose types \(a,b\) with different density vectors, and write

\( A_j=f_{aj},\qquad B_j=f_{bj},\qquad \rho_j=B_j/A_j. \)

The ratios \(\rho_j\) cannot all equal one constant \(c\). Otherwise \(B_j=cA_j\) for every \(j\), and normalization would imply

\( 1=\sum_jd_jB_j=c\sum_jd_jA_j=c, \)

contradicting the choice of different density vectors. Consequently, there are cells \(p,q\) with \(\rho_p<\rho_q\). Set \(\Delta=\rho_q-\rho_p>0\).

A positive allocation saturating every cell

Define the initial allocation by

\( r^0_{tj}=\mu_t d_j f_{tj}. \)

Every entry is positive. Normalization gives the required type totals:

\( \sum_jr^0_{tj}=\mu_t\sum_jd_jf_{tj}=\mu_t. \)

For every cell, its initial load is exactly its length:

\( \sum_t\frac{r^0_{tj}}{f_{tj}}=d_j\sum_t\mu_t=d_j. \)

We will perturb this allocation while preserving all type totals and reducing every cell load.

A perturbation reducing every cell load

Define \(x_p=1\), \(x_q=-1\), and \(x_j=0\) for all other cells. Define

\( y_j=-\rho_jx_j-\Delta d_jB_j. \)

Both perturbation vectors have zero sum. For \(x\) this follows directly from its definition. For \(y\), normalization of type \(b\) gives

\( \sum_jy_j=-(\rho_p-\rho_q)-\Delta\sum_jd_jB_j=\Delta-\Delta=0. \)

Moreover, in each cell,

\( \frac{x_j}{A_j}+\frac{y_j}{B_j} =\frac{x_j}{A_j}-\frac{\rho_jx_j}{B_j}-\Delta d_j =-\Delta d_j, \)

where the cancellation uses \(\rho_j=B_j/A_j\).

Thus adding a positive multiple of \(x\) to type \(a\)'s allocation and the same multiple of \(y\) to type \(b\)'s allocation preserves their total masses and strictly reduces every cell load.

Choosing the perturbation size

To retain positivity, choose the positive rational number

\( \eta=\min\left\{1,\ \min_j\frac{r^0_{aj}}{2(1+|x_j|)},\ \min_j\frac{r^0_{bj}}{2(1+|y_j|)}\right\}. \)

Set

\( r_{aj}=r^0_{aj}+\eta x_j,\qquad r_{bj}=r^0_{bj}+\eta y_j, \)

and leave every other type's allocation unchanged.

For every cell,

\( |\eta x_j| \leq\frac{r^0_{aj}|x_j|}{2(1+|x_j|)} <\frac{r^0_{aj}}2, \)

and similarly \( |\eta y_j|<r^0_{bj}/2 \). Hence every resulting entry \(r_{tj}\) remains strictly positive. In particular, every cell receives positive total mass.

The zero sums of \(x\) and \(y\) preserve all type totals. Finally, the cell loads satisfy

\( \sum_t\frac{r_{tj}}{f_{tj}} =d_j+\eta\left(\frac{x_j}{A_j}+\frac{y_j}{B_j}\right) =d_j-\eta\Delta d_j<d_j, \)

because \(\eta,\Delta,d_j\) are all positive. This verifies every feasibility requirement.

Algorithm and binary complexity

For decision, compare \(f_{tj}\) with \(f_{1j}\) for every \(t,j\). Return infeasible if all comparisons are equal, and feasible otherwise. This requires at most \(m(\tau-1)\) exact rational comparisons, each taking polynomial time in \(L\).

For construction, choose a differing pair of types and apply the formulas above. They use polynomially many rational arithmetic operations and comparisons. Each computed quantity has polynomial binary length: its formula uses a bounded number of arithmetic operations on input rationals, and taking a minimum selects an existing candidate without enlarging its representation. The resulting rational allocation therefore has polynomial output length and is computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

This conclusion follows directly from the mass constraints; the finite-agent Robertson–Webb query bound is not needed.
