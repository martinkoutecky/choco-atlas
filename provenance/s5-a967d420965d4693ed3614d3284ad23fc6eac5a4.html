The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\) of asylum-seeker types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type records a complete ranking over \(M\times W\), a priority class, and common burden-size \(1\), and given member-state quotas and wait-time capacities scaled per capita, can a type-mass version of cumulative offers compute a stable allocation \(x_{t,m,w}\ge 0\)? Require \(\sum_{m,w}x_{t,m,w}=\mu_t\), respect the scaled capacities and quotas, and define stability to exclude any positive mass of a type that prefers a contract its member state would accept under the mass choice rule.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(M\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) states and \(W\subset\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\) a nonempty finite set of wait times. Each type has burden-size \(1\), a strict complete preference order \(P_t\) over \(M\times W\), and a priority class for each state; preferences need not favor shorter waits.
The input gives nonnegative binary-rational quotas \(q_j\) and capacities \(r_j^w\), satisfying \(\sum_jq_j\geq1\) and \(\sum_wr_j^w\geq\max\{q_j,1\}\) for every state \(j\). Write \(L\) for the total input bit length.
To preserve identities across repeated offers, realize type \(t\) as the interval \(I_t=[0,\mu_t)\) with Lebesgue mass. Each state's strict priority orders individuals first by its priority class, then by the input index of their type, then by increasing interval coordinate.
Offers and assignments are finite unions of intervals with rational endpoints. For an offer set \(O\), state choice \(C_j(O)\) scans individuals in priority order, accepting each at her shortest offered wait with residual capacity, accepting at most one contract per individual, and stopping at accepted mass \(q_j\). Split interval portions whenever a capacity or quota is reached. Quotas are choice stopping thresholds, not required minimum loads.
An assignment \(Y\) gives almost every individual exactly one contract; its aggregate masses satisfy \(x_{t,j,w}\geq0\), \(\sum_{j,w}x_{t,j,w}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_tx_{t,j,w}\leq r_j^w\), and \(\sum_{t,w}x_{t,j,w}\leq q_j\).
Call \(Y\) stable if there are no type \(t\), contract \((j,w)\), and positive-mass interval union \(B\subseteq I_t\) whose individuals strictly prefer \((j,w)\) to their assignments, such that \(C_j(Y_j\cup\{(a,j,w):a\in B\})\) accepts positive mass of those new contracts. Existing contracts of those individuals remain offered in this test.
A mass cumulative-offer mechanism starts with no offers, lets unheld portions offer their best contracts not previously offered, retains every past offer, and recomputes state choices; it terminates when no unheld portion has an untried contract.
Does every instance admit a stable allocation, and is finding one polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,|W|,L\), or NP-hard? Can a mass cumulative-offer mechanism always compute such an allocation within that polynomial-time bound?
For finite societies with identical burden-sizes, cumulative offers under this state choice rule are stable and strategy-proof. [Caspari–Khanna](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21147v1)
Stable and fair fractional allocations exist in two-sided markets with discrete-concave valuations; that result uses a different choice model and does not establish the guarantee asked here. Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize total mass to one and interpret quotas and capacities in these per-capita units.
  • Carry over both capacity conditions in the discrete model: each state's total capacity is at least its quota and at least the entire society's mass.
  • Use nonempty state and wait-time sets, nonnegative rational wait times, quotas, and capacities, and strict complete preferences without imposing monotonicity in wait time.
  • Interpret priority information as state-specific classes; break ties by input type index and then by a fixed coordinate within each type's mass.
  • Preserve individual identities using intervals, and use finite rational interval unions to give offers and assignments finite computational representations.
  • Extend state choice by processing divisible portions in priority order, choosing the earliest available offered wait, and splitting at capacity and quota boundaries.
  • Treat quotas as stopping thresholds rather than minimum assignment requirements; divisible mass stops exactly at the threshold.
  • Define positive-mass blocking by augmenting existing state contracts, retaining a deviator's existing contract when testing another contract at the same state, and requiring positive acceptance of the new offers.
  • Specify cumulative offers as retaining all past offers and allowing only currently unheld portions to make their next untried offer.

Stable allocations are polynomial-time computable, but literal cumulative offers can take exponential time

Every instance admits a stable allocation, and one can be computed in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|W|,L\).

The cumulative-offer process specified in the question has no polynomial worst-case bound on its number of offer/recomputation steps. This remains true when eligible offers are batched: a family with \(\tau=2\), \(m=3\), and \(|W|=1\) requires exponentially many steps under every finitely terminating legal batching schedule.

Here, a step means adding eligible new offers and then recomputing choices. The lower bound concerns executing these steps; it does not exclude computing an eventual outcome through an algorithm that compresses many steps.

1. The ordered-polymatroid theorem used

For a normalized, monotone, submodular function \(g:2^E\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\), write

\(P(g)=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^E:x(A)\leq g(A)\text{ for every }A\subseteq E\}\).

Fix a strict total order \(\succ\) on \(E\). Say that \(e\) is dominated at \(x\in P(g)\) if:

  • No positive increase of \(x_e\) alone is feasible.
  • For every \(f\) with \(e\succ f\), no positive exchange \(x+\alpha(\mathbf1_e-\mathbf1_f)\) is feasible.

The ordered-polymatroid stable-allocation theorem of Iwata and Yokoi finds a common feasible vector for two ordered polymatroids such that every coordinate is dominated on at least one side. Its running time is \(O(|E|^3\gamma)\), where \(\gamma\) is the time for computing saturation and exchange capacities. Integer-valued rank functions yield an integral output. See Definition 1.1, the algorithm in Section 3, and Theorem 6.6 of their [technical report](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf).

We will construct such an auxiliary problem, verify its hypotheses, and prove that its solution yields stability for the choice rule in the question.

2. Constructing the auxiliary problem

Let

\(E=\{(t,j,w):t\in[\tau],\,j\in M,\,w\in W\}\),

and put \(n=|E|=\tau m|W|\). Define the sets

\(E_t=\{(t,j,w):j\in M,\ w\in W\}\),

\(E_j=\{(t,j,w):t\in[\tau],\ w\in W\}\),

and

\(E_{j,w}=\{(t,j,w):t\in[\tau]\}\).

The first feasible region imposes the type supplies:

\(P_H=\{x\geq0:x(E_t)\leq\mu_t\text{ for every }t\}\).

The second imposes state quotas and wait capacities:

\(P_F=\{x\geq0:x(E_j)\leq q_j,\ x(E_{j,w})\leq r_j^w\text{ for every }j,w\}\).

2.1 Both regions are polymatroids

Define

\(h(A)=\sum_{t:A\cap E_t\neq\varnothing}\mu_t\).

This is a nonnegative weighted coverage function, so it is normalized, monotone, and submodular. Moreover, \(P(h)=P_H\): the inequalities for \(A=E_t\) give the type constraints, and those constraints imply every other rank inequality.

For the state side, define

\(f(A)=\sum_j\min\left\{q_j,\ \sum_{w:A\cap E_{j,w}\neq\varnothing}r_j^w\right\}\).

This function is also normalized and monotone. To check submodularity, fix a state \(j\). Adding an element in a wait class already represented in a set has zero marginal contribution. Adding an element in an unrepresented wait class has marginal contribution

\(\min\{r_j^w,\,(q_j-s)_+\}\),

where \(s\) is the sum of capacities of the represented wait classes. This marginal contribution cannot increase when the underlying set grows. Thus each summand is submodular, and so is \(f\).

We also have \(P(f)=P_F\). Indeed, since \(\sum_w r_j^w\geq q_j\),

\(f(E_j)=q_j,\qquad f(E_{j,w})=\min\{q_j,r_j^w\}\).

Consequently, the rank inequalities imply all quota and capacity constraints. Conversely, if those constraints hold, then for every \(A\subseteq E\),

\(x(A\cap E_j)\leq\min\left\{q_j,\ \sum_{w:A\cap E_{j,w}\neq\varnothing}r_j^w\right\}\).

Summing over states gives \(x(A)\leq f(A)\).

2.2 The auxiliary orders

On the type side, order each \(E_t\) according to \(P_t\), and concatenate the type blocks in any fixed order. Call the resulting total order \(\succ_H\).

On the state side, concatenate the state blocks in any fixed order. Within \(E_j\):

  • Order types according to state \(j\)'s priority classes and then their input indices.
  • Within one type, order its contracts according to \(P_t\), restricted to state \(j\).

Call this total order \(\succ_F\).

The within-type state order is an auxiliary construction. We do not identify it with the state's shortest-wait choice rule. The interval construction below establishes the required connection.

Apply the ordered-polymatroid theorem to obtain \(x\in P_H\cap P_F\) such that every \(e\in E\) is dominated on at least one side.

3. Characterizing domination in this construction

Write

\(H_t=x(E_t),\qquad Q_j=x(E_j),\qquad R_{j,w}=x(E_{j,w})\).

3.1 Type-side domination

For \(e=(t,j,w)\), type-side domination is equivalent to:

  • \(H_t=\mu_t\); and
  • every contract of type \(t\) with positive assigned mass is weakly preferred to \((j,w)\) under \(P_t\).

If \(H_t<\mu_t\), increasing \(x_e\) is feasible. If \(H_t=\mu_t\), an increase can be obtained by decreasing any positive coordinate of the same type. Thus domination holds exactly when no such coordinate is worse than \(e\).

3.2 State-side domination gives a protected tight constraint

If \(e=(t,j,w)\) is state-side dominated, at least one of the following holds:

  • Wait-capacity protection: \(R_{j,w}=r_j^w\), and every positive coordinate in \(E_{j,w}\) precedes or equals \(e\) in \(\succ_F\).
  • Quota protection: \(Q_j=q_j\), and every positive coordinate in \(E_j\) precedes or equals \(e\) in \(\succ_F\).

Here is the complete case analysis.

If both \(R_{j,w}<r_j^w\) and \(Q_j<q_j\), then a small increase of \(x_e\) is feasible, contradicting domination.

If \(R_{j,w}=r_j^w\), decreasing any positive coordinate in the same wait class permits a small increase of \(x_e\), while preserving the state total. Hence every such coordinate must precede or equal \(e\). This gives wait-capacity protection.

The remaining case has \(R_{j,w}<r_j^w\) and \(Q_j=q_j\). Decreasing any positive coordinate at state \(j\) permits a sufficiently small increase of \(x_e\): the state total is preserved, and the target wait class has positive slack. Therefore every positive coordinate at that state must precede or equal \(e\). This gives quota protection.

4. The auxiliary allocation assigns the entire population

Suppose some type is not fully assigned. Then \(H_t<\mu_t\) for some \(t\), and therefore \(x(E)<1\).

Every coordinate \((t,j,w)\) of this type fails type-side domination, so it must be state-side dominated. In particular, for every \(j,w\), at least one of

\(Q_j=q_j\quad\text{or}\quad R_{j,w}=r_j^w\)

holds.

If \(Q_j<q_j\) for some state, all its wait capacities must consequently be tight. But then

\(Q_j=\sum_wR_{j,w}=\sum_wr_j^w\geq q_j\),

a contradiction. Thus every state quota is tight, giving

\(x(E)=\sum_jq_j\geq1\),

again a contradiction.

Hence \(x(E)=1\). Since \(H_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), every type satisfies \(H_t=\mu_t\).

5. Lifting the auxiliary allocation to stable intervals

For each type \(t\), list its contracts in preference order:

\(e_{t,1}\succ_t e_{t,2}\succ_t\cdots\succ_t e_{t,m|W|}\).

Partition \(I_t=[0,\mu_t)\) into consecutive intervals of lengths \(x_{e_{t,1}},x_{e_{t,2}},\ldots\), assigning each interval its corresponding contract. Ignore empty intervals.

Call this assignment \(Y\). It is feasible, assigns almost every individual exactly once, and gives earlier coordinates of each type weakly better contracts.

We now prove its stability.

5.1 Feasible assignments are accepted from their own offers

For any feasible assignment \(Y\), we have \(C_j(Y_j)=Y_j\), up to null sets.

There is only one offered contract per individual. During the priority scan, the accepted mass in a wait class never exceeds its total assigned mass, which is at most its capacity. Likewise, the accepted total never exceeds the assigned state load, which is at most its quota. Consequently, no positive assigned portion is rejected.

5.2 Every potential block meets a protected constraint

Suppose a positive-mass set \(B\subseteq I_t\) strictly prefers \(e=(t,j,w)\) to its assignments.

Because \(B\) has positive mass, some positive assigned mass of type \(t\) is on a contract worse than \(e\). By Section 3.1, \(e\) is not type-side dominated. It is therefore state-side dominated.

Define

\(b_t(e)=\sum_{f\in E_t:\,f\succeq_t e}x_f\).

By our interval construction, all type-\(t\) individuals assigned contracts weakly preferred to \(e\) lie in \([0,b_t(e))\). Every individual who strictly prefers \(e\) to her assignment lies in \([b_t(e),\mu_t)\), apart from irrelevant endpoints.

Consider the protected tight constraint supplied by Section 3.2. Every individual supplying its assigned mass has higher priority at state \(j\) than every individual of \(B\):

  • A contract of a different type preceding \(e\) belongs to a type with higher state priority.
  • A contract of type \(t\) preceding or equaling \(e\) is weakly preferred to \(e\), so its assigned individuals have coordinates below \(b_t(e)\).

None of these protected individuals belongs to \(B\). Hence, in the test

\(C_j(Y_j\cup\{(a,j,w):a\in B\})\),

their offers remain unchanged and they are scanned before all individuals of \(B\). The scan accepts their original contracts, as in Section 5.1.

If the protected constraint is the wait capacity, these individuals fill \(r_j^w\) before any individual of \(B\) is reached. If it is the state quota, they fill \(q_j\) before any individual of \(B\) is reached.

In either case, no positive mass of new contracts from \(B\) is accepted. This contradicts the proposed block.

Thus \(Y\) is stable under precisely the blocking test in the question, including the retention of each deviator's existing contract.

6. Polynomial running time and output size

The auxiliary ground set has \(n=\tau m|W|\) elements.

For either feasible region, saturation and exchange capacities are easy to compute. More generally, suppose a region is defined by nonnegativity and upper constraints \(x(A)\leq b_A\). Then

\(\widehat c(x,e)=\min_{A:\,e\in A}(b_A-x(A))\).

For distinct coordinates \(e,f\),

\(\overline c(x,e,f)=\min\left\{x_f,\ \min_{A:\,e\in A,\ f\notin A}(b_A-x(A))\right\}\),

where the inner minimum over an empty family is \(+\infty\).

These formulas follow directly: increasing \(e\) tightens exactly the constraints containing \(e\); exchanging \(f\) for \(e\) tightens exactly those containing \(e\) but not \(f\), while nonnegativity requires the exchange amount to be at most \(x_f\).

Our type region has \(\tau\) upper constraints, and the state region has \(m+m|W|\). All constraint sums can be maintained, so each oracle evaluation takes \(O(n)\) arithmetic operations. The cited algorithm therefore uses \(O(n^4)\) arithmetic operations.

To obtain a binary running-time guarantee, take a common denominator \(D\) of all masses, quotas, and capacities. For example, their denominators' product suffices and has \(O(L)\) bits. Scale these quantities by \(D\), and run the integral version of the algorithm without creating individual clones.

Its updates use additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of integral saturation and exchange capacities. Every allocation coordinate is bounded by the total supply \(D\); input bounds and residuals have polynomial bit length. Thus the arithmetic has polynomial bit cost.

After dividing by \(D\), the output masses and interval endpoints are rational numbers of polynomial bit length. The interval assignment contains at most \(n\) nonempty intervals.

This proves polynomial-time solvability in the required parameters.

7. Exponentially many cumulative-offer steps

We now give a lower bound that permits arbitrary batching of eligible offers.

7.1 The instance family

For an integer \(k\geq1\), put

\(\varepsilon=2^{-(k+1)},\qquad c=\frac12-\varepsilon\).

There are two types, \(a,b\), each of mass \(\frac12\), three states \(X,Y,Z\), and one wait time, \(W=\{0\}\).

Set

\(q_X=q_Y=c,\qquad q_Z=2\varepsilon\),

and set every wait capacity to \(1\). Thus the quotas sum to \(1\), and all required capacity hypotheses hold.

Suppressing the common wait time, preferences are

\(P_a:X\succ Y\succ Z,\qquad P_b:Y\succ X\succ Z\).

State priorities rank:

  • \(b\) above \(a\) at \(X\);
  • \(a\) above \(b\) at \(Y\);
  • either type first at \(Z\).

Coordinates break priorities within each type as specified in the question.

The dimensions are fixed, and the input length is \(L=\Theta(k)\).

7.2 Permanent rejection in this special case

With one wait time and capacity \(1\), each state chooses its highest-priority offered mass up to its quota. Adding offers cannot restore a previously rejected individual: her higher-priority offered competition can only grow.

Consequently, throughout any legal cumulative-offer execution, an individual holds at most one contract. Previously rejected contracts never become held again, and new offers come only from unheld individuals.

7.3 Each recomputation can introduce only a small amount of second-choice offers

At any point, let:

  • \(F_a\) be the cumulative mass of type \(a\) that has offered to \(X\);
  • \(S_a\) be the cumulative mass of type \(a\) that has offered to \(Y\);
  • \(F_b\) be the cumulative mass of type \(b\) that has offered to \(Y\);
  • \(S_b\) be the cumulative mass of type \(b\) that has offered to \(X\).

These are masses of distinct individuals who have made the indicated offers.

At \(X\), type \(b\) has higher priority, so the chosen type-\(b\) mass is \(\min\{S_b,c\}\). The cumulative rejected mass of type-\(a\) first-choice offers is therefore

\(R_a=\max\{0,F_a+\min\{S_b,c\}-c\}\).

Since \(F_a\leq\frac12=c+\varepsilon\),

\(R_a\leq\varepsilon+\min\{S_b,c\}\).

Every individual counted in \(S_a\) previously had her first-choice offer rejected. Permanent rejection therefore gives \(S_a\leq R_a\).

Immediately before an offer/recomputation step, the mass eligible to make a new type-\(a\) second-choice offer is at most \(R_a-S_a\). Thus

\(\Delta S_a\leq\varepsilon+\min\{S_b,c\}-S_a\).

The symmetric argument gives

\(\Delta S_b\leq\varepsilon+\min\{S_a,c\}-S_b\).

These bounds use the choices before the step, so they also apply when both types' eligible offers are batched together. Summing yields

\(\Delta S_a+\Delta S_b\leq2\varepsilon\),

because \(\min\{S_a,c\}-S_a\leq0\) and \(\min\{S_b,c\}-S_b\leq0\).

This bound concerns new second-choice offers. A step may also contain first- or third-choice offers; those do not increase \(S_a+S_b\).

7.4 Termination requires all individuals to have offered their second choice

First, any terminating execution assigns the entire population.

Otherwise, some positive unheld mass has exhausted all three contracts. If any state had load below its quota, that state would accept every offered individual: its wait capacity is \(1\), and its offered population has mass at most \(1\). Exhausted unheld individuals have offered to every state, so every state would have to be full.

But the quotas sum to \(1\), and holdings are disjoint. Full states would leave no positive unheld mass, a contradiction.

Therefore all states are full at termination, including \(Z\), whose load is \(2\varepsilon\).

An individual offers to \(Z\) only after rejection of her second-choice offer. Type \(a\) is the higher-priority type at her second-choice state \(Y\), so her rejected second-choice mass is exactly

\((S_a-c)_+\).

Similarly, type \(b\)'s rejected second-choice mass is \((S_b-c)_+\). Hence, at termination,

\(2\varepsilon\leq(S_a-c)_++(S_b-c)_+\leq2\varepsilon\),

where the last inequality follows from \(S_a,S_b\leq\frac12=c+\varepsilon\).

Equality forces

\(S_a=S_b=\frac12\).

Thus \(S_a+S_b\) starts at \(0\), must reach \(1\), and increases by at most \(2\varepsilon\) per step. Every finitely terminating legal batching schedule consequently requires at least

\(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}=2^k=2^{\Omega(L)}\)

offer/recomputation steps.

7.5 The instance nevertheless has a simple stable allocation

Assign type \(a\)'s interval \([0,c)\) to \(Y\) and its remaining interval to \(Z\). Assign type \(b\)'s interval \([0,c)\) to \(X\) and its remaining interval to \(Z\).

Type \(a\) cannot block at \(X\), which is filled by the higher-priority type \(b\). Its individuals at \(Z\) cannot block at \(Y\), which is filled by earlier coordinates of type \(a\). The symmetric statements hold for type \(b\).

Thus the exponential lower bound concerns the literal offer process despite the existence of a compact stable answer.
