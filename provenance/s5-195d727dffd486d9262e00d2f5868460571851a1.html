1. Result

The support-encoded problem is polynomial-time solvable: for every fixed rational $b\ge 0$, an additive $\epsilon$-maximizer can be computed in time
$$ \operatorname{poly}\bigl(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$
This holds without any connectivity assumption.

For the statistical question, define
$$ \alpha_b=\frac{e^{2b}}{(1+e^{2b})^2}. $$
Under the stated sampling assumptions and $\gamma_\mu>0$, with high probability, every additive $\epsilon$-maximizer satisfies
$$ \boxed{ \|\widehat\theta-\theta^*\|_2 \le \frac{2(1+e^{2b})^2}{\gamma_\mu} \sqrt{\frac{\bar k\log n}{N}} + \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon}{\alpha_b\gamma_\mu}}. } $$
The second term is a deterministic consequence of the objective gap. The first term is the given exact-maximizer guarantee.

If $b=0$, the feasible set consists of the zero vector, so computation and estimation are trivial. The proof below treats $b>0$.

2. Convexity, curvature, and finite-precision evaluation

Write $F=-Q_\mu$, and define
$$ \ell(t)=\log(1+e^t). $$
For distinct items, let
$$ a_{ij} = \sum_{\substack{S,\sigma:\ i,j\in S\\ i\text{ precedes }j}} \frac{\mu_{S,\sigma}}{|S|-1}. $$
Then $a_{ij}+a_{ji}=w_{ij}$ and
$$ F(\theta) = \sum_{i<j} \left[ a_{ij}\ell(\theta_j-\theta_i) + a_{ji}\ell(\theta_i-\theta_j) \right]. $$
These rational coefficients can be computed directly from the support using $O(\tau n^2)$ pair occurrences. Their encoding lengths remain polynomial in the input length.

The derivatives are
$$ \ell'(t)=\frac{e^t}{1+e^t}, \qquad \ell''(t)=\frac{e^t}{(1+e^t)^2}. $$
In particular, $F$ is convex. Since $\ell''$ is even, its Hessian is
$$ \nabla^2F(\theta) = \sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\ell''(\theta_i-\theta_j) (e_i-e_j)(e_i-e_j)^\mathsf T. $$
For $\theta\in\Theta_b$, every coordinate difference belongs to $[-2b,2b]$. On this interval,
$$ \alpha_b\le \ell''(t)\le\frac14. $$
Consequently, in the positive-semidefinite order,
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_b\mathcal L_\mu \preceq \nabla^2F(\theta) \preceq \frac14\mathcal L_\mu. } $$

We also need a gradient bound independent of small masses. Put
$$ d_i=\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}. $$
A panel containing $i$ contributes its mass to this sum, because its $|S|-1$ incident pairs each have weight $\mu_{S,\sigma}/(|S|-1)$. Thus
$$ d_i=\sum_{S,\sigma:\ i\in S}\mu_{S,\sigma}\le1. $$
Each pairwise derivative has magnitude at most its coefficient, giving
$$ |\partial_iF(\theta)|\le d_i\le1. $$

Finally, at any rational feasible point, both $F$ and its gradient admit rational approximations to absolute accuracy $2^{-p}$ in time polynomial in the point’s encoding length, $n,\tau,L,p$, with constants depending on fixed $b$. Here is an explicit justification.

All exponential arguments belong to the fixed interval $[-2b,2b]$. Taylor truncation gives
$$ \left| e^t-\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{t^j}{j!} \right| \le e^{2b}\frac{(2b)^{J+1}}{(J+1)!}. $$
Taking $J=O_b(p+1)$ makes this at most $2^{-p}$. All terms are rational with polynomial encoding length.

For positive rational $v$ in a fixed bounded interval containing the possible values of $1+e^t$, use
$$ \log v = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{q^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad q=\frac{v-1}{v+1}. $$
The interval can be chosen with $|q|\le q_b<1$, so $O_b(p+1)$ terms suffice. Perturbing the exponential approximation causes only a bounded additional error in the logarithm and logistic derivative. Computing individual terms with $O(p+\log n)$ precision controls their weighted sums: the total objective coefficient is
$$ \sum_{S,\sigma} \frac{\mu_{S,\sigma}}{|S|-1} \binom{|S|}{2} =\frac{\bar k}{2}\le\frac n2, $$
and each coordinate’s incident coefficient is at most one.

Thus no exact comparison of transcendental values is required.

3. Polynomial bit complexity

Concavity alone does not establish the requested dependence on $\log(1/\epsilon)$. We give an ellipsoid argument with explicit finite-precision margins.

3.1. A full-dimensional rational domain

Let $d=n-1$ and parameterize the zero-sum subspace by
$$ T(x_1,\ldots,x_d) = \left(x_1,\ldots,x_d,-\sum_{i=1}^d x_i\right). $$
Define
$$ K= \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^d: |x_i|\le b\text{ for all }i,\ \left|\sum_{i=1}^d x_i\right|\le b \right\}, \qquad f(x)=F(Tx). $$
Membership in $K$ and separation from $K$ use exact rational linear inequalities.

Set
$$ r_0=\frac bn, \qquad R=n\max\{1,b\}. $$
Then
$$ B(0,r_0)\subseteq K\subseteq B(0,R). $$
Indeed, if $\|x\|_2\le b/n$, then each coordinate has magnitude at most $b$, and
$$ \left|\sum_{i=1}^d x_i\right| \le\sqrt d\,\|x\|_2\le b. $$
The outer containment follows from the coordinate bounds.

By the chain rule,
$$ \partial_i f(x) = \partial_iF(Tx)-\partial_nF(Tx). $$
Hence $|\partial_i f|\le2$, and we may use the rational Lipschitz bound
$$ \|\nabla f(x)\|_2\le G, \qquad G=2n. $$

3.2. The ellipsoid fact used

We use the standard rational central-cut ellipsoid theorem in its following form. Starting from $B(0,R)$ in dimension $d\ge2$, successive nonzero rational cuts through the current rational center can be implemented with rational rounding so that:

  • the next ellipsoid contains the retained half of the current ellipsoid;
  • after $T$ cuts its volume is at most the initial volume times $\exp(-T/(Cd))$, for an absolute constant $C$;
  • arithmetic and encoding lengths are polynomial in $d,T$, the encoding of $R$, and the maximum encoding length of the cut normals.

Rounding with a small outward enlargement preserves containment while retaining the volume decrease. This is the numerical form of the classical ellipsoid method; see the [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver treatment](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1988.pdf). In dimension one, rational interval halving supplies the same needed conclusion.

3.3. A ball of near-optimal points

Compactness of $K$ and continuity of $f$ give a minimizer $x^*$; write $f^*=f(x^*)$. Define
$$ t=\frac{\epsilon}{16GR}, \qquad z=(1-t)x^*, \qquad r=tr_0. $$
Here $0<t<1$. By convexity of $K$,
$$ B(z,r)\subseteq K: $$
every point of this ball has the form $(1-t)x^*+tu$ with $u\in B(0,r_0)$.

For every $y\in B(z,r)$,
$$ \|y-x^*\|_2 \le t(R+r_0)\le2tR. $$
The Lipschitz bound therefore gives
$$ f(y)\le f^*+2GtR=f^*+\frac{\epsilon}{8}. $$
Thus there is a ball of known radius $r$, although its center is unknown, consisting entirely of near-optimal feasible points.

3.4. Rational cuts and termination

At each ellipsoid center $c$:

  • If $c\notin K$, choose a violated defining inequality and use its normal for a central cut. The retained half-space contains $K$.
  • If $c\in K$, record it and compute a rational vector $g$ satisfying

$$ \|g-\nabla f(c)\|_2\le\frac{\epsilon}{32R}. $$
If $g\ne0$, retain the half-space
$$ g^\mathsf T(x-c)\le0. $$

The gradient accuracy is achieved by approximating each coordinate within $\epsilon/(32Rn)$. The output can be rounded to a common dyadic denominator using $O(\log(Rn/\epsilon))$ bits, so cut normals have polynomial encoding length.

Suppose a feasible center has
$$ f(c)>f^*+\frac{\epsilon}{2}. $$
For any $y\in B(z,r)$, convexity and $\|y-c\|_2\le2R$ imply
$$ \begin{aligned} g^\mathsf T(y-c) &\le \nabla f(c)^\mathsf T(y-c) +\frac{\epsilon}{32R}\|y-c\|_2\\ &\le f(y)-f(c)+\frac{\epsilon}{16}\\ &< \frac{\epsilon}{8}-\frac{\epsilon}{2} +\frac{\epsilon}{16} = -\frac{5\epsilon}{16}. \end{aligned} $$
Therefore every cut made at such a center preserves the entire ball $B(z,r)$.

If $g=0$, then $\|\nabla f(c)\|_2\le\epsilon/(32R)$, and convexity gives
$$ f(c)-f^* \le \nabla f(c)^\mathsf T(c-x^*) \le\frac{\epsilon}{16}. $$
In this case we can immediately return $c$.

Otherwise, perform sufficiently many cuts that
$$ T=O\left(d^2\left(1+\log\frac Rr\right)\right) $$
and the final ellipsoid volume is smaller than $\operatorname{vol}(B(z,r))$. If every recorded feasible center had gap greater than $\epsilon/2$, all cuts would preserve $B(z,r)$, contradicting that volume bound. Hence at least one recorded feasible center has gap at most $\epsilon/2$.

Evaluate $f$ at the recorded feasible centers to absolute accuracy $\epsilon/8$, and return the one with smallest approximate value. Its true objective gap is at most
$$ \frac{\epsilon}{2}+2\frac{\epsilon}{8} =\frac{3\epsilon}{4}<\epsilon. $$
Mapping it through $T$ gives a rational point satisfying all constraints exactly.

For fixed $b>0$,
$$ \log\frac Rr = O_b\bigl(1+\log n+\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$
The iteration count, cut encodings, evaluation costs, and output encoding length are therefore polynomial in the requested parameters. Neither $N$ respondents nor all possible types are expanded. This proves the computational claim.

4. Deterministic control from the optimization gap

Let $\theta^0$ be an exact maximizer of $Q_\mu$, equivalently a minimizer of $F$, over $\Theta_b$.

Integrating the Hessian lower bound along the feasible segment from $x$ to $y$ gives, for all $x,y\in\Theta_b$,
$$ F(y) \ge F(x)+\nabla F(x)^\mathsf T(y-x) +\frac{\alpha_b}{2} (y-x)^\mathsf T\mathcal L_\mu(y-x). $$
Optimality of $\theta^0$ on the convex feasible set implies
$$ \nabla F(\theta^0)^\mathsf T(\theta-\theta^0)\ge0 \qquad \text{for every }\theta\in\Theta_b. $$
This follows by differentiating $F$ at the initial endpoint of the feasible segment toward $\theta$.

An additive $\epsilon$-maximizer satisfies
$$ F(\widehat\theta)-F(\theta^0)\le\epsilon. $$
Combining these facts proves
$$ \boxed{ (\widehat\theta-\theta^0)^\mathsf T \mathcal L_\mu (\widehat\theta-\theta^0) \le\frac{2\epsilon}{\alpha_b}. } $$

When $\gamma_\mu>0$, the Laplacian is positive definite on the zero-sum subspace. In particular, $\theta^0$ is unique. Since $\widehat\theta-\theta^0$ belongs to that subspace,
$$ \boxed{ \|\widehat\theta-\theta^0\|_2 \le \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon}{\alpha_b\gamma_\mu}}. } $$
These statements are deterministic and hold simultaneously for every additive $\epsilon$-maximizer.

5. Statistical guarantee and representation identification

Write $k_j=|S_j|$. For the empirical type frequencies,
$$ NQ_\mu(\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{1}{k_j-1} \sum_{r<s} \left[ \theta_{\sigma_j(r)} -\log\left(e^{\theta_{\sigma_j(r)}}+ e^{\theta_{\sigma_j(s)}}\right) \right]. $$
This is exactly the full rank-breaking objective for the explicit respondent list. Moreover,
$$ N\bar k=\sum_{j=1}^N k_j, \qquad Nw_{i\ell} = \sum_{j:\ i,\ell\in S_j}\frac{1}{k_j-1}. $$
Thus the list’s comparison-graph Laplacian is $N\mathcal L_\mu$. Because the panels are fixed before sampling, $\bar k$ and $\mathcal L_\mu$ are deterministic.

The cited full rank-breaking theorem applies: rankings are independent Plackett–Luce draws with common $\theta^*\in\Theta_b$, panel sizes are at least two, and the comparison graph is connected. Its conclusion gives, on a high-probability event,
$$ \|\theta^0-\theta^*\|_2 \le \frac{2(1+e^{2b})^2\sqrt{N\bar k\log n}} {\lambda_2(N\mathcal L_\mu)}. $$
This application is the representation identification stated in the question. [Hajek–Oh–Xu, Theorem 4](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2014/file/daadbd06d5082478b7677bea9812b575-Paper.pdf)

On that same event, the deterministic bound from Section 4 and the triangle inequality give, simultaneously for every additive $\epsilon$-maximizer,
$$ \|\widehat\theta-\theta^*\|_2 \le \frac{2(1+e^{2b})^2\sqrt{N\bar k\log n}} {\lambda_2(N\mathcal L_\mu)} + \sqrt{ \frac{2N\epsilon} {\alpha_b\lambda_2(N\mathcal L_\mu)} }. $$
Since $\lambda_2(N\mathcal L_\mu)=N\gamma_\mu$, this is precisely the bound in Section 1.
