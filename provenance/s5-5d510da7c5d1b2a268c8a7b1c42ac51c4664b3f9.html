The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given interchangeable network-role types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), initial target opinions \(b_t^0\), stubbornness values \(d_t\), and a rational column-stochastic influence matrix \(A\), a finite horizon \(h\), seed-mass budget \(\kappa\), and threshold \(\theta\), is there a vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\) whose target cumulative score is at least \(\theta\)? Seeding mass \(x_t\) sets that mass's target opinion and stubbornness to \(1\). For unseeded members, let \(z_t^0=b_t^0\), \(\bar z_t^j=(x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^j)/\mu_t\), and \(z_t^{j+1}=d_t b_t^0+(1-d_t)\sum_{s\in\mathcal T}A_{s,t}\bar z_s^j\); the score is \(J(x)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}[x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^h]\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) interchangeable network-role types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Input also comprises binary rationals \(b_t^0,d_t\in[0,1]\), a nonnegative rational column-stochastic matrix \(A\in\mathbb Q^{\tau\times\tau}\), a unary-encoded horizon \(h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), and binary rationals \(\kappa\in[0,1]\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb Q\). Write \(m=|\{(s,t):A_{s,t}>0\}|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A choice \(x\in\mathbb R^{\mathcal T}\) satisfies \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\). At time zero, the selected mass in each type receives target opinion and stubbornness \(1\), permanently; the remaining mass retains initial opinion \(b_t^0\) and stubbornness \(d_t\).
Every unseeded member of type \(t\) uses the same influence weights \(A_{s,t}\) on source-type mean opinions. Define \(z_t^0=b_t^0\), \(\bar z_t^j=[x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^j]/\mu_t\), and \(z_t^{j+1}=d_t b_t^0+(1-d_t)\sum_s A_{s,t}\bar z_s^j\) for \(0\le j<h\). These formulas apply also when \(x_t=\mu_t\).
The target cumulative score at the horizon is \(J(x)=\sum_t[x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)z_t^h]\). Other candidates evolve independently and do not enter this score.

Is deciding whether such an \(x\) exists with \(J(x)\ge\theta\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,h\).

For a finite list of agents with whole-agent seeding, the cumulative-score decision problem is NP-hard already at horizon \(1\), with zero initial target opinions and zero initial stubbornness. [Source](https://arkasaha.github.io/papers/infvote.pdf)
No result settling this exact divisible-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Conclusion

The divisible-mass decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard already when \(h=1\), all \(b_t^0=d_t=0\), all masses are equal, and \(A\) is symmetric with exactly four positive entries per column. The proof permits arbitrary real seed masses.

Consequently, there is no algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,h\) unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). The construction below even uses dyadic rational inputs.

A quadratic characterization of independence number

Let \(G\) be a finite simple graph, let \(B\) be its adjacency matrix, and let \(\alpha(G)\) denote its maximum independent-set size. Define the simplex

\(\displaystyle \Delta=\left\{p\in\mathbb R^{V(G)}:p_v\ge0,\ \sum_vp_v=1\right\}.\)

Lemma. The following exact identity holds:

\(\displaystyle \min_{p\in\Delta}p^\mathsf T(I+B)p=\frac1{\alpha(G)}.\)

Proof. Write

\(\displaystyle Q(p)=\sum_vp_v^2+2\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E(G)}p_up_v.\)

Suppose adjacent vertices \(u,v\) both have positive coordinates. Hold all other coordinates fixed and preserve \(t=p_u+p_v\). Set

\(\displaystyle C_u=\sum_{\substack{w\ne u,v\\\{u,w\}\in E(G)}}p_w,\qquad C_v=\sum_{\substack{w\ne u,v\\\{v,w\}\in E(G)}}p_w.\)

The terms involving \(u\) or \(v\) are

\(\displaystyle p_u^2+p_v^2+2p_up_v+2p_uC_u+2p_vC_v=t^2+2tC_v+2p_u(C_u-C_v).\)

This expression is affine in \(p_u\) on the interval \([0,t]\). Therefore one endpoint has value no greater than the current value. Moving to that endpoint removes one positive coordinate without increasing \(Q\).

Repeat until the positive support is independent. This terminates because each step decreases the support size. If the resulting vector is \(p'\), with support size \(s\), then \(s\le\alpha(G)\), and Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\(\displaystyle Q(p)\ge Q(p')=\sum_v(p'_v)^2\ge\frac{(\sum_vp'_v)^2}{s}=\frac1s\ge\frac1{\alpha(G)}.\)

Conversely, assigning weight \(1/\alpha(G)\) to each vertex of a maximum independent set gives equality. This proves the lemma.

Reduction to the divisible-mass problem

We use the established theorem that deciding whether a simple \(3\)-regular graph has an independent set of size at least \(k\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. It is the case of degree \(3\) of the theorem in [*Independence and Irredundance in \(k\)-Regular Graphs*](https://combinatorialpress.com/ars-articles/volume-049-ars-articles/independence-and-irredundance-in-k-regular-graphs/).

Let the source instance be \((G,k)\), where \(G\) has \(n\) vertices and \(1\le k\le n\). Thresholds outside this range are trivially decidable and do not affect the hardness statement.

Preparing equal dyadic masses

A cubic graph has even order because \(3n=2|E(G)|\). Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\ge2n\), and put

\(\displaystyle r=\frac{N-2n}{4}.\)

This is a nonnegative integer: \(n\) is even, and \(N\ge8\). Form \(H\) as the disjoint union of two copies of \(G\) and \(r\) copies of the complete graph on four vertices. Then \(H\) is simple and cubic, has \(N\) vertices, and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \alpha(H)=2\alpha(G)+r.\)

Set \(K=2k+r\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \alpha(H)\ge K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha(G)\ge k.\)

Also, \(2\le K\le N\) and \(N<4n\).

Constructing the society

Use one type for each vertex of \(H\), and let \(B_H\) be its adjacency matrix. Set

\(\displaystyle \tau=N,\qquad \mu_t=\frac1N,\qquad b_t^0=d_t=0,\qquad h=1,\qquad \kappa=\frac1N,\qquad A=\frac{I+B_H}{4}.\)

Every column of \(I+B_H\) contains exactly four ones. Hence \(A\) is nonnegative and column-stochastic. It is symmetric, so its row sums also equal one. All masses are positive and sum to one.

For a feasible seed vector \(x\), define \(p_t=Nx_t\). Its constraints become

\(\displaystyle 0\le p_t\le1,\qquad\sum_tp_t=1.\)

Thus feasible vectors \(p\) are exactly the simplex \(\Delta\); the upper bounds follow automatically from nonnegativity and the sum constraint.

Because \(z_t^0=0\), the time-zero type means and the time-one unseeded opinions are

\(\displaystyle \bar z_t^0=p_t,\qquad z_t^1=\sum_sA_{s,t}p_s.\)

Using both the budget constraint and the row sums of \(A\), the score is

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} J(x) &=\frac1N+\frac1N\sum_t(1-p_t)\sum_sA_{s,t}p_s\\ &=\frac1N+\frac1N\left(\sum_sp_s\sum_tA_{s,t}-\sum_{s,t}A_{s,t}p_sp_t\right)\\ &=\frac2N-\frac1{4N}p^\mathsf T(I+B_H)p. \end{aligned} \)

The lemma therefore proves the exact optimum

\(\displaystyle \max_xJ(x)=\frac2N-\frac1{4N\alpha(H)}.\)

This identity optimizes over all feasible real vectors. In particular, no whole-agent restriction has entered the argument.

Choosing a dyadic threshold

Define, for positive integers \(a\),

\(\displaystyle F(a)=\frac2N-\frac1{4Na}.\)

This function is strictly increasing. Choose

\(\displaystyle q=\left\lceil\log_2(8NK^2)\right\rceil,\qquad \theta=\frac{\left\lfloor2^qF(K)\right\rfloor}{2^q}.\)

Then \(\theta\) is dyadic and \(\theta\le F(K)\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle F(K)-F(K-1)=\frac1{4NK(K-1)}>\frac1{8NK^2}\ge2^{-q}.\)

Since rounding down loses less than \(2^{-q}\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle F(K-1)<\theta\le F(K).\)

If \(\alpha(H)\ge K\), the optimum is at least \(F(K)\), so the society is a yes-instance. If \(\alpha(H)<K\), integrality gives \(\alpha(H)\le K-1\), so its optimum is at most \(F(K-1)<\theta\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \exists x:\ J(x)\ge\theta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha(H)\ge K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha(G)\ge k. \)

This completes the many-one reduction.

Running times and scope

Reduction size and time. The constructed instance has \(\tau=N<4n\), \(m=4\tau\), and \(h=1\). Every nonzero matrix entry is \(1/4\); the masses, budget, and threshold have \(O(\log\tau)\) bits. With sparse matrix encoding, its length and construction time are \(O(\tau\log\tau)\). With explicitly listed dense entries, they are \(O(\tau^2+\tau\log\tau)\). Threshold rounding uses exact integer arithmetic on \(O(\log\tau)\)-bit integers.

Thus hardness holds even for \(m=4\tau\) and fixed horizon \(1\). In particular, an algorithm of running time \(f(h)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Evaluating a specified choice. For a rational vector \(x\) of total encoding length \(B\), the recurrence evaluates \(J(x)\) in

\(\displaystyle O\bigl((h+1)(m+\tau)\bigr)\)

rational arithmetic operations. A common denominator for the fixed recurrence coefficients has \(O(L+B)\) bits; after \(h\) iterations, intermediate rational values have \(O((h+1)(L+B))\) bits. Using elementary integer arithmetic gives the conservative bit-time bound

\(\displaystyle O\!\left((h+1)(m+\tau)\bigl((h+1)(L+B)\bigr)^2\right).\)

Horizon zero. For completeness, when \(h=0\),

\(\displaystyle J(x)=\sum_t\mu_tb_t^0+\sum_t(1-b_t^0)x_t.\)

Sorting the coefficients \(1-b_t^0\) and filling types in decreasing order solves this continuous knapsack problem in \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations, with polynomial bit complexity in \(L\).

The target classification is therefore settled as \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. No membership claim in \(\mathrm{NP}\) for arbitrary horizons is needed or asserted.
