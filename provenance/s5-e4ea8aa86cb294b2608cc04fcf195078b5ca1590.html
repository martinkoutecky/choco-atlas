The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many cohorts \(i\) with rational masses \(\alpha_i\), weights \(w_i\), bounded value supports, and rational piecewise-constant value densities \(F_i\), together with per-capita supply \(\rho\), compute welfare-maximizing interim allocation and waiting-cost schedules \(q_i(v)\) and \(c_i(v)\) for the population measure \(\mu(i,dv)=\alpha_i\,dF_i(v)\). Require \(0\le q_i(v)\le1\), \(c_i(v)\ge0\), truthful reporting \(v q_i(v)-c_i(v)\ge v q_i(r)-c_i(r)\) for all \(v,r\), voluntary participation \(v q_i(v)-c_i(v)\ge0\), and supply \(\sum_i\alpha_i\int q_i(v)\,dF_i(v)\le\rho\); maximize \(\sum_i\alpha_i w_i\int (v q_i(v)-c_i(v))\,dF_i(v)\).

The exact welfare decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\)

The decisive fact is that, on a constant-density interval, welfare from a cutoff allocation is convex in the mass allocated. Consequently, every cutoff inside such an interval can be replaced by a lottery between its endpoints, preserving resource use and weakly increasing welfare. This gives an exact rational linear program of polynomial size. An explicit greedy algorithm also solves it.

1. Incentive compatibility eliminates the waiting-cost variables

Fix a cohort and suppress its index. Write \(u(v)=vq(v)-c(v)\).

For \(v>r\), the two incentive constraints imply
\[ (v-r)q(r)\le u(v)-u(r)\le(v-r)q(v). \]
Thus \(q\) is nondecreasing.

Individual rationality at zero and nonnegative waiting costs give \(u(0)=-c(0)=0\). Summing the displayed inequalities over a partition of \([0,v]\) bounds \(u(v)\) between the lower and upper Riemann sums of \(q\). A bounded monotone function is Riemann integrable, so
\[ u(v)=\int_0^v q(t)\,dt, \qquad c(v)=vq(v)-\int_0^v q(t)\,dt. \]

Conversely, every nondecreasing measurable \(q:[0,b]\to[0,1]\), equipped with these costs, satisfies all the requirements. Indeed, monotonicity gives \(c(v)\ge0\), and the integral gives \(u(v)\ge0\). For \(v>r\),
\[ u(v)-u(r)=\int_r^v q(t)\,dt\ge(v-r)q(r), \]
which is the incentive constraint for reporting \(r\). For \(v<r\), the same constraint follows from
\[ u(r)-u(v)=\int_v^r q(t)\,dt\le(r-v)q(r). \]

These arguments hold at every value, including allocation discontinuities.

Tonelli’s theorem, applied to the nonnegative integrand, now gives
\[ \int_0^b u(v)\,dF(v) = \int_0^b (1-F(t))q(t)\,dt. \]

Therefore the original problem is exactly optimization over nondecreasing allocation functions, with welfare
\[ W=\sum_i\alpha_iw_i\int_0^{b_i}(1-F_i(t))q_i(t)\,dt. \]

2. Every monotone allocation is a mixture of cutoffs

For a cutoff \(a\in[0,b_i]\), define
\[ q_i^a(v)=\mathbf 1_{\{v\ge a\}}, \qquad c_i^a(v)=a\mathbf 1_{\{v\ge a\}}. \]
Its utility is \((v-a)_+\). Its resource use and weighted welfare within the cohort are, respectively,
\[ S_i(a)=1-F_i(a), \qquad R_i(a)=w_i\int_a^{b_i}(1-F_i(t))\,dt. \]

To justify the mixture representation without imposing continuity on \(q_i\), use layer cake:
\[ q_i(v)=\int_0^1\mathbf 1_{\{q_i(v)>z\}}\,dz. \]
Because \(q_i\) is nondecreasing, each set \(\{v:q_i(v)>z\}\) is an upper interval. Let \(a_i(z)\) be its infimum, using \(a_i(z)=b_i\) when it is empty.

The set differs from \([a_i(z),b_i]\) by at most one endpoint. Such differences have both Lebesgue measure zero and \(F_i\)-measure zero, because \(F_i\) is atomless. Also, \(a_i(z)\) is nondecreasing in \(z\), hence measurable. Tonelli’s theorem therefore yields
\[ \int q_i(v)\,dF_i(v)=\int_0^1 S_i(a_i(z))\,dz \]
and
\[ w_i\int_0^{b_i}(1-F_i(t))q_i(t)\,dt = \int_0^1 R_i(a_i(z))\,dz. \]

Thus every feasible allocation’s resource–welfare pair is an average of cutoff pairs. The cutoff \(b_i\) supplies the zero pair \((0,0)\).

3. Only density-partition endpoints are needed

Write the supplied partition for cohort \(i\) as
\[ 0=a_{i,0}<a_{i,1}<\cdots<a_{i,k_i}=b_i, \qquad k_i\le m, \]
with density \(h_{i,j}>0\) on the \(j\)-th interval.

Consider one interval \([\ell,r]\) of density \(h>0\). Put \(s_r=S_i(r)\). For \(a\in[\ell,r]\),
\[ S_i(a)=s_r+h(r-a). \]
Writing \(s=S_i(a)\), direct integration gives
\[ R_i(a) = R_i(r)+w_i\left(s_r(r-a)+\frac h2(r-a)^2\right) = R_i(r)+\frac{w_i}{2h}(s^2-s_r^2). \]

As a function of \(s\), this expression has second derivative \(w_i/h\ge0\). It is therefore convex.

Set
\[ \theta=\frac{S_i(a)-S_i(r)}{S_i(\ell)-S_i(r)}\in[0,1]. \]
The denominator is positive because \(h>0\) and \(\ell<r\). Convexity gives
\[ S_i(a)=\theta S_i(\ell)+(1-\theta)S_i(r), \]
\[ R_i(a)\le\theta R_i(\ell)+(1-\theta)R_i(r). \]

Hence replacing cutoff \(a\) by the indicated lottery between \(\ell\) and \(r\) preserves allocation mass and weakly increases welfare. This also covers \(w_i=0\).

Applying this replacement to the cutoff mixture from Section 2 proves:

Endpoint reduction. Every feasible schedule can be replaced by a feasible mixture of density-partition endpoint cutoffs with the same resource use and at least as much welfare.

Conversely, any such finite mixture is feasible at every value: each cutoff schedule is incentive compatible, individually rational, and has nonnegative costs, and these inequalities are preserved by convex combinations.

4. An exact polynomial-size rational linear program

For each endpoint, define
\[ S_{i,j}=S_i(a_{i,j}), \qquad R_{i,j}=R_i(a_{i,j}). \]
These numbers are rational. To compute them, let
\[ d_{i,j}=a_{i,j}-a_{i,j-1}. \]
Starting with \(S_{i,k_i}=R_{i,k_i}=0\), use
\[ S_{i,j-1}=S_{i,j}+h_{i,j}d_{i,j}, \]
\[ R_{i,j-1} = R_{i,j} +\frac{w_i d_{i,j}}2\left(S_{i,j-1}+S_{i,j}\right). \]

Let \(x_{i,j}\) be the probability assigned to endpoint cutoff \(a_{i,j}\). The exact decision problem is equivalent to feasibility of
\[ x_{i,j}\ge0, \qquad \sum_{j=0}^{k_i}x_{i,j}=1 \quad\text{for every }i, \]
\[ \sum_i\alpha_i\sum_{j=0}^{k_i}S_{i,j}x_{i,j}\le\rho, \]
\[ \sum_i\alpha_i\sum_{j=0}^{k_i}R_{i,j}x_{i,j}\ge B. \]

There are at most \(\tau(m+1)\) variables. The endpoint reduction proves both directions of this equivalence, including thresholds equal to the optimum.

For completeness, the following algorithm establishes polynomial-time solvability directly.

5. Exact optimization by concave hulls and greedy allocation

For each cohort, form the least concave majorant \(H_i\) of its endpoint points \((S_{i,j},R_{i,j})\). Equivalently,
\[ H_i(s)= \max\left\{ \sum_jx_jR_{i,j}: x_j\ge0,\ \sum_jx_j=1,\ \sum_jx_jS_{i,j}=s \right\}. \]

Its graph is the upper boundary of the convex hull of these finitely many points. Thus it is piecewise linear, and its vertices are endpoint points.

The points are already strictly ordered by their resource coordinate, from \(S_{i,k_i}=0\) to \(S_{i,0}=1\). Their welfare coordinates are nondecreasing in that order. The upper hull can be computed by maintaining a stack: whenever the last two segment slopes increase, delete their shared vertex; collinear intermediate vertices may also be deleted.

To verify this construction, an increasing pair of slopes places the shared vertex below the chord between its neighbors. Each deletion therefore preserves domination of all processed points. At termination the slopes are nonincreasing, so the resulting chain is concave and dominates every point. Any concave majorant must dominate every chord between its retained vertices, proving that the chain is the least concave majorant.

Write its successive vertices as
\[ (s_{i,0},r_{i,0}),\ldots,(s_{i,\ell_i},r_{i,\ell_i}), \]
where the first is \((0,0)\) and the last has resource coordinate \(1\). Segment \(k\) has population capacity and marginal welfare
\[ C_{i,k}=\alpha_i(s_{i,k}-s_{i,k-1}), \qquad \lambda_{i,k} = \frac{r_{i,k}-r_{i,k-1}}{s_{i,k}-s_{i,k-1}}. \]
These slopes are nonnegative and nonincreasing within each cohort.

Sort all segments by decreasing \(\lambda_{i,k}\), breaking equal-slope ties so that earlier segments of a cohort precede later ones. Fill their capacities in this order until total allocation equals \(\rho\). This is possible because
\[ \sum_{i,k}C_{i,k}=\sum_i\alpha_i=1>\rho. \]

Here is a direct optimality proof. Temporarily drop the requirement that a cohort’s segments be filled in order. The relaxed problem is
\[ \max\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}y_{i,k}, \qquad 0\le y_{i,k}\le C_{i,k}, \qquad \sum_{i,k}y_{i,k}\le\rho. \]

Let \(y^*\) be the greedy solution, and let \(\lambda_*\) be the slope of its last used segment. For any relaxed feasible \(y\), segments preceding the last used segment are full in \(y^*\), and subsequent segments are empty. Consequently,
\[ \sum_{i,k}(\lambda_{i,k}-\lambda_*)(y_{i,k}-y^*_{i,k})\le0. \]
Also, \(\lambda_*\ge0\) and \(\sum y\le\sum y^*=\rho\). Adding these inequalities gives
\[ \sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}y_{i,k} \le \sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}y^*_{i,k}. \]

Finally, within-cohort slopes are nonincreasing and ties respect segment order. Therefore the greedy solution fills a prefix of each cohort’s segments, followed by at most one partial segment. It satisfies the ordering requirement that was dropped.

A filled prefix ends at a hull vertex. A partial segment is implemented by the convex combination of its two endpoint cutoffs. Thus the greedy solution is an attainable optimum of the original problem.

The concavification and monotone-marginal greedy viewpoint also appears in [Redistributive Allocation Mechanisms](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/728111). The endpoint reduction and exact arithmetic analysis here establish the complexity claim for the stated input representation.

6. Bit complexity and conclusion

Let \(N=\sum_i(k_i+1)\le\tau(m+1)\).

Endpoint coordinates require a polynomial number of rational arithmetic operations. Their bit lengths are polynomial in \(L\): the displayed formulas expand into sums of polynomially many products of a bounded number of input rationals. A common denominator can be chosen as a fixed power of the product of the input denominators, whose bit length is \(O(L)\).

Hull slopes and population capacities use differences, products, and quotients of endpoint coordinates, so their bit lengths are also polynomial in \(L\). Hull construction takes \(O(N)\) arithmetic operations, and sorting takes \(O(N\log N)\) exact rational comparisons.

The greedy procedure uses polynomially many additions and at most one partial segment. Its welfare is the sum of the full segments’ rational welfare increments and, if present, the last segment’s rational slope times the remaining supply. All these calculations have polynomial bit length. Comparison with the rational threshold \(B\) is therefore exact and takes polynomial time.

Conclusion. The optimum is attained and rational, and the exact welfare decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).
