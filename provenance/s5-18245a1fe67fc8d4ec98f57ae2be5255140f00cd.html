The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses of buyer and seller types, where each type fixes its value, per-capita budget or supply, and compatibility, along with a price increment \(\varepsilon\), can one compute a type-level polyhedral clinching allocation and transfers in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length, with \(\mathrm{LW} \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\) whenever the paper’s price-increment condition holds?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. There are nonempty buyer and seller supports \(N,M\), with \(m=|M|\) and \(\tau=|N|+m\); their masses \(a_i,b_j>0\) sum jointly to \(1\). All numerical input is rational, with total binary bit length \(L\).
Buyer type \(i\) has per-unit value \(v_i>0\) and per-capita budget \(B_i\geq0\); seller type \(j\) has known per-unit value \(\rho_j>0\) and per-capita supply \(s_j\geq0\). Goods are divisible and homogeneous; a public graph \(E\subseteq N\times M\) specifies compatibility between every member of adjacent types. Put \(\beta_i=a_iB_i\) and \(S_j=b_js_j\).
Type-level allocation and transfers are aggregate quantities \(w_{ij},p_i,r_j\geq0\), distributed uniformly within each type, with \(p_i\leq\beta_i\) and \(\sum_iw_{ij}\leq S_j\). For \(x_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\), define \(\mathrm{LW}(w)=\sum_i\min(v_ix_i,\beta_i)+\sum_j\rho_j(S_j-\sum_iw_{ij})\); \(\mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\) is its maximum over compatible supply-feasible allocations.
Define the type-level polyhedral clinching auction by treating each buyer type as one aggregate buyer. Add a virtual buyer \(z_j\) adjacent only to seller \(j\), with value \(\rho_j\) and unlimited budget; use a fixed order consisting of the input buyer order followed by the input seller order for virtual buyers. Initialize allocation, payments, revenues, and buyer clocks \(c_i\) to zero, and demands \(d_i\) to infinity.
At a current state, let \(R_j=S_j-\sum_kw_{kj}\). For buyer \(i\) and a feasible nonnegative vector \(\xi\) on its incident edges with \(\sum_j\xi_{ij}\leq d_i\), let \(Q_i(\xi)\) be the set of vectors \((u_k)_{k\ne i}\) admitting compatible nonnegative transactions \(y_{kj}\) with \(\sum_jy_{kj}=u_k\leq d_k\) and \(\sum_{k\ne i}y_{kj}+\xi_{ij}\leq R_j\), taking \(\xi_{ij}=0\) on nonincident edges.
A clinching pass visits all augmented buyers in the fixed order. Each chooses any componentwise maximal feasible \(\xi\) satisfying \(Q_i(\xi)=Q_i(0)\), updates \(w_{ij}\leftarrow w_{ij}+\xi_{ij}\), \(p_i\leftarrow p_i+c_i\sum_j\xi_{ij}\), and \(r_j\leftarrow r_j+c_i\xi_{ij}\), then recomputes its demand.
Demand is \((\beta_i-p_i)/c_i\) when \(0<c_i<v_i\), infinity when \(c_i=0<v_i\), and zero when \(c_i\geq v_i\); virtual buyers use unlimited budgets. After each clinching pass, increase one buyer's clock by \(\varepsilon>0\), recompute its demand, and advance cyclically through the fixed order. Stop when all demands vanish; cancel virtual allocations and payments, subtracting each virtual payment from its seller's revenue. Virtual allocations represent retained seller supply.
Every real and virtual value is a positive integer multiple of \(\varepsilon\). Let \(v_{\min},v_{\max}\) be their minimum and maximum, and impose \(\varepsilon(v_{\max}-v_{\min})\leq v_{\min}^{2}\).
Can an exact terminal allocation and transfers of this type-level auction satisfying \(\mathrm{LW}\geq\tfrac12\mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\) be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is producing such an outcome NP-hard?

Partial result: exponential output size, with the welfare bound repaired

Under the usual convention that exact output quantities are written explicitly as binary numerators and denominators, polynomial-time computation is impossible in general. This follows from an unconditional output-size lower bound, already with one seller and two buyer types.

The construction below also satisfies the required welfare guarantee. It repairs the erroneous optimum calculation in NOTES. It does not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness or settle computation with compressed exact output.

1. Input family and admissible increments

Let $K=2^t$, where $t\geq1$. There are two buyer types and one seller type, with both compatibility edges present. Choose
$$ a_1=a_2=\frac14,\qquad b_j=\frac12,\qquad B_1=B_2=4,\qquad s_j=2. $$
Thus the masses are positive and sum to one, and
$$ \beta_1=\beta_2=1,\qquad S_j=1. $$
Set
$$ \rho_j=1,\qquad v_1=v_2=v=2+\frac1K. $$
The augmented buyer order is buyer one, buyer two, then the seller’s virtual buyer. Here $m=1$ and $\tau=3$.

Every increment satisfying the value-integrality requirements has the form
$$ \varepsilon=\frac1q,\qquad q=hK,\qquad h\in\mathbb Z_{>0}. $$
Indeed, $1$ being an integer multiple of $\varepsilon$ forces $\varepsilon=1/q$ for a positive integer $q$. Buyer-value integrality then requires
$$ qv=\frac{q(2K+1)}K\in\mathbb Z. $$
Since $\gcd(K,2K+1)=1$, this is equivalent to $K\mid q$.

Conversely, every such increment satisfies the stated increment condition:
$$ \varepsilon(v_{\max}-v_{\min}) =\frac{1+1/K}{q} \leq\frac{1+1/K}{K} \leq\frac34 <1=v_{\min}^2. $$
Fix an admissible $q$ and define
$$ T=qv=2q+h. $$

For the prescribed choice $\varepsilon=1/K$, the complete input has binary length $L=\Theta(\log K)$. The society alone has this length as well.

2. Unique clinching quantities with one seller

Let $R$ denote residual supply and let
$$ D_{-i}=\sum_{k\ne i}d_k, $$
allowing infinite demands.

Because every augmented buyer is adjacent to the sole seller,
$$ Q_i(0)= \left\{ (u_k)_{k\ne i}: 0\leq u_k\leq d_k,\quad \sum_{k\ne i}u_k\leq R \right\}. $$
The maximum feasible total allocation to the other buyers is
$$ C_i=\min(R,D_{-i}). $$
This maximum is attained: with finitely many buyers, their demand bounds permit allocating any total up to their sum, and an infinite demand can absorb any required finite quantity.

If buyer $i$ takes $\xi$, the capacity available to the others becomes $R-\xi$. Therefore
$$ Q_i(\xi)=Q_i(0) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R-\xi\geq C_i. $$
The forward implication follows by considering a vector attaining total $C_i$; the reverse implication follows because every vector in $Q_i(0)$ has total at most $C_i$.

Consequently, the unique maximal clinch is
$$ \xi_i=\min\bigl(d_i,R-\min(R,D_{-i})\bigr). $$
There is only one incident edge, so there is no choice among distinct componentwise maximal vectors. Thus, for each fixed admissible increment, the entire trajectory is unique.

3. Exact auction trajectory

3.1. Until the virtual buyer drops

While its clock is below $1$, the virtual buyer has infinite demand. This prevents either real buyer from clinching.

Until the virtual clock reaches $1$, both real clocks are at most $1$. Their payments are zero, and each real demand is at least $1$. Their combined demand therefore prevents the virtual buyer from clinching.

When the virtual clock reaches $1$, both real clocks equal $1$, their demands equal $1$, and residual supply remains $1$. The virtual demand becomes zero and stays zero.

3.2. A cycle of real clock increases

For each integer $k$ with
$$ q\leq k\leq T-1, $$
consider the equal-clock state where both real clocks are $k/q$. We prove that
$$ d_1=d_2=R=D_k,\qquad D_k=\frac{q^2}{k^2}. $$
The preceding argument establishes this at $k=q$. Updates of the virtual clock and their subsequent passes have no effect.

Suppose the invariant holds and $k\leq T-2$. Increasing buyer one’s clock to $(k+1)/q$ leaves its remaining budget unchanged, so its demand becomes
$$ \frac{k}{k+1}D_k. $$
Buyer one cannot clinch because buyer two still demands all residual supply. Buyer two then clinches
$$ \delta_{2,k} =D_k-\frac{k}{k+1}D_k =\frac{q^2}{k^2(k+1)}. $$
At a fixed positive clock, paying for a clinch reduces demand by exactly the allocated quantity. Thus both demands and residual supply now equal
$$ \frac{k}{k+1}D_k. $$

Next, buyer two’s clock increases to $(k+1)/q$, reducing its demand to
$$ \frac{k^2}{(k+1)^2}D_k. $$
Buyer one then clinches
$$ \delta_{1,k} =\frac{k}{k+1}D_k-\frac{k^2}{(k+1)^2}D_k =\frac{q^2}{k(k+1)^2}. $$
Both demands and residual supply consequently become
$$ \frac{k^2}{(k+1)^2}D_k =\frac{q^2}{(k+1)^2} =D_{k+1}. $$
No further clinch occurs in that pass, since each remaining real buyer’s demand equals the residual supply. This proves the invariant.

3.3. Final dropout and transfers

At $k=T-1$, buyer one’s next clock increase reaches
$$ \frac Tq=v, $$
so its demand becomes zero. Buyer two still demands exactly $D_{T-1}$ and therefore clinches all remaining supply. Its payment exhausts its remaining budget, making its demand zero. The auction terminates.

The terminal allocations are consequently
$$ x_1=q^2\sum_{k=q}^{T-2}\frac1{k(k+1)^2}, \qquad x_2=1-x_1. $$

Immediately before buyer one drops, its remaining budget equals its clock times its demand:
$$ 1-p_1 =\frac{T-1}{q}\frac{q^2}{(T-1)^2} =\frac q{T-1}. $$
Hence
$$ p_1=1-\frac q{T-1},\qquad p_2=1,\qquad r_j=p_1+p_2. $$
The virtual buyer never receives any allocation or makes any payment, so cancellation changes nothing.

4. Repaired welfare calculation

4.1. Exact optimum, including retained supply

For any feasible allocation $y_1,y_2\geq0$ with $y_1+y_2\leq1$,
$$ \mathrm{LW}(y) =\min(vy_1,1)+\min(vy_2,1)+1-y_1-y_2. $$
Define
$$ g(y)=\min(vy,1)-y. $$
For $0\leq y\leq1/v$,
$$ g(y)=(v-1)y, $$
which increases because $v>1$. For $y\geq1/v$,
$$ g(y)=1-y, $$
which decreases. Thus
$$ g(y)\leq1-\frac1v $$
for every $y\geq0$, with equality at $y=1/v$.

It follows that
$$ \mathrm{LW}(y) =1+g(y_1)+g(y_2) \leq3-\frac2v. $$
Since $v>2$, allocating $1/v$ to each buyer is feasible and attains equality. Therefore
$$ \mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}=3-\frac2v. $$
In particular, because $v\leq5/2$,
$$ \mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\leq\frac{11}{5}. $$

4.2. Terminal welfare exceeds the required bound

Every positive real clinch occurs at a clock strictly below $v$. Summing its payment contributions gives
$$ p_i\leq vx_i. $$
Also $p_i\leq1$, so
$$ \min(vx_i,1)\geq p_i. $$
All supply is allocated to the real buyers at termination. Consequently,
$$ \mathrm{LW}(w)\geq p_1+p_2 =2-\frac q{T-1}. $$
Because $h\geq1$,
$$ T-1=2q+h-1\geq2q, $$
and hence
$$ \mathrm{LW}(w)\geq\frac32. $$
Combining the bounds yields the stronger guarantee
$$ \frac{\mathrm{LW}(w)}{\mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}} \geq \frac{3/2}{11/5} =\frac{15}{22} >\frac12. $$
This establishes the required welfare guarantee directly, without applying an external auction theorem.

5. Exponential denominator size

The partial-fraction identity
$$ \frac1{k(k+1)^2} =\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}-\frac1{(k+1)^2} $$
gives
$$ x_1=q^2\left( \frac1q-\frac1{T-1} -\sum_{\ell=q+1}^{T-1}\frac1{\ell^2} \right). $$
Let $D$ be the reduced denominator of $x_1$.

Consider any prime $p$ satisfying
$$ \frac{3q}{2}<p<2q. $$
Since $h=q/K\leq q/2$,
$$ 2q\leq T-1<\frac{5q}{2}. $$
Therefore $p$ occurs among the summation indices. Furthermore,
$$ 2p>3q>T-1, $$
so it is the only multiple of $p$ in that range.

Neither $q$ nor $T-1$ is divisible by $p$: we have
$$ q<p<T-1<2p. $$
Thus every term in the expression for $x_1$, except $-q^2/p^2$, has denominator coprime to $p$. Write their sum as $A/B$, where $A,B$ are integers, $B>0$, and $p\nmid B$. Then
$$ x_1=\frac{Ap^2-q^2B}{Bp^2}. $$
Its numerator is congruent to $-q^2B$ modulo $p$, which is nonzero because $p>q$ and $p\nmid B$. Neither factor of $p$ cancels in reduction. Hence
$$ p^2\mid D. $$
Since this holds for every prime in the interval,
$$ \log D\geq 2\sum_{3q/2<p<2q}\log p, $$
where $\log$ denotes the natural logarithm.

Use the prime number theorem in its Chebyshev-function form:
$$ \vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p=x+o(x) \qquad(x\longrightarrow\infty). $$
This is a standard equivalent formulation of the prime number theorem; see [Forster, Corollary 11.6](https://www.math.lmu.de/~forster/v/ann/annth_11.pdf).

Here $q\longrightarrow\infty$, and $2q$ is composite. Therefore
$$ \sum_{3q/2<p<2q}\log p =\vartheta(2q)-\vartheta(3q/2) =\frac q2+o(q). $$
Consequently,
$$ \log D\geq q+o(q). $$
In particular, for all sufficiently large $q$,
$$ \log_2 D\geq\frac{q}{2\log 2}. $$
The denominator alone thus requires $\Omega(q)$ binary digits.

If $x_1$ is output as any integer numerator divided by a positive integer denominator, its reduced denominator divides the output denominator. Allowing unreduced fractions therefore does not evade this lower bound.

6. Proven complexity consequence and remaining questions

For the prescribed increment $\varepsilon=1/K$, we have $q=K$ and $L=\Theta(\log K)$. The unique terminal allocation requires
$$ \Omega(K)=2^{\Omega(L)} $$
explicit output bits. Any algorithm writing that output must take at least that much time. Thus no algorithm can always produce the requested explicit rational outcome in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

The obstruction also holds if the algorithm may choose its increment: every admissible choice has $q\geq K$, and the denominator lower bound is uniform over those choices for sufficiently large $K$.

This settles the impossibility of polynomial-time explicit rational output. It does not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness: excessive output length is not a hardness reduction. Nor does it exclude efficient computation using a specified compressed representation. The full requested classification therefore remains partial.
