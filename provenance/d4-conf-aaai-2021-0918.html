The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(T\subseteq 2^C\) of approval types with rational masses \(\mu_A\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational mass price \(p>0\), find a BSP committee and supporting allocation, with no prescribed committee size. Choose \(W\subseteq C\), prices \(\rho_c\in[0,1]\), and masses \(x_{A,U}\geq 0\) for \(A\in T\) and \(U\subseteq W\cap A\), where \(\sum_U x_{A,U}=\mu_A\), each used bundle costs at most one, and \(\rho_c\sum_{A,U:c\in U}x_{A,U}=p\) for each \(c\in W\). Require BSP stability: no coalition formed from submasses of the \((A,U)\) cohorts can switch to newly proposed candidates \(W'\subseteq C\setminus W\), use balanced per-candidate prices, stay within each participant’s unit budget, make every participant weakly prefer the new utility/payment pair under the paper’s utility-first and lower-payment tie-break, and raise strictly more than \(p\) for every proposed candidate.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) have \(m\geq1\) candidates. A society is given by its support: \(T\subseteq2^C\), listing its \(\tau=|T|\) approval types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The input also contains a binary rational \(p>0\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Each individual has budget \(1\).
Find a committee \(W\subseteq C\), with no prescribed size, prices \(\rho_c\in[0,1]\), and masses \(x_{A,U}\geq0\) for \(U\subseteq W\cap A\), satisfying \(\sum_Ux_{A,U}=\mu_A\), \(x_{A,U}>0\Rightarrow\sum_{c\in U}\rho_c\leq1\), and \(\rho_c\sum_{A,U:c\in U}x_{A,U}=p\) for every \(c\in W\). A member of cohort \((A,U)\) has utility \(|U|\) and payment \(\sum_{c\in U}\rho_c\); approvals outside the purchased bundle give no utility.
A blocking deviation chooses \(\varnothing\neq W'\subseteq C\setminus W\), common prices \(q_c\in[0,1]\), and nonnegative real submasses \(y_{A,U,R,V}\), indexed by \(R\subseteq U\) and \(V\subseteq W'\cap A\), with \(\sum_{R,V}y_{A,U,R,V}\leq x_{A,U}\). Participants drop \(R\), retain \(U\setminus R\), and purchase \(V\).
For every positive submass, set \(b=\sum_{c\in U}\rho_c\) and \(b'=\sum_{c\in U\setminus R}\rho_c+\sum_{c\in V}q_c\). Require \(b'\leq1\) and either \(|U\setminus R|+|V|>|U|\), or \(|U\setminus R|+|V|=|U|\) and \(b'\leq b\). Require also \(q_c\sum_{A,U,R,V:c\in V}y_{A,U,R,V}>p\) for every \(c\in W'\). The allocation is balanced stably priceable (BSP) exactly when no such deviation exists.
Output prices and nonzero allocation masses as binary rationals, or report that no BSP allocation exists; blocking deviations may use real prices and submasses.

Is this exact BSP search problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For explicitly listed discrete voters and a fixed candidate price, BSP committees can be found in polynomial time, and whether a given committee is BSP can be verified in polynomial time. [Source](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/proceedings/comsoc-2021/PetersEtAl-2.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Zero-mass types are omitted, so the listed support contains exactly the positive-mass types.
  • Participants may drop any subset of their current purchased bundle while retaining the remaining purchases, as required by the discrete stability definition.
  • Proposed committees must be nonempty, excluding the vacuous unchanged deviation admitted by the literal weak-preference formulation.
  • Submasses of a cohort may take different deviation actions; their total cannot exceed that cohort's mass.
  • Deviation prices lie in [0,1], matching the discrete per-person payment bound; utility comes only from approved candidates purchased by that participant.
  • The computational output uses binary-rational prices and a sparse list of nonzero binary-rational allocation masses; deviations are quantified over real values.
  • Allow reporting nonexistence rather than assuming existence for the continuous formulation; the empty committee remains an admissible output when stable.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The exact BSP search problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). A BSP allocation always exists, and the algorithm below produces one with exactly one nonzero allocation mass per approval type.

Here, “binary rationals” means rationals represented by binary-encoded numerators and denominators.

The construction uses the largest-demanding-group idea underlying the supplied discrete-voter result ([Theorem 7 and Appendix A.10](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/proceedings/comsoc-2021/PetersEtAl-2.pdf)). We give a direct proof for the stated mass formulation, including stability against real-valued deviations and polynomial bit complexity.

1. A characterization of blocking deviations

Fix any feasible allocation. For each cohort \((A,U)\), define its remaining budget and its maximum replaceable payment by

\(r_{A,U}=1-\sum_{d\in U}\rho_d,\qquad h_{A,U}=\max\bigl(\{r_{A,U}\}\cup\{\rho_d:d\in U\}\bigr).\)

For an unelected candidate \(c\) and a real price \(q\in(0,1]\), define

\(M_c(q)=\sum_{\substack{A,U:\ c\in A\\h_{A,U}\ge q}}x_{A,U}.\)

We claim that the allocation is BSP exactly when

\(qM_c(q)\le p\quad\text{for every }c\in C\setminus W\text{ and every real }q\in(0,1].\)

First, suppose this inequality fails for some \(c,q\). Every cohort counted in \(M_c(q)\) can buy \(c\):

  • If \(r_{A,U}\ge q\), it retains all of \(U\) and buys \(c\), increasing utility within budget.
  • Otherwise, some \(d\in U\) satisfies \(\rho_d\ge q\). It replaces \(d\) by \(c\), preserving utility and weakly decreasing payment.

Let all these cohorts participate in full. Their total payment to \(c\) is \(qM_c(q)>p\), so they form a blocking deviation.

Conversely, suppose an arbitrary blocking deviation exists. Choose a candidate \(c_*\in W'\) of minimum deviation price, and write \(q=q_{c_*}\). Since every deviation candidate receives strictly more than \(p>0\), all deviation prices are positive.

Consider a positive submass purchasing \(c_*\). Write \(k=|V|\) and \(\ell=|R|\). Then \(k\ge1\), and the utility requirement implies \(k\ge\ell\). Because every new candidate costs at least \(q\),

\(kq\le\sum_{d\in V}q_d.\)

If \(k=\ell\), the equal-utility payment condition gives

\(kq\le\sum_{d\in V}q_d\le\sum_{d\in R}\rho_d\le\ell h_{A,U}=kh_{A,U}.\)

If \(k>\ell\), the budget condition instead gives

\(kq\le\sum_{d\in V}q_d\le r_{A,U}+\sum_{d\in R}\rho_d\le(\ell+1)h_{A,U}\le kh_{A,U}.\)

Thus every submass purchasing \(c_*\) comes from a cohort satisfying \(h_{A,U}\ge q\). The submass constraints imply that its total purchasing mass is at most \(M_{c_*}(q)\). Consequently,

\(qM_{c_*}(q)\ge q\sum_{A,U,R,V:\ c_*\in V}y_{A,U,R,V}>p.\)

This proves the characterization, including for real prices and real submasses.

2. Computing a largest demanding group

Our algorithm maintains one bundle \(U_A\) and one remaining budget \(r_A\) for each approval type \(A\). Hence all members of a type always make identical purchases.

For an unelected candidate \(c\), call a collection of submasses \(s_A\in[0,\mu_A]\) a *demanding group* if its total mass \(M=\sum_A s_A\) is positive and

\(s_A>0\ \Longrightarrow\ c\in A\text{ and }r_A\ge p/M.\)

Such a group can fund \(c\) exactly by charging each participant \(p/M\).

A largest demanding group can always be chosen as a union of whole approval types. To see this, take any demanding group of mass \(M\) and put

\(H=\{A\in T:c\in A,\ r_A\ge p/M\},\qquad M_H=\sum_{A\in H}\mu_A.\)

Every participating type belongs to \(H\), so \(M_H\ge M\). Moreover, every type in \(H\) satisfies

\(r_A\ge p/M\ge p/M_H.\)

Thus taking every member of every type in \(H\) gives a demanding group of mass at least \(M\).

This observation also provides an efficient computation. Sort the types approving \(c\) as \(A_1,\ldots,A_s\), with

\(r_{A_1}\ge\cdots\ge r_{A_s},\qquad M_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mu_{A_i}.\)

The entire prefix through \(A_j\) is demanding exactly when

\(r_{A_j}M_j\ge p.\)

The set \(H\) constructed above is one of these prefixes, including all types tied at its threshold. Therefore the largest feasible prefix has mass at least that of every demanding group, even one using arbitrary real submasses.

3. The algorithm and feasibility

Initialize \(W=\varnothing\), \(U_A=\varnothing\), and \(r_A=1\) for every \(A\in T\). Repeat the following steps:

  • For every unelected candidate, compute all feasible prefixes described above.
  • If none exists, stop.
  • Choose a feasible prefix of maximum mass across all unelected candidates. Break ties arbitrarily.
  • If its candidate is \(c\), its type set is \(H\), and its mass is \(M\), set \(\rho_c=p/M\), add \(c\) to \(W\), and, for every \(A\in H\), replace \(U_A\) by \(U_A\cup\{c\}\) and \(r_A\) by \(r_A-\rho_c\).

Each iteration elects a new candidate, so there are at most \(m\) iterations.

The demanding-group condition guarantees \(r_A\ge\rho_c\) for every purchasing type. Thus remaining budgets stay nonnegative, and

\(r_A=1-\sum_{d\in U_A}\rho_d\)

holds throughout. Furthermore, \(0<\rho_c\le1\), since the purchasing types have remaining budgets at most \(1\).

At termination, output

\(x_{A,U_A}=\mu_A\)

for every type, with all other allocation masses zero. For each elected candidate, its purchasing types have exactly the mass \(M\) used when it was elected. Therefore its total payment is \(\rho_cM=p\). All feasibility conditions follow. Prices of unelected candidates may be set to zero if the output format requires them.

4. Proof of stability

For the final allocation, abbreviate

\(h_A=\max\bigl(\{r_A\}\cup\{\rho_d:d\in U_A\}\bigr).\)

By Section 1, it suffices to rule out an unelected candidate \(c\) and a real \(q\in(0,1]\) such that

\(q\sum_{\substack{A:\ c\in A\\h_A\ge q}}\mu_A>p.\)

Suppose such \(c,q\) exist. Let

\(S=\{A:c\in A,\ h_A\ge q\},\qquad M=\sum_{A\in S}\mu_A.\)

Then \(M>0\) and \(p/M<q\).

If no elected candidate has price at least \(q\), every type in \(S\) must have final remaining budget at least \(q\): all its purchased-candidate prices are strictly below \(q\). Hence \(S\) is a demanding group for \(c\) at termination, contradicting the stopping condition.

Otherwise, let \(t\) be the first iteration that elects a candidate at price at least \(q\). We show that every type in \(S\) had remaining budget at least \(q\) immediately before iteration \(t\).

If its final remaining budget is at least \(q\), this follows because remaining budgets only decrease. Otherwise, membership in \(S\) implies that it purchased some candidate \(d\) with \(\rho_d\ge q\). Candidate \(d\) was elected at iteration \(t\) or later. Immediately before purchasing \(d\), the type had remaining budget at least \(\rho_d\ge q\); therefore it also had remaining budget at least \(q\) before iteration \(t\).

Thus, before iteration \(t\), the whole group \(S\) could fund the still-unelected candidate \(c\) at price \(p/M<q\). Section 2 shows that the maximum demanding-group mass available then was at least \(M\). The greedy choice therefore charged

\(\rho_t\le p/M<q,\)

contradicting the definition of \(t\).

No violating \(c,q\) exist. The allocation is consequently BSP against all permitted real-valued deviations.

5. Exact arithmetic and polynomial running time

Write the input masses as \(\mu_A=n_A/d_A\), and write \(p=a/b\), using positive integer numerators and denominators. Define

\(D=\prod_{A\in T}d_A,\qquad w_A=D\mu_A.\)

The integers \(w_A\) are positive and satisfy \(\sum_Aw_A=D\). Since the denominator encodings are part of the input, \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

Every selected group has mass \(S/D\), where \(S\) is an integer satisfying \(1\le S\le D\). Its price is therefore

\(\rho=\frac{aD}{bS},\)

whose numerator and denominator have \(O(L)\) bits.

After \(k\) selections with integer group weights \(S_1,\ldots,S_k\), every remaining budget can be represented with denominator

\(b\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_i.\)

Its numerator lies between zero and that denominator. Hence remaining budgets have \(O((m+1)L)\)-bit representations. Exact sorting, prefix feasibility checks, and budget updates consequently use only polynomial-length integer arithmetic.

Each iteration performs at most \(m\) sorts of at most \(\tau\) types and scans their prefixes. Including the final stopping check, the number of arithmetic operations and comparisons is

\(O\!\left(m^2\tau\log(\tau+1)\right).\)

Their bit costs are polynomial in \(m,L\). The output contains at most \(m\) prices and exactly \(\tau\) nonzero masses, each attached to a bundle of at most \(m\) candidates.

Thus the algorithm has polynomial bit running time and polynomial output length. It always returns a BSP allocation; the “no BSP allocation exists” outcome is unnecessary for this formulation.
