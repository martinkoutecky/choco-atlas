The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given at most three categories \(M\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), complete monotone rankings over \(2^M\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), baseline type budgets \(\bar b_t\), and a rational radius \(\epsilon>0\), consider positive budget vectors \(b'\) with \(\lVert b'-\bar b\rVert_\infty<\epsilon\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t b'_t=1\). Decide whether, for almost every such \(b'\), there are prices \(p\) and masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}=q_j\), and positive \(x_{t,S}\) is assigned only to bundles \(S\) that are affordable and most preferred among the affordable bundles for type \(t\) at budget \(b'_t\).

Classification

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete under polynomial-time many-one reductions, already when \(m=1\). All numerical data in the hardness construction can be dyadic rationals.

Consequently:

  • It is polynomial-time solvable if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
  • It is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions.
  • Under many-one reductions, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard if and only if \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

Thus the proposed dichotomy needs a qualification about reductions. The complete classification is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-completeness.

1. Equilibrium at a specified budget vector

First, consider a rational budget vector \(b\in H\) with positive coordinates. Let \(B\) denote its encoding length, and put \(N=2\tau 2^m+m+2\).

Lemma 1. Equilibrium existence at \(b\) can be decided in time \(N^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(L+B)\).

Proof. Consider the hyperplanes in price space given by \(p_j=0\) and \(p(S)=b_t\), for every type \(t\) and nonempty bundle \(S\). An arrangement of \(O(N)\) hyperplanes in dimension \(m\) has \(N^{O(m)}\) faces, including faces of lower dimension.

These faces can be enumerated incrementally. When adding a hyperplane, intersect each existing face with its two open sides and with the hyperplane itself, retaining the nonempty intersections. Each nonemptiness test is a rational linear-programming problem. Strict inequalities can be tested by replacing them with inequalities having a common slack \(\eta\), imposing \(0\le\eta\le1\), and checking whether the maximum attainable slack is positive. Retain the faces satisfying \(p_j\ge0\).

On each face, every affordability comparison is fixed, including whether equality holds. Therefore each type has a fixed set \(D_t\) of demanded bundles: the highest-ranked bundles among its affordable bundles.

Market clearing on this face is equivalent to feasibility of the following linear program:

\(\displaystyle x_{t,S}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{S\in D_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t\sum_{\substack{S\in D_t\\j\in S}}x_{t,S}=q_j.\)

The program has at most \(\tau2^m\) variables. It permits exactly the splits among tied demanded bundles allowed in the question. No budget-exhaustion condition is imposed.

Polynomial-time rational linear programming applies because all coefficients are rational and the numbers of variables, constraints, and encoded bits are polynomially bounded. Testing all faces proves the stated running time. \(\square\)

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{coNP}\)

A failed budget vector alone is insufficient as a certificate: failure might occur only on a set of measure zero. We construct a polynomial-size family of hyperplanes outside which equilibrium existence is locally constant.

2.1 A uniform family of budget hyperplanes

An affordability pattern specifies, for every pair \(t,S\), whether \(p(S)\le b_t\) or \(p(S)>b_t\). Its price-realization system consists of the selected inequalities and \(p_j\ge0\).

For a fixed pattern, demanded bundles are fixed. Hence allocation feasibility is independent of \(b\): it is precisely the market-clearing linear program from Lemma 1. If that program is feasible, equilibrium exists at \(b\) with this pattern exactly when its price-realization system is feasible.

We now eliminate prices from all such systems uniformly.

Start with a pool containing both possible affordability rows,

\(\displaystyle p(S)\le b_t,\qquad -p(S)<-b_t,\)

and the rows \(-p_j\le0\). Apply Fourier–Motzkin elimination syntactically to this entire pool, keeping every generated row.

For completeness, eliminating one variable combines each lower bound with each upper bound. The resulting comparison is strict if either constituent bound is strict. This preserves existential feasibility: finitely many lower and upper bounds admit a common value exactly when every lower–upper comparison holds, with the corresponding strictness. If one side has no bounds, a sufficiently large or sufficiently small value satisfies the other side.

If a pool contains \(R\) rows, one elimination produces at most \(R+R^2\) rows. After eliminating the \(m\) prices, the final pool therefore contains at most \(K=O_m(N^{2^m})\) rows. They have the form \(0\le f(b)\) or \(0<f(b)\), where \(f\) is a homogeneous rational linear form.

Every row generated while eliminating any individual affordability pattern occurs in this pool: this follows inductively because each pattern starts with a subset of the initial pool and uses the same elimination rule.

All initial coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Without division, an elimination combines two rows by positive integer multipliers equal to their eliminated coefficients. If coefficients have absolute value at most \(C\), the new coefficients have absolute value at most \(2C^2\). Thus, after \(m\) eliminations, their bit lengths are \(O(2^m)\), independently of \(\tau\).

Let \(\mathcal F\) be the family of nonzero forms in the final pool. It can be constructed in time \(N^{O(2^m)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\).

2.2 Constancy on budget cells

On any region where every \(f\in\mathcal F\) has a fixed nonzero sign, every projected price-realization system has a fixed truth value. Identically zero rows also have fixed truth values. Allocation feasibility for each pattern is already independent of \(b\).

It follows that equilibrium existence is constant on each such region.

This argument also establishes measurability: the equilibrium budget set is a finite union of sets described by finitely many linear weak or strict inequalities.

No nonzero homogeneous form \(f\) vanishes identically on \(H\). Indeed, a linear functional constant on \(H=\{b:\mu\cdot b=1\}\) must be a scalar multiple of \(\mu\); if its constant value is zero, that scalar is zero. Consequently, each set \(H\cap\{f=0\}\), for \(f\in\mathcal F\), is either empty or a proper affine hyperplane of \(H\).

For \(\tau\ge2\), their finite union has relative Lebesgue measure zero. Therefore failure on a positive-measure subset of \(U\) is equivalent to failure at some \(b\in U\) satisfying \(f(b)\ne0\) for every \(f\in\mathcal F\). Such a point lies in a relatively open region of failure.

2.3 A polynomial-size certificate

Suppose such a region exists. Fix its signs \(\sigma_f\in\{-1,1\}\). Consider the linear program maximizing \(\eta\), subject to

\(\displaystyle \mu\cdot b=1,\qquad 0\le\eta\le1,\qquad b_t\ge\eta,\)

\(\displaystyle \bar b_t-\epsilon+\eta\le b_t\le\bar b_t+\epsilon-\eta,\qquad \sigma_f f(b)\ge\eta\quad(f\in\mathcal F).\)

A point in the region satisfies all these inequalities for some positive \(\eta\), because there are finitely many strict inequalities. The feasible set is bounded, so a maximizing vertex exists with positive slack.

There are \(\tau+1\) variables and polynomially many rational constraints when \(m\le3\). At a vertex, \(\tau+1\) independent active constraints determine the coordinates. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound imply a polynomial encoding length in \(\tau,L\), since the constraint coefficients have polynomial encoding length. Thus the failed region contains a polynomial-size rational budget vector.

A verifier can:

  • Construct \(\mathcal F\).
  • Check that the supplied rational \(b\) belongs to \(U\) and satisfies \(f(b)\ne0\) for every \(f\in\mathcal F\).
  • Apply Lemma 1 to verify nonexistence of equilibrium at \(b\).

Acceptance certifies failure on a relatively open subset of \(U\), hence on a set of positive measure.

When \(\tau=1\), both \(H\) and \(U\) consist of the baseline point. The stipulated zero-dimensional measure convention makes the question exactly equilibrium existence at that point, which Lemma 1 decides.

This proves membership in \(\mathrm{coNP}\). The certificate verifier runs in \(N^{O(2^m)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time.

3. Hardness with one category

We reduce the complement of SUBSET-SUM to the stated problem.

Use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem with positive binary-encoded integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and target \(B\), asking whether some subset sums to \(B\). We may restrict to \(1\le B\le A=\sum_i a_i\), since larger targets are immediate no-instances. The underlying completeness result is established in [Karp’s paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

3.1 Construction

There is one category \(g\). Choose a power of two \(D>8(A+1)\), and define

\(\displaystyle c=\frac14,\qquad \delta=\frac1{2D},\qquad \lambda=\frac12-\frac AD-\delta,\qquad \epsilon=\frac1{128}.\)

Choose a positive dyadic \(\rho\) satisfying \(n\rho<\epsilon/16\), and put

\(\displaystyle e=\frac{\rho}{D}\sum_{i=1}^n i a_i.\)

The society has \(\tau=n+3\) supported types:

| Type | Mass | Baseline budget | Ranking |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(i\), for \(1\le i\le n\) | \(a_i/D\) | \(1+i\rho\) | \(\{g\}\succ\varnothing\) |
| Special type \(s\) | \(c+\delta\) | \(1\) | \(\{g\}\succ\varnothing\) |
| High-budget indifferent type \(h\) | \(c\) | \(1+\lambda/4-4e\) | \(\{g\}\sim\varnothing\) |
| Low-budget indifferent type \(z\) | \(\lambda\) | \(1-c/4=15/16\) | \(\{g\}\sim\varnothing\) |

Set the supply to

\(\displaystyle q=c+\frac{B+1/4}{D}.\)

The distinct candidate baseline budgets ensure that the construction does not require duplicate supported preference-and-baseline pairs.

All rankings are complete, transitive, and monotone. Every numerical input is dyadic.

The masses sum to one because

\(\displaystyle \frac AD+(c+\delta)+c+\lambda=1.\)

Since \(D>8(A+1)\), we have \(A/D+\delta<1/8\), and therefore \(\lambda>3/8\). All masses are positive.

Baseline normalization holds exactly:

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\mu_t\bar b_t=1+e+\frac{c\lambda}{4}-4ce-\frac{\lambda c}{4}=1,\)

using \(4c=1\).

Let \(C=c+\delta+A/D\) be the total mass of strictly interested types. The supply satisfies

\(\displaystyle c<q<C<\frac38<1.\)

Finally,

\(\displaystyle e<\frac{\epsilon}{128},\qquad \bar b_h>1+\frac3{32}-\frac{\epsilon}{32}.\)

Together with \(\epsilon=1/128\), these estimates imply, for every \(b'\in U\),

\(\displaystyle b'_h>\max\{b'_s,b'_1,\ldots,b'_n\},\qquad b'_z<\min\{b'_s,b'_1,\ldots,b'_n\}.\)

For example, every core budget lies between \(1-\epsilon\) and \(1+17\epsilon/16\); the high budget exceeds \(1+3/32-33\epsilon/32\); and the low budget is below \(15/16+\epsilon\). These bounds are strictly separated.

The construction has polynomial encoding length and can be performed in polynomial time.

3.2 Exact equilibrium criterion at generic budgets

Consider a budget vector in \(U\) where the \(n+1\) strictly interested types have pairwise distinct budgets. List them in decreasing budget order, and let \(r_k\) be the cumulative mass of the first \(k\) types, with \(r_0=0\).

For a price admitting exactly this prefix of strictly interested types, their consumption is forced to equal \(r_k\). The high-budget indifferent type is affordable and can consume any mass between zero and \(c\). The low-budget indifferent type is unaffordable.

Thus the exactly attainable supplies at such prices form the interval

\(\displaystyle I_k=[r_k,r_k+c].\)

Every prefix occurs: choose a price between successive strictly interested budgets. For the empty prefix, choose it between the largest strictly interested budget and \(b'_h\); for the full prefix, choose it between \(b'_z\) and the smallest strictly interested budget.

For any \(q\in I_k\), allocate the item to all strictly interested types in the prefix, allocate item mass \(q-r_k\) to type \(h\), and allocate the empty bundle to everyone else. This satisfies affordability, demand, and exact clearing.

Other price ranges cannot clear the constructed supply. Above \(b'_h\), nobody can consume the item. At or below \(b'_z\), all strictly interested types must consume it, forcing mass \(C>q\). At a price equal to a strictly interested budget, that type is affordable and its entire mass must consume the item, so no additional interval appears.

Consequently, equilibrium exists exactly when \(q\in\bigcup_k I_k\).

The endpoints of the intervals increase with \(k\). Adjacent intervals have a gap precisely when the next type’s mass exceeds \(c\). Every candidate type has mass \(a_i/D<c\), so candidate steps create no gap. The special type has mass \(c+\delta\), so it creates exactly one gap.

Let \(I=\{i:b'_i>b'_s\}\), and write \(v=\sum_{i\in I}a_i/D\). The unique gap is

\(\displaystyle (v+c,\ v+c+\delta).\)

Because \(c<q<C\), equilibrium fails exactly when \(v+c<q<v+c+\delta\). Writing the integer \(Z=\sum_{i\in I}a_i\), this condition becomes

\(\displaystyle Z<B+\frac14<Z+\frac12.\)

For integers \(Z,B\), this is equivalent to \(Z=B\). We have therefore proved:

At every budget vector in \(U\) with distinct strictly interested budgets, equilibrium fails if and only if the candidates richer than \(s\) form a subset summing to \(B\).

3.3 Every candidate subset occurs on an open budget region

Fix an arbitrary subset \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\). Choose pairwise distinct numbers \(u_i\) with \(|u_i|<\epsilon/8\), positive for \(i\in I\) and negative for \(i\notin I\).

Set

\(\displaystyle b'_i=1+u_i,\qquad b'_s=1,\qquad b'_h=\bar b_h,\)

and enforce normalization by setting

\(\displaystyle b'_z=\bar b_z-\frac1\lambda\sum_i\frac{a_i}{D}(u_i-i\rho).\)

The normalization equation holds because the change in the low type’s weighted budget cancels the changes in candidate weighted budgets.

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle |b'_i-\bar b_i|<\frac{3\epsilon}{16},\)

and

\(\displaystyle |b'_z-\bar b_z|<\frac{A/D}{\lambda}\frac{3\epsilon}{16}<\frac{\epsilon}{16},\)

using \(A/D<1/8\) and \(\lambda>3/8\). Thus \(b'\in U\), all strictly interested budgets are distinct, and exactly the candidates in \(I\) are richer than \(s\).

All these ordering and neighborhood inequalities are strict. They therefore remain valid on a nonempty relatively open neighborhood in \(H\).

3.4 Correctness of the reduction

If SUBSET-SUM has a solution \(I\), Section 3.3 gives a nonempty relatively open region on which the richer candidates are exactly \(I\). Section 3.2 proves nonexistence throughout that region. Hence equilibrium does not exist for almost every budget in \(U\).

If SUBSET-SUM has no solution, Section 3.2 proves existence at every budget in \(U\) with distinct strictly interested budgets. The exceptional equal-budget sets are finitely many proper hyperplanes of \(H\), so they have relative measure zero. Hence equilibrium exists for almost every budget in \(U\).

Therefore the constructed society is a yes-instance exactly when the SUBSET-SUM instance is a no-instance. This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction from the complement of SUBSET-SUM, proving \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hardness already for \(m=1\).

4. Consequences and running times

Combining Sections 2 and 3 proves \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-completeness.

For explicit running-time bounds, with \(N=2\tau2^m+m+2\):

  • Equilibrium at a specified rational budget vector of bit length \(B\): \(N^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(L+B)\).
  • Verification of a certificate against almost-everywhere existence: \(N^{O(2^m)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\).
  • Deterministic decision by enumerating the budget-arrangement cells and testing each cell meeting \(U\): \(N^{O(2^m\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\).

The last bound follows from the \(O_m(N^{2^m})\) budget hyperplanes constructed above and arrangement enumeration in dimension \(\tau-1\).

The finite-agent result in [Babaioff–Nisan–Talgam-Cohen, Proposition 3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1911.09992) concerns finitely many indivisible items. Here, even with one category, a strictly interested type contributes an entire positive mass when its budget crosses the price. The reduction uses a gap between that forced mass and the supply, while the indifferent type supplies only a limited amount of adjustable consumption.

Finally, the reduction direction matters. An oracle for the present problem decides SUBSET-SUM by negating its answer, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under Turing reductions. Under many-one reductions, an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard language in \(\mathrm{coNP}\) would imply \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\); conversely, that equality would make every \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete language \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. This explains the qualification in the opening classification.
