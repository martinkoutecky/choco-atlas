The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite item set \(I\), finite buyer-type set \(T\), rational valuations \(v_t(i)\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\), a piecewise-constant arrival stream \(\rho:[0,1]\to T\) with rational breakpoints, and a rational threshold \(R\), does there exist a measurable pricing and allocation policy with revenue at least \(R\)? At time \(s\), the arriving mass of type \(\rho(s)\) is allocated among currently available items at utility-maximizing prices, with seller tie-breaking; feasibility requires \(\sum_i x_i(s)\leq 1\) and \(\int_0^1 x_i(s)\,ds\leq q_i\). Every mass assigned item \(i\) at time \(s\) must weakly prefer that purchase to every item still available at every later time \(r\geq s\), at its price then; revenue is \(\int_0^1\sum_i p_i(s)x_i(s)\,ds\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) buyer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Input consists of \(m\geq1\) goods \(I\), nonnegative rational unit-demand valuations \(v_t(i)\), nonnegative rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\), a known piecewise-constant stream \(\rho:[0,1]\to T\) with \(k\) rationally specified intervals satisfying \(\mu_t=\operatorname{Leb}\{s:\rho(s)=t\}\), and a rational threshold \(R\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A policy consists of measurable nonnegative finite real prices \(p_i(s)\) and allocation rates \(x_i(s)\geq0\), with \(\sum_{i\in I}x_i(s)\leq1\) almost everywhere and \(\int_0^1x_i(s)\,ds\leq q_i\). Rates divide arriving mass into purchases of single goods; they do not give individual buyers fractional bundles.
Define availability by \(A(s)=\{i\in I:\int_0^s x_i(a)\,da<q_i\}\), and require \(x_i(s)=0\) whenever \(i\notin A(s)\), almost everywhere. Unsold stock need not be cleared.
The always-available outside option \(0\) has utility \(0\), price \(0\), and rate \(x_0(s)=1-\sum_{i\in I}x_i(s)\). Write \(u_t(i,s)=v_t(i)-p_i(s)\) and \(u_t(0,s)=0\).
Future envy-freeness requires, for almost every \(s\), every \(i\in A(s)\cup\{0\}\) with \(x_i(s)>0\), every \(r\in[s,1]\), and every \(j\in A(r)\cup\{0\}\), that \(u_{\rho(s)}(i,s)\geq u_{\rho(s)}(j,r)\). Thus purchases and nonpurchases maximize current utility, and the seller breaks all ties, including ties with nonpurchase.
Is deciding whether such a policy achieves \(\int_0^1\sum_{i\in I}p_i(s)x_i(s)\,ds\geq R\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
For discrete unit-demand buyers and indivisible items, revenue maximization with future envy-freeness is APX-hard even with a known arrival order and seller tie-breaking ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.01529)).
No result settling this precise continuous mass-and-stream problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted valuations and supplies to nonnegative rationals and prices to nonnegative finite reals, consistent with the discrete market domains; a good is available exactly while its residual supply is positive.
  • Normalized total buyer mass to one, used unit arrival density on the given time horizon, and required the explicitly supplied support masses to equal the stream's type durations.
  • Added the zero-utility nonpurchase option and imposed future envy-freeness on nonpurchasing mass as well as purchasing mass, reflecting the discrete condition on every buyer.
  • Allowed seller tie-breaking between purchase and nonpurchase at zero utility, as required by the discrete hardness construction.
  • Interpreted allocation rates as splitting mass among unit-demand buyers, allowed leftover inventory, and imposed buyer-level conditions almost everywhere in arrival time while retaining comparison with every later time.
  • Specified the stream interval count and total binary input length as complexity parameters.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when every supply is \(1\), every valuation belongs to \(\{0,1,2\}\), all masses are dyadic rationals, and each supported type appears in exactly one stream interval.

The proof below bounds revenue for arbitrary measurable policies, including policies that split arriving mass among purchases and nonpurchase.

1. Construction from Vertex Cover

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Vertex Cover problem on finite simple undirected graphs. Given a graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(K\), it asks whether there is a set \(D\subseteq V\) with \(|D|\leq K\) containing an endpoint of every edge. See [Karp’s original paper](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

Write \(n=|V|\geq1\), \(h=|E|\), and let \(c(G)\) denote the minimum vertex-cover size. We may restrict to \(0\leq K\leq n\).

Choose \(b=\lceil\log_2(n+h+1)\rceil\) and \(\delta=2^{-b}\). Thus \((n+h)\delta<1\).

Construct the following society.

  • Goods: One good \(i_z\) for each vertex \(z\in V\), with supply \(q_{i_z}=1\).
  • Vertex types: For each vertex \(z\), a type \(a_z\) of mass \(\delta\), with \(v_{a_z}(i_z)=2\) and value \(0\) for every other good.
  • Edge types: For each edge \(e=\{z,w\}\), a type \(a_e\) of mass \(\delta\), with value \(1\) for \(i_z,i_w\) and value \(0\) for every other good.
  • Dummy type: One type with all valuations zero and mass \(1-(n+h)\delta>0\).

The stream consists first of all vertex types, then all edge types, and finally the dummy type. Each type occupies one interval of length equal to its mass. Orders within the vertex and edge phases are arbitrary and fixed.

Set the revenue threshold to \(R=\delta(2n+h-K)\).

We will prove the exact identity

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}(G)=\delta\bigl(2n+h-c(G)\bigr).\)

2. Availability and individual rationality

Consider any feasible policy for this constructed instance.

Since \(x_i(s)\leq1\) almost everywhere, for every \(s<1\),

\(\displaystyle \int_0^s x_i(a)\,da\leq s<1=q_i.\)

Consequently, every good is available at every time before \(1\). In particular, every good remains available throughout both the vertex and edge phases.

Fix a set of arrival times of full measure on which all policy conditions hold; call these times regular. At any regular time, a purchased good must provide utility at least zero, by comparison with the outside option at the same time. Thus

\(\displaystyle x_i(s)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_i(s)\leq v_{\rho(s)}(i).\)

Therefore the instantaneous revenue is at most \(2\) during any vertex interval, at most \(1\) during any edge interval, and zero during the dummy interval. Purchases of goods valued at zero necessarily have price zero.

3. Upper bound for every measurable policy

Let the edge phase be \(B=[n\delta,(n+h)\delta)\). Define

\(\displaystyle C=\bigl\{z\in V:\text{ there exists }r\in B\text{ with }p_{i_z}(r)\leq1\bigr\}.\)

This set is used only to analyze the policy.

A later low price discounts the entire earlier vertex block

Fix \(z\in C\), and choose a witnessing time \(r\in B\) with \(p_{i_z}(r)\leq1\).

Every regular arrival time \(s\) of type \(a_z\) precedes \(r\). Good \(i_z\) is available at \(r\), and its utility for this type at that time is

\(\displaystyle v_{a_z}(i_z)-p_{i_z}(r)=2-p_{i_z}(r)\geq1.\)

Future envy-freeness requires every option receiving positive rate at \(s\) to give utility at least \(1\). The outside option gives utility zero, and every other good gives utility at most zero because its valuation is zero and prices are nonnegative. Hence only \(i_z\) can receive positive rate. Since the rates, including the outside option, sum to one, \(x_{i_z}(s)=1\).

The comparison with \(i_z\) at time \(r\) then gives

\(\displaystyle 2-p_{i_z}(s)\geq2-p_{i_z}(r),\)

so \(p_{i_z}(s)\leq p_{i_z}(r)\leq1\).

Thus the entire vertex block for \(z\) contributes revenue at most \(\delta\). This argument legitimately uses an individual witnessing time \(r\): the task requires comparison with every future time.

For a vertex outside \(C\), the individual-rationality bound gives revenue at most \(2\delta\). Summing over vertex blocks yields

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Rev}_{V}\leq\delta(2n-|C|).\)

Only edges incident to \(C\) can generate revenue

Let \(E(C)=\{e\in E:e\cap C\neq\varnothing\}\).

Consider an edge \(e=\{z,w\}\notin E(C)\). By the definition of \(C\), both endpoint goods have price strictly greater than \(1\) at every time in the edge phase. At regular arrivals of type \(a_e\), neither endpoint good can be purchased, because its utility would be negative. Any other purchased good has valuation zero and therefore price zero.

Consequently, this edge block generates zero revenue. Each block corresponding to an edge in \(E(C)\) generates at most \(\delta\), by individual rationality and the rate bound. Therefore

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Rev}_{E}\leq\delta|E(C)|.\)

The dummy block contributes zero. Combining the bounds,

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Rev}\leq\delta\bigl(2n-|C|+|E(C)|\bigr).\)

Let \(u(C)=h-|E(C)|\), the number of edges uncovered by \(C\). Adding one endpoint of each uncovered edge produces a vertex cover of size at most \(|C|+u(C)\). Hence

\(\displaystyle c(G)\leq |C|+u(C).\)

It follows that every feasible policy satisfies

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Rev}\leq\delta\bigl(2n+h-|C|-u(C)\bigr)\leq\delta\bigl(2n+h-c(G)\bigr).\)

No integrality or constancy assumption was made about the policy.

4. A policy attaining the bound

Let \(D\) be any vertex cover. Use constant prices throughout the horizon:

\(\displaystyle p_{i_z}(s)=1\text{ if }z\in D,\qquad p_{i_z}(s)=2\text{ if }z\notin D.\)

Allocate each vertex block entirely to its own good. For each edge block, choose an endpoint in \(D\) and allocate the entire block to that endpoint’s good. Allocate the dummy block to nonpurchase.

Each good receives total allocation at most \((n+h)\delta<1\), so all supply and availability conditions hold, and every good remains available throughout the horizon.

Future envy-freeness holds as follows.

  • A vertex type in \(D\) obtains utility \(1\) from its own good; all other options have utility at most zero.
  • A vertex type outside \(D\) obtains utility zero from its own good; all other options have utility at most zero.
  • An edge type obtains utility zero from its assigned endpoint good. Its other endpoint good has utility at most zero, and all remaining goods have negative utility.
  • The dummy type obtains utility zero from nonpurchase; all goods have negative utility.

Prices and availability are constant, so these comparisons hold at every future time. Purchases at utility zero are permitted by the stipulated seller tie-breaking rule.

The revenue is

\(\displaystyle \delta\bigl(|D|+2(n-|D|)+h\bigr)=\delta(2n+h-|D|).\)

Taking \(D\) to be a minimum vertex cover attains the upper bound. Therefore

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}(G)=\delta\bigl(2n+h-c(G)\bigr).\)

5. Polynomial reduction and conclusion

The construction has \(m=n\) goods and \(\tau=k=n+h+1\) supported types and stream intervals. All masses and interval endpoints are dyadic rationals with bit length \(O(\log(n+h+1))\). The valuation matrix, supplies, and threshold also have polynomial total encoding length.

Using the proved identity,

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}(G)\geq R\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta(2n+h-c(G))\geq\delta(2n+h-K)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c(G)\leq K.\)

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction from Vertex Cover, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. A decision algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The decisive constraint survives divisible mass: even an arbitrarily small later purchase at price at most \(1\) forces the entire corresponding earlier vertex block to pay at most \(1\).
