The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses μ_t, rational per-claim amounts a_t, and a rational estate per claimant e with 0<e<A:=∑_t μ_t a_t, replicate the society by a factor k and give it estate ke. Determine the limiting Shapley payoff per claimant for each type as k grows through denominator-compatible values; in the designated-type case with a_{t^†}=1, decide whether that limiting payoff is less than ½.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a positive binary-rational claim \(a_t\); the binary-rational estate per claimant satisfies \(0<e<A:=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(\tau,L\) (there is no separate \(m\) parameter).
For every positive integer \(k\) with \(k\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\) for all types, form a claimant set \(N_k\) containing \(k\mu_t\) claimants of type \(t\), each with unchanged claim \(a_t\), and estate \(ke\). Its bankruptcy game is \(v_k(S)=\max\{0,ke-\sum_{i\in N_k\setminus S}a_{t(i)}\}\).
Let \(\phi_t^{(k)}\) be the Shapley payoff of any individual claimant of type \(t\), and let \(p_t:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi_t^{(k)}\) along these compatible integers, when the limit exists. The computational question asks for the exact limits; its decision version designates a supported type \(t^\dagger\) with \(a_{t^\dagger}=1\) and asks whether \(p_{t^\dagger}<\tfrac12\).

Is exact computation of these limiting payoffs polynomial-time solvable, and is the designated-type decision problem in \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hard?

1. Replication and the cited theorem

Write each mass in lowest terms, and let \(d\) be the least common multiple of its denominator. An integer \(k\) is compatible exactly when \(d\mid k\).

The base problem \(N_d\) has \(d\mu_t\) claimants of type \(t\), estate \(de\), and total claim \(dA\). Its claims are positive, and \(0<de<dA\). For every positive integer \(r\), the problem \(N_{rd}\) is precisely its \(r\)-fold replication.

Chun–Thomson’s Theorem 1 states that, for a fixed finite claims problem, each individual’s random-arrival award under replication converges to that individual’s proportional award. Its hypotheses therefore hold here, and its conclusion gives \(a_t(de)/(dA)=ea_t/A\). [Replication theorem](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/eco/rcer/papers/rcer_512.pdf).

For completeness, the next section proves the Shapley identification and convergence directly.

2. Direct proof of the limiting formula

Fix a supported type \(t\), put \(a=a_t\), and consider one of its claimants \(i\) in \(N_k\).

Shapley payoffs equal expected random-arrival awards

In a permutation used to compute the Shapley value, let \(S\) be the set preceding \(i\), and let \(R=N_k\setminus(S\cup\{i\})\). Write \(C_R=\sum_{j\in R}a_{t(j)}\). The marginal contribution of \(i\) is

\[ v_k(S\cup\{i\})-v_k(S) =(ke-C_R)_+-(ke-C_R-a)_+ =\min\{a,(ke-C_R)_+\}, \]

where \(x_+=\max\{0,x\}\). The last equality follows by considering \(ke-C_R\leq0\), \(0<ke-C_R<a\), and \(ke-C_R\geq a\).

Reverse the permutation. Then \(R\) becomes the set arriving before \(i\), so this marginal contribution is exactly the award obtained by paying earlier claimants their claims until the estate is exhausted. Reversal preserves the uniform distribution on permutations. Averaging proves the asserted identification.

Concentration of preceding claims

Generate a uniform random arrival order by assigning independent uniform priorities \(U_j\) on \([0,1]\) and sorting increasingly. Ties have probability zero, and symmetry makes all orders equally likely.

Conditional on \(U_i=u\), let

\[ B_k(u)=\sum_{j\ne i}a_{t(j)}\mathbf 1_{\{U_j<u\}}. \]

The indicators are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter \(u\). Define the fixed finite quantity \(M_2=\sum_s\mu_s a_s^2\). Then

\[ \mathbb E[B_k(u)]=u(kA-a), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(B_k(u)) =u(1-u)(kM_2-a^2) \leq \frac{kM_2}{4}. \]

Thus the mean of \(B_k(u)/k\) tends to \(uA\), while its variance tends to zero. Chebyshev’s inequality implies

\[ \frac{B_k(u)}k\longrightarrow uA \quad\text{in probability}. \]

Set \(q=e/A\), so \(0<q<1\). The random-arrival award conditional on \(U_i=u\) is

\[ X_k(u)=\min\{a,(ke-B_k(u))_+\}. \]

If \(u<q\), put \(\delta=e-uA>0\). For all sufficiently large \(k\), \(a/k<\delta/2\); on the event \(\lvert B_k(u)/k-uA\rvert<\delta/2\), one has \(B_k(u)<ke-a\), hence \(X_k(u)=a\). That event has probability tending to one.

If \(u>q\), put \(\delta=uA-e>0\). On the event \(\lvert B_k(u)/k-uA\rvert<\delta/2\), one has \(B_k(u)>ke\), hence \(X_k(u)=0\). Again, its probability tends to one.

Since \(0\leq X_k(u)\leq a\), these statements imply

\[ \mathbb E[X_k(u)]\longrightarrow \begin{cases} a,&u<q,\\ 0,&u>q. \end{cases} \]

The conditional expectation is bounded by the integrable constant \(a\). The exceptional point \(u=q\) has measure zero. Therefore dominated convergence gives

\[ \phi_t^{(k)} =\int_0^1\mathbb E[X_k(u)]\,du \longrightarrow \int_0^q a\,du =\frac{ea_t}{A}. \]

This argument applies along every compatible integer \(k\), proving existence of all requested limits.

3. Exact computation and bit complexity

Use the usual binary numerator–denominator encoding. Write

\[ \mu_t=\frac{r_t}{b_t}, \qquad a_t=\frac{c_t}{d_t}, \qquad e=\frac{h}{f}, \]

with positive integer numerators and denominators. Define

\[ Q=f\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t d_t, \qquad S=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}r_t c_t\frac{Q}{b_t d_t}, \qquad H=h\frac Qf, \qquad J_t=c_t\frac Q{d_t}. \]

Every indicated division is exact, and

\[ A=\frac SQ,\qquad e=\frac HQ,\qquad a_t=\frac{J_t}{Q}. \]

Accordingly, output the exact rational numbers

\[ p_t=\frac{HJ_t}{QS}. \]

The total binary length of the denominator factors is at most \(L\), so \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Every other integer above also has \(O(L)\) bits: products involve input integers and \(Q\), and summing \(\tau\) terms adds at most \(O(\log\tau)=O(L)\) bits.

There are \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations on these integers. Schoolbook integer arithmetic therefore computes all exact fractions in \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations, with total output length \(O(\tau L)\). Fractions need not be reduced to represent the answers exactly. If reduced fractions are required, elementary Euclidean reduction gives the conservative bound \(O(\tau L^3)\).

For the designated type, compare the integers

\[ 2H<S. \]

This decides the problem exactly in \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations, including equality cases. No replicated population is constructed.

4. Why finite-instance hardness does not transfer

The cited NP-completeness theorem concerns a unit claimant’s Shapley payoff in a finite bankruptcy game. [Discrete hardness theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.22208v2).

The present question concerns its limit under replication. The proof above identifies that limit solely through \(e/A\). Hence the finite-instance hardness result does not establish hardness of the limiting problem; the explicit rational-arithmetic algorithm settles the latter in polynomial time.
