The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu\) over utility types \(t\), item labels \(I\), a population-to-supply ratio \(r\), and \(k\) rounds, find a nonnegative mass distribution \(q_{t,h}\) over histories \(h=(S_1,\ldots,S_k)\) of integral bundles such that \(\sum_h q_{t,h} = \mu_t\), each item has normalized capacity \(1/r\) in every round, and every supported type-history satisfies overall proportionality: \(\sum_l u_t(S_l) \ge (k/r) \sum_o u_t(o)\); among these schedules maximize aggregate welfare.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) utility types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) item labels \(I\). Each type has additive, time-independent utilities \(u_t(o)\in\mathbb Q\), which may be positive, negative, or zero. The input includes a binary rational population-to-supply ratio \(r\geq2\) and a positive integer \(k\), encoded in unary, with \(k/r\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\). Let \(L\) be the total binary encoding length of the rational input data.
A history is \(h=(S_1,\ldots,S_k)\), with \(S_\ell\subseteq I\); empty bundles are allowed. A schedule assigns nonnegative rational masses \(q_{t,h}\) satisfying \(\sum_hq_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and exhaustive allocation \(\sum_t\sum_{h:o\in S_\ell}q_{t,h}=1/r\) for every \(o\in I\) and every round \(\ell\).
Every pair with \(q_{t,h}>0\) must satisfy overall proportionality: \(\sum_{\ell=1}^ku_t(S_\ell)\geq(k/r)\sum_{o\in I}u_t(o)\). Maximize aggregate welfare \(\sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}\sum_{\ell=1}^ku_t(S_\ell)\) over such schedules.

Is exact welfare maximization for this mass allocation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies with \(n\geq2\) and \(k\in n\mathbb Z_{>0}\), an overall proportional and Pareto-optimal sequence always exists and can be found by exponential enumeration; computing such a sequence is also FPT in \(n+m\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.01644)
No result settling this support-encoded mass welfare problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the type masses to sum to one.
  • Specified additive, time-independent, binary rational utilities of arbitrary sign, matching the mixed goods-and-chores model.
  • Interpreted every item capacity as an equality because the discrete model requires exhaustive allocation, including chores.
  • Carried over the anchor's population and round conditions as rational r ≥ 2 and positive integral k/r.
  • Encoded k in unary so polynomial time may depend on the number of explicitly described rounds; included all rational data in L.
  • Allowed empty bundles and specified rational schedule masses.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Exact welfare maximization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(\tau=2\), \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\), \(r=k=2\), and all utilities are positive integers. The two types can moreover have identical utilities except for a one-unit difference on one item.

We prove this by a polynomial reduction from positive-integer SUBSET SUM: given binary-encoded positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_N,T\), determine whether some subset sums to \(T\). This is a standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard problem; see [Knapsack and Subset Sum with Small Items](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.04035v1).

1. Encoding subset sums by bounded integer relations

Given the SUBSET SUM instance, put \(A=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_i\) and \(M=1+T+A\). Introduce item labels

\(\displaystyle I=\{p_1,\ldots,p_N,b_1,\ldots,b_N,s\}\),

with positive integer weights

\(\displaystyle w_{p_i}=M5^{i-1}+a_i,\qquad w_{b_i}=M5^{i-1},\qquad w_s=M\sum_{i=1}^{N}5^{i-1}+T.\)

For a vector \(d\) indexed by \(I\), write \(w\cdot d=\sum_{o\in I}w_od_o\).

Encoding lemma. The SUBSET SUM instance is a YES-instance if and only if there exists \(d\in\{-1,0,1\}^{I}\) satisfying \(d_s=1\) and \(w\cdot d=0\).

Proof. Suppose such a vector exists. Write \(\alpha_i=d_{p_i}\) and \(\beta_i=d_{b_i}\). Expanding the equality gives

\(\displaystyle 0=M\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+\alpha_i+\beta_i)5^{i-1}+T+\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_i\alpha_i.\)

The last two terms have absolute value at most \(T+A=M-1\). They are a multiple of \(M\), so they must equal zero. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle T+\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_i\alpha_i=0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+\alpha_i+\beta_i)5^{i-1}=0.\)

Each coefficient \(c_i=1+\alpha_i+\beta_i\) belongs to \(\{-1,0,1,2,3\}\). Reducing the second equality modulo \(5\) forces \(c_1=0\), since zero is the only multiple of \(5\) in this set. Dividing by \(5\) and repeating forces every \(c_i=0\).

Thus \(\alpha_i+\beta_i=-1\). Because both coordinates belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), the only possibilities are

\(\displaystyle (\alpha_i,\beta_i)=(-1,0)\quad\text{or}\quad(0,-1).\)

It follows that \(\sum_{i:\alpha_i=-1}a_i=T\), giving the required subset.

Conversely, let \(J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}\) satisfy \(\sum_{i\in J}a_i=T\). Set \(d_s=1\), and choose

\(\displaystyle (d_{p_i},d_{b_i})=(-1,0)\ \text{for }i\in J,\qquad (d_{p_i},d_{b_i})=(0,-1)\ \text{for }i\notin J.\)

Every coefficient \(1+d_{p_i}+d_{b_i}\) vanishes, and the remaining term is \(T-\sum_{i\in J}a_i=0\). Hence \(w\cdot d=0\). This proves the lemma.

2. Constructing the society

Use the item labels above, and set

\(\displaystyle \tau=2,\qquad \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad r=k=2.\)

These satisfy all input promises, including \(k/r=1\). Define utilities by

\(\displaystyle u_2(o)=2w_o,\qquad u_1(o)=2w_o+\mathbf{1}_{\{o=s\}}.\)

All utilities are positive integers. Let \(W=\sum_{o\in I}w_o\).

For a history \(h=(S_1,S_2)\), define its count vector and deviation vector by

\(\displaystyle x_o(h)=\mathbf{1}_{\{o\in S_1\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{o\in S_2\}},\qquad d_o(h)=x_o(h)-1.\)

Thus \(x(h)\in\{0,1,2\}^{I}\) and \(d(h)\in\{-1,0,1\}^{I}\).

Since \(k/r=1\), proportionality for a positive-mass history is precisely

\(\displaystyle 2w\cdot d(h)+d_s(h)\geq 0\quad\text{for type }1,\)

and

\(\displaystyle 2w\cdot d(h)\geq 0\quad\text{for type }2.\)

3. Exhaustive allocation forces equality in every used history

We first show that every positive-mass history, for either type, satisfies \(w\cdot d(h)\geq 0\).

This follows directly for type \(2\). For type \(1\), the number \(w\cdot d(h)\) is an integer. If it were negative, it would be at most \(-1\); since \(d_s(h)\leq 1\), we would have

\(\displaystyle 2w\cdot d(h)+d_s(h)\leq -2+1=-1,\)

contradicting proportionality.

Now consider any feasible schedule. Exhaustive allocation in each of the two rounds gives, for every item \(o\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}x_o(h)=\frac12+\frac12=1.\)

Also, \(\sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}=\mu_1+\mu_2=1\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}d_o(h)=0\)

for every item, and hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}\,w\cdot d(h)=0.\)

Every summand is nonnegative. Consequently, every positive-mass history satisfies

\(\displaystyle w\cdot d(h)=0.\)

For type \(1\), proportionality now further implies \(d_s(h)\geq 0\), or equivalently \(x_s(h)\in\{1,2\}\).

This argument applies to arbitrary rational mixtures of histories.

4. Computing the optimum in NO-instances

For any feasible schedule, its welfare is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}\sum_{o\in I}u_t(o)x_o(h)=2\sum_{o\in I}w_o\sum_{t,h}q_{t,h}x_o(h)+\sum_hq_{1,h}x_s(h).\)

Using exhaustive allocation, this becomes

\(\displaystyle 2W+\sum_hq_{1,h}x_s(h).\)

Suppose the SUBSET SUM instance is a NO-instance. A positive-mass type-\(1\) history with \(x_s(h)=2\) would give a vector \(d(h)\) with \(d_s(h)=1\) and \(w\cdot d(h)=0\), contradicting the encoding lemma. Thus every such history has \(x_s(h)=1\), and every feasible schedule has welfare

\(\displaystyle 2W+\sum_hq_{1,h}=2W+\frac12.\)

There is a feasible schedule achieving this value: for each type, assign mass \(1/4\) to each of the histories \((I,\varnothing)\) and \((\varnothing,I)\). Every history receives every item exactly once, so proportionality holds with equality. Every item receives total mass \(1/2\) in each round.

Therefore, in a NO-instance, the exact optimum is \(2W+1/2\).

5. Computing the optimum in YES-instances

Suppose the SUBSET SUM instance is a YES-instance. By the encoding lemma, choose \(d\in\{-1,0,1\}^{I}\) with \(d_s=1\) and \(w\cdot d=0\). Define

\(\displaystyle x^{(1)}=\mathbf{1}+d,\qquad x^{(2)}=\mathbf{1}-d.\)

Both vectors belong to \(\{0,1,2\}^{I}\), and they satisfy

\(\displaystyle x^{(1)}_o+x^{(2)}_o=2\quad\text{for every }o,\qquad w\cdot x^{(1)}=w\cdot x^{(2)}=W.\)

Also, \(x^{(1)}_s=2\) and \(x^{(2)}_s=0\).

We explicitly realize these counts while meeting the supply constraint in each round. For any vector \(x\in\{0,1,2\}^{I}\), define

\(\displaystyle H(x)=\bigl(\{o:x_o\geq 1\},\{o:x_o=2\}\bigr).\)

This history has count vector \(x\). Let \(H^{\mathrm{rev}}(x)\) denote the history obtained by swapping its rounds.

For type \(t\), assign mass \(1/4\) to each of \(H(x^{(t)})\) and \(H^{\mathrm{rev}}(x^{(t)})\), merging identical histories if necessary. These masses sum to \(1/2\) for each type. In each round, type \(t\) receives item \(o\) with mass \(x^{(t)}_o/4\). Hence its total allocated mass is

\(\displaystyle \frac{x^{(1)}_o+x^{(2)}_o}{4}=\frac12,\)

as required.

Every type-\(1\) history has utility \(2W+2\), exceeding its proportionality threshold \(2W+1\). Every type-\(2\) history has utility \(2W\), exactly its threshold. Thus the schedule is feasible, with welfare

\(\displaystyle \frac12(2W+2)+\frac12(2W)=2W+1.\)

No schedule can have greater welfare: the formula from Section 4 and the bounds \(x_s(h)\leq 2\), \(\sum_hq_{1,h}=1/2\) give

\(\displaystyle 2W+\sum_hq_{1,h}x_s(h)\leq 2W+1.\)

Therefore, in a YES-instance, the exact optimum is \(2W+1\).

6. Encoding length and complexity conclusion

There are \(m=2N+1\) items. Each constructed weight has binary length \(O(N+\log M)\), because \(5^{N-1}\) has binary length \(O(N)\). Moreover, \(\log M\) is polynomially bounded by the SUBSET SUM input length. All utilities and the threshold \(2W+1\) are therefore computable with polynomial encoding length and in polynomial time.

We have proved the exact equivalence:

  • A YES-instance has optimum \(2W+1\).
  • A NO-instance has optimum \(2W+1/2\).

Thus deciding whether the optimum is at least \(2W+1\) solves SUBSET SUM. Exact welfare maximization is consequently \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with the fixed parameters and positive utilities stated above. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,k,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
