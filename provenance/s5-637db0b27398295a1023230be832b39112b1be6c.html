The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational influence rates \(\kappa_{tu}\), and a per-capita message budget \(b\), consider populations of size \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) voters of each type and independent directed edges from type \(t\) to type \(u\) live with probability \(\kappa_{tu}/N\). Each type includes its initial ranking; use the paper’s three-candidate pessimistic revision rule and MI-IC diffusion. Choose fractions \(x_{t,I}\) of each type to seed with each nonempty message vector \(I\in\{-,\cdot,+\}^3\), subject to \(\sum_I x_{t,I}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,I}|I|x_{t,I}\leq b\). Is there a polynomial-time algorithm that finds a feasible allocation maximizing the limit, as \(N\to\infty\), of expected margin increase divided by \(N\), or achieves a stated approximation ratio?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Each type has a strict ranking of \(C=\{c_0,c_1,c_2\}\); types may share rankings but have different influence rates. Here \(m=3\), and \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Input also includes nonnegative binary rational rates \(\kappa_{tu}\) and budget \(b>0\); \(L\) is the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{t,I}\geq0\), for \(I\in\{-,\cdot,+\}^3\setminus\{(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\}\), satisfying \(\sum_Ix_{t,I}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,I}|I|x_{t,I}\leq b\), where \(|I|\) counts nonempty coordinates.
For sufficiently large \(N\) with all \(N\mu_t\) integral, assign vector \(I\) to \(\lfloor Nx_{t,I}\rfloor\) distinct type-\(t\) voters before drawing the graph. Each ordered pair of distinct voters of types \(t,u\) has a live edge independently with probability \(\kappa_{tu}/N\).
MI-IC uses this single live graph for every message: a voter receives a signed candidate message exactly when reachable from a seed sending it, including paths of length zero. Repeated copies count once; every received message is forwarded, including messages subsequently canceled. Seeds also receive other seeds’ messages.
Cancel opposite signs about each candidate, yielding effective signs \(q_c\in\{-,\cdot,+\}\). For initial ranking \(a\succ d\succ e\), pessimistic revision makes the voter choose \(d\) exactly when \((q_a=\cdot\land q_d=+)\lor(q_a=-\land q_d\in\{\cdot,+\})\); otherwise the voter chooses \(a\).
Let \(V_i^{(N)}(x)\) be the resulting vote count and \(M_N(x)=V_0^{(N)}(x)-\max\{V_1^{(N)}(x),V_2^{(N)}(x)\}\). Define \(F(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\mathbb E[M_N(x)-M_N(0)]\) along the integral population sizes, and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\sup_{x\text{ feasible}}F(x)\).
A multiplicative \(\rho\)-approximation returns a feasible rational allocation \(x\) with \(F(x)\geq\rho\,\mathrm{OPT}\).

Does this continuous problem admit an algorithm running in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\) with some polynomial-time computable ratio \(\rho(m,\tau,L)\in(0,1]\), or is achieving every such positive ratio \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite voter networks, three-candidate pessimistic revision admits no polynomial-time positive multiplicative approximation, even with a size-dependent ratio, unless \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\). [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.03779v2).
No result settling this exact continuous allocation problem was located in the literature search.

  • Specified nonnegative influence rates, a positive rational budget, strict initial rankings, and exactly three candidates; zero influence rates represent absent connections.
  • Allowed distinct types with identical rankings because their influence profiles may differ.

  • Specified graph-independent seeding, disjoint seed groups, floor rounding, and a limit along population sizes making all type counts integral; a zero allocation coordinate creates no seeds.

Conclusion and computational convention

Under the usual binary-output interpretation of a polynomial-time computable ratio, achieving every such positive ratio is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard, already with \(m=3\), budget \(b=1\), dyadic masses, and no influence between distinct types.

The computational convention matters: evaluating the ratio returns an explicitly encoded positive binary rational in polynomial time. Consequently, its value is at least \(2^{-p(L)}\) for some fixed polynomial \(p\). More generally, the proof below rules out every ratio satisfying such a lower bound.

This is a reduction for the continuous problem itself; it does not transfer the [finite-network result supplied in the question](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.03779).

1. An integer gadget with initial imbalance two

We reduce from binary-encoded \(\mathsf{SUBSET\ SUM}\): given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_s\) and an integer target \(B_0\) with \(0<B_0<\sum_i a_i\), decide whether some subset sums to \(B_0\).

Put \(w_i=4a_i\), \(A=\sum_i w_i\), and \(B=4B_0\). Thus every \(w_i\), as well as \(A\) and \(B\), is divisible by four, and \(0<B<A\).

Choose a power of two \(W>16(A+B+1)\), taking the smallest such power, and define

\( H=\frac{W-2A-2B}{4}. \)

This is a positive integer satisfying \(H>A+B\). Introduce four additional weights

\( d_1=H,\qquad d_2=H+B+1,\qquad d_3=H+A,\qquad d_4=H+B-1. \)

The total weight is exactly \(W\), since

\( A+d_1+d_2+d_3+d_4=4H+2A+2B=W. \)

Initially place all original weights and \(d_1,d_2\) on side one, and \(d_3,d_4\) on side two. Their weight difference is

\( A+d_1+d_2-d_3-d_4=2. \)

Claim. These weights admit an exactly balanced partition if and only if the original subset-sum instance is a yes-instance.

Proof. A balanced side has weight

\( W/2=2H+A+B. \)

It must contain exactly two additional weights. Indeed, with at most one additional weight its weight is at most \(H+2A<2H+A+B\); with at least three it is at least \(3H>2H+A+B\).

For each possible pair, the required contribution from the original weights is:

| Additional weights selected | Required original weight |
|---|---:|
| \(d_1,d_2\) | \(A-1\) |
| \(d_3,d_4\) | \(1\) |
| \(d_1,d_3\) | \(B\) |
| \(d_2,d_4\) | \(A-B\) |
| \(d_1,d_4\) | \(A+1\) |
| \(d_2,d_3\) | \(-1\) |

The first two are impossible because original weights are divisible by four. The last two are outside the interval \([0,A]\). The remaining possibilities are a subset of weight \(B\), or its complement of weight \(A-B\). Both are equivalent to the original instance having a subset summing to \(B_0\). \(\square\)

Because \(W\) is even, every unbalanced partition has integer imbalance at least two.

2. A random graph lemma

The following elementary lemma supplies the nearly indivisible voting blocks.

Lemma. Fix \(K\geq16\), put \(\varepsilon=e^{-K/4}\), and fix \(k\geq K\). In the directed random graph on \(n\) vertices in which every ordered pair of distinct vertices is an edge independently with probability \(k/n\), the following hold as \(n\to\infty\):

  • With probability tending to one, there is a strongly connected component containing more than \((1-2\varepsilon)n\) vertices.
  • Every fixed deterministic vertex set of positive limiting density intersects that component with probability tending to one.

Proof. First exclude directed cuts of intermediate size. For a fixed set \(S\) of size \(j\), the probability that no edge leaves \(S\) is at most

\( \exp\!\left(-Kj(n-j)/n\right). \)

Write \(u=j/n\) and \(r=\min(u,1-u)\). The binomial coefficient satisfies

\( \binom{n}{j}\leq \exp(nh(u)), \)

where \(h(u)=-u\log u-(1-u)\log(1-u)\). Moreover,

\( h(u)=h(r)\leq r(\log(1/r)+1). \)

For \(\varepsilon\leq u\leq1-\varepsilon\), we have \(r\geq\varepsilon\), so

\( h(u)\leq r(K/4+1), \qquad Ku(1-u)\geq Kr/2. \)

A union bound therefore gives

\( \Pr(\text{some such }S\text{ has no outgoing edge}) \leq n\exp\!\left(-n\varepsilon(K/4-1)\right), \)

which tends to zero.

Consider a graph without any such intermediate cut. Its condensation into strongly connected components is a directed acyclic graph. If every component had fewer than \(\varepsilon n\) vertices, successively removing sink components would produce a union with no outgoing edge and size between \(\varepsilon n\) and \(2\varepsilon n\). This contradicts the cut property. Hence some component \(Q\) has at least \(\varepsilon n\) vertices.

Choose \(v\in Q\). Let \(R^+(v)\) be its forward reachable set and \(R^-(v)\) its backward reachable set. The first has no outgoing edge; the second has no incoming edge. An intermediate set with no incoming edge is also excluded, because its complement would be an intermediate set with no outgoing edge.

Both reachable sets contain \(Q\), so both have more than \((1-\varepsilon)n\) vertices. Their intersection is exactly \(Q\). Consequently,

\( |Q|=|R^+(v)\cap R^-(v)|>(1-2\varepsilon)n. \)

This proves the first assertion.

Since \(\varepsilon<1/4\), such a component is unique. The random graph distribution is invariant under vertex permutations. Conditional on its existence and its size \(q\), its vertex set is therefore uniform among all \(q\)-element subsets.

For a fixed set \(S\) of size \(j\), its probability of avoiding the component is at most

\( \frac{\binom{n-q}{j}}{\binom{n}{j}} \leq (1-q/n)^j \leq (2\varepsilon)^j. \)

If \(j/n\) has a positive limit, this tends to zero. \(\square\)

3. Constructing the continuous instance

Let \(n\) denote the bit length of the original subset-sum instance, and set \(K=2^n\). Small instances with \(K<16\) can be handled separately.

Create one type for each original and additional weight, so \(\tau=s+4\). A type of weight \(w\) has mass

\( \mu=w/W. \)

All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one.

Give the types initially on side one ranking \(c_1\succ c_2\succ c_0\), and those initially on side two ranking \(c_2\succ c_1\succ c_0\). Set

\( \kappa_{tt}=KW,\qquad \kappa_{tu}=0\text{ for }t\neq u,\qquad b=1. \)

Thus types never influence one another. Within a type of weight \(w\), the population size is \(n_t=Nw/W\), and the edge probability is

\( \frac{\kappa_{tt}}{N} =\frac{KW}{N} =\frac{Kw}{n_t}. \)

The lemma applies with \(k=Kw\geq K\), using the same \(\varepsilon=e^{-K/4}\) for every type. These probabilities are valid for all sufficiently large \(N\), as required by the question.

The constructed input has polynomial bit length. In particular, \(\log K=n\), \(\log W=O(n)\), and every rate is an integer with \(O(n)\) bits.

4. Every allocation induces an ideal block assignment

Fix any allocation \(x\), including a real allocation.

For each type \(t\), let \(R_t(x)\) be the set of signed messages occurring in vectors \(I\) with \(x_{t,I}>0\). Each message in this set is seeded on a deterministic set of positive limiting density: its number of seeds is the sum of the corresponding \(\lfloor Nx_{t,I}\rfloor\).

By the lemma, with probability tending to one, every such seed set intersects the large strongly connected component of its type. There are only finitely many types and signed messages, so these events hold simultaneously with probability tending to one.

Every voter in that component consequently receives exactly the messages in \(R_t(x)\):

  • Every message in \(R_t(x)\) has a seed in the component and reaches every component voter.
  • No other message is seeded in the type.
  • No message arrives from another type.
  • Forwarding continues after cancellation, as stipulated in the question.

After cancellation and revision, all component voters make the same choice. Denote that choice by \(\sigma_t(x)\). It is either \(c_1\) or \(c_2\), because revision can only select the initial first or second candidate.

Define the ideal block assignment by having every voter of type \(t\) choose \(\sigma_t(x)\). If \(W_1(x)\) is the total integer weight assigned to \(c_1\), its ideal normalized margin is

\( M^{\mathrm{ideal}}(x) =-\frac{1+|2W_1(x)-W|/W}{2}. \)

On the simultaneous high-probability event, actual and ideal votes differ on at most \(2\varepsilon N\) voters. Changing one vote between \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) changes the margin by at most one. Hence their normalized margins differ by at most \(2\varepsilon\).

The normalized margins are bounded, so the exceptional event contributes a quantity tending to zero to their expected difference. Therefore,

\( \limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb E[M_N(x)-M_N(0)] \leq \frac{2-|2W_1(x)-W|}{2W}+2\varepsilon. \)

Here we used the initial weight imbalance of two. This argument bounds the expected margin directly; it does not interchange expectation and maximum.

In particular, whenever the ideal block assignment is unbalanced,

\( F(x)\leq2\varepsilon. \)

Indeed, its nonzero integer imbalance is at least two. The displayed limsup bound also shows that this conclusion needs no separate general theorem establishing existence of the limit.

For a rational allocation, its ideal assignment is computable in polynomial time: inspect which coordinates have positive allocation, collect their signed messages, cancel signs, and apply the stated revision rule.

5. The approximation gap

Yes-instances

Suppose the subset-sum instance is a yes-instance. Section 1 supplies an exactly balanced partition of the block weights.

For every type whose candidate differs between the initial and balanced assignments, seed every voter of that type with a single negative message about its initial top candidate. Seed no voters in unchanged types.

This allocation is rational and feasible: its cost is the total mass of flipped types, which is at most \(b=1\). Every flipped voter receives the negative message at path length zero and chooses its second candidate. There are no messages from other types.

Thus the resulting votes are exactly balanced for every admissible \(N\), independently of the graph. Its gain is exactly

\( F(x)=1/W. \)

Consequently, \(\mathrm{OPT}\geq1/W\).

No-instances

If the subset-sum instance is a no-instance, no assignment of whole blocks is balanced. Every allocation therefore has an unbalanced ideal assignment. Section 4 gives

\( \mathrm{OPT}\leq2\varepsilon=2e^{-K/4}. \)

The no-instance optimum need not be zero. The reduction explicitly controls the possible gains outside the large strongly connected components.

6. Why every explicitly computable positive ratio is excluded

Suppose a polynomial-time algorithm returns a feasible rational allocation satisfying the approximation guarantee for some positive ratio \(\rho(3,\tau,L)\), and that this ratio is computable as an explicit binary rational in polynomial time.

There is a fixed polynomial \(p\) bounding the number of bits in its denominator. Because its numerator is a positive integer,

\( \rho(3,\tau,L)\geq2^{-p(L)}. \)

For our reduction, \(L\) is polynomial in \(n\), whereas \(K=2^n\). Hence, for all sufficiently large \(n\),

\( 2e^{-2^n/4}<\frac{2^{-p(L)}}{W} \leq\frac{\rho(3,\tau,L)}{W}. \)

On a yes-instance, the returned allocation must therefore satisfy

\( F(x)\geq\rho(3,\tau,L)\,\mathrm{OPT} \geq\frac{\rho(3,\tau,L)}{W} >2\varepsilon. \)

Its ideal block assignment must be balanced, since every unbalanced ideal assignment has gain at most \(2\varepsilon\).

We can now decide subset sum in polynomial time:

  • Construct the continuous instance and run the approximation algorithm.
  • Decode its ideal block assignment.
  • Accept exactly when that assignment is balanced.

On yes-instances the guarantee forces acceptance. On no-instances a balanced block assignment does not exist. The finitely many source lengths below the threshold can be handled by exhaustive search, a constant amount of work for the fixed hypothetical algorithm.

Thus such an approximation algorithm would imply \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\).

The explicit-ratio convention is substantive. A notion of polynomial-time computability based solely on requested absolute precision can admit super-exponentially small real ratios that have no polynomial-length explicit binary encoding. The theorem proved here uses the binary-output interpretation stated at the outset.

5. Approximation gap

The yes-instance allocation is valid. Seeding every voter of a flipped type costs exactly that type’s mass, and the total cost is at most one. Integral population sizes make these full-type allocations exact despite flooring.

Path-length-zero reception ensures every flipped voter switches to its second candidate. No other message can interfere. The resulting balance is deterministic, and the gain is exactly \(1/W\).

For an unbalanced ideal assignment, the integer imbalance is at least two. Thus its ideal gain is nonpositive, and the established error bound gives \(F(x)\leq2\varepsilon\). The no-instance upper bound follows for the objective values defined by the task.

The proof correctly allows positive residual gains in no-instances; it does not incorrectly claim a zero-versus-positive gap.

6. Ratio bound and polynomial decoding

Under explicit binary rational output, polynomial computation time bounds the denominator’s encoding length. Positivity then yields the claimed lower bound \(2^{-p(L)}\).

Because \(L\) and \(\log W\) are polynomially bounded in the source length while \(K=2^n\), the separation inequality holds for all sufficiently large source lengths.

A yes-instance output must therefore have a balanced ideal assignment. A no-instance admits no balanced assignment at all. Decoding requires only exact positivity tests on rational allocations, message cancellation, revision, and integer weight addition, all in polynomial time.

Handling the finitely many smaller source lengths separately is legitimate for each fixed hypothetical approximation algorithm.

The proof establishes hardness under the stated encoding convention. It expressly does not establish the same claim for every possible representation of polynomial-time computable real ratios. Given the binary input and rational output setting, its explicit convention is a natural reading of the task.
