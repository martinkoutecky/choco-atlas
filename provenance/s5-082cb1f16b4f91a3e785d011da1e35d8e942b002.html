2. Feasibility and the inherited classification

Write $x_0=0$, $x_4=1$, and define

$$ w_j=v(x_{j-1},x_j),\qquad j\in\{1,2,3,4\}. $$

Because every cohort has the same valuation and the assignment is bijective, the envy inequalities are equivalent to

$$ \max_j w_j-\min_j w_j\le\varepsilon. $$

Indeed, the comparison between the cohorts receiving a minimum-value piece and a maximum-value piece gives this necessary inequality. Conversely, this inequality implies every required pairwise comparison.

Thus masses and role labels do not affect feasibility. Removing masses gives a reduction to the four-individual problem. Introducing four distinct roles with masses $1/4$ gives the reverse reduction. Both leave the evaluator, tolerance, and cuts unchanged; assignments are transferred by relabeling.

The cited theorem establishes $\mathsf{PPAD}$-completeness for four identical, potentially non-monotone valuations. Its preliminaries use nonnegative continuous valuations, zero values for empty intervals, Lipschitz normalization, and the stated computational representation. Consequently, the collective problem inherits the stipulated classification. [Hollender–Rubinstein, Theorem 8.3 and Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.02075v2#Thmtheorem8.3).

3. Proven: an exact equal-value partition exists

This existence argument uses nonnegativity, continuity, and zero values for empty intervals; monotonicity is unnecessary.

Parameterize partitions by the simplex

$$ \Delta=\left\{p\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{4}:\sum_{j=1}^{4}p_j=1\right\}. $$

For $p\in\Delta$, set $x_0(p)=0$ and $x_j(p)=\sum_{h=1}^{j}p_h$. Define

$$ w_j(p)=v(x_{j-1}(p),x_j(p)),\qquad M(p)=\max_jw_j(p), $$

and

$$ d_j(p)=M(p)-w_j(p),\qquad S(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}d_j(p). $$

The Lipschitz hypothesis makes $v$ continuous. Therefore all these functions are continuous. Every $d_j$ is nonnegative.

Define

$$ T_j(p)=\frac{p_j+d_j(p)}{1+S(p)}. $$

The denominator is positive, every coordinate is nonnegative, and

$$ \sum_{j=1}^{4}T_j(p)=\frac{1+S(p)}{1+S(p)}=1. $$

Thus $T$ is a continuous self-map of the nonempty compact convex set $\Delta$. Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem gives $p^\ast=T(p^\ast)$. Its coordinate equations imply

$$ d_j(p^\ast)=p_j^\ast S(p^\ast). $$

Suppose $S(p^\ast)>0$. Choose $j$ attaining $M(p^\ast)$. Then $d_j(p^\ast)=0$, hence $p_j^\ast=0$. This piece is empty, so its value is zero. Since it attains the maximum and every value is nonnegative, all four values are zero. That implies $S(p^\ast)=0$, a contradiction.

Therefore $S(p^\ast)=0$. Its nonnegative summands all vanish, proving

$$ w_1(p^\ast)=w_2(p^\ast)=w_3(p^\ast)=w_4(p^\ast). $$

The exact cuts can be irrational. The next construction produces rational approximate cuts.

4. Proven: a quadratic-grid algorithm

4.1. Evaluate all grid intervals

Let

$$ q=\left\lceil\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\right\rceil,\qquad N=2^{q+5},\qquad k=q+5. $$

Then

$$ \frac{32}{\varepsilon}\le N<\frac{64}{\varepsilon}. $$

For every integer pair $0\le a\le b\le N$, evaluate $v(a/N,b/N)$ at precision $k$. Denote the returned rational by $\widehat v_{a,b}$. The guarantee is

$$ \left|\widehat v_{a,b}-v(a/N,b/N)\right|\le\frac1N. $$

There are exactly

$$ \frac{(N+1)(N+2)}2 $$

such evaluations.

4.2. Group intervals into overlapping value bands

For each integer $\ell$, define the half-open band

$$ J_\ell=\left[\frac{8\ell}{N},\frac{8(\ell+2)}{N}\right). $$

An interval pair $(a,b)$ belongs to the edge list $E_\ell$ when $\widehat v_{a,b}\in J_\ell$.

Every approximation belongs to exactly two bands. Specifically, if

$$ t=\left\lfloor\frac{N\widehat v_{a,b}}8\right\rfloor, $$

those bands have indices $t-1$ and $t$. This follows directly from the half-open inequalities defining $J_\ell$.

Because $v$ takes values in $[0,1]$,

$$ -\frac1N\le\widehat v_{a,b}\le1+\frac1N. $$

Since $N$ is divisible by $8$, it suffices to consider

$$ \ell\in\{-2,-1,\ldots,N/8\}. $$

There are $O(N)$ bands, and each interval pair appears in two edge lists. Hence

$$ \sum_\ell |E_\ell|=O(N^2). $$

All band assignments can be computed by exact rational arithmetic.

4.3. Search for a path of four edges

For each band, seek integers

$$ 0=a_0\le a_1\le a_2\le a_3\le a_4=N $$

such that $(a_{h-1},a_h)\in E_\ell$ for every $h\in\{1,2,3,4\}$.

Use four successive reachability layers. Initialize $R_0=\{0\}$ and compute

$$ R_h=\{b:\text{there exists }a\in R_{h-1}\text{ with }(a,b)\in E_\ell\}, \qquad h\in\{1,2,3,4\}. $$

Store one predecessor for each reached position. If $N\in R_4$, reconstruct the path and output

$$ x_1=\frac{a_1}{N},\qquad x_2=\frac{a_2}{N},\qquad x_3=\frac{a_3}{N}. $$

Assign the positional pieces to the four listed cohorts in order. Separate reachability layers ensure that exactly four edges are used, including when some edges have $a=b$.

4.4. Soundness

For an accepted path, all four approximate values lie in one band of width $16/N$. Therefore their spread is strictly less than $16/N$.

Each true value differs from its approximation by at most $1/N$. Consequently,

$$ \max_jw_j-\min_jw_j < \frac{16}{N}+\frac2N = \frac{18}{N} \le \frac{9\varepsilon}{16} < \varepsilon. $$

Thus every output is feasible.

4.5. Termination

Take an exact equal-value partition from Section 3. Round each internal cut downward to the grid with denominator $N$. This preserves cut order and moves each cut by less than $1/N$.

Each piece has at most two moved endpoints. The Lipschitz bound changes each piece value by at most $2/N$. Since the original values were equal, the rounded values have spread at most $4/N$. Their approximations therefore have spread at most

$$ \frac4N+\frac2N=\frac6N. $$

Let $u$ be the minimum of these four approximations, and put

$$ \ell=\left\lfloor\frac{Nu}{8}\right\rfloor. $$

Then

$$ \frac{8\ell}{N}\le u<\frac{8(\ell+1)}N. $$

Every approximation is at least $u$ and at most $u+6/N$. Hence every approximation lies below

$$ \frac{8(\ell+1)}N+\frac6N = \frac{8\ell+14}{N} < \frac{8(\ell+2)}N. $$

All four approximations therefore belong to $J_\ell$. The rounded partition supplies the required path of four edges in $E_\ell$, so the algorithm terminates.

4.6. Running time

Here $m=1$ and $\tau=4$ are fixed constants.

Let $E$ denote the supplied upper bound for one evaluator call on endpoints whose combined encoding length is $O(q+1)$, at precision $q+5$. Evaluator outputs have encoding length at most their running time.

Building the bands requires $O(N^2)$ evaluations and $O(N^2)$ rational computations. Processing one band takes $O(N+|E_\ell|)$ reachability operations: initialize arrays of length $N+1$, then scan its edge list once for each of four layers. Summing over all bands gives $O(N^2)$ operations.

A bit-operation bound is therefore

$$ O\!\left( L+\varepsilon^{-2} \left[E+\operatorname{poly}(L+q+E)\right] \right). $$

Each cut has encoding length $O(q+1)$.

If $E$ is uniformly bounded by a fixed polynomial in $L+q$, this becomes

$$ O\!\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\operatorname{poly}(L+q)\right). $$

This is polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$, but exponential in $q=\lceil\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\rceil$. In particular, it gives a polynomial-time algorithm when both $1/\varepsilon$ and $E$ are polynomially bounded in $L$.

5. Proven: conditional complexity consequences

5.1. Polynomial-time solvability

Let $\mathcal C$ denote the cake search problem.

If $\mathcal C$ has a polynomial-time solution algorithm, composing it with the reductions witnessing $\mathsf{PPAD}$-hardness solves every $\mathsf{PPAD}$ problem in polynomial time.

Conversely, $\mathsf{PPAD}$ membership provides a polynomial-time reduction from $\mathcal C$ to End-of-Line, together with a polynomial-time solution decoder. A polynomial-time algorithm for every $\mathsf{PPAD}$ problem would therefore solve $\mathcal C$.

Thus

$$ \mathcal C\text{ is polynomial-time solvable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{every }\mathsf{PPAD}\text{ problem is polynomial-time solvable}. $$

This equivalence supplies neither direction unconditionally.

5.2. The consequence of search $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness

Use the following reduction convention: every source input must map to a valid target instance, and every valid target solution must decode correctly whenever the source input has a solution.

Suppose satisfying-assignment search reduces to $\mathcal C$ through polynomial-time functions $f$ and $g$. Thus $f(\varphi)$ is a valid cake instance for every formula $\varphi$, and, if $\varphi$ is satisfiable, every feasible cake output $y$ makes $g(\varphi,y)$ a satisfying assignment.

Compose $f$ with the stipulated $\mathsf{PPAD}$-membership reduction. This produces an End-of-Line instance $H(\varphi)$ and a polynomial-time decoder $D_\varphi$ taking every valid End-of-Line witness to a feasible cake output.

End-of-Line is total, and its witnesses have polynomial length and are polynomial-time verifiable. Consider the following certificate verifier for unsatisfiability:

  • Verify that the certificate $z$ solves $H(\varphi)$.
  • Compute $y=D_\varphi(z)$ and $a=g(\varphi,y)$.
  • Accept exactly when $a$ is not a satisfying assignment of $\varphi$.

If $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable, totality supplies a valid $z$, and no decoded string can satisfy $\varphi$. Hence an accepting certificate exists.

If $\varphi$ is satisfiable, every valid $z$ decodes to a feasible cake output, which reduction correctness maps to a satisfying assignment. Thus no certificate is accepted.

Unsatisfiability consequently belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$. For every language $A\in\mathsf{NP}$, reduce $A$ to satisfiability. Its complement then reduces to unsatisfiability and belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$. This shows $\mathsf{NP}$ is closed under complementation, so

$$ \mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}. $$

The argument uses discrete End-of-Line certificates; it does not assume that exact envy inequalities at the tolerance boundary can be verified from finitely many approximate evaluations.
