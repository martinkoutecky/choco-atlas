The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of chore species \(E\), additive cost types \(T\) with rational costs \(a_{t,e}\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_e\), compute a minimum-cost finite-support rational allocation \(x_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{E}\). Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_e=\rho_e\), and, for every pair of support bundles \(x_{t,b},x_{u,b'}>0\), \(c_t(b)\le \frac{3}{2}\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,b+b')\), where \(c_t(b)=\sum_e a_{t,e}b_e\) and \(\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,q)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{E},\,0\le z\le q}\max\{c_t(z),c_t(q-z)\}\). The objective is \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}c_t(b)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(E\) be a finite set of \(m\geq1\) chore species. A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), encoded in binary, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Costs \(a_{t,e}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\) and per-capita supplies \(\rho_e\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\) are binary encoded; costs need not be normalized. Write \(c_t(b)=\sum_e a_{t,e}b_e\) for an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}^{E}\); empty bundles are allowed.
An allocation consists of finitely many positive binary-rational masses \(x_{t,b}\), with all other entries zero, satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_e=\rho_e\). Bundle coordinates have no imposed upper bound.
For \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}^{E}\), define \(\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,q)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}^{E},\,0\leq z\leq q}\max\{c_t(z),c_t(q-z)\}\), with coordinatewise inequalities.
Require \(c_t(b)\leq\frac32\operatorname{MMS}_t(2,b+b')\) for every ordered pair with \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(x_{u,b'}>0\), including pairs of the same type and bundle; repeated species represent distinct chore copies.
Compute an allocation minimizing \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}c_t(b)\), reporting infeasibility or nonattainment if applicable. Its cost-threshold decision version asks whether such an allocation has cost at most a given \(K\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including \(K\) when present.

Is this exact minimum-cost allocation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard via its cost-threshold decision version?

For finitely many agents with additive nonnegative chore costs, round-robin computes a \(\frac32\)-PMMS allocation in polynomial time; this guarantees feasibility, not minimum social cost. Result

The cost-threshold decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions, already for two types of equal mass and integer costs. Consequently, the exact optimization problem is not polynomial-time solvable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The reduction below accounts for arbitrary bundle coordinates, fractional masses, and every required ordered-pair comparison.

1. A balancing lemma

Lemma. Suppose a bundle \(b\) has integer total cost \(s\), and every individual copy in \(b\) has integer cost at most an integer \(N\geq1\). Let \(g\) be a species of cost one. Then

\(\operatorname{MMS}(2,b+N\mathbf e_g)=\left\lceil(s+N)/2\right\rceil.\)

Consequently, for every integer \(k\) with \(0\leq k\leq N\),

\(\operatorname{MMS}(2,b+k\mathbf e_g)\leq\left\lceil(s+N)/2\right\rceil.\)

Proof. Distribute the copies of \(b\) between two parts, always putting the next copy into the part with smaller current cost. If the current difference is \(d\leq N\) and the next copy has cost \(w\leq N\), the new difference is \(|d-w|\leq N\). Thus the final difference is an integer at most \(N\).

Now distribute the \(N\) unit-cost copies in the same fashion. The difference decreases by one until it reaches zero, after which it alternates between zero and one. The final maximum cost is therefore \(\lceil(s+N)/2\rceil\). This is also a lower bound, because the two integer part costs sum to \(s+N\).

For \(k\leq N\), delete \(N-k\) unit copies from this partition. Neither part cost increases, proving the stated upper bound. \(\square\)

2. Encoding PARTITION despite unrestricted multiplicities

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given binary-encoded positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) with sum \(2B\), decide whether some subset sums to \(B\). The standard complexity fact is recalled, for example, in [Mertens’s paper on number partitioning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9807077).

We may restrict to \(n\geq4\) and even total sum. Odd-total instances are negative, and instances with fewer than four entries can be decided by constant-size enumeration. Such exceptional instances can be replaced by the fixed positive instance \((1,1,1,1)\) or the fixed negative instance \((1,1,1,5)\), as appropriate.

Define

\(W=\max_i w_i,\qquad R=n+1,\qquad M=nW+1,\qquad d_i=MR^{i-1},\)

\(\Delta=d_n+W,\qquad H=(n+1)\Delta,\qquad Q=nH+\sum_{i=1}^n d_i+B.\)

For each index \(i\), define two positive integers

\(u_{i,0}=H+d_i,\qquad u_{i,1}=H+d_i+w_i.\)

Two properties will be useful:

\(\sum_{i=1}^n(u_{i,0}+u_{i,1})=2Q,\qquad 3\max_{i,j}u_{i,j}\leq Q.\)

The first follows from \(\sum_iw_i=2B\). For the second, every \(u_{i,j}\leq H+\Delta\), while \(H\geq3\Delta\) and \(n\geq4\). Hence

\(3u_{i,j}\leq3(H+\Delta)\leq4H\leq nH\leq Q.\)

Encoding claim. A nonnegative integer vector \((b_{i,j})\) satisfies

\(\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(u_{i,0}b_{i,0}+u_{i,1}b_{i,1}\bigr)=Q\)

if and only if

\(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1}=1\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n w_i b_{i,1}=B.\)

Proof. Let \(r=\sum_{i,j}b_{i,j}\). First,

\(\sum_i d_i+B\leq n(d_n+W)=n\Delta<H,\)

so \(Q<(n+1)H\). Since every \(u_{i,j}\geq H\), the equation implies \(r\leq n\).

If \(r\leq n-1\), its left side is at most

\((n-1)(H+\Delta)<nH\leq Q,\)

a contradiction. Thus \(r=n\).

Subtracting \(nH\) from the equation gives

\(M\sum_i(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1})R^{i-1}+\sum_iw_i b_{i,1} =M\sum_iR^{i-1}+B.\)

Both \(\sum_iw_i b_{i,1}\) and \(B\) lie between zero and \(nW<M\). Reduction modulo \(M\) therefore yields

\(\sum_iw_i b_{i,1}=B.\)

Subtract this equality and divide by \(M\). We obtain

\(\sum_i(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1})R^{i-1}=\sum_iR^{i-1}.\)

Every coefficient \(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1}\) is at most \(n<R\). Uniqueness of base-\(R\) expansion forces every coefficient to equal one.

Conversely, the two stated conditions directly imply the original equation. \(\square\)

Thus this encoding works even when repeated copies and arbitrarily large coordinates are initially permitted.

3. The allocation instance

Introduce \(2n+1\) species:

\(E=\{e_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq n,\ j\in\{0,1\}\}\cup\{g\}.\)

There are two types, \(P\) and \(A\), with

\(\mu_P=\mu_A=\tfrac12.\)

Set

\(a_{i,j}=6u_{i,j},\qquad C=6Q,\qquad N=2Q,\)

so \(C=3N\). Define the costs as follows.

| Type | Species \(e_{i,j}\) | Species \(g\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(P\) | \(a_{i,j}\) | \(1\) |
| \(A\) | \(a_{i,j}+1\) | \(0\) |

The supplies and threshold are

\(\rho_{e_{i,j}}=\tfrac14,\qquad \rho_g=Q=\tfrac N2,\qquad K=3Q=\tfrac C2.\)

Every \(a_{i,j}\) is a multiple of six. Moreover, the preceding bound gives

\(a_{i,j}=6u_{i,j}\leq2Q=N.\)

4. Any allocation at the threshold yields a partition

Suppose a valid allocation has social cost at most \(K\).

For any allocation satisfying the supply equations, its social cost equals

\(\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j}\rho_{e_{i,j}} +\sum_b x_{A,b}\sum_{i,j}b_{e_{i,j}} +\sum_b x_{P,b}b_g.\)

Indeed, assigning an original species to \(A\) adds one to its baseline cost \(a_{i,j}\), and assigning \(g\) to \(P\) adds one to its baseline cost zero.

Since \(\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j}=12Q\), the first term is \(3Q=K\). The other terms are nonnegative. Consequently, cost at most \(K\) forces both other terms to vanish. Because all supported masses are positive:

  • every supported \(P\)-bundle contains only original species;
  • every supported \(A\)-bundle contains only copies of \(g\).

Normalize the masses of type \(A\) by dividing by \(\mu_A=\tfrac12\). Its mean number of copies of \(g\) is

\(\rho_g/\mu_A=N.\)

Hence some supported \(A\)-bundle consists of \(k\) copies of \(g\), with \(k\leq N\). Fix such a bundle.

Consider any supported \(P\)-bundle \(b\), and write \(s=c_P(b)\). Every copy in \(b\) has integer cost at most \(N\). Fairness against the fixed \(A\)-bundle, together with the balancing lemma, implies

\(s\leq\tfrac32\operatorname{MMS}_P(2,b+k\mathbf e_g) \leq\tfrac32\left\lceil(s+N)/2\right\rceil \leq\tfrac34(s+N+1).\)

Rearranging yields

\(s\leq3N+3=C+3.\)

But \(s\) and \(C\) are multiples of six. Therefore

\(c_P(b)=s\leq C.\)

Meanwhile, all original supplies go to type \(P\), so its mean bundle cost is

\(\frac1{\mu_P}\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j}\rho_{e_{i,j}} =2K=C.\)

A finite distribution whose values are all at most \(C\) and whose mean is \(C\) must assign positive mass only to values equal to \(C\). Thus every supported \(P\)-bundle has cost exactly \(C\).

Choose any such bundle. Dividing its cost equation by six gives

\(\sum_{i,j}u_{i,j}b_{e_{i,j}}=Q.\)

The encoding claim forces one choice per index and

\(\sum_iw_i b_{e_{i,1}}=B.\)

These choices constitute a solution of the original PARTITION instance.

The tight mean-cost argument is precisely what prevents fractional cohort splitting from bypassing the integer encoding.

5. A partition yields a valid allocation at the threshold

Conversely, suppose \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in S}w_i=B\).

Define a bundle \(b\) by taking \(e_{i,1}\) for \(i\in S\) and \(e_{i,0}\) otherwise. Define \(\bar b\) by making the opposite choice at every index. Since the complementary subset also sums to \(B\), both bundles have \(P\)-cost \(C\).

Use the allocation

\(x_{P,b}=\tfrac14,\qquad x_{P,\bar b}=\tfrac14,\qquad x_{A,N\mathbf e_g}=\tfrac12.\)

These positive dyadic masses satisfy both type-mass constraints. Each original species occurs in exactly one of \(b,\bar b\), giving supply \(\tfrac14\). The supply of \(g\) is \(N/2=Q\). Social cost is \(C/2=K\).

We check every required ordered pair.

Pairs received by type \(P\), compared against another \(P\)-bundle. Each supported \(P\)-bundle has cost \(C\), so any combined pair has total \(P\)-cost \(2C\). Every two-part partition has maximum cost at least \(C\). Hence

\(C\leq\tfrac32\operatorname{MMS}_P(2,b'+b'')\)

for all \(b',b''\in\{b,\bar b\}\), including identical bundles.

Pairs received by type \(P\), compared against the \(A\)-bundle. The balancing lemma gives, for either \(b'\in\{b,\bar b\}\),

\(\operatorname{MMS}_P(2,b'+N\mathbf e_g) =\left\lceil(C+N)/2\right\rceil=2N.\)

Thus

\(c_P(b')=C=3N=\tfrac32\operatorname{MMS}_P(2,b'+N\mathbf e_g).\)

Pairs received by type \(A\). Its allocated bundle has own cost zero. Every required inequality therefore holds, since the corresponding MMS is nonnegative. This includes its comparison with itself.

The allocation is consequently valid.

6. Polynomial encoding and conclusion

The construction uses \(m=2n+1\) species and \(\tau=2\) types. All its integers have bit length

\(O\!\left(n\log(n+1)+\log W\right).\)

Indeed, \(d_n=(nW+1)(n+1)^{n-1}\), and the remaining numbers require only polynomially many arithmetic operations on integers of this bit length. The large coordinate \(N\) and supply \(Q\) are encoded in binary; the reduction never expands their copies.

We have proved the exact equivalence

\(\text{PARTITION is positive} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed instance admits a valid allocation of cost at most }K.\)

This establishes the requested \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. General questions about attainment do not affect the reduction: every positive instance has the explicit finite allocation constructed above.

1. Balancing lemma

The lemma is valid in the task’s setting of nonnegative chore costs.

During the greedy allocation of the copies of \(b\), the difference between the two part costs remains at most \(N\): if the current difference is \(d\) and the next cost is \(w\), assigning that copy to the cheaper part gives difference \(|d-w|\), which is at most \(N\) when \(0\leq d,w\leq N\).

The initial difference is zero. Therefore, after allocating all copies of \(b\), the difference is an integer between zero and \(N\). Adding \(N\) unit-cost copies reduces that difference to zero and then leaves a final difference of zero or one. Since the total cost is \(s+N\), the resulting maximum part cost is exactly \(\lceil(s+N)/2\rceil\).

Every partition has maximum part cost at least this quantity, because both part costs are integers summing to \(s+N\). Thus the asserted MMS equality follows.

Deleting \(N-k\) of the added unit copies produces a valid partition of \(b+k\mathbf e_g\) without increasing either part cost. This proves the stated upper bound for every integer \(0\leq k\leq N\).

The lemma is existential; its copy-by-copy description need not run in polynomial time for the reduction to be polynomial.

2. Source problem and exceptional instances

The source problem is stated precisely: PARTITION on binary-encoded positive integers, asking for a subset with half the total sum. The cited [Mertens paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9807077) identifies the perfect-partition decision problem as NP-complete.

The restriction to even total sum and \(n\geq4\) is justified:

  • Odd-total instances are negative.
  • Instances with fewer than four entries can be decided using a constant number of subset checks.
  • The replacement \((1,1,1,1)\) is positive.
  • The replacement \((1,1,1,5)\) is negative: its target is four, the three unit entries sum to only three, and any subset containing five exceeds four.

These replacements preserve the answer and satisfy the desired restrictions.

\(\sum_i d_i+B\leq n\Delta<H\). Thus \(Q<(n+1)H\).

\(0\leq\sum_iw_i b_{i,1}\leq nW<M\), and \(0\leq B<M\). Reduction modulo \(M\) therefore gives equality of these quantities, not merely congruence.

The converse follows by substitution. Thus the claim handles arbitrary nonnegative integer coordinates; no restriction to zero-one bundles has been assumed.
