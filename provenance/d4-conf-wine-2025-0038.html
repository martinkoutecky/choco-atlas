The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given recipient types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(d\) item classes with rational per-recipient supplies \(q_j\), and type-specific sizes \(s_{tj}\), clear denominators with \(D\). For each \(r\), form the finite instance with \(rD\mu_t\) recipients of type \(t\) and \(rDq_j\) items of class \(j\); let \(\kappa_t(r)\) be the paper’s bin-packing MMS and set \(\kappa_t^\infty=\sup_r\kappa_t(r)\). Determine the complexity of finding a finite-support mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\ge0\), where \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d\) is an integral bundle, satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\), and \(c_t(b)\le\frac43\kappa_t^\infty+\frac43\) whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\).

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) recipient types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=d\ge1\) item classes with binary-rational supplies \(q_j\ge0\) per recipient and binary-rational type-specific sizes \(0<s_{tj}\le1\). Bins have capacity \(1\). For an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d\), let \(c_t(b)\) be its optimal bin-packing cost under sizes \(s_{tj}\), with \(c_t(0)=0\).
Let \(D\) be the least common multiple of the denominators of all masses and supplies. For each integer \(r\ge1\), put \(n_r=rD\) and define
\(\kappa_t(r)=\min\{\max_{1\le\ell\le n_r}c_t(b^\ell): b^\ell\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d,\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{n_r}b^\ell=rDq\}\), allowing empty bundles; set \(\kappa_t^\infty=\sup_{r\ge1}\kappa_t(r)\).
A mass allocation is a finitely supported family of nonnegative rational numbers \(x_{t,b}\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\) for every class \(j\). Can a mass allocation with this \(\frac43\) ordinal guarantee and additive loss \(\frac43\) always be found in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is attaining this guarantee NP-hard?

Polynomial-time construction

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, an oracle-free algorithm produces a mass allocation satisfying the stronger bound

\(\displaystyle c_t(b)\le \left\lceil \frac54\kappa_t^\infty+\frac14\right\rceil\)

for every supported bundle. The algorithm uses one-bin configurations, approximate knapsack optimization, and a finite rational distribution over integral bundles.

Throughout, all integers and rational numbers are represented in binary.

1. The replication benchmark

For every type \(t\),

\(\displaystyle \kappa_t^\infty=\kappa_t(1).\)

Indeed, concatenate \(r\) copies of a partition witnessing \(\kappa_t(1)\). This produces \(rD\) bundles with total item vector \(rDq\), each having cost at most \(\kappa_t(1)\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \kappa_t(r)\le \kappa_t(1)\)

for every positive integer \(r\). Since the supremum includes \(r=1\), equality follows.

This observation concerns the benchmark only; our algorithm does not compute it.

If \(q=0\), assigning the empty bundle to every type solves the problem. Henceforth suppose \(q\ne0\).

2. A fractional one-bin lower bound

Fix one type, and temporarily suppress its subscript. Define its finite set of one-bin configurations by

\(\displaystyle \mathcal A=\left\{a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_ja_j\le1\right\}.\)

Finiteness follows from \(s_j>0\). Every unit vector belongs to \(\mathcal A\), because \(s_j\le1\).

Consider the configuration covering LP

\(\displaystyle \lambda=\min\left\{\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}y_a: \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}a_jy_a\ge q_j\ \text{for every }j,\quad y_a\ge0\right\}. \)

Writing \(Q=\sum_jq_j\), singleton configurations show that \(\lambda\le Q\). The optimum is attained: the LP has finitely many variables, and its feasible solutions with objective at most \(Q\) form a nonempty compact set.

Its dual is

\(\displaystyle \lambda=\max\left\{\sum_jq_j\pi_j: \pi_j\ge0,\quad \sum_ja_j\pi_j\le1\ \text{for every }a\in\mathcal A\right\}. \)

The singleton constraints imply \(0\le\pi_j\le1\), so the dual feasible region is compact.

Let \(k=\kappa_t(1)\). Packing the bundles of an optimal first-replication partition separately uses at most \(Dk\) bins. Dividing the multiplicity of each resulting configuration by \(D\) gives a feasible configuration LP solution with objective at most \(k\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \lambda\le k=\kappa_t^\infty.\)

The following construction approximates this fractional lower bound without enumerating \(\mathcal A\).

3. Polynomial-time fractional packing

We prove the algorithmic fact needed below:

Lemma. In polynomial time, one can find polynomially many configurations \(a^i\in\mathcal A\) and nonnegative rational coefficients \(y_i\) such that

\(\displaystyle \sum_i y_i a^i\ge q, \qquad B:=\sum_i y_i<\frac54\lambda+\frac14. \)

All inequalities between vectors here are coordinatewise.

3.1. Approximate configuration optimization

For a nonnegative rational vector \(\pi\), put

\(\displaystyle M(\pi)=\max_{a\in\mathcal A}\pi\cdot a.\)

We first describe a polynomial-time algorithm returning a feasible configuration \(a\) with

\(\displaystyle \pi\cdot a\ge\frac45M(\pi).\)

Let \(U_j=\lfloor1/s_j\rfloor\). No feasible configuration contains more than \(U_j\) items of class \(j\).

Represent the available \(U_j\) copies by binary packages: packages of multiplicities \(1,2,4,\ldots\), with the final package truncated so that their sum is \(U_j\). Every integer between \(0\) and \(U_j\) is a subset sum of these package multiplicities. To verify this, successive package multiplicities never exceed one plus the sum of the preceding multiplicities; induction then shows that the attainable subset sums form the entire required interval.

A package of multiplicity \(h\) has weight \(hs_j\) and profit \(h\pi_j\). Each package fits individually, and their total number \(N\) is polynomial in the input length. Optimizing feasible subsets of these packages is exactly the original configuration optimization problem.

If all package profits vanish, return the empty configuration. Otherwise, let \(p_{\max}\) be the largest package profit and set

\(\displaystyle K=\frac{p_{\max}}{5N}.\)

Round each package profit \(p\) down to \(\lfloor p/K\rfloor\). Each rounded profit is at most \(5N\), so their total is at most \(5N^2\). Dynamic programming over total rounded profit, storing the minimum attainable weight, solves the rounded instance in polynomial time using exact rational arithmetic.

For an optimal original subset, rounding loses less than \(NK=p_{\max}/5\). The returned subset consequently has original profit at least

\(\displaystyle M(\pi)-\frac{p_{\max}}5\ge\frac45M(\pi),\)

where the last inequality uses \(p_{\max}\le M(\pi)\), since the most profitable package fits individually.

3.2. Recovering a primal solution

For completeness, we give the recovery argument; approximate pricing alone does not establish the lemma.

We use the standard rational implementation of the central-cut ellipsoid method. Its relevant property is that, starting with a containing ball of radius \(R\), polynomially many rational cuts suffice to obtain an enclosing ellipsoid of volume smaller than a ball of radius \(\rho\), with running time polynomial in the dimension, cut encoding lengths, and \(\log(R/\rho)\). Each update preserves the intersection with the halfspace supplied by the cut. The underlying volume contraction is described in [Goemans’s ellipsoid notes](https://math.mit.edu/~goemans/18433S07/ellipsoid.pdf).

Fix \(\delta=1/8\), and consider a rational threshold \(h\). Start with the ball of radius \(m\) centered at the midpoint of \([0,1]^m\). At each queried center \(\pi\):

  • If \(\pi\notin[0,1]^m\), cut using a violated box inequality.
  • If \(q\cdot\pi<h\), cut using \(q\cdot\pi\ge h\).
  • Otherwise, run the approximate configuration optimizer. If its returned configuration satisfies \(a\cdot\pi>1\), cut using \(a\cdot\pi\le1\). If it satisfies \(a\cdot\pi\le1\), accept \(\pi\).

Every accepted point satisfies \(M(\pi)\le5/4\). Thus \((4/5)\pi\) is feasible for the full configuration dual, and acceptance implies

\(\displaystyle h\le q\cdot\pi\le\frac54\lambda.\)

We next show what can be recovered when the procedure makes sufficiently many cuts without acceptance.

Let \(S\) contain all configurations used in its cuts, together with all singleton configurations. Define

\(\displaystyle w_S=\max\left\{q\cdot\pi: \pi\ge0,\quad a\cdot\pi\le1\ \text{for every }a\in S\right\}. \)

Singleton configurations make its feasible region a subset of \([0,1]^m\), so its optimum is attained.

Set

\(\displaystyle U=\max_jU_j,\qquad \theta=\frac{\delta}{4(Q+1)},\qquad \rho=\frac{\theta}{8mU},\qquad u=\frac{1}{4mU}(1,\ldots,1). \)

Suppose \(w_S\ge h+\delta\), and let \(\pi^*\) attain \(w_S\). Consider

\(\displaystyle \bar\pi=(1-\theta)\pi^*+\theta u.\)

For every \(a\in S\), its coordinates are at most \(U\), and hence \(a\cdot u\le1/4\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle a\cdot\bar\pi\le1-\frac{3\theta}{4}.\)

Each coordinate of \(\bar\pi\) is at least \(\theta/(4mU)\) and at most \(1-3\theta/4\). Also,

\(\displaystyle q\cdot\bar\pi \ge w_S-\theta Q \ge h+\frac{3\delta}{4}. \)

These margins show that the entire Euclidean ball of radius \(\rho\) around \(\bar\pi\) satisfies the box constraints, the constraints for \(S\), and \(q\cdot\pi\ge h\). Explicitly, a perturbation \(v\) with \(\lVert v\rVert_2\le\rho\) changes any configuration inner product by at most

\(\displaystyle \lVert a\rVert_2\rho\le mU\rho=\frac{\theta}{8},\)

and changes the objective by at most

\(\displaystyle Q\rho\le\frac{\delta}{32}.\)

Every ellipsoid update preserves this ball: the ball satisfies every original inequality used in a cut, and therefore also the corresponding central halfspace.

It follows that, once the ellipsoid volume becomes smaller than a radius-\(\rho\) ball without acceptance, necessarily

\(\displaystyle w_S<h+\delta.\)

This requires polynomially many cuts, because \(\log U\) and \(\log(Q+1)\) are polynomially bounded by the input length.

Now solve the ordinary, explicitly listed covering LP using configurations in \(S\). It is feasible because it contains singleton configurations. By finite LP duality, its optimum is \(w_S<h+\delta\).

Finally, binary-search the threshold. Initially take lower endpoint \(0\) and upper endpoint \((5/4)Q+1\). The upper endpoint cannot be accepted, since acceptance implies \(h\le(5/4)\lambda\le(5/4)Q\). At each midpoint, acceptance updates the lower endpoint; a volume termination updates the upper endpoint and stores its configuration set.

Stop when the interval width is at most \(\delta\). The lower endpoint is always at most \((5/4)\lambda\), so the final upper endpoint \(h\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle h\le\frac54\lambda+\delta.\)

Solving the LP from its stored configuration set yields

\(\displaystyle B<h+\delta\le\frac54\lambda+\frac14.\)

This proves the lemma. The explicitly listed LP has polynomial size. An optimal basic solution can be chosen with at most \(m\) positive coefficients, since it has \(m\) covering constraints. Its rational encoding length is polynomial.

4. Making the expected supply exact

The covering solution might oversupply some classes. We can remove that oversupply while preserving feasible configurations and the total coefficient \(B\).

For a coordinate \(j\), scan the configurations, retaining their \(j\)-coordinates until their cumulative contribution reaches \(q_j\). At a crossing configuration with coefficient \(y_i\) and coordinate \(a^i_j>0\), let \(r\) be the still-required contribution. Replace it by:

  • coefficient \(r/a^i_j\) on the original configuration;
  • coefficient \(y_i-r/a^i_j\) on the same configuration with coordinate \(j\) set to zero.

Set coordinate \(j\) to zero in all later configurations.

Both replacement coefficients are nonnegative. Both configurations fit in one bin, because deleting items preserves feasibility. The operation makes the \(j\)-contribution exactly \(q_j\), preserves every other coordinate’s total contribution, and preserves the sum of coefficients.

Applying this to all coordinates gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_i y_i a^i=q,\qquad \sum_i y_i=B.\)

There is at most one split per coordinate. Starting with at most \(m\) configurations, we therefore finish with at most \(2m\).

The encoding length remains polynomial. Take an initial common denominator for the LP coefficients and supplies. Each crossing divides a rational number with the current common denominator by an integer at most \(U\); there are at most \(m\) crossings. A common denominator for all resulting coefficients thus gains at most \(m\log U\) bits.

5. Converting fractional bins into integral bundles

We now prove the distributional rounding step.

Suppose we have \(p\) feasible one-bin configurations satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{p}y_i a^i=q,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{p}y_i=B.\)

Write \(y_i=k_i+f_i\), where \(k_i=\lfloor y_i\rfloor\) and \(0\le f_i<1\). Let

\(\displaystyle F_0=0,\qquad F_i=\sum_{\ell=1}^{i}f_\ell,\qquad F=F_p.\)

For \(u\in[0,1)\), define

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon_i(u)= \#\{z\in\mathbb Z:u+z\in[F_{i-1},F_i)\}. \)

Because the interval has length \(f_i<1\), this count belongs to \(\{0,1\}\). Under uniform \(u\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[\varepsilon_i(u)]=f_i.\)

Indeed, translating the integration interval \([0,1)\) by all integers partitions the real line, so the integral of this count equals the length of \([F_{i-1},F_i)\).

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \sum_i\varepsilon_i(u) =\#\{z\in\mathbb Z:u+z\in[0,F)\} \le\lceil F\rceil. \)

Define the integral bundle

\(\displaystyle b(u)=\sum_i\bigl(k_i+\varepsilon_i(u)\bigr)a^i.\)

It has mean

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[b(u)]=\sum_i y_i a^i=q.\)

Packing each configuration separately gives

\(\displaystyle c_t(b(u)) \le\sum_i k_i+\lceil F\rceil =\lceil B\rceil. \)

This construction is a finite rational distribution. The vector \(\varepsilon(u)\) changes only at fractional parts of the endpoints \(F_i\). These rational breakpoints partition \([0,1)\) into at most \(p+1\) intervals. On each interval choose a rational interior point and give its bundle probability equal to the interval length.

Thus the distribution has at most \(p+1\le2m+1\) supported bundles, all with rational probabilities and polynomial encoding length. Large multiplicities are stored in binary; they are never expanded into individual bins or items.

6. Combining recipient types

Run Sections 2–5 separately for each type \(t\). Let \(p_{t,b}\) denote the resulting rational probability distribution. It satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_b p_{t,b}=1,\qquad \sum_b b\,p_{t,b}=q. \)

Define

\(\displaystyle x_{t,b}=\mu_t p_{t,b}.\)

Then

\(\displaystyle \sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t,\)

and, using \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b} =\sum_t\mu_tq_j =q_j. \)

Hence this is a mass allocation.

For every supported bundle, the preceding bounds imply

\(\displaystyle c_t(b)\le\lceil B_t\rceil \le\left\lceil\frac54\kappa_t^\infty+\frac14\right\rceil. \)

Finally,

\(\displaystyle \left\lceil\frac54\kappa_t^\infty+\frac14\right\rceil \le\frac54\kappa_t^\infty+\frac54 \le\frac43\kappa_t^\infty+\frac43, \)

where the last inequality follows because the difference of the right-hand expressions is \((\kappa_t^\infty+1)/12>0\).

There are at most \(\tau(2m+1)\) supported type–bundle pairs. All computations, including rational arithmetic and the explicitly listed LPs, take time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Neither the population of size \(D\) nor the benchmark values are computed.
