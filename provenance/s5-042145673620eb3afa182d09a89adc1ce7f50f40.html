The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two finite supported type sets \(A\) and \(B\), positive rational masses \((\alpha_a)_{a\in A}\) and \((\beta_b)_{b\in B}\) each summing to \(1\), and rational cardinal utilities \(u_{ab},v_{ab}\) with strict rankings over opposite-side types, decide whether there is exactly one stable mass flow. A feasible flow has \(x_{ab}\geq 0\), row sums \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\alpha_a\), and column sums \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\beta_b\); define \(\bar u_a(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha_a}\sum_b x_{ab}u_{ab}\) and \(\bar v_b(x)=\frac{1}{\beta_b}\sum_a x_{ab}v_{ab}\). Stability requires, for every \((a,b)\), that \(u_{ab}\leq\bar u_a(x)\) or \(v_{ab}\leq\bar v_b(x)\).

Write $p=|A|$, $q=|B|$, and let $S$ denote the set of stable feasible flows.

2. Existence and the structure of a unique stable flow

2.1 A stronger support-based stability condition

Call a feasible flow $x$ ordinally stable if, for every pair $(a,b)$, at least one of the following holds:

$$ x_{ab'}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ u_{ab'}\geq u_{ab} \qquad\text{for every }b'\in B, $$

or

$$ x_{a'b}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ v_{a'b}\geq v_{ab} \qquad\text{for every }a'\in A. $$

Thus at least one endpoint receives its entire mass from partners ranked at least as highly as the other endpoint.

Ordinal stability implies the stability in the question. Indeed, the first condition implies $\bar u_a(x)\geq u_{ab}$ by averaging, and the second implies $\bar v_b(x)\geq v_{ab}$.

Proposition 1. Every valid instance has an ordinally stable feasible flow.

Proof. Choose a positive integer $D$ clearing all mass denominators. Replace type $a$ by $D\alpha_a$ copies and type $b$ by $D\beta_b$ copies. There are $D$ copies on each side.

Each copy ranks opposite-side types according to its utilities. Refine preferences arbitrarily among copies of the same type, preserving every comparison between different types. The resulting individual preference lists are strict and complete.

Run deferred acceptance. The algorithm terminates because each proposer makes at most $D$ proposals. Its final matching is perfect: an unmatched proposer would have proposed to every receiver, all receivers would then hold proposals, and balance would leave no proposer unmatched. It is stable: a proposer preferring a receiver to its final partner must previously have proposed to that receiver and been rejected; that receiver's final partner is preferred to the rejected proposer.

Aggregate the matching into a flow by assigning mass $1/D$ to each matched individual pair. The marginals are exactly $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

If ordinal stability failed at $(a,b)$, there would be positive entries $x_{ab'}$ and $x_{a'b}$ satisfying

$$ u_{ab'}<u_{ab}, \qquad v_{a'b}<v_{ab}. $$

Choose a copy of $a$ matched to a copy of $b'$, and a copy of $b$ matched to a copy of $a'$. These two copies prefer each other to their assigned partners. Their preferences follow from strict comparisons between different types, independently of the refinements. They form a blocking pair, contradicting individual stability. $\square$

The expansion can be exponentially large in $L$. This proof establishes existence without asserting a polynomial-time construction.

2.2 A unique stable flow must be a transportation vertex

The support graph of $x$ is the bipartite graph on $A\cup B$ whose edges are the pairs with $x_{ab}>0$.

Proposition 2. Every instance has an ordinally stable flow with forest support. If $S$ is a singleton, its unique member has forest support and is a vertex of the transportation polytope.

Proof. Start with the ordinally stable flow from Proposition 1.

Suppose its support contains a cycle. A bipartite cycle has even length. Assign alternating values $+1$ and $-1$ to its edges and zero elsewhere, obtaining a matrix $d$. At every vertex of the cycle the two incident values cancel, so

$$ \sum_b d_{ab}=0, \qquad \sum_a d_{ab}=0. $$

For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both $x+\varepsilon d$ and $x-\varepsilon d$ are nonnegative and have the prescribed marginals. Their supports are contained in the support of $x$.

Ordinal stability is preserved when support edges are deleted: every universal comparison over the original positive entries remains true over a subset. Hence both perturbations are ordinally stable.

This already proves that an ordinally stable flow with a support cycle cannot be the unique stable flow.

To obtain forest support, instead choose

$$ \varepsilon=\min\{x_{ab}:d_{ab}=-1\}. $$

Then $x+\varepsilon d$ is feasible and ordinally stable, and at least one positive support edge disappears. No new support edge appears. Repeating eliminates all cycles after finitely many steps.

It remains to verify the vertex assertion. Suppose a feasible flow $x$ has forest support and

$$ x=\frac{y+z}{2} $$

for feasible flows $y,z$. Every zero entry of $x$ is zero in both $y$ and $z$, by nonnegativity. Thus $y-z$ is supported on the forest and has zero row and column sums. At a leaf, the sole incident entry of $y-z$ must be zero. Repeatedly deleting leaves proves $y-z=0$. Therefore $y=z=x$, so $x$ is a vertex. $\square$

If the support forest has $c$ connected components, it has $\tau-c\leq\tau-1$ positive entries. Moreover, every positive entry has an explicit formula involving only masses.

For a support edge $(a,b)$, delete that edge and let $C$ be the resulting component containing $a$. Summing row marginals over $A\cap C$ and subtracting column marginals over $B\cap C$ cancels every internal flow. The only positive flow crossing the boundary is $x_{ab}$, directed from $a$ to $b$. Therefore

$$ x_{ab} = \sum_{a'\in A\cap C}\alpha_{a'} - \sum_{b'\in B\cap C}\beta_{b'}. $$

Thus any unique stable flow is determined by its forest support and the masses. Its entries have polynomial rational encoding length: each is a signed sum of input masses, whose denominators can be cleared using a product with bit length bounded by the total mass encoding length.

This is a necessary structural condition, not a sufficient uniqueness criterion.

3. An exact local isolation test

A point $x\in S$ is isolated in $S$ if some open neighborhood of $x$ contains no other member of $S$.

Fix a rational stable flow $x$. Define

$$ h_{ab}=\bar u_a(x)-u_{ab}, \qquad g_{ab}=\bar v_b(x)-v_{ab}, $$

and, for a direction matrix $d$, define

$$ U_a(d)=\frac{1}{\alpha_a}\sum_b d_{ab}u_{ab}, \qquad V_b(d)=\frac{1}{\beta_b}\sum_a d_{ab}v_{ab}. $$

Consider directions satisfying the feasibility conditions

$$ \sum_b d_{ab}=0, \qquad \sum_a d_{ab}=0, \qquad d_{ab}\geq0\quad\text{whenever }x_{ab}=0, $$

together with these conditions for each pair:

  • If $h_{ab}>0$ or $g_{ab}>0$, impose no additional condition.
  • If $h_{ab}=0$ and $g_{ab}<0$, impose $U_a(d)\geq0$.
  • If $h_{ab}<0$ and $g_{ab}=0$, impose $V_b(d)\geq0$.
  • If $h_{ab}=g_{ab}=0$, impose

$$ U_a(d)\geq0\quad\text{or}\quad V_b(d)\geq0. $$

These cases exhaust all pairs because $x$ is stable.

Proposition 3. The flow $x$ is nonisolated in $S$ if and only if these conditions admit a nonzero direction $d$.

Proof. First suppose such a direction exists. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, every entry of $x+td$ is nonnegative for $0\leq t\leq\varepsilon$:

  • at a zero entry this follows from $d_{ab}\geq0$;
  • at a positive entry it follows by choosing $\varepsilon$ small enough.

The marginals are unchanged.

Every strictly positive value among the $h_{ab}$ and $g_{ab}$ remains positive for sufficiently small $t$, since the averages depend linearly on $t$. A pair with no strictly positive protecting value is protected by one of the imposed nonnegative directional derivatives. Hence

$$ x+td\in S\qquad(0\leq t\leq\varepsilon). $$

Since $d\neq0$, this proves nonisolation.

Conversely, choose a neighborhood of $x$ in which every strictly negative $h_{ab}$ or $g_{ab}$ remains negative. Such a neighborhood exists by continuity and finiteness of the pair set.

If $x$ is nonisolated, choose a distinct stable $y$ in this neighborhood and set $d=y-x$. The marginal and zero-entry conditions follow from feasibility.

If $h_{ab}=0$ and $g_{ab}<0$, then the column-side inequality still fails at $y$. Stability of $y$ therefore requires

$$ \bar u_a(y)\geq u_{ab}=\bar u_a(x), $$

which is $U_a(d)\geq0$. The other singly tight case is symmetric. If both values are zero, stability of $y$ requires at least one of the two directional derivatives to be nonnegative. Thus $d$ satisfies all the conditions and is nonzero. $\square$

Let

$$ k=\bigl|\{(a,b):h_{ab}=g_{ab}=0\}\bigr|. $$

The doubly tight pairs form a matching. For any fixed $a$, the equation $\bar u_a(x)=u_{ab}$ can hold for at most one $b$, because the row entries are distinct. Similarly, each $b$ belongs to at most one doubly tight pair. Consequently,

$$ k\leq\min\{p,q\}. $$

Choose one protecting inequality for each doubly tight pair. This produces $2^k$ rational polyhedral cones. A cone contains a nonzero direction exactly when, after adding

$$ -1\leq d_{ab}\leq1, $$

one of the $2pq$ linear systems obtained by setting a coordinate to $+1$ or $-1$ is feasible. Indeed, any nonzero direction can be scaled so that its largest absolute coordinate equals one.

Polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore gives an exact isolation test in

$$ 2^k\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,\ell(x)), $$

where $\ell(x)$ is the encoding length of the supplied rational flow. In particular, local isolation is polynomial-time decidable when $k=0$.

The next example shows why this does not give a global uniqueness algorithm.

4. An isolated stable flow and a separate continuum

Take three types on each side, with masses

$$ \alpha=\left(\frac15,\frac15,\frac35\right), \qquad \beta=\left(\frac25,\frac3{10},\frac3{10}\right), $$

and utilities

$$ u= \begin{pmatrix} 1&5&2\\ 0&3&6\\ 0&2&6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad v= \begin{pmatrix} 5&3&6\\ 3&6&4\\ 2&2&1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Every required row of $u$ and column of $v$ has distinct entries.

For convenience write $z=5x$. Its row sums are $(1,1,3)$ and its column sums are $(2,3/2,3/2)$.

4.1 An isolated ordinally stable flow

Consider

$$ z^\star= \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\ 0&0&1\\ 2&1/2&1/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x^\star=\frac15z^\star. $$

Its averages are

$$ \bar u(x^\star)=\left(5,6,\frac43\right), \qquad \bar v(x^\star)=\left(2,\frac83,3\right). $$

The first two rows receive their respective maximum utilities and therefore protect all their incident pairs. For the third row:

  • $(a_3,b_1)$ is protected by $u_{a_3b_1}=0$;
  • $(a_3,b_2)$ is protected by $\bar v_{b_2}=8/3\geq2$;
  • $(a_3,b_3)$ is protected by $\bar v_{b_3}=3\geq1$.

Thus $x^\star$ is stable. It is also ordinally stable: the positive partners in column $b_2$ have column utilities $3,2$, and those in column $b_3$ have utilities $4,1$, so these columns protect the two relevant pairs involving $a_3$.

Define the open set

$$ N=\{y:\bar v_{b_2}(y)<3,\ \bar v_{b_3}(y)<4\}. $$

It contains $x^\star$. If $y\in N$ is stable, stability at $(a_1,b_2)$ forces

$$ \bar u_{a_1}(y)\geq5. $$

Since $5$ is the unique maximum in the first row, all of $a_1$ must be assigned to $b_2$. Likewise, stability at $(a_2,b_3)$ forces $\bar u_{a_2}(y)\geq6$, so all of $a_2$ must be assigned to $b_3$.

The marginals then determine every remaining entry, giving $y=x^\star$. Hence

$$ N\cap S=\{x^\star\}, $$

and $x^\star$ is isolated.

There are no doubly tight pairs at this flow. The only row equalities occur at $(a_1,b_2)$ and $(a_2,b_3)$, where the column averages are strictly below the corresponding column utilities. Thus this example has $k=0$.

4.2 A distinct continuum of stable flows

For

$$ 0\leq t\leq\frac1{10}, $$

define

$$ z(t)= \begin{pmatrix} 0&t&1-t\\ 0&1&0\\ 2&1/2-t&1/2+t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x(t)=\frac15z(t). $$

All entries are nonnegative. The row and column sums are the same as those of $z^\star$. Direct calculation gives

$$ \bar u(x(t)) = \left( 2+3t,\ 3,\ \frac{4+4t}{3} \right), $$

and

$$ \bar v(x(t)) = \left( 2,\ \frac{14+2t}{3},\ \frac{13-10t}{3} \right). $$

In particular,

$$ \bar v_{b_2}(x(t))\geq\frac{14}{3}, \qquad \bar v_{b_3}(x(t))\geq4. $$

Here is a check of all nine stability conditions:

  • For $a_1$, its row average is at least $2$, protecting $(a_1,b_1)$ and $(a_1,b_3)$. Column $b_2$ protects $(a_1,b_2)$ because its average exceeds $3$.
  • For $a_2$, its row average is $3$, protecting $(a_2,b_1)$ and $(a_2,b_2)$. Column $b_3$ protects $(a_2,b_3)$ because its average is at least $4$.
  • For $a_3$, the utility at $b_1$ is zero. Columns $b_2,b_3$ protect its other pairs because their averages are at least $2,1$, respectively.

Therefore every $x(t)$ is stable. Different parameters give different flows, and none equals $x^\star$.

Since $\{x^\star\}$ is relatively open in $S$ by isolation and relatively closed because singletons are closed, while $S\setminus\{x^\star\}$ is nonempty, $S$ is disconnected.

Consequence. Local uniqueness at a known ordinally stable flow does not imply global uniqueness. This failure persists even when the local stability conditions contain no doubly tight disjunctions.

5. A sharp cardinal-utility threshold with fixed rankings

The following family proves that uniqueness cannot be characterized using only masses and preference rankings.

Fix

$$ \alpha=\left(\frac1{10},\frac1{10},\frac45\right), \qquad \beta=\left(\frac45,\frac1{10},\frac1{10}\right), $$

and, for a rational parameter $K>1$, let

$$ u= \begin{pmatrix} 2&1&0\\ 2&1&0\\ 2&1&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad v= \begin{pmatrix} K&K&K\\ 1&1&1\\ 0&0&0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

All preference rankings remain unchanged as $K$ varies over $K>1$.

Proposition 4. This instance has exactly one stable flow if and only if $K\leq7$.

Proof. First, every stable flow must satisfy

$$ x_{a_1b_1}=\frac1{10}. $$

Indeed, $\bar u_{a_1}\leq2$, with equality only when all of $a_1$ is assigned to $b_1$. Also $\bar v_{b_1}<K$ for every feasible flow: column $b_1$ has mass $4/5$, whereas only mass $1/10$ is available from its unique highest-utility type $a_1$. Stability at $(a_1,b_1)$ therefore forces $\bar u_{a_1}=2$.

Suppose $K\leq7$. If $x_{a_2b_1}<1/10$, then $\bar u_{a_2}<2$, and

$$ \bar v_{b_1} = \frac{K/10+x_{a_2b_1}}{4/5} \leq \frac{7/10+x_{a_2b_1}}{4/5} <1. $$

Then $(a_2,b_1)$ blocks. Thus $x_{a_2b_1}=1/10$. The marginals now determine the entire flow:

$$ x^0= \begin{pmatrix} 1/10&0&0\\ 1/10&0&0\\ 3/5&1/10&1/10 \end{pmatrix}. $$

This candidate is stable. The first two rows receive their maximum utility $2$. The third row has average $13/8$, protecting its pairs with $b_2,b_3$; its pair with $b_1$ is protected by the column-side utility $v_{a_3b_1}=0$. Hence uniqueness holds for $K\leq7$, including equality.

Now suppose $K>7$. For

$$ 0\leq\varepsilon\leq \min\left\{\frac1{10},\frac{K-7}{10}\right\}, $$

define

$$ x^\varepsilon= \begin{pmatrix} 1/10&0&0\\ 1/10-\varepsilon&\varepsilon&0\\ 3/5+\varepsilon&1/10-\varepsilon&1/10 \end{pmatrix}. $$

These flows are feasible. Their averages are

$$ \bar u(x^\varepsilon) = \left( 2,\ 2-10\varepsilon,\ \frac{13}{8}+\frac54\varepsilon \right), $$

and

$$ \bar v(x^\varepsilon) = \left( \frac{K+1}{8}-\frac54\varepsilon,\ 10\varepsilon,\ 0 \right). $$

The parameter bounds give

$$ \bar u_{a_2}\geq1, \qquad \bar u_{a_3}\geq1, \qquad \bar v_{b_1}\geq1. $$

All pairs involving $a_1$ are protected by its maximum utility. The pair $(a_2,b_1)$ is protected by column $b_1$; the other pairs involving $a_2$ are protected by its row average. For $a_3$, its pair with $b_1$ has column utility zero, and its other pairs are protected by its row average.

Thus every $x^\varepsilon$ is stable. The parameter interval has positive length when $K>7$, proving nonuniqueness. $\square$

For the finite unit-capacity model, uniqueness is characterized by the ordinal mutual-first removal criterion in [Narang and Narahari](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.05652v2). Proposition 4 shows that a criterion depending only on rankings cannot characterize uniqueness for the rational type-mass model.

6. Global complexity bounds

6.1 Exact representation by rational polytopes

For each $a$, let

$$ T_a=\{u_{ab}:b\in B\}. $$

For $t\in\prod_aT_a$, define

$$ r_b(t)= \max\left(\{v_{ab}:u_{ab}>t_a\}\cup\{0\}\right). $$

Let $P_t$ consist of feasible flows satisfying

$$ \sum_bx_{ab}u_{ab}\geq\alpha_at_a \qquad(a\in A), $$

and

$$ \sum_ax_{ab}v_{ab}\geq\beta_br_b(t) \qquad(b\in B). $$

Proposition 5.

$$ S=\bigcup_{t\in\prod_aT_a}P_t. $$

Proof. If $x\in P_t$, then for any pair $(a,b)$:

  • if $u_{ab}\leq t_a$, its row average satisfies $\bar u_a(x)\geq t_a\geq u_{ab}$;
  • otherwise, its column average satisfies $\bar v_b(x)\geq r_b(t)\geq v_{ab}$.

Thus $x$ is stable.

Conversely, let $x$ be stable. Each row average is a convex combination of its row entries, so

$$ t_a=\max\{u_{ab}:u_{ab}\leq\bar u_a(x)\} $$

is well defined. The row inequalities hold.

If $u_{ab}>t_a$, then necessarily $u_{ab}>\bar u_a(x)$; otherwise that entry would contradict maximality of $t_a$. Stability forces $\bar v_b(x)\geq v_{ab}$ for every such $a$. Taking the maximum proves the column inequality. If there is no such $a$, nonnegativity of utilities gives $\bar v_b(x)\geq0=r_b(t)$. Hence $x\in P_t$. $\square$

All constraints are weak, so this representation treats equality cases exactly.

6.2 An exact exponential algorithm

There are $q^p$ polytopes. Each has $pq$ variables and a rational description of polynomial encoding length. Every nonempty polytope is bounded because feasibility implies $0\leq x_{ab}\leq1$.

The standard rational linear-programming theorem supplies polynomial-time feasibility testing and exact linear optimization over such a polytope; it does not require full dimensionality.

Find a feasible reference point $x^0$ in the first nonempty polytope. Existence is guaranteed by Proposition 1. For every nonempty $P_t$, minimize and maximize every coordinate. Return yes exactly when all coordinate extrema equal the corresponding coordinates of $x^0$.

If all extrema agree, every point of every $P_t$ equals $x^0$. Conversely, a different stable flow belongs to some $P_t$ and differs in some coordinate, which the corresponding optimization detects.

Interchanging the two sides gives running time

$$ \min\{q^p,p^q\}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

Thus uniqueness is polynomial-time decidable when the smaller support size is fixed. If either support size is one, the marginals already determine the unique feasible flow.

6.3 Polynomial certificates of nonuniqueness

Proposition 6. Nonuniqueness has polynomial-size rational certificates. Consequently, uniqueness belongs to $\mathrm{coNP}$.

Proof. Suppose $S$ contains distinct points.

If some $P_t$ contains distinct points, a coordinate takes different values there. Its minimum and maximum are attained at vertices and differ, giving two distinct vertices. Here the usual bounded-polytope theorem applies: a nonempty bounded polytope is the convex hull of its vertices, and linear extrema can be attained at vertices.

Otherwise, every nonempty $P_t$ is a singleton. Since their union is not a singleton, choose points from two different singleton polytopes; each is a vertex.

It remains to bound vertex encoding lengths. Set $d=pq$. Clear denominators separately in each constraint of $P_t$. The resulting integer coefficients and right-hand sides have bit length

$$ H=\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

At a vertex, the active constraint normals span the ambient $d$-dimensional space. Otherwise a nonzero direction annihilating all active normals would permit sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions: inactive inequalities have positive slack, and there are finitely many constraints. This would contradict extremality.

Choose $d$ independent active constraints. Cramer's rule expresses each coordinate as a ratio of determinants of integer matrices whose entries have magnitude at most $2^H$. Expanding a determinant gives the bound

$$ |\det|\leq d!\,2^{dH}. $$

Thus every numerator and denominator has bit length $O(dH+d\log d)$, and the complete vertex has polynomial encoding length.

A certificate consists of two such distinct rational stable flows. Exact rational arithmetic checks nonnegativity, marginals, every stability disjunction, and distinctness in polynomial time.

Proposition 1 proves existence on every valid input. Therefore failure of uniqueness always means that such a certificate exists. $\square$

In particular, $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness of uniqueness under polynomial-time many-one reductions would imply

$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

Indeed, reductions to a language in $\mathrm{coNP}$ would give $\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{coNP}$; taking complements gives the reverse inclusion. This is a conditional restriction, not a proof of polynomial-time solvability.

7. What remains open

The structural and local results do not settle global uniqueness. In particular, Section 4 rules out inferring global uniqueness from local isolation at an ordinally stable transportation vertex, while Section 5 rules out a characterization based solely on preference rankings and masses.

A complete resolution still requires either a polynomial-time global algorithm or an applicable hardness reduction.
