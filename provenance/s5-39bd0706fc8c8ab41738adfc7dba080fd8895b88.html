The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite support \(T\), rational masses \(p_t>0\) with \(\sum_t p_t=1\), claims \(c_t\ge 0\), a rational per-creditor endowment \(e\in[0,\sum_t p_t c_t]\), and a fixed admissible gain-loss function \(\phi\), compute a maximizing family of award distributions \((\nu_t)_{t\in T}\), where each \(\nu_t\) is supported on \([0,c_t]\), subject to \(\sum_t p_t\int x\,d\nu_t(x)=e\), maximizing \(\sum_t p_t\int [x+\phi(x-c_t)]\,d\nu_t(x)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its nonempty support \(T\): the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, with binary rational masses \(p_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a binary rational claim \(c_t\geq0\), used as its reference point; the binary rational per-creditor endowment satisfies \(0\leq e\leq\sum_t p_tc_t\).
Fix a common function \(\phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\), continuous and strictly increasing, with \(\phi(0)=0\), strictly convex on \((-infty,0)\), strictly concave on \((0,\infty)\), and \(|\phi(-z)|>\phi(z)\) for \(z>0\). Assume its values at rational arguments are rational and computable exactly in polynomial time in the argument's bit length.
An allocation consists of Borel probability measures \(\nu_t\) on \([0,c_t]\), representing the distribution of awards within each type, satisfying \(\sum_t p_t\int x\,d\nu_t(x)=e\).
Compute an allocation maximizing \(\sum_t p_t\int [x+\phi(x-c_t)]\,d\nu_t(x)\); a computed allocation is represented by finite lists of rational atoms and rational probabilities. Balance is imposed on aggregate mass, with no additional constraints coupling the measures.
Is this exact mass-allocation optimization problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard, for each fixed admissible \(\phi\) satisfying the computational assumption? State running times in \(m=1\) (one resource), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\).
For finitely many individual creditors, allocating sequentially in increasing order of claims, giving each the smaller of its claim and the remaining endowment, maximizes utilitarian welfare and is computable in polynomial time; equal-claim ties may be broken arbitrarily. [Verified discrete result](https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/handle/2318/1684987/461494/BankruptcyRDPs_R2.pdf).
That result concerns individual awards and does not classify the mass-allocation problem above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Claims were not explicitly encoded; require binary rational nonnegative claims and define L as total input bit length.
  • Admissibility was undefined; use continuity, strict increase, normalization at zero, strict convexity for losses, strict concavity for gains, and loss dominance. The contradictory derivative parenthesis for gains is interpreted according to the stated concavity; the later manuscript retains that typo.
  • The fixed function had no computational model; require polynomial-time exact rational evaluation on rational arguments to make bit complexity well-defined.
  • Award distributions are probability measures, and finite rational atomic descriptions provide the computational representation of an optimum.
  • The normalized balance constraint is an aggregate mass constraint, without a joint lottery or additional coupling constraints.
  • Set m=1 because there is one divisible resource; require nonempty support and retain zero claims and boundary endowments, which the supplied domains allow.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

The exact mass-allocation problem is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed admissible \(\phi\). For \(m=1\), an optimal allocation can be computed using \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) exact arithmetic/comparison operations, or \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2)\) bit operations with classical integer arithmetic. The output has at most \(\tau+1\) atoms in total.

The proof below directly addresses distributions within types.

  • Endpoint reduction.

Write \(u_c(x)=x+\phi(x-c)\) for \(0\leq x\leq c\). If \(c=0\), the only feasible measure is \(\delta_0\), where \(\delta_a\) denotes the probability measure concentrated at \(a\).

Suppose \(c>0\). Continuity of \(\phi\) at zero and convexity on negative arguments imply convexity on \([-c,0]\): inequalities involving the endpoint zero follow by taking limits of inequalities with negative endpoints. Consequently, for every \(x\in[0,c]\),

\[ u_c(x)\leq \left(1-\frac{x}{c}\right)\phi(-c)+\frac{x}{c}c =\phi(-c)+\left(1-\frac{\phi(-c)}{c}\right)x. \]

For completeness, the inequality is strict when \(0<x<c\). Set \(z=x-c\), and choose \(v\) with \(z<v<0\). Strict convexity on \([-c,v]\) puts \(\phi(z)\) strictly below the chord joining those endpoints. Convexity on \([-c,0]\) puts \(\phi(v)\) below the chord joining \(-c\) and zero. Combining these inequalities puts \(\phi(z)\) strictly below the latter chord.

Let \(\mu_t=\int x\,d\nu_t(x)\). Replace every positive-claim type’s measure by

\[ \widehat{\nu}_t= \left(1-\frac{\mu_t}{c_t}\right)\delta_0+ \frac{\mu_t}{c_t}\delta_{c_t}. \]

This preserves its mean and therefore aggregate balance. Integrating the chord inequality shows that it weakly increases welfare. Thus it suffices to optimize over endpoint mixtures. This reduction applies to arbitrary feasible Borel probability measures.

  • The resulting linear optimization and its ordering.

For each positive-claim type define its resource capacity \(q_t=p_tc_t\), its allocated resource \(y_t=p_t\mu_t\), and its welfare gain per unit of resource

\[ \rho(c)=1-\frac{\phi(-c)}{c}. \]

Endpoint mixtures attain welfare

\[ B+\sum_{t:c_t>0}\rho(c_t)y_t, \qquad B=\sum_t p_t\phi(-c_t), \]

subject to

\[ 0\leq y_t\leq q_t, \qquad \sum_{t:c_t>0}y_t=e. \]

Conversely, every vector satisfying these constraints defines feasible endpoint mixtures with probability \(y_t/q_t\) at \(c_t\). Hence this finite linear optimization is exactly equivalent to the original optimization.

Its coefficients are strictly decreasing in the claim. Indeed, if \(0<a<b\), the strict chord inequality proved above gives

\[ \phi(-a)<\frac{a}{b}\phi(-b). \]

Dividing by \(a\) and rearranging yields \(\rho(a)>\rho(b)\). Thus the coefficients can be ordered simply by sorting the rational claims; no evaluations of \(\phi\) are needed.

  • Algorithm and proof of optimality.

If \(e=0\), return \(\nu_t=\delta_0\) for every type. This is feasible and is the only possible allocation: all awards are nonnegative and every \(p_t\) is positive.

Otherwise, list the positive-claim types as \(1,\ldots,n\), where \(n\leq\tau\), in nondecreasing claim order. Break equal-claim ties arbitrarily. Define

\[ Q_0=0,\qquad Q_j=\sum_{i=1}^j q_i. \]

Because \(0<e\leq Q_n\), there is a smallest index \(k\) with \(Q_k\geq e\). Set

\[ y_i^*= \begin{cases} q_i,&i<k,\\ e-Q_{k-1},&i=k,\\ 0,&i>k. \end{cases} \]

These quantities satisfy all capacity constraints and sum to \(e\).

To prove optimality, take any feasible vector \(y\), and set \(r=\rho(c_k)\). Equal resource totals imply

\[ \sum_i\rho(c_i)(y_i^*-y_i) = \sum_i\bigl(\rho(c_i)-r\bigr)(y_i^*-y_i). \]

Every summand on the right is nonnegative. For \(i<k\), both factors are nonnegative because \(\rho(c_i)\geq r\) and \(y_i^*=q_i\geq y_i\). For \(i>k\), both factors are nonpositive because \(\rho(c_i)\leq r\) and \(y_i^*=0\leq y_i\). The summand for \(i=k\) is zero. Therefore \(y^*\) maximizes the linear objective.

Return

\[ \nu_i^*= \begin{cases} \delta_{c_i},&i<k,\\ (1-\theta)\delta_0+\theta\delta_{c_k},&i=k,\\ \delta_0,&i>k, \end{cases} \qquad \theta=\frac{e-Q_{k-1}}{p_kc_k}. \]

For zero-claim types, return \(\delta_0\). Omit atoms with zero probability. All atoms and probabilities are rational, and the endpoint reduction and linear optimality proof establish global optimality among the original measures.

  • Exact running time and representation size.

Sorting requires \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) rational comparisons. Computing capacities, finding the threshold, and forming \(\theta\) require \(O(\tau)\) further exact arithmetic operations.

All required numbers have \(O(L)\) bits. To verify this, write the input rationals as integer numerator-denominator pairs and let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of \(e\) and all \(p_t,c_t\). Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Every capacity and prefix sum has a denominator dividing \(D\). Moreover,

\[ 0\leq Q_j\leq\sum_t p_tc_t\leq\max_t c_t, \]

whose binary magnitude is bounded by the input length. Thus their numerators over denominator \(D\) also have \(O(L)\) bits. The residual and the ratio defining \(\theta\) retain this bound.

Using this common denominator, classical arithmetic gives the stated bound of \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2)\) bit operations. Only the threshold type can require two atoms; every other type requires one. The total output encoding length is \(O(L)\).

The algorithm does not evaluate \(\phi\). If the optimal welfare value is also requested, the assumed exact polynomial-time evaluation of \(\phi\) makes that additional computation polynomial-time as well.
