The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 11 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite male types \(M\) and female types \(W\), rational masses \((\alpha_m)_{m\in M}\) and \((\beta_w)_{w\in W}\), and a finite rational distribution over preference profiles \(\Omega\), does there exist a coupling \(x=(x_{m,w})_{m\in M,w\in W}\ge0\) with row sums \(\alpha\) and column sums \(\beta\) that is stable in every \(\omega\in\Omega\)? Stability means that for every type pair \((m,w)\), either \(\sum_{w':\,w\succ_m^\omega w'}x_{m,w'}=0\) or \(\sum_{m':\,m\succ_w^\omega m'}x_{m',w}=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: nonempty finite sets of male types \(M\) and female types \(W\), each type having positive binary rational mass. Let \(\tau=|M|+|W|\) be the support size, not the number of possible types, and let \(m=\max\{|M|,|W|\}\). Normalize \(\sum_{a\in M}\alpha_a=\sum_{b\in W}\beta_b=1\).
The input also explicitly lists a nonempty support \(\Omega\) of \(k\) preference profiles with positive binary rational probabilities summing to \(1\). Each profile specifies, for every type, a strict complete ranking of all opposite-side types; all partners are acceptable. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
A coupling is a real matrix \(x\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{b\in W}x_{a,b}=\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_{a\in M}x_{a,b}=\beta_b\). It is stable in profile \(\omega\) if, for every \((a,b)\in M\times W\), either \(\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a^\omega b'}x_{a,b'}=0\) or \(\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b^\omega a'}x_{a',b}=0\). Certainly stable means stable in every profile in \(\Omega\).

Is deciding whether a certainly stable coupling exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete agents with strict complete rankings and an explicitly listed joint distribution over profiles, existence of a certainly stable matching is NP-complete, even with only \(16\) profiles. [Aziz et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-019-00650-0)
No result settling this precise coupling-existence problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The mass normalization was unspecified; both sides have total mass one, carrying over the balanced market while allowing different numbers of supported types.
  • The preference domain was unspecified; each profile uses strict complete rankings over all opposite-side types, with every partner acceptable.
  • Only positive-mass types and positive-probability profiles are listed; both society sides and the profile support are nonempty.
  • The profile distribution is represented explicitly, with positive binary rational probabilities summing to one.
  • Coupling entries may be arbitrary nonnegative real numbers; all mass must be matched through the prescribed marginal equalities.
  • For running-time notation, m denotes the larger opposite-side ranking domain, tau the combined society support size, k the profile support size, and L the total binary input length.

Result

The coupling-existence problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(|M|=|W|=n\), every type has mass \(1/n\), and there are at most \(16\) profiles.

The key fact is that, for uniform masses, any certainly stable coupling contains a certainly stable discrete matching in its positive support.

1. Stability is preserved by restricting support

For a nonnegative matrix \(x\), define its positive support by
\( E(x)=\{(a,b):x_{a,b}>0\}. \)

Lemma 1. Suppose \(x\) is stable in a profile \(\omega\). If \(y\) is a coupling satisfying \(E(y)\subseteq E(x)\), then \(y\) is stable in \(\omega\).

Proof. Fix a pair \((a,b)\). Stability of \(x\) means that at least one of
\( \sum_{b':\,b\succ_a^\omega b'}x_{a,b'} \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{a':\,a\succ_b^\omega a'}x_{a',b} \)
is zero.

Every summand is nonnegative. Consequently, a zero sum forces all its summands to be zero. Support containment forces the corresponding entries of \(y\) to be zero as well. Thus the same sum is zero for \(y\), proving its stability at \((a,b)\). This holds for every pair. \(\square\)

Applying the lemma separately to each profile shows that support restriction also preserves certain stability.

2. Uniform masses give an exact discrete equivalence

Lemma 2. Suppose \(|M|=|W|=n\) and \(\alpha_a=\beta_b=1/n\) for every type. A certainly stable coupling exists if and only if a perfect matching stable in every listed profile exists.

Proof.

First, let \(\mu:M\to W\) be a perfect matching, and define
\( y^\mu_{a,b}= \begin{cases} 1/n,&b=\mu(a),\\ 0,&b\ne\mu(a). \end{cases} \)
This is a coupling with the required margins.

In any profile \(\omega\), its two dissatisfaction sums at \((a,b)\) are
\( \sum_{b':\,b\succ_a^\omega b'}y^\mu_{a,b'} =\frac1n\,\mathbf{1}\{b\succ_a^\omega\mu(a)\} \)
and
\( \sum_{a':\,a\succ_b^\omega a'}y^\mu_{a',b} =\frac1n\,\mathbf{1}\{a\succ_b^\omega\mu^{-1}(b)\}. \)
Both sums are positive exactly when \((a,b)\) is a discrete blocking pair. Hence \(y^\mu\) is stable in \(\omega\) exactly when \(\mu\) is stable there. This proves one direction.

Conversely, let \(x\) be a certainly stable coupling. Consider the finite bipartite graph with vertex classes \(M,W\) and edge set \(E(x)\). For \(S\subseteq M\), let \(N(S)\subseteq W\) be its neighborhood. The margin equations give
\( \frac{|S|}{n} =\sum_{a\in S}\sum_{b\in N(S)}x_{a,b} \leq \sum_{b\in N(S)}\sum_{a\in M}x_{a,b} =\frac{|N(S)|}{n}. \)
Thus \(|N(S)|\geq|S|\) for every \(S\subseteq M\). Hall’s marriage theorem applies and supplies a matching saturating \(M\); because both vertex classes have size \(n\), this is a perfect matching \(\mu\).

The coupling \(y^\mu\) defined above satisfies \(E(y^\mu)\subseteq E(x)\). By Lemma 1 it is stable in every listed profile. The displayed characterization of its dissatisfaction sums then shows that \(\mu\) is stable in every profile. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial reduction and hardness

The discrete result needed here is precisely Corollary 6 of [Aziz et al., *Stable Matching with Uncertain Linear Preferences*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-019-00650-0): deciding whether a certainly stable matching exists in the explicitly listed joint probability model is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with \(16\) profiles. Their setting uses equal numbers of men and women, strict complete rankings, and universal acceptability.

Take such a discrete instance with \(n\) agents on each side. Construct the coupling instance as follows:

  • Introduce one male type for each man and one female type for each woman.
  • Assign mass \(1/n\) to every type.
  • Copy every profile’s rankings.

Give the listed profiles equal positive probabilities. Their precise probabilities do not affect certain stability. If duplicate profiles occur, merge them; this leaves at most \(16\) profiles.

Every stable matching in the source instance is perfect: an incomplete matching leaves both a man and a woman unmatched, and their pair blocks because all partners are acceptable. Therefore Lemma 2 proves the equivalence
\( \text{the discrete instance has a certainly stable matching} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed instance has a certainly stable coupling}. \)

The construction is polynomial. Each mass \(1/n\), represented by a binary numerator and denominator, requires \(O(\log n)\) bits. Rankings are copied without expansion, and profile probabilities require polynomially many bits. Moreover, the constructed parameters are \(m=n\) and \(\tau=2n\).

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the stated restricted family.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) for arbitrary rational masses

Real-valued witnesses require some care. We prove that every yes-instance has a rational witness of polynomial encoding length.

Lemma 3. If a certainly stable coupling exists, then one exists whose positive-support graph is a forest.

Proof. Start with any certainly stable coupling \(x\). If its positive-support graph contains a cycle, the cycle has even length because the graph is bipartite. Label its edges alternately with plus and minus signs.

Let \(t>0\) be the minimum entry of \(x\) on the minus edges. Add \(t\) on the plus edges and subtract \(t\) on the minus edges.

At every cycle vertex, one incident entry increases and another decreases by the same amount. Thus all row and column sums remain unchanged. Entries remain nonnegative, at least one minus edge becomes zero, and no previously zero entry becomes positive. Lemma 1 preserves certain stability.

Each operation strictly decreases the number of positive entries. After finitely many operations, the positive-support graph is a forest. \(\square\)

Now write all input masses as binary-encoded fractions, and let \(Q\) be the product of their denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), since the sum of the denominators’ encoding lengths is at most \(L\).

Let \(z\) be the forest-supported coupling supplied by Lemma 3. Consider any positive edge \(e=(a,b)\). Deleting \(e\) from its tree leaves two components; let \(C\) be the component containing \(a\). Subtracting the column-margin equations for women in \(C\) from the row-margin equations for men in \(C\) gives
\( z_{a,b} = \sum_{u\in M\cap C}\alpha_u - \sum_{v\in W\cap C}\beta_v. \)

To justify this identity, every edge internal to \(C\) appears once in each sum and cancels. The only positive edge crossing its boundary is \(e\), whose male endpoint lies in \(C\), so its contribution is \(+z_{a,b}\).

Consequently, \(Qz_{a,b}\) is an integer. Also \(0\leq z_{a,b}\leq1\), so every entry has the form \(r_{a,b}/Q\) with an integer satisfying \(0\leq r_{a,b}\leq Q\). Each numerator therefore has \(O(L)\) bits. Since there are at most \(|M||W|\) entries, the whole witness has polynomial encoding length.

A verifier computes \(Q\), reads the integer numerators, and checks by exact integer arithmetic:

  • nonnegativity;
  • every prescribed row and column sum;
  • for every profile and pair, whether at least one of the two dissatisfaction sums is zero.

There are polynomially many checks in \(m,\tau,k,L\), and all integers involved have polynomial bit length. Thus verification runs in polynomial time, proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Conclusion

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Allowing divisible mass does not defeat the discrete hardness: uniform margins force a perfect matching inside the positive support, and that matching inherits stability simultaneously across all profiles. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,k,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
