The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(d>9\), rational \(B,\sigma>0\), \(\varepsilon>0\), and rational masses \(\nu_{ij,y}\ge 0\) summing to \(1\) over \(1\le i<j\le d\) and \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\), with positive-mass pairs forming a connected graph, compute a rational \(\hat w\) satisfying \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\hat w=0\) and \(\|\hat w\|_\infty\le B\) whose mass-weighted BTL negative log-likelihood is within \(\varepsilon\) of its minimum.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m=d>9\) be the number of items, and let \(B,\sigma,\varepsilon\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\) be inputs. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \((i,j,y)\), where \(1\le i<j\le m\) and \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\nu_{ij,y}\); masses sum to \(1\), and omitted types have mass zero. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The graph with edges \(\{i,j\}\) whenever \(\nu_{ij,+1}+\nu_{ij,-1}>0\) must be connected.
The outcome \(y=+1\) favors item \(i\). For \(w\in\mathbb R^m\), the BTL probability of outcome \(y\) is \((1+\exp(-y(w_i-w_j)/\sigma))^{-1}\). Define \(W=\{w\in\mathbb R^m:\mathbf1^\top w=0,\ \|w\|_\infty\le B\}\) and \(F_\nu(w)=\sum_{(i,j,y)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)}\nu_{ij,y}\log(1+\exp(-y(w_i-w_j)/\sigma))\), using natural logarithms.
The computational problem is to return \(\hat w\in W\cap\mathbb Q^m\) with \(F_\nu(\hat w)\le\min_{w\in W}F_\nu(w)+\varepsilon\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the accuracy parameter.

Is this additive-accuracy optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite comparison samples, bounded BTL maximum likelihood estimation attains the minimax squared error rate \(m\sigma^2/n\) in the standardized comparison-Laplacian seminorm, with constants depending on \(B/\sigma\), under a sample-size condition involving the Laplacian pseudoinverse; this is a statistical result, not a bit-complexity classification. [Shah et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.6618)
Convergence rates for gradient descent and MM algorithms for BTL likelihood estimation are also known; they do not by themselves classify the rational-input problem stated here. [Vojnovic et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/vojnovic20a/vojnovic20a.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made epsilon a positive binary rational so the accuracy request has a finite input encoding; included it in total input length L.
  • Fixed the orientation of each pair so y=+1 favors i, and specified natural logarithms and the mass-normalized negative log-likelihood.
  • Used m=d to express running times in the requested society convention.
  • Did not impose the finite-sample threshold on the mass optimization input: it concerns the statistical minimax bound and has no sample-count counterpart in the stated computational question.

Result

The problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof uses rational ellipsoid optimization with approximate gradients. Crucially, the running time depends logarithmically on \(B/\sigma\) and on \(1/\varepsilon\). Neither a statistical error bound nor a strong convexity bound is needed.

1. Normalize the feasible region

If \(\varepsilon\ge 1\), return \(\hat w=0\). Indeed, the masses sum to one, so \(F_\nu(0)=\log 2<1\), while \(F_\nu(w)\ge 0\).

Henceforth assume \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Put \(n=m-1\) and \(a=B/\sigma\). Define the rational linear map

\(Ax=(x_1,\ldots,x_n,-\sum_{k=1}^n x_k)\).

Then \(w=BAx\) gives a bijection between \(W\) and

\(K=\{x\in\mathbb R^n: |x_k|\le 1\text{ for every }k,\ |\sum_{k=1}^n x_k|\le 1\}\).

Membership in \(K\), and separation from \(K\), use only rational arithmetic. Moreover, \(K\) is compact and convex, contains the Euclidean ball of radius \(1/m\) centered at zero, and is contained in the ball of radius \(R=m\).

Let \(h(t)=\log(1+\exp(t))\). For each listed type define

\(b_{ij,y}=-yaA^\top(e_i-e_j)\).

The normalized objective is

\(f(x)=F_\nu(BAx)=\sum_{(i,j,y)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)}\nu_{ij,y}h(b_{ij,y}^\top x)\).

Since \(h''(t)=\exp(t)/(1+\exp(t))^2>0\), the function \(f\) is convex. It attains its minimum \(f_*\) on \(K\). Also,

\(0\le f_*\le f(0)=\log 2<1\).

We next obtain a Lipschitz bound whose binary length is small. The identity \(A^\top A=I_n+\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top\) gives \(\|A\|_2=\sqrt m\). Consequently,

\(\|b_{ij,y}\|_2\le a\sqrt{2m}\le 2ma=:C\).

Because \(0<h'(t)<1\) and the masses sum to one,

\(\|\nabla f(x)\|_2\le\sum_{(i,j,y)}\nu_{ij,y}\|b_{ij,y}\|_2\le C\).

Integrating the gradient along a line segment shows that

\(|f(x)-f(z)|\le C\|x-z\|_2\).

Although \(C\) can be exponentially large, \(\log(1+C)=O(L+\log m)\).

2. Polynomial-time rational value and gradient oracles

We first justify the elementary function computations.

Lemma. Given a rational \(t\) of binary length \(\ell\) and an integer \(k\ge 1\), rational approximations to \(h(t)\) and \(h'(t)\), each with absolute error at most \(2^{-k}\), can be computed in time polynomial in \(\ell,k\).

Proof. Set \(u=|t|\) and \(q=\exp(-u)\). The identities

\(h(t)=\max\{t,0\}+\log(1+q)\)

and

\(h'(t)=1/(1+q)\) for \(t\ge 0\), while \(h'(t)=q/(1+q)\) for \(t<0\),

avoid computing an exponentially large exponential.

If \(u\ge k+4\), approximate \(q\) by zero. Since \(e>2\), the error is at most \(2^{-(k+4)}\).

Otherwise, approximate \(\exp(-u)\) by its degree-\(N\) Taylor polynomial, choosing \(N+1\ge 8(k+5)\). Taylor’s remainder satisfies

\(\left|\exp(-u)-\sum_{r=0}^{N}(-u)^r/r!\right|\le u^{N+1}/(N+1)!\).

The factorial bound \(s!\ge(s/e)^s\), obtained by integrating \(\log x\), and \(e<3\), imply

\(u^{N+1}/(N+1)!\le(eu/(N+1))^{N+1}\le(3/8)^{N+1}<2^{-(k+4)}\).

Clip this rational approximation to the interval \([0,1]\); clipping cannot increase its error.

For a rational \(v\in[0,1]\), put \(z=v/(2+v)\), so \(0\le z\le 1/3\). Then

\(\log(1+v)=2\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}z^{2r+1}/(2r+1)\).

After \(J\) terms, the remainder is at most

\(2z^{2J+1}/((2J+1)(1-z^2))\le(9/4)3^{-(2J+1)}\).

Taking \(J=k+4\) makes this at most \(2^{-(k+4)}\). Finally, each of the functions \(\log(1+v)\), \(1/(1+v)\), and \(v/(1+v)\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz on \([0,1]\). Thus the stated formulas produce the required accuracy.

All series have \(O(k)\) terms. Exact rational intermediate numerators and denominators have polynomial binary length in \(\ell,k\), proving the time bound. \(\square\)

Apply the lemma separately to the \(\tau\) listed types. Since the masses sum to one, scalar value errors bounded by \(\delta\) give

\(|\widetilde f(x)-f(x)|\le\delta\).

For gradients, approximate every scalar derivative within

\(\theta=\eta/(2(C+1))\).

Before coordinate rounding, the gradient error is at most \(C\theta\le\eta/2\). Round each coordinate to a dyadic rational with error at most \(\eta/(2m)\). The additional Euclidean error is at most \(\sqrt n\,\eta/(2m)\le\eta/2\). Hence the returned rational vector \(g(x)\) satisfies

\(\|g(x)-\nabla f(x)\|_2\le\eta\).

The oracle running times are polynomial in the query length, \(m,\tau,L\), and the binary lengths of the requested tolerances. In particular, the gradient precision requires only logarithmic dependence on \(1+C\). The dyadic vector \(g(x)\) has polynomial binary length independently of the query point, since each coordinate has magnitude at most \(C+\eta\).

3. A ball of nearly optimal feasible points

Let \(x_*\in K\) be a minimizer. Define

\(\lambda=\varepsilon/8,\qquad z=(1-\lambda)x_*,\qquad \rho=\varepsilon/(8m(1+2a))\).

We claim that the entire Euclidean ball \(\mathcal B_2(z,\rho)\) lies in \(K\) and has objective value at most \(f_*+\varepsilon/4\).

Every coordinate constraint and each of the two sum constraints has slack at least \(\lambda\) at \(z\). A perturbation \(v\) with \(\|v\|_2\le\rho\) satisfies

\(|v_k|\le\rho\le\lambda,\qquad |\sum_kv_k|\le\sqrt n\,\rho\le m\rho\le\lambda\).

Thus \(z+v\in K\).

By convexity and \(f(0)<1\),

\(f(z)\le(1-\lambda)f_*+\lambda f(0)\le f_*+\lambda\).

The Lipschitz bound then gives

\(f(z+v)\le f_*+\lambda+C\rho\le f_*+\varepsilon/4\),

because \(C\rho=\varepsilon\,2a/(8(1+2a))\le\varepsilon/8\).

Finally,

\(\log(R/\rho)=\log(8m^2(1+2a)/\varepsilon)=O(L+\log m)\).

Thus this ball has a radius suitable for polynomial-time ellipsoid analysis, even when a minimizer lies on the boundary.

4. Rational ellipsoid algorithm and its correctness

We use the following standard finite-precision central-cut ellipsoid result: starting with a rational radius-\(R\) ball in dimension \(n\ge 2\), after \(O(n^2\log(R/\rho))\) nonzero rational central cuts, the algorithm produces an enclosing ellipsoid whose volume is smaller than that of a radius-\(\rho\) ball. Each updated ellipsoid contains the intersection of its predecessor with the supplied halfspace. If cut vectors have polynomial binary length, all centers, updates, and computations have polynomial binary complexity in the dimension, number of cuts, cut lengths, and radius encodings. This is the rational implementation of the central-cut method described in [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver, *The Ellipsoid Method*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4_4).

Set

\(\delta=\varepsilon/16,\qquad \eta=\varepsilon/(16m)\).

Start the rational ellipsoid algorithm with the radius-\(R=m\) ball. At each rational center \(c\):

  • If \(c\notin K\), use a violated defining inequality of \(K\) to supply a central cut containing \(K\).
  • If \(c\in K\), record \(c\) and \(\widetilde f(c)\), and compute \(g(c)\) with the error bound above.
  • If \(g(c)=0\), return \(c\). Otherwise supply the central cut \(g(c)^\top(x-c)\le 0\).

For step 1, if a defining inequality is \(\ell^\top x\le\beta\) and \(\ell^\top c>\beta\), the central halfspace \(\ell^\top(x-c)\le 0\) contains \(K\).

If step 3 returns \(c\), convexity gives

\(f(c)-f_*\le\nabla f(c)^\top(c-x_*)\le\eta\|c-x_*\|_2\le2m\eta=\varepsilon/8\).

This is already sufficient.

Otherwise, run enough cuts to obtain the volume reduction in the stated theorem. At least one recorded feasible center must satisfy

\(f(c)\le f_*+\varepsilon/2\).

To prove this, suppose instead that every feasible queried center has \(f(c)>f_*+\varepsilon/2\). For any \(x\in\mathcal B_2(z,\rho)\), convexity and the gradient error imply

\(g(c)^\top(x-c)\le f(x)-f(c)+\eta\|x-c\|_2\).

Both \(x,c\) lie in \(K\), whose diameter is at most \(2m\). Section 3 therefore yields

\(g(c)^\top(x-c)<\varepsilon/4-\varepsilon/2+2m\eta=-\varepsilon/8<0\).

Thus every objective cut preserves \(\mathcal B_2(z,\rho)\). Every feasibility cut also preserves it. Consequently, every enclosing ellipsoid must contain that ball, contradicting the final volume bound. This proves the claim.

Choose a recorded feasible center \(\hat x\) with smallest approximate value. For the center \(c\) just established,

\(f(\hat x)\le\widetilde f(\hat x)+\delta\le\widetilde f(c)+\delta\le f(c)+2\delta\le f_*+5\varepsilon/8\).

Return \(\hat w=BA\hat x\). It is rational, lies exactly in \(W\), and satisfies the required objective guarantee.

5. Bit complexity and conclusion

The number of ellipsoid iterations is

\(O(m^2(L+\log m))\).

Feasibility cuts have small integer normals. Objective-cut normals are dyadic vectors of polynomial binary length. The rational ellipsoid theorem therefore guarantees polynomial-length query points and polynomial-time updates. Each value or gradient evaluation processes the \(\tau\) listed types with polynomial precision, so its bit complexity is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The final rational output also has polynomial binary length.

This proves the requested classification. The argument applies to every stated connected instance; indeed, connectivity is unnecessary for this additive optimization guarantee.
