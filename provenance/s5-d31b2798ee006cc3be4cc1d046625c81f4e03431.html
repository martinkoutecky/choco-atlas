The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two unit-mass populations with rational distributions over seeker and employer types, where each type is a cohort plus a strict ranking of opposite-side cohorts, and reference scale \(N\), hidden evolution events choose a side and a \(1/N\) mass unit, then swap two uniformly chosen adjacent labels in that unit's ranking. With at most one comparison query per round to a selected current type's ranking, can a causal policy maintain a feasible coupling \(x_t\) whose blocking-pair mass \(\sum_{u,v} E_{u\to v}(x_t)F_{v\to u}(x_t)\) is \(O((\log N)^2/N^2)\) in expectation and with high probability after \(c_0N^2\log N\) rounds?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are two unit-mass populations, each with \(m\) nonempty cohorts, where \(2\le m\le N\). A type consists of its cohort \(c(u)\) and a strict, complete ranking \(\succ_u\) of opposite-side cohorts; members of the same cohort are indifferent. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the combined support size, not the number of possible types, and \(L\) is the input bit length.
Each population consists of \(N\) persistently labelled parcels of mass \(1/N\); admissible masses are multiples of \(1/N\). Cohort assignments are fixed and arbitrary. Initially, each parcel independently draws a uniformly random ranking of opposite-side cohorts; the resulting initial support is supplied, and parcels receive a canonical labelling within each initial type.
In each round, the policy may ask one comparison between two cohort labels in a selected parcel's current ranking, then choose its allocation. Afterwards, one hidden event independently chooses a side uniformly, a parcel uniformly, and a position uniformly from \(\{1,\ldots,m-1\}\), and swaps the labels at that position and the next. Events and current type memberships are not revealed.
An allocation is a nonnegative rational parcel coupling \(y_t\) with every row and column sum \(1/N\); parcels may split their allocation arbitrarily. Its aggregation by true current types is \(x_t\), with row masses \(p_t(u)\) and column masses \(q_t(v)\). The policy is causal: its choices use only the initial input, previous comparisons, and its own randomness.
Define \(E_{u\to v}(x_t)=\sum_{w:\,c(v)\succ_u c(w)}x_t(u,w)\), \(F_{v\to u}(x_t)=\sum_{z:\,c(u)\succ_v c(z)}x_t(z,v)\), and \(B_t=\sum_{u,v}E_{u\to v}(x_t)F_{v\to u}(x_t)\), evaluated after the hidden event.
The required guarantee is: for every fixed \(t\ge c_0N^2\log N\), \(\mathbb E[B_t]\le C(\log N)^2/N^2\), and for every constant \(a>0\), \(\Pr[B_t>C_a(\log N)^2/N^2]\le N^{-a}\), for sufficiently large \(N\). Constants are uniform over cohort assignments; probability includes initialization, evolution, and policy randomness.
Does maintaining this guarantee admit a causal policy with polynomial preprocessing and polynomial computation per round in \(m,\tau,L,N\), or is maintaining it NP-hard?
For discrete stable marriage with independently uniform initial rankings and one comparison and one hidden random adjacent swap per round, a polynomial-time algorithm maintains \(O((\log n)^2)\) blocking pairs at each fixed round after \(c_0n^2\log n\), in expectation and with high probability ([verified result](https://arxiv.org/html/1509.01988v2)). No result settling the specified mass-coupling problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made N an integer population scale and required masses to be multiples of 1/N, so a homogeneous mass unit can always undergo the specified swap; parcels remain divisible for allocation.
  • Used m nonempty cohorts on each side, with 2 <= m <= N, complete strict cohort rankings, and indifference within a cohort.
  • Restored independent uniformly random initial rankings at the parcel level, rather than arbitrary initial rankings, to retain the anchor's stated warm-up time.
  • Interpreted the supplied support as known initially; subsequent rankings and support changes are hidden.
  • Gave mass units persistent canonical labels so comparisons remain addressable without revealing current types.
  • Fixed one independent evolution event per round, with uniform side, parcel, and adjacent-position selection; evaluation occurs after evolution.
  • Represented the policy's implementable allocation by a rational parcel coupling, whose aggregation defines the feasible current type coupling.
  • Defined both willingness masses using allocation to strictly worse cohorts, with no blocking through indifference.
  • Interpreted high probability pointwise at every fixed eligible round, with arbitrary polynomial failure exponent, rather than simultaneously over an infinite run.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Resolved: the required guarantee is unattainable

No causal policy satisfies the stated guarantee, even with unlimited computation and full knowledge of all rankings immediately before each hidden event. The obstruction already occurs for \(m=2\) and combined support size \(\tau\leq 8\).

I prove that, for an admissible sequence of cohort assignments, every policy satisfies, at every fixed round and for sufficiently large \(N\),

\[ \mathbb E[B_t]\geq \frac{1}{2048N}, \qquad \Pr\!\left[B_t\geq \frac{1}{256N}\right]\geq \frac{1}{256}. \]

These bounds contradict both required guarantees. Thus the proposed polynomial-time-versus-NP-hardness dichotomy has an infeasible premise.

1. An admissible two-cohort construction

Take \(N\) to be a sufficiently large power of two and set \(m=2\). In each population, denoted \(A\) and \(B\), assign \(3N/4\) parcels to cohort \(1\) and \(N/4\) parcels to cohort \(2\). Both cohorts are nonempty.

A ranking is determined by its favorite cohort, which is initially independently uniform on \(\{1,2\}\). Every type mass is a multiple of \(1/N\), hence a binary rational for these choices of \(N\). There are at most four types per population.

For the proof, grant the policy complete knowledge of the current rankings before it selects its allocation. The allocation must still precede the independent hidden event. Proving impossibility under this additional information proves impossibility for the specified policies.

2. The blocking functional for two cohorts

Fix a round, its state before the hidden event, and the allocation \(y\) selected by the policy.

For \(a,b\in\{1,2\}\), let \(R^A_{ab}\) be the mass of population-\(A\) parcels whose own cohort is \(a\) and whose favorite opposite-side cohort is \(b\). Let \(D^A_{ab}\) be the mass these parcels allocate to their less-preferred cohort:

\[ D^A_{ab} = \sum_{\substack{i\in A:\ c(i)=a\\ i\text{ favors }b}} \ \sum_{\substack{j\in B:\ c(j)=3-b}}y(i,j). \]

Write \(S^A_{ab}=R^A_{ab}-D^A_{ab}\) for their allocation to their favorite cohort. Define \(R^B_{ab},D^B_{ab},S^B_{ab}\) analogously, with the first index always denoting own cohort and the second favorite cohort.

Let \(\mathcal B^-\) denote the blocking functional for this allocation before the event. Then

\[ \mathcal B^-=\sum_{a,b=1}^{2}D^A_{ab}D^B_{ba}. \]

Indeed, for an \(A\)-type with own cohort \(a\) and favorite cohort \(b\), the quantity \(E_{u\to v}\) is nonzero only when \(v\) belongs to cohort \(b\), and then equals \(D^A_{ab}\). The corresponding \(F_{v\to u}\) is nonzero only when that \(B\)-type favors cohort \(a\), and then equals \(D^B_{ba}\). This gives exactly the four displayed terms.

This identity allows arbitrary parcel splitting.

3. Exact effect of one hidden event

Put \(\bar a=3-a\) and \(\bar b=3-b\). Since \(m=2\), every event reverses the selected parcel’s ranking.

Suppose the event selects an \(A\)-parcel with own cohort \(a\) and favorite cohort \(b\). Let \(g\) be its allocation to cohort \(b\), and let \(\ell=1/N-g\) be its allocation to cohort \(\bar b\).

The event removes \(\ell\) from \(D^A_{ab}\) and adds \(g\) to \(D^A_{a\bar b}\). The \(B\)-side dissatisfaction masses remain unchanged: they depend on \(B\)-rankings and the fixed cohorts of their partners. Consequently,

\[ B_t=\mathcal B^- -\ell D^B_{ba}+gD^B_{\bar b a}. \]

The symmetric formula holds when a \(B\)-parcel is selected. Averaging over the \(2N\) equally likely selected parcels gives

\[ \mathbb E[B_t\mid\text{current state and chosen allocation}] = \left(1-\frac1N\right)\mathcal B^-+\frac{H}{2N}, \]

where

\[ H= \sum_{a,b=1}^{2}S^A_{ab}D^B_{\bar b a} + \sum_{a,b=1}^{2}S^B_{ba}D^A_{\bar a b}. \]

To verify the subtraction coefficient, the removed contributions sum to \(\mathcal B^-\) over all \(A\)-parcels and to another \(\mathcal B^-\) over all \(B\)-parcels. Each parcel is selected with probability \(1/(2N)\).

The same individual-event formula also shows that every event satisfies

\[ B_t\geq\mathcal B^- -\frac1N, \]

because \(\ell\leq1/N\), every dissatisfaction mass is at most \(1\), and the added term is nonnegative.

4. A typical state forces substantial expected blocking

Set \(\delta=1/16\). Consider the event \(G\) that the state before the hidden event satisfies

\[ R^A_{12}\geq\frac{5}{16},\quad R^B_{12}\geq\frac{5}{16},\quad R^A_{22}\geq\delta,\quad R^B_{22}\geq\delta. \]

For every allocation, the entire opposite-side cohort \(2\) has mass only \(1/4\). Thus \(S^A_{12}\leq1/4\) and \(S^B_{12}\leq1/4\). On \(G\), this forces

\[ D^A_{12}\geq\delta, \qquad D^B_{12}\geq\delta. \]

In particular, the expression for \(H\) implies

\[ H\geq S^A_{22}D^B_{12}+S^B_{22}D^A_{12} \geq\delta\bigl(S^A_{22}+S^B_{22}\bigr). \]

There are two cases.

Case 1: At least one of \(S^A_{22},S^B_{22}\) is at least \(\delta/2\).

Then \(H\geq\delta^2/2\), so the exact conditional expectation yields

\[ \mathbb E[B_t\mid\text{state and allocation}] \geq\frac{\delta^2}{4N}. \]

Case 2: Both \(S^A_{22}\) and \(S^B_{22}\) are less than \(\delta/2\).

Since \(R^A_{22},R^B_{22}\geq\delta\), both \(D^A_{22}\) and \(D^B_{22}\) exceed \(\delta/2\). Hence \(\mathcal B^-\geq\delta^2/4\). For \(N\geq2\),

\[ \mathbb E[B_t\mid\text{state and allocation}] \geq \left(1-\frac1N\right)\frac{\delta^2}{4} \geq\frac{\delta^2}{4N}. \]

Therefore, on \(G\), every selected allocation satisfies

\[ \mathbb E[B_t\mid\text{state and allocation}] \geq\frac{1}{1024N}. \]

5. The typical-state event persists at every fixed round

It remains to check the distribution of the state, including the effect of canonical initial labelling.

Within a fixed cohort containing \(K\) parcels, let \(X\) count those favoring cohort \(2\). Initially, \(X\) has distribution \(\operatorname{Bin}(K,1/2)\). Conditional on an event selecting that cohort, its count increases with probability \((K-X)/K\) and decreases with probability \(X/K\).

The binomial probabilities \(\pi_K(k)=2^{-K}\binom Kk\) satisfy

\[ \pi_K(k)\frac{K-k}{K} = \pi_K(k+1)\frac{k+1}{K}. \]

Thus this count transition preserves the binomial distribution. The four cohort counts initially have independent binomial distributions. Each event updates one count, selecting its cohort with the fixed probability \(K/(2N)\). Each such coordinate update preserves the product distribution, so their mixture does too.

Canonical labelling does not change these counts or their transition probabilities. Comparisons and allocations do not affect their evolution. Consequently, at every fixed round,

\[ NR^A_{12},NR^B_{12}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(3N/4,1/2), \qquad NR^A_{22},NR^B_{22}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(N/4,1/2). \]

For a binomial variable with mean \(\mu\), the multiplicative Chernoff bound gives
\(\Pr[X\leq(1-\varepsilon)\mu]\leq\exp(-\varepsilon^2\mu/2)\).
The first two thresholds defining \(G\) are \(5/6\) of their means, and the last two are \(1/2\) of their means. A union bound therefore gives

\[ \Pr(G) \geq1-2e^{-N/192}-2e^{-N/64} \geq1-4e^{-N/192}. \]

Combining this with Section 4 proves, for sufficiently large \(N\) and every fixed round,

\[ \mathbb E[B_t] \geq\frac{1-4e^{-N/192}}{1024N} \geq\frac{1}{2048N}. \]

No independence between the policy’s allocation and \(G\) is needed: the conditional bound holds for every allocation on every state in \(G\).

6. A constant-probability lower bound

The high-probability requirement also fails directly.

In Case 1, suppose \(S^A_{22}\geq\delta/2\); the \(B\)-side case is symmetric. For each \(A\)-parcel of type \((2,2)\), let \(g_i\) be its allocation to \(B\)-cohort \(2\).

There are at most \(N/4\) such parcels. Those with \(g_i<\delta/N\) contribute at most \(\delta/4\) in total. Therefore the parcels with \(g_i\geq\delta/N\) contribute at least \(\delta/4\). Since each contributes at most \(1/N\), there are at least \(\delta N/4\) of them.

The hidden event selects one of these parcels with probability at least \(\delta/8\). After its ranking reverses, its allocation \(g_i\) becomes dissatisfaction toward cohort \(1\). Thus \(D^A_{21}\geq g_i\) after the event, while \(D^B_{12}\geq\delta\) remains unchanged. Hence

\[ B_t\geq D^A_{21}D^B_{12}\geq\frac{\delta^2}{N}. \]

In Case 2, \(\mathcal B^-\geq\delta^2/4\). For \(N\geq2048\), the individual-event bound gives, for every event,

\[ B_t\geq\frac{\delta^2}{4}-\frac1N \geq\frac{\delta^2}{8} \geq\frac{\delta^2}{N}. \]

Consequently, on every state in \(G\),

\[ \Pr\!\left[B_t\geq\frac{1}{256N} \mid\text{state and allocation}\right]\geq\frac{1}{128}. \]

Using \(\Pr(G)\geq1/2\) for sufficiently large \(N\) proves

\[ \Pr\!\left[B_t\geq\frac{1}{256N}\right]\geq\frac{1}{256}. \]

7. Consequence for the task

Since \(N/(\log N)^2\) tends to infinity, the expectation lower bound exceeds \(C(\log N)^2/N^2\) for every fixed \(C\) once \(N\) is sufficiently large. Likewise, for every fixed \(C_a\), the probability of exceeding \(C_a(\log N)^2/N^2\) is at least \(1/256\) for sufficiently large \(N\). This already contradicts the requested tail guarantee for \(a=1\).

These bounds hold at every fixed round, including rounds beyond the prescribed burn-in.

The cited discrete theorem uses strict rankings of individual players and swaps adjacent individual players. ([Kanade–Leonardos–Magniez](https://arxiv.org/html/1509.01988v2).) In the specified cohort model, one swap reverses preference between entire cohorts, allowing one parcel’s changed preference to interact with a positive mass of dissatisfied counterparts.

The specified guarantee is therefore impossible. This settles feasibility without any computational-complexity assumption.

  • Admissibility and scope of the construction

Taking sufficiently large powers of two ensures that \(3N/4\) and \(N/4\) are integers and that every type mass is a binary rational. Both cohorts are nonempty, and \(m=2\) satisfies the task’s restrictions.

There are two possible rankings per own cohort, hence at most four types per population and combined support size \(\tau\leq 8\).

Granting complete knowledge of the rankings before the event is a valid strengthening of the policy’s information. The proof retains the crucial restriction that the allocation precedes the independent hidden event.

  • The two-cohort blocking identity

The definitions of \(R\), \(D\), and \(S\) are consistent with the parcel coupling’s marginal constraints: each parcel allocates total mass \(1/N\), so favorite and less-preferred allocation masses sum to the corresponding type mass.

For an \(A\)-type with own cohort \(a\) and favorite cohort \(b\), its contribution \(E_{u\to v}\) is \(D^A_{ab}\) precisely when the target’s cohort is \(b\). For the corresponding \(B\)-type to have nonzero \(F_{v\to u}\), its favorite must be \(a\).

Therefore the identity \(\mathcal B^-=\sum_{a,b=1}^{2}D^A_{ab}D^B_{ba}\) is correct. It also handles absent types through zero masses. No restriction to unsplit allocations is used.

  • Effect of the hidden event

With two cohorts, the only adjacent swap reverses the selected parcel’s ranking. For an \(A\)-parcel of type \((a,b)\), the change removes \(\ell\) from \(D^A_{ab}\) and adds \(g\) to \(D^A_{a\bar b}\).

The \(B\)-side dissatisfaction masses remain unchanged because neither their rankings nor their partners’ cohort labels change. Thus the displayed individual-event formula is correct.

Summing the removed contributions over \(A\)-parcels gives \(\mathcal B^-\); summing over \(B\)-parcels gives another \(\mathcal B^-\). Since each parcel is selected with probability \(1/(2N)\), the subtraction coefficient is exactly \(1/N\).

The addition terms have the stated indices in \(H\). Consequently, the conditional expectation formula is exact.

The bound \(B_t\geq\mathcal B^- -1/N\) also follows: the removed parcel mass is at most \(1/N\), the multiplying dissatisfaction mass is at most one, and the added contribution is nonnegative.

  • Allocation-independent lower bound on \(G\)

The opposite population’s cohort \(2\) has total mass \(1/4\), so \(S^A_{12}\leq1/4\) and \(S^B_{12}\leq1/4\). The defining thresholds of \(G\) therefore imply \(D^A_{12},D^B_{12}\geq1/16=\delta\).

In Case 1, \(H\geq\delta^2/2\), and its contribution to the conditional expectation is at least \(\delta^2/(4N)\).

In Case 2, both dissatisfaction masses \(D^A_{22},D^B_{22}\) exceed \(\delta/2\), so \(\mathcal B^-\geq\delta^2/4\). For \(N\geq2\), the comparison \((1-1/N)\delta^2/4\geq\delta^2/(4N)\) is valid.

Since \(\delta^2=1/256\), the resulting conditional lower bound \(1/(1024N)\) is correct. The cases exhaust all allocations.

  • Stationarity of the cohort counts

The initial counts are independent binomial variables because rankings are drawn independently within the four fixed cohorts.

Conditional on selecting a cohort of size \(K\), uniform parcel selection gives exactly the stated count transition probabilities. The detailed-balance identity is correct, including the boundary transitions.

Each coordinate update preserves its binomial distribution and leaves the other coordinates unchanged. The coordinate-selection probabilities depend only on fixed cohort sizes. Thus the mixture preserves the initial product distribution.

Canonical labelling does not invalidate this argument: uniform selection among persistent parcel labels still gives transition probabilities determined solely by the current count. Comparisons and allocations do not change the count process.

The stationarity asserted here is unconditional. The proof does not incorrectly assume that the same binomial law holds conditional on the policy’s observations.

  • Concentration and the expectation bound

The means are \(3N/8\) for the first two counts and \(N/8\) for the last two. Their thresholds are respectively \(5/6\) and \(1/2\) of those means.
