The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite catalogue of valuation types with masses summing to one, piecewise-constant valuations over finitely many divisible items in k regions, and capacities normalized per unit population, construct a mass allocation assigning every mass element to exactly one region and satisfying EF with bounded charity, with envy checked supportwise against every occupied type-and-bundle configuration.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) valuation types with positive binary-rational masses \(p_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=k\geq1\) divisible cakes, one per region. Partition each cake into common constant-density cells; let \(B\) be the total number of cells and \(r(s)\) the region of cell \(s\). Cell capacities \(c_s>0\), measured per unit population, and densities \(a_{ts}\geq0\) are binary rationals, with \(\sum_s a_{ts}c_s=1\) for every type. Region totals need not be equal. Write \(v_t(x)=\sum_s a_{ts}x_s\).
A mass allocation is a finite list of configurations \((t_h,r_h,x^h,z_h)\), with rational masses \(z_h>0\) and rational bundles \(x^h\geq0\), where \(x_s^h=0\) unless \(r(s)=r_h\). Require \(\sum_{h:t_h=t}z_h=p_t\) and \(\sum_h z_hx_s^h\leq c_s\). Thus every mass element receives exactly one region assignment; a type may split across configurations. Bundles are consumption per mass element, and resource use is mass times bundle.
Let \(q_s=c_s-\sum_hz_hx_s^h\), and let \(q^{(r)}\) retain only the residual cells in region \(r\). EF with bounded charity means \(v_{t_h}(x^h)\geq v_{t_h}(x^\ell)\) for every occupied pair \(h,\ell\), and \(v_{t_h}(x^h)\geq v_{t_h}(q^{(r)})\) for every occupied configuration \(h\) and every region \(r\). Unallocated resources are permitted; ties satisfy these weak inequalities.

Is constructing such an allocation, or determining that none exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,B,L\), or is it NP-hard, where \(L\) is the total binary input length?

For finitely many agents with piecewise-constant valuations, EF with bounded charity can always be constructed in polynomial time; requiring complete allocation instead makes EF existence NP-complete. [Zhou et al. (2024)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/28861/29638)
No result settling this precise mass-allocation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the divisible-item domain to one cake per region, so m=k; common constant-density cells encode piecewise-constant valuations.
  • Specified positive rational cell capacities, nonnegative rational densities, and each type's total resource valuation normalized to one; imposed no equal-region restriction.
  • Interpreted capacities as resources per unit population and bundles as consumption per mass element, with feasibility determined by mass-weighted consumption.
  • Made the computational allocation a finite rational list of occupied configurations, allowing each type to split across regions and bundles.
  • Restored partial resource allocation and defined bounded charity as comparison with the entire residual bundle of each region separately, without mass-weighting that comparison.
  • Used weak, supportwise envy inequalities and included infeasibility determination without assuming that the discrete existence guarantee extends to masses.

Result

The problem is solvable in polynomial time. Every valid instance admits an EF allocation with bounded charity. An allocation can be constructed with at most \(\tau m\) occupied configurations and polynomial binary encoding length.

The construction uses a linear Fisher market. The crucial step is to turn each type’s purchases into population groups receiving bundles from individual regions.

1. The auxiliary Fisher market

Let \(S_+=\{s:\text{there exists }t\text{ with }a_{ts}>0\}\). Cells outside \(S_+\) are worthless to every type and will remain unallocated.

For each \(s\in S_+\), create a divisible good of supply \(1\), representing the entire cell. Create one buyer for each type \(t\), with budget \(p_t\) and linear utility coefficients

\(\displaystyle d_{ts}=a_{ts}c_s.\)

Thus purchasing an amount \(w_{ts}\) of this normalized good corresponds to consuming \(c_sw_{ts}\) units of cell \(s\).

We use the following established algorithmic result: a linear Fisher market with positive rational budgets and nonnegative rational utility coefficients admits an equilibrium computable exactly in polynomial time, provided every buyer values some good and every good is valued by some buyer. Rational equilibrium prices and allocations of polynomial encoding length can be obtained. See Devanur, Papadimitriou, Saberi, and Vazirani, [*Market Equilibrium via a Primal–Dual Algorithm for a Convex Program*](https://www.nikhildevanur.com/pubs/DPSV-JACM.pdf), particularly Section 2, Lemma 7.1, and Theorem 8.12.

All hypotheses hold here:

  • Each budget \(p_t\) is positive.
  • Each retained good is valued by at least one buyer, by the definition of \(S_+\).
  • Each buyer values some retained good, since \(\sum_s d_{ts}=\sum_s a_{ts}c_s=1\).
  • All market data are rational.

Compute rational equilibrium prices \(P_s\) and allocations \(w_{ts}\).

2. Properties of the equilibrium

Here \(P_s\) is the price of one unit of the normalized good, hence the price of the entire original cell.

Every \(P_s\) is positive. Indeed, if a retained good had price zero, a buyer valuing it could obtain arbitrarily large utility at no cost, contradicting the existence of a utility-maximizing bundle under that buyer’s budget.

Market clearing therefore gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_t w_{ts}=1\qquad(s\in S_+).\)

Define

\(\displaystyle \beta_t=\max_{s\in S_+}\frac{d_{ts}}{P_s}>0.\)

Each buyer spends its entire budget: otherwise it could purchase more of a positively valued good and increase its utility. Thus

\(\displaystyle \sum_{s\in S_+}P_sw_{ts}=p_t.\)

Furthermore,

\(\displaystyle w_{ts}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d_{ts}}{P_s}=\beta_t.\)

To justify this implication, suppose a buyer purchased a positive amount of a good with a strictly smaller utility-to-price ratio. Moving a positive amount of expenditure from that good to a maximizing good would preserve expenditure and strictly increase utility, contradicting optimality.

Define the price per unit of original cell capacity by

\(\displaystyle \lambda_s=\frac{P_s}{c_s}\qquad(s\in S_+).\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle a_{ts}\leq\beta_t\lambda_s,\)

with equality whenever \(w_{ts}>0\).

3. Constructing the mass allocation

For each type \(t\) and region \(r\), define

\(\displaystyle z_{tr}=\sum_{\substack{s\in S_+\\r(s)=r}}P_sw_{ts}.\)

Whenever \(z_{tr}>0\), create one configuration of type \(t\), assigned to region \(r\), with mass \(z_{tr}\) and bundle

\(\displaystyle x_s^{tr}= \begin{cases} c_sw_{ts}/z_{tr},&s\in S_+\text{ and }r(s)=r,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\)

There are at most \(\tau m\) configurations. All masses and bundles are rational, and each bundle uses only its assigned region.

Every occupied bundle has price exactly \(1\):

\(\displaystyle \sum_{s\in S_+}\lambda_sx_s^{tr} =\frac{1}{z_{tr}}\sum_{\substack{s\in S_+\\r(s)=r}}P_sw_{ts} =1.\)

4. Verification of feasibility and fairness

Population masses

For every type \(t\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{r:z_{tr}>0}z_{tr} =\sum_{s\in S_+}P_sw_{ts} =p_t.\)

Thus every type’s entire mass receives a region assignment.

Resource capacities

If \(z_{tr}=0\), then \(w_{ts}=0\) for every retained cell \(s\) in region \(r\): the summands defining \(z_{tr}\) are nonnegative and every \(P_s\) is positive.

Hence, for each \(s\in S_+\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\substack{t,r\\z_{tr}>0}}z_{tr}x_s^{tr} =c_s\sum_t w_{ts} =c_s.\)

Every retained cell is fully allocated. Cells outside \(S_+\) receive no allocation, so all capacity constraints hold.

Utility of an occupied configuration

For an occupied configuration \((t,r)\), every cell with \(x_s^{tr}>0\) satisfies \(w_{ts}>0\), and therefore \(a_{ts}=\beta_t\lambda_s\). It follows that

\(\displaystyle v_t(x^{tr}) =\sum_s a_{ts}x_s^{tr} =\beta_t\sum_{s\in S_+}\lambda_sx_s^{tr} =\beta_t.\)

In particular, all occupied configurations of the same type give that type exactly the same utility.

Envy-freeness

Consider any occupied configuration \((u,r')\), evaluated by any type \(t\). The inequality \(a_{ts}\leq\beta_t\lambda_s\) gives

\(\displaystyle v_t(x^{ur'}) \leq\beta_t\sum_{s\in S_+}\lambda_sx_s^{ur'} =\beta_t.\)

Every occupied configuration \((t,r)\) has own utility \(\beta_t\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle v_t(x^{tr})\geq v_t(x^{ur'}).\)

This proves every required occupied-pair envy inequality.

Bounded charity

The residual vector satisfies

\(\displaystyle q_s= \begin{cases} 0,&s\in S_+,\\ c_s,&s\notin S_+. \end{cases}\)

For \(s\notin S_+\), every \(a_{ts}=0\). Consequently, for every type \(t\) and region \(r'\),

\(\displaystyle v_t(q^{(r')})=0.\)

Since occupied configurations have utility \(\beta_t>0\), every bounded-charity inequality holds.

5. Exact polynomial complexity

The auxiliary market has \(\tau\) buyers and at most \(B\) goods. Computing the products \(d_{ts}=a_{ts}c_s\) produces a market of encoding length polynomial in \(m,\tau,B,L\).

The exact Fisher-market algorithm returns rational \(P_s\) and \(w_{ts}\) of polynomial encoding length in polynomial time. Constructing \(z_{tr}\) and \(x_s^{tr}\) requires only polynomially many rational additions, multiplications, and divisions. Each division uses a strictly positive denominator. These operations preserve polynomial encoding length.

Thus the resulting list of at most \(\tau m\) configurations is an exact, polynomial-size output computable in polynomial time. No expansion of the rational population masses into individual agents is needed.

6. Why population splitting matters

The construction uses precisely the stipulated interpretation: bundles are consumption per mass element, and resource use is mass times bundle. A coordinate \(x_s^{tr}\) may exceed \(c_s\); feasibility is ensured by the proved aggregate capacity equations.

Splitting a type’s population across regions permits every resulting group to receive a bundle of price \(1\) and the same utility. A finite indivisible agent cannot generally undergo this splitting, so the construction does not transfer to the finite-agent complete-allocation problem.
