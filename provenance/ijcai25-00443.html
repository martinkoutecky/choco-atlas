The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), weights \(w_t>0\), a finite set \(G\) of item classes, rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\), and additive values \(u_t(g)\), decide whether there exists a finite-support family \(\lambda_{t,z}\ge0\), \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{G}\), such that \(\sum_z\lambda_{t,z}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,z}\lambda_{t,z}z_g\le\rho_g\) for every \(g\), and \(\sum_g z_g u_t(g)\ge (w_t/W)V_t\) whenever \(\lambda_{t,z}>0\), where \(W=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\) and \(V_t=\sum_g\rho_g u_t(g)\); if the answer is YES, construct \(\lambda\).

Result

For every fixed \(C\geq1\), the feasibility problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds already with one supported type, weight \(1\), supplies \(\rho_g=1/2\), and strictly positive dyadic utilities.

Consequently, a polynomial-time feasibility-and-construction algorithm would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. With one type, every assigned bundle must attain equality

Suppose \(\tau=1\). Its mass is \(1\), and \(W=w_1\), so its threshold is

\(\displaystyle V=\sum_g\rho_gu(g).\)

Write \(\lambda_z\) for the allocation masses. Any feasible family satisfies

\(\displaystyle V\leq\sum_z\lambda_z\sum_gu(g)z_g=\sum_gu(g)\sum_z\lambda_zz_g\leq\sum_gu(g)\rho_g=V.\)

The first inequality uses the bundle thresholds and \(\sum_z\lambda_z=1\); the second uses supply feasibility and nonnegative utilities.

Thus equality holds throughout. Since the family has finite support, every bundle with positive allocation mass satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_gu(g)z_g=V.\)

This observation prevents randomization from compensating for bundles whose utility strictly exceeds the threshold.

2. An integer encoding of \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\)

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\): given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), determine whether some subset sums to half their total. Its classical hardness is established in [Karp’s paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We may assume the total \(A=\sum_i a_i\) is even: doubling every input number preserves the answer and ensures this condition.

Define positive integers

\(\displaystyle B=n+1,\qquad K=nA+1,\qquad D=\sum_{i=1}^nB^{i-1},\)

\(\displaystyle H=KD+A,\qquad M=(n+1)H.\)

For each index \(i\), introduce two coefficients

\(\displaystyle c_{i,0}=M+KB^{i-1},\qquad c_{i,1}=M+KB^{i-1}+a_i.\)

Set

\(\displaystyle R=nM+KD+\frac A2.\)

We prove that the nonnegative integer solutions of

\[\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}c_{i,b}z_{i,b}=R\tag{1}\]

are exactly those satisfying

\[\displaystyle z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}=1\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad \sum_i a_i z_{i,1}=\frac A2.\tag{2}\]

In particular, repeated units cannot create additional solutions.

Proof. Let \(z\) satisfy (1), and write

\(\displaystyle q=\sum_{i,b}z_{i,b},\qquad s_i=z_{i,0}+z_{i,1},\qquad S=\sum_i a_i z_{i,1}.\)

Every coefficient is at least \(M\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle R=nM+KD+\frac A2\leq nM+H<(n+1)M.\)

Therefore \(q\leq n\).

Each coefficient has the form \(M+\ell_{i,b}\), where \(0\leq\ell_{i,b}\leq H\). Hence

\(\displaystyle E:=\sum_{i,b}\ell_{i,b}z_{i,b}\leq qH\leq nH<M.\)

Equation (1) becomes

\(\displaystyle qM+E=nM+\left(KD+\frac A2\right).\)

Both \(E\) and \(KD+A/2\) lie in the interval from \(0\), inclusive, to \(M\), exclusive. Uniqueness of quotient and remainder upon division by \(M\) therefore gives

\[\displaystyle q=n,\qquad K\sum_i s_iB^{i-1}+S=KD+\frac A2.\tag{3}\]

Because \(a_i\leq A\) and \(\sum_i z_{i,1}\leq n\),

\(\displaystyle 0\leq S\leq nA<K,\qquad 0\leq A/2<K.\)

Reducing (3) modulo \(K\) consequently gives \(S=A/2\). Subtracting this equality from (3) and dividing by \(K\) yields

\(\displaystyle \sum_i s_iB^{i-1}=\sum_iB^{i-1}.\)

Each \(s_i\) satisfies \(0\leq s_i\leq n=B-1\). Both sides are therefore base-\(B\) representations with valid digits. Uniqueness of this representation implies \(s_i=1\) for every \(i\). This proves (2).

Conversely, substituting (2) into the left side of (1) gives

\(\displaystyle nM+K\sum_iB^{i-1}+\frac A2=R.\)

Thus (2) implies (1), completing the proof.

3. Constructing the society

Create one supported type with mass and weight

\(\displaystyle \mu_1=w_1=1.\)

There are \(m=2n\) item classes, indexed by \((i,b)\), with

\(\displaystyle \rho_{i,b}=\frac12.\)

Choose a power of two \(Q=2^k\) strictly larger than every \(c_{i,b}\), and define

\(\displaystyle u_1(i,b)=\frac{c_{i,b}}Q.\)

All utilities are strictly positive dyadic rationals below \(1\). The weight ratio is \(1\), satisfying every fixed bound \(C\geq1\).

The threshold is exactly

\(\displaystyle V_1=\frac1{2Q}\sum_{i,b}c_{i,b}=\frac1Q\left(nM+KD+\frac A2\right)=\frac RQ.\)

Polynomial encoding size. Since \(D\leq n(n+1)^{n-1}\), its bit length is \(O(n\log(n+1))\). The integers \(K,H,M,c_{i,b},R\), and the exponent \(k\), have magnitudes whose binary lengths are bounded by

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(n\log(n+1)+\log A\right).\)

Thus the \(2n\) dyadic utilities and all other society data can be produced in polynomial time and have polynomial total bit length.

Feasibility implies a partition

Suppose the society is feasible. By Section 1, every bundle in its allocation support satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i,b}u_1(i,b)z_{i,b}=\frac RQ.\)

Multiplication by \(Q\) gives equation (1). Section 2 then implies that \(z_{i,1}\in\{0,1\}\) and

\(\displaystyle \sum_i a_i z_{i,1}=\frac A2.\)

At least one bundle has positive mass, because the allocation masses sum to \(1\). Selecting such a bundle therefore produces a partition.

A partition gives an explicit feasible family

Conversely, suppose \(x\in\{0,1\}^n\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_i a_i x_i=\frac A2.\)

Define two bundles by

\(\displaystyle z_{i,1}=x_i,\qquad z_{i,0}=1-x_i,\)

\(\displaystyle \bar z_{i,1}=1-x_i,\qquad \bar z_{i,0}=x_i.\)

The complementary choices also sum to \(A/2\). By Section 2, both bundles have utility exactly \(R/Q\).

Assign mass \(1/2\) to each bundle:

\(\displaystyle \lambda_{1,z}=\lambda_{1,\bar z}=\frac12.\)

For every class,

\(\displaystyle \frac12z_{i,b}+\frac12\bar z_{i,b}=\frac12=\rho_{i,b}.\)

Thus both thresholds and every supply constraint hold, giving an explicit feasible family with two bundles.

This proves a polynomial-time many-one reduction and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. General feasible instances have polynomial-size certificates

We now prove membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), including a bound on the size of a witnessing family.

For a general type, define its rational threshold

\(\displaystyle h_t=\frac{w_t}{W}V_t.\)

These thresholds have polynomial bit length: they are obtained by rational additions, multiplications, and divisions from the explicitly listed input numbers, and \(W>0\).

Suppose a feasible finite-support family exists. For each of its bundles, repeatedly delete a unit whenever the remaining bundle still meets the threshold. This terminates because the number of units decreases. Replacing each original bundle by its resulting bundle preserves type masses and can only decrease resource use.

When \(h_t=0\), the resulting bundle is zero. When \(h_t>0\), every positive coordinate of a resulting bundle has \(u_t(g)>0\) and satisfies

\(\displaystyle u_t(z)-u_t(g)<h_t.\)

Because \(u_t(z)\geq z_gu_t(g)\), this implies

\(\displaystyle (z_g-1)u_t(g)<h_t,\qquad z_g\leq\left\lceil\frac{h_t}{u_t(g)}\right\rceil.\)

Coordinates with zero utility are zero. Every coordinate therefore has polynomial bit length.

It remains to bound the number and bit lengths of allocation masses. Fix the finite set of type-bundle pairs produced by this trimming. Introduce one variable for each such pair and one nonnegative slack variable for each supply constraint. The constraints become

\(\displaystyle Ax=b,\qquad x\geq0,\)

where there are \(d=\tau+m\) rows, \(b=(\mu,\rho)\), and \(A\) has integer entries of polynomial bit length. A bundle column has its type indicator in the first \(\tau\) rows and its bundle coordinates in the remaining rows; a slack column has a single resource entry equal to \(1\).

Choose a feasible vector with the fewest positive coordinates. Its corresponding columns must be linearly independent. Indeed, if they were dependent, there would be a nonzero vector \(v\) supported on those coordinates with \(Av=0\). Reverse its sign if necessary so some \(v_j>0\), and set

\(\displaystyle \epsilon=\min_{j:v_j>0}\frac{x_j}{v_j}.\)

Then \(x-\epsilon v\) remains nonnegative and feasible, and has fewer positive coordinates, a contradiction.

Thus at most \(d\) variables are positive. If there are \(r\) such variables, their independent columns admit an invertible \(r\)-by-\(r\) row submatrix. Cramer’s rule shows that their values are rational with polynomial bit length. Explicitly, clearing the denominators of the corresponding entries of \(b\) produces integers of polynomial bit length; determinants of these matrices have polynomial bit length because a determinant with entries bounded in absolute value by \(U\) has absolute value at most \(r!U^r\).

Discarding slack variables gives a witnessing allocation family containing at most \(\tau+m\) type-bundle pairs, with polynomial-bit integer bundles and rational masses.

Such a certificate can be checked in polynomial time using exact rational arithmetic. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

5. Consequence for the requested algorithm

The decision problem is therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for every fixed \(C\geq1\). The reduction also handles construction explicitly: a partition produces a two-bundle feasible family, and any feasible family for the constructed society contains a bundle encoding a partition.

Accordingly, the requested polynomial-time decision-and-construction algorithm cannot exist unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
