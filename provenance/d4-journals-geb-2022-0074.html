The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many student types, each with a strict program ranking, score vector, and eligibility, and programs with rational per-capita capacities, compute a stable mass allocation \(x_{t,c}\) that is student-optimal among stable allocations. In a type-level IDAM, each type proposes to its best currently available program and a program may split mass at a score cutoff: does the process terminate and return such an allocation, and what is its complexity in the number of programs, support size, and input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
There are \(m\ge1\) programs with positive rational per-capita capacities \(q_c\) and rational minimum scores \(b_c\). Type \(t\) has rational scores \(z_{tc}\) and a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) over programs and the unmatched option \(\varnothing\); eligibility means \(z_{tc}\ge b_c\). Scores at each program are distinct across different support types; mass within a type is divisible and indistinguishable.
An allocation satisfies \(x_{td}\ge0\), \(\sum_{d\in C\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{td}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{tc}\le q_c\), with \(x_{tc}=0\) unless \(t\) is eligible and \(c\succ_t\varnothing\).
It is stable if there are no eligible \((t,c)\) and \(d\) with \(x_{td}>0\) and \(c\succ_t d\) such that either \(\sum_u x_{uc}<q_c\), or \(x_{uc}>0\) for some \(u\) with \(z_{tc}>z_{uc}\).
Student-optimal means that, for every stable allocation \(y\), every type \(t\), and every ranking prefix \(B\), \(\sum_{d\in B}x_{td}\ge\sum_{d\in B}y_{td}\).
Define synchronous mass IDAM using cumulative offered amounts \(a_{tc}\), initially zero. Programs hold \(h_{tc}=\min\{a_{tc},\max\{0,q_c-\sum_{u:z_{uc}>z_{tc}}a_{uc}\}\}\), splitting the cutoff type when necessary.
In each round, each type's active unheld mass proposes to its best acceptable eligible program satisfying \(a_{tc}<\max\{0,q_c-\sum_{u:z_{uc}>z_{tc}}a_{uc}\}\); add these proposals to \(a\) simultaneously and recompute \(h\). Held mass stays committed until rejected; active mass with no such program becomes permanently unmatched. Stop when no proposal is made.

Is exact computation of a student-optimal stable mass allocation polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?
Does the specified mass IDAM always terminate at such an allocation, and what are its worst-case round count and bit complexity in \(m,\tau,L\)?

For finite students with score priorities, unbounded IDAM under straightforward choices terminates at the student-optimal stable matching; standard deferred acceptance computes that matching in polynomial time ([Bó–Hakimov](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2022.07.001)).
For real-valued stable allocation, strongly polynomial algorithms and side-optimal stable allocations are known; a related greedy procedure can require arbitrarily many steps even with two agents on each side ([Baïou–Balinski, corrected version](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total student mass to one so capacities are measured per capita; capacities are positive binary rationals.
  • Included the unmatched option in each strict ranking and excluded assignments to programs ranked below it.
  • Specified rational scores and minimum scores, with eligibility determined by the weak minimum-score inequality used in the centralized admissions definition.
  • Carried over strict score priorities across different support types; allowed splitting only within an indistinguishable type, without adding a tie-breaking rule.
  • Defined mass stability through positive mass desiring a move and either vacant capacity or strictly lower-priority assigned mass.
  • Interpreted student optimality as dominance of every ranking-prefix mass for every support type.
  • Specified synchronous rounds, cumulative offers, commitment of held portions, and further proposals by rejected portions; availability requires positive acceptance of additional mass.
  • Used termination when no proposal is made, following the formal cumulative-offer stopping condition.

Result

Exact computation of a student-optimal stable mass allocation is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

The specified synchronous mass IDAM always terminates at that allocation for rational input. However, its round-by-round execution has exponential worst-case round count and bit complexity: \(2^{\Theta(L)}\), already with \(m=\tau=2\).

More precisely, let \(D\) be the least common multiple of the denominators of the normalized masses and capacities. Counting only rounds that make a positive proposal, IDAM takes at most \(mD\) rounds. A dyadic family below takes exactly \(D-1\) rounds.

1. Notation and basic properties of IDAM

Let \(E\) consist of the acceptable eligible pairs:

\[ E=\{(t,c):z_{tc}\ge b_c,\ c\succ_t\varnothing\}. \]

Only pairs in \(E\) can receive proposals or allocation. Write \(u>_c t\) when \(z_{uc}>z_{tc}\), and define

\[ R_{tc}(a)=\max\left\{0,q_c-\sum_{u>_c t}a_{uc}\right\}. \]

Thus \(h_{tc}=\min\{a_{tc},R_{tc}(a)\}\). Call an edge available when \(a_{tc}<R_{tc}(a)\), and closed otherwise.

Cumulative offers never decrease. Consequently, residuals never increase, and an edge that closes remains closed.

Let \(M_t\) denote the mass of type \(t\) already declared permanently unmatched. Its active mass is

\[ r_t=\mu_t-M_t-\sum_c h_{tc}. \]

We first establish that the procedure remains feasible and that

\[ 0\le a_{tc}\le\mu_t. \]

Initially these assertions hold. Suppose type \(t\) proposes its active mass \(r_t\) to an available edge \((t,c)\). Availability implies \(h_{tc}=a_{tc}\), so

\[ a_{tc}+r_t =a_{tc}+\mu_t-M_t-\sum_jh_{tj} \le\mu_t. \]

After simultaneous proposals, a holding on an edge that received no proposal cannot increase, because its offer stays fixed and its residual cannot increase. The holding on the proposed edge increases by at most \(r_t\). Therefore the new total holding of type \(t\) is at most \(\mu_t-M_t\).

Declaring active mass permanently unmatched also preserves this inequality. Program feasibility follows directly from the holding formula: scanning types in decreasing score order accepts their offers until capacity is exhausted.

These observations prove the assertions inductively.

A further consequence will be used repeatedly:

Preference progression. If \((t,c)\) is currently available, type \(t\) has never proposed to a program worse than \(c\), and has never been declared permanently unmatched.

Indeed, a proposal to a worse program would mean that \(c\) was already closed. Permanent unmatched status would likewise mean that every acceptable eligible edge was closed. Both contradict current availability.

2. A rejection invariant valid against every stable allocation

Fix any stable allocation \(y\), allowing its entries to be arbitrary real numbers. At every IDAM state, the following inequalities hold for every \((t,c)\in E\):

\[ y_{tc}\le R_{tc}(a), \tag{1} \]

\[ \sum_{j\succ_t c}y_{tj}\le\mu_t-a_{tc}. \tag{2} \]

The sum in (2) runs over programs; unacceptable or ineligible programs have zero allocation.

Initially \(a=0\). Inequality (1) follows from program feasibility, and (2) from type feasibility.

Assume both inequalities hold before a round, and write \(a'\) for its new cumulative offers.

Updating the demand inequality

Consider a type \(t\) proposing to \(c\). Every acceptable eligible program preferred to \(c\) is closed. Hence, for each such program \(j\),

\[ h_{tj}=R_{tj}(a). \]

By preference progression, \(M_t=0\), and type \(t\) holds no mass at programs worse than \(c\). Also, \(h_{tc}=a_{tc}\), because \((t,c)\) is available. Thus its proposal gives

\[ a'_{tc}=a_{tc}+r_t =\mu_t-\sum_{j\succ_t c}h_{tj}. \]

Using the old inequality (1),

\[ \sum_{j\succ_t c}y_{tj} \le\sum_{j\succ_t c}R_{tj}(a) =\sum_{j\succ_t c}h_{tj} =\mu_t-a'_{tc}. \]

Thus (2) holds for the updated offer. It remains unchanged on edges receiving no proposal. This establishes (2) for all edges simultaneously.

Updating the rejection bound

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that

\[ y_{tc}>R_{tc}(a') \]

for some \((t,c)\in E\). Put \(H=\sum_{u>_c t}a'_{uc}\). The preceding strict inequality implies

\[ H+y_{tc}>q_c. \]

Program feasibility of \(y\) gives

\[ \sum_{u>_c t}y_{uc}\le q_c-y_{tc}<H. \]

Therefore some higher-priority type \(u>_c t\) satisfies \(y_{uc}<a'_{uc}\). Its offer is positive, so \((u,c)\in E\). Applying the already established new inequality (2),

\[ \sum_{j\succ_u c}y_{uj}+y_{uc} \le\mu_u-a'_{uc}+y_{uc} <\mu_u. \]

Thus type \(u\) has positive mass at an option worse than \(c\). Meanwhile, \(y_{tc}>0\), and \(t\) has lower priority than \(u\) at \(c\). This is precisely a blocking pair, contradicting stability of \(y\).

Consequently (1) also holds after the round. Induction proves both invariants.

This argument compares IDAM with all real-valued stable allocations; it does not restrict comparison allocations to a rational grid.

3. Termination, stability, and student optimality

Finite termination

All offers, holdings, active masses, and unmatched masses remain integer multiples of \(1/D\). This follows because the updates use only addition, subtraction, minima, and maxima.

Every proposal round therefore increases \(\sum_{t,c}a_{tc}\) by at least \(1/D\). On the other hand,

\[ \sum_{t,c}a_{tc}\le\sum_t m\mu_t=m. \]

Hence the number \(N\) of proposal rounds satisfies

\[ N\le mD. \]

There is at most one additional final round that makes no proposal and declares any remaining active mass unmatched.

Stability of the terminal allocation

Set \(x_{tc}=h_{tc}\) at termination, and assign the remaining type mass to \(\varnothing\).

Suppose type \(t\) has positive mass at an option worse than an acceptable eligible program \(c\). Then \((t,c)\) is closed at termination:

  • If the worse option is a program, preference progression shows that \(c\) was already closed when that proposal was made.
  • If the worse option is \(\varnothing\), every available edge was exhausted before that mass became permanently unmatched.

For a closed edge, \(a_{tc}\ge R_{tc}(a)\). We claim that the program is filled entirely by types with priority at least that of \(t\):

\[ \sum_{u>_c t}h_{uc}+h_{tc}=q_c. \]

To see this, let \(H=\sum_{u>_c t}a_{uc}\).

If \(H\ge q_c\), higher-priority offers alone fill the program. If \(H<q_c\), all higher-priority offers are held, and

\[ h_{tc}=R_{tc}(a)=q_c-H. \]

In either case, \(c\) has neither slack capacity nor a positive holding of a lower-priority type. Hence \((t,c)\) cannot block. This proves stability.

Student optimality

Let \(y\) be any stable allocation and \(B\) any ranking prefix for type \(t\).

If \(x_t(B)=\mu_t\), then \(x_t(B)\ge y_t(B)\) follows from feasibility.

Otherwise, some positive mass of \(t\) lies outside \(B\). Every acceptable eligible program \(c\in B\) is preferred to that mass, and therefore is closed. The rejection invariant gives

\[ y_{tc}\le R_{tc}(a)=h_{tc}=x_{tc}. \]

Summing over the programs in \(B\) proves \(y_t(B)\le x_t(B)\). A prefix containing \(\varnothing\) already contains every option that can receive positive allocation, so that case was covered by \(x_t(B)=\mu_t\).

Thus IDAM satisfies exactly the stated student-optimality definition.

The student-optimal allocation is unique: mutual prefix dominance between two such allocations makes every prefix sum equal; differences of consecutive prefix sums determine every entry.

4. Polynomial-time computation of the outcome

The preceding proof establishes correctness of IDAM, but its termination bound is not polynomial in the input bit length. A different algorithm computes its outcome efficiently.

Exact reduction to stable allocation

Construct a bipartite stable-allocation instance with:

  • one row vertex for each support type, with quota \(\mu_t\);
  • one column vertex for each program, with quota \(q_c\);
  • precisely the edges \(E\), with edge upper bound \(\pi_{tc}=\mu_t\);
  • row preferences inherited from \(\succ_t\);
  • column preferences ordered by decreasing score.

All vertex quotas are positive. Preferences are strict: this is given for types, and follows from distinct support-type scores for programs. The instance has at most \(\tau m\) edges.

Unallocated row quota represents unmatched mass.

To verify the stability correspondence, define

\[ P_{tc}(x)=\sum_{j\succeq_t c}x_{tj}, \qquad Q_{tc}(x)=\sum_{u:\,z_{uc}\ge z_{tc}}x_{uc}. \]

The task’s blocking condition is equivalent to

\[ P_{tc}(x)<\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad Q_{tc}(x)<q_c. \]

The first inequality means positive mass lies below \(c\). The second means that \(c\) has slack or positive mass below \(t\) in its priority order.

The standard stable-allocation condition on an edge with \(x_{tc}<\pi_{tc}\) excludes exactly these simultaneous inequalities. If \(x_{tc}=\pi_{tc}=\mu_t\), then \(P_{tc}(x)=\mu_t\) automatically. Thus the stable allocations in the two models correspond exactly.

The algorithmic theorem and the preference comparison

For finite stable-allocation instances with strict preferences and nonnegative edge bounds, Baïou–Balinski’s corrected paper supplies a strongly polynomial arithmetic algorithm for a stable allocation, followed by a polynomial arithmetic algorithm for the row-optimal allocation. Stable allocations have identical row sums. Their row-optimality relation implies that, whenever \(x_{tc}<y_{tc}\), \(x_{tj}=0\) for every \(j\) worse than \(c\). See Theorem 2, Lemma 8, Theorem 10, and Corollary 4 in the [corrected paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

This comparison implies the required prefix dominance. Indeed, if \(y_t(B)>x_t(B)\), some \(c\in B\) has \(y_{tc}>x_{tc}\). Equality of row sums forces \(x_{tj}>y_{tj}\) for some program \(j\notin B\). Since \(j\) is worse than \(c\), this contradicts the quoted row-optimality relation. Prefixes containing \(\varnothing\) again have total mass \(\mu_t\).

Therefore the computed row-optimal allocation is student-optimal in the task’s sense, and hence equals IDAM’s unique outcome.

Exact arithmetic

The cited constructions use additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of quotas, allocations, and residual quantities. Starting with the common denominator \(D\), these operations preserve that denominator. Feasibility bounds allocation entries, while capacities and auxiliary residuals have polynomial bit length.

Moreover,

\[ \log D\le L, \]

because an least common multiple is at most the product of the input denominators. Thus scaling by \(D\) permits exact integer arithmetic with polynomial-length integers throughout. Together with the polynomial arithmetic-operation count and polynomial preprocessing, this yields polynomial bit complexity in \(m,\tau,L\).

5. An exponential family for the specified synchronous rule

Let \(k\ge2\), \(D=2^k\), \(\varepsilon=1/D\), and \(n=D/2-1\).

There are two types and two programs, \(A,B\). Specify

\[ \mu_1=(n+2)\varepsilon,\qquad \mu_2=n\varepsilon, \]

\[ q_A=(n+1)\varepsilon,\qquad q_B=n\varepsilon. \]

The masses sum to one. Both programs are acceptable and eligible for both types. Preferences are

\[ A\succ_1 B\succ_1\varnothing, \qquad B\succ_2 A\succ_2\varnothing. \]

Program priorities are reversed:

\[ 2>_A1,\qquad 1>_B2. \]

For example, use scores \(z_{2A}=z_{1B}=2\), \(z_{1A}=z_{2B}=1\), with minimum scores zero.

Round recurrence

In round one, type \(1\) offers \((n+2)\varepsilon\) to \(A\), and type \(2\) offers \(n\varepsilon\) to \(B\). Their holdings are respectively \((n+1)\varepsilon\) and \(n\varepsilon\). Type \(1\) has active mass \(\varepsilon\).

Both initial edges are closed permanently: type \(1\)’s offer exceeds \(A\)’s capacity, and type \(2\)’s offer equals \(B\)’s capacity.

For each \(j=1,\ldots,n\), the next two rounds proceed as follows.

In round \(2j\), type \(1\) offers \(\varepsilon\) to \(B\). Its cumulative offer there becomes \(j\varepsilon\). This displaces \(\varepsilon\) of type \(2\), producing holdings

\[ h_{1B}=j\varepsilon,\qquad h_{2B}=(n-j)\varepsilon, \]

\[ h_{2A}=(j-1)\varepsilon,\qquad h_{1A}=(n+2-j)\varepsilon. \]

Type \(2\) now has active mass \(\varepsilon\).

In round \(2j+1\), type \(2\) offers that mass to \(A\), producing

\[ h_{2A}=j\varepsilon,\qquad h_{1A}=(n+1-j)\varepsilon, \]

while the holdings at \(B\) stay unchanged. Type \(1\) again has active mass \(\varepsilon\).

These proposals satisfy the availability test:

  • Before round \(2j\), type \(1\)’s offer to \(B\) is \((j-1)\varepsilon<n\varepsilon=q_B\), and type \(1\) has highest priority there.
  • Before round \(2j+1\), type \(2\)’s offer to \(A\) is \((j-1)\varepsilon<(n+1)\varepsilon=q_A\), and type \(2\) has highest priority there.

This verifies the recurrence inductively.

After round \(2n+1\), type \(1\)’s offer to \(B\) equals \(q_B\). Both its edges are now closed, so its remaining active mass \(\varepsilon\) becomes unmatched. Type \(2\) has no active mass. The terminal allocation is

\[ x_{1A}=\varepsilon,\qquad x_{1B}=n\varepsilon,\qquad x_{1\varnothing}=\varepsilon, \]

\[ x_{2A}=n\varepsilon,\qquad x_{2B}=x_{2\varnothing}=0. \]

The number of proposal rounds is exactly

\[ 2n+1=D-1=2^k-1. \]

All numerical inputs are dyadic, and their total encoding length is \(\Theta(k)\). Consequently the round count is \(2^{\Omega(L)}\), even with \(m=\tau=2\).

6. Round count and bit complexity

After sorting program priorities, one round can be implemented with \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations: priority scans compute residuals and holdings, and preference scans select proposals.

Scaling masses and capacities by \(D\) makes these integer operations. All integers have \(O(L)\) bits, so elementary addition and comparison give \(O(m\tau L)\) bit operations per round.

For round-by-round simulation, therefore,

\[ T_{\mathrm{bit}} \le \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)+O(m\tau L N) \le \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)+O(m^2\tau LD). \]

Since \(D\le2^L\), this is \(2^{O(L)}\). The explicit family forces \(2^{\Omega(L)}\) rounds, and therefore at least that many operations in any execution that processes every round. Thus both worst-case round count and round-by-round bit complexity are \(2^{\Theta(L)}\).

This exponential simulation cost does not apply to computing the terminal allocation directly, which is polynomial-time solvable by Section 4.

For the elementary boundary cases, \(m=1\) permits at most one proposal round; \(\tau=1\) permits at most \(m\), because every round either completes the allocation or closes the proposed program.
