The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), an ordered finite grade set \(G\), a target \(p\in C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported complete grade vectors \(r\in G^C\), a budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), and a unique-winner or co-winner convention, is there a nonnegative mass transfer \(x_{r,s}\) from supported input types \(r\) to output vectors \(s\in G^C\), with \(\sum_s x_{r,s}\le\mu_r\) for every \(r\) and \(\sum_{r\ne s}x_{r,s}\le\beta\), such that \(p\) wins? Define candidate \(c\)'s majority grade as the least \(g\in G\) for which \(\sum_{r:r(c)\preceq g}\mu'_r\ge\tfrac12\), where \(\mu'\) is the resulting distribution.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty finite candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and let \(G\) be an explicitly listed nonempty finite grade set with a strict linear order \(\prec\), higher being better; write \(t=|G|\).
A society is given by its support \(R\subseteq G^C\): the list of the \(\tau=|R|\) complete grade vectors with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_r\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(t^m\) of possible types.
The input also specifies \(p\in C\), a binary-rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), and either the unique-winner or co-winner convention. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,t,L\).
An admissible change consists of real masses \(x_{r,s}\ge0\), indexed by \(r\in R\) and \(s\in G^C\), satisfying \(\sum_s x_{r,s}\le\mu_r\) and \(\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{s\ne r}x_{r,s}\le\beta\). Changing a complete vector costs its changed mass, regardless of how many candidate grades change; output vectors need not belong to \(R\).
Extend \(\mu\) by zero outside \(R\). The resulting distribution is \(\mu'_u=\mu_u-\mathbf{1}_{u\in R}\sum_s x_{u,s}+\sum_{r\in R}x_{r,u}\) for \(u\in G^C\).
For any distribution \(\nu\), define \(M_c(\nu)=\min_{\prec}\{g\in G:\sum_{u:u(c)\preceq g}\nu_u\ge\tfrac12\}\). Winners have the highest majority grade, with no further tie-breaking: success means \(M_p(\mu')\succ M_c(\mu')\) for every \(c\ne p\) in the unique-winner convention, and \(M_p(\mu')\succeq M_c(\mu')\) in the co-winner convention. Promise that \(p\) initially fails the selected success condition.

Is deciding whether an admissible change makes \(p\) successful polynomial-time solvable, or NP-hard, under each winner convention?

Discrete constructive bribery, charging one unit per changed complete vote, is NP-hard even with two grades, under both winner conventions ([Yang, 2017](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2017/pdfs/p1169.pdf)). No result settling this exact mass-transfer problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made normalization explicit: positive supported masses sum to one, with zero mass outside the listed support.
  • Specified real nonnegative transfer masses and a binary-rational budget; unchanged mass remains at its original type, as expressed by the resulting-distribution formula.
  • Specified higher grades as better, winners by highest majority grade, and no additional tie-breaking; co-winner success includes unique victory.
  • Restored the constructive-instance promise that the target initially fails the selected winner condition.
  • Retained the graded question's lower-half quantile at threshold one-half rather than importing the discrete text's problematic median index.
  • Made candidate and grade sets nonempty and the grade order explicit; included grade-set size and total input bit length among running-time variables.

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable under both the unique-winner and the co-winner convention.

The algorithm solves at most \(t\) rational linear programs, each with at most \(\tau\) variables and \(2\tau+m\) constraints. Its running time is
\( \operatorname{poly}(L)+t\,\operatorname{poly}(m+\tau+L) \).
In particular, it never enumerates the \(t^m\) possible output vectors.

1. Every change can use one destination vector

List the grades as \(g_1\prec\cdots\prec g_t\). Define the complete vector \(w\) by
\(w(p)=g_t\) and \(w(c)=g_1\) for every \(c\ne p\).

Lemma. Every admissible change can be replaced by an admissible change of no greater cost that sends all changed mass to \(w\). If the original change makes \(p\) successful, the replacement does too, under either convention.

Proof. Consider an admissible change \(x\), producing distribution \(\nu\). Transfers \(x_{r,r}\) cancel in the resulting distribution and incur no cost, so discard them. Write
\(q_r=\sum_{s\ne r}x_{r,s}\).
Then \(0\le q_r\le\mu_r\), and
\[ \]
\( \displaystyle \nu=\sum_{r\in R}(\mu_r-q_r)\delta_r +\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{s\ne r}x_{r,s}\delta_s, \)
where \(\delta_u\) denotes unit mass at vector \(u\).

Replace every destination by \(w\), obtaining
\( \displaystyle \eta=\sum_{r\in R}(\mu_r-q_r)\delta_r +\left(\sum_{r\in R}q_r\right)\delta_w. \)

This distribution is implemented by transferring \(q_r\) from \(r\) to \(w\) for each \(r\ne w\). If \(w\in R\), its term in the displayed expression is simply \(\mu_w\delta_w\), so no transfer from \(w\) is necessary. The replacement therefore costs
\( \sum_{r\in R,\ r\ne w}q_r\le\sum_r q_r\le\beta \).

For a candidate \(c\) and grade \(h\), define its cumulative mass by
\(F_c^\rho(h)=\sum_{u:u(c)\preceq h}\rho_u\).
Direct subtraction gives
\( \displaystyle F_c^\eta(h)-F_c^\nu(h) =\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{s\ne r}x_{r,s} \left(\mathbf 1_{w(c)\preceq h}-\mathbf 1_{s(c)\preceq h}\right). \)

For \(p\), every summand is nonpositive because \(w(p)\) is the highest grade. For a rival, every summand is nonnegative because \(w(c)\) is the lowest grade. Consequently,
\(F_p^\eta(h)\le F_p^\nu(h)\) and
\(F_c^\eta(h)\ge F_c^\nu(h)\) for every rival \(c\).

For every grade below \(M_p(\nu)\), the cumulative mass for \(p\) under \(\nu\) is strictly below \(\tfrac12\); it remains so under \(\eta\). Hence \(M_p(\eta)\succeq M_p(\nu)\).
At \(h=M_c(\nu)\), the rival’s cumulative mass under \(\eta\) is at least \(\tfrac12\), so \(M_c(\eta)\preceq M_c(\nu)\).
Both success conditions are therefore preserved. \(\square\)

2. A compact description of all necessary changes

Let \(S=R\setminus\{w\}\). By the lemma, it suffices to choose masses \(y_r\), indexed by \(r\in S\), satisfying
\( \displaystyle 0\le y_r\le\mu_r,\qquad \sum_{r\in S}y_r\le\beta. \)
Transfer \(y_r\) from \(r\) to \(w\), and denote the resulting distribution by \(\nu_y\).

Define the initial cumulative masses
\( \displaystyle A_{c,j}=\sum_{r\in R:r(c)\preceq g_j}\mu_r \quad(1\le j\le t), \)
and set \(A_{c,0}=0\).

For each \(1\le i\le t\), define
\( \displaystyle B_i(y)=\sum_{r\in S:r(p)\prec g_i}y_r. \)
In particular, \(B_1(y)=0\).

For \(i\ge2\), the destination gives \(p\) a grade strictly above \(g_{i-1}\), so
\( \displaystyle F_p^{\nu_y}(g_{i-1})=A_{p,i-1}-B_i(y). \)
By the definition of the lower median,
\( \displaystyle M_p(\nu_y)\succeq g_i \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B_i(y)>A_{p,i-1}-\tfrac12. \)
This equivalence also holds for \(i=1\): the left side is automatic, and the right side reads \(0>-\tfrac12\).

For every rival \(c\) and \(1\le k\le t\), changing a portion already graded at most \(g_k\) leaves its cumulative contribution unchanged. Changing a portion graded above \(g_k\) adds that portion to the cumulative mass. Thus
\( \displaystyle F_c^{\nu_y}(g_k) =A_{c,k}+\sum_{r\in S:r(c)\succ g_k}y_r. \)
Consequently,
\( \displaystyle M_c(\nu_y)\preceq g_k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A_{c,k}+\sum_{r\in S:r(c)\succ g_k}y_r\ge\tfrac12. \)

These equivalences retain the strict inequality required for raising \(p\)’s lower median.

3. Linear programs for the two conventions

If \(m=1\), \(p\) is already successful under either convention. Such an instance cannot satisfy the initial-failure promise. Hence assume \(m\ge2\).

For the co-winner convention, consider every \(i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}\) and put \(k=i\).

For the unique-winner convention, consider every \(i\in\{2,\ldots,t\}\) and put \(k=i-1\). The lowest grade cannot be the majority grade of a unique winner when a rival exists.

For each considered pair \((i,k)\), solve the following ordinary, closed linear program:

\( \displaystyle \text{maximize } B_i(y) \)

subject to

\( \displaystyle 0\le y_r\le\mu_r\quad(r\in S), \)

\( \displaystyle \sum_{r\in S}y_r\le\beta, \)

\( \displaystyle A_{c,k}+\sum_{r\in S:r(c)\succ g_k}y_r \ge\tfrac12 \quad(c\ne p). \)

If it is infeasible, discard this pair. Otherwise, let its optimal value be \(z_i\). Accept the instance exactly when some considered pair satisfies
\( \displaystyle z_i>A_{p,i-1}-\tfrac12. \)

The feasible region is closed and bounded by the coordinate bounds. Whenever it is nonempty, the maximum is attained. Therefore the strict comparison is equivalent to the existence of a feasible mass transfer with the required strict median inequality.

Soundness

Suppose a pair passes the test, and take an optimal feasible solution \(y\).
Section 2 gives \(M_p(\nu_y)\succeq g_i\) and \(M_c(\nu_y)\preceq g_k\) for every rival.

For co-winners, \(k=i\), so \(p\)’s majority grade is at least every rival’s.
For unique winners, \(k=i-1\), so
\(M_p(\nu_y)\succeq g_i\succ g_{i-1}\succeq M_c(\nu_y)\).
The constructed transfer is admissible and successful.

Completeness

Suppose some admissible change succeeds. By Section 1, there is a successful change using only destination \(w\), represented by a feasible vector \(y\).
Write \(M_p(\nu_y)=g_i\).

Under the co-winner convention, every rival has majority grade at most \(g_i\). Thus \(y\) satisfies the program with \(k=i\).

Under the unique-winner convention, necessarily \(i\ge2\), and every rival has majority grade at most \(g_{i-1}\). Thus \(y\) satisfies the program with \(k=i-1\).

In either case,
\(B_i(y)>A_{p,i-1}-\tfrac12\).
The optimal value is at least \(B_i(y)\), so the corresponding pair passes the test. This proves completeness.

4. Exactness and running time

The algorithm uses the standard theorem that rational linear programming admits polynomial-time feasibility testing and exact optimization in the binary encoding length. This includes feasible regions of lower dimension; no interior-point assumption is needed. See [MIT’s notes on polynomial-time linear programming](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

All its hypotheses hold here:

  • Each program has \(n=|S|\le\tau\) variables and \(2n+m\) inequalities.
  • Constraint and objective coefficients belong to \(\{0,1,-1\}\).
  • Right-hand sides are input rationals or sums of input rationals, and have \(O(L)\) bits.
  • Each feasible region is bounded.

A dense encoding of one program therefore has size
\(O((m+\tau)(\tau+L))\).
The cumulative masses can be computed using \(O(m(t+\tau))\) rational additions after identifying grade ranks. Constructing and solving at most \(t\) programs takes
\( \operatorname{poly}(L)+t\,\operatorname{poly}(m+\tau+L) \)
bit operations.

Exact rational comparison handles the strict threshold. Indeed, when feasible, each program has a rational optimal vertex of polynomial encoding length. An accepted program therefore also supplies a rational successful transfer.

5. Why the strict comparison matters

Consider two grades \(\ell\prec h\), two candidates \(p,c\), and initial mass \(1\) on the vector \((p:\ell,c:h)\).
Transferring mass \(q\) to \((p:h,c:\ell)\) makes \(p\) a co-winner exactly when \(q\ge\tfrac12\), and a unique winner exactly when \(q>\tfrac12\).

At budget \(\beta=\tfrac12\), both candidates have lower median \(\ell\). Co-winner success is possible, but unique-winner success is impossible. The algorithm distinguishes these cases because it compares the attained optimum strictly with the threshold.

Thus both conventions are settled, including their boundary behavior.
