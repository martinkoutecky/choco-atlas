The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of hub types \(H\), positive rational masses \(\mu_h\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric metric \(d\) on the depot and hubs, treat each type cohort as one player. For each \(A\subseteq H\), let \(c(A)\) be the minimum depot-tour cost visiting the hubs in \(A\). Decide whether nonnegative per-capita allocation rates \(x_h\) exist such that \(\sum_{h\in H}\mu_hx_h=c(H)\), \(\mu_hx_h\le c(\{h\})\) for every \(h\), and \(\sum_{g\ne h}\mu_gx_g\le c(H\setminus\{h\})\) for every \(h\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(H\) of \(\tau\) hub types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_h\) satisfying \(\sum_{h\in H}\mu_h=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also supplies a binary-rational symmetric metric \(d\) on \(H\cup\{0\}\), where \(0\) is a distinct depot. Let \(m=\tau+1\) be the number of locations and \(L\) the total input bit length.
Each whole type cohort is one player. For \(A\subseteq H\), let \(c(A)\) be the minimum cost of a tour starting and ending at the depot and visiting every hub in \(A\) exactly once; set \(c(\varnothing)=0\) and \(c(\{h\})=2d(0,h)\). Tour costs are determined by the metric and are not supplied separately.
A feasible allocation consists of real per-capita rates \(x_h\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{h\in H}\mu_hx_h=c(H)\), and, for every \(h\in H\), \(\mu_hx_h\leq c(\{h\})\) and \(\sum_{g\in H\setminus\{h\}}\mu_gx_g\leq c(H\setminus\{h\})\).

Is deciding whether a feasible allocation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete subadditive games, semicore non-emptiness has a polynomial-time arithmetic test when the requisite coalition costs are supplied. [Discrete characterization](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-4164447/v1).
A later published abstract states \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness of semicore non-emptiness for traveling salesman games, without specifying whether the hardness holds for symmetric metrics. [Published result](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10458-026-09757-5). No result explicitly addressing this mass formulation was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Distances were not given an encoding; require binary-rational metric entries and define L as the total input bit length.
  • Specify a distinct depot, a nonempty support, m as the total number of locations, and real nonnegative allocation rates.
  • Specify depot-return tours visiting each participating hub exactly once, with empty-coalition cost zero and singleton cost twice the depot distance.
  • Clarify that coalition costs must be determined from the input metric rather than supplied as additional input.

1. Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions, even when all masses and distances are dyadic rationals and the distance matrix is a symmetric metric. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof below constructs the metric explicitly; it does not assume that coalition costs are supplied.

2. Eliminating the masses and characterizing feasibility

Write \(n=|H|\), \(C=c(H)\), and \(C_{-h}=c(H\setminus\{h\})\). Define the total payment of cohort \(h\) by \(y_h=\mu_hx_h\). Since every mass is positive, this substitution is a bijection between nonnegative rate vectors and nonnegative payment vectors.

The allocation conditions become

\(\displaystyle \sum_{h\in H}y_h=C,\qquad C-C_{-h}\leq y_h\leq 2d(0,h).\)

For a metric, deleting a hub from a tour and shortcutting proves \(C_{-h}\leq C\). Concatenating an optimal deletion tour with the singleton tour, and shortcutting, proves

\(\displaystyle C\leq C_{-h}+2d(0,h).\)

Thus the lower bounds \(a_h=C-C_{-h}\) satisfy \(0\leq a_h\leq u_h=2d(0,h)\). Also, concatenating all singleton tours and shortcutting gives \(C\leq\sum_hu_h\).

Consequently, a feasible payment vector exists exactly when \(\sum_ha_h\leq C\). To verify sufficiency explicitly, if \(\sum_hu_h>\sum_ha_h\), set

\(\displaystyle \lambda=\frac{C-\sum_ha_h}{\sum_hu_h-\sum_ha_h},\qquad y_h=a_h+\lambda(u_h-a_h).\)

Then \(0\leq\lambda\leq1\), every payment satisfies its bounds, and the payments sum to \(C\). If the denominator is zero, both sums equal \(C\), and \(y_h=a_h\) works.

Define the feasibility margin

\(\displaystyle F(H,d)=c(H)-\sum_{h\in H}\bigl(c(H)-c(H\setminus\{h\})\bigr).\)

We have proved

\(\displaystyle \text{feasible allocation exists}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F(H,d)\geq0.\)

Equivalently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{h\in H}c(H\setminus\{h\})\geq(n-1)c(H).\)

This explains the polynomial arithmetic test when the relevant costs are supplied. The remaining question concerns computing its sign from the metric.

3. Two metric constructions

We will use two constructions whose tour costs can be proved exactly.

Joining games at their common depot. Suppose several metric games have disjoint hub sets and a common depot. Retain each component’s metric, and for hubs \(u,v\) in different components define

\(\displaystyle d(u,v)=d(u,0)+d(0,v).\)

These distances form a metric. For completeness, if \(u,v\) belong to the same component and \(w\) belongs elsewhere, its triangle inequality follows from

\(\displaystyle d(u,v)\leq d(u,0)+d(0,v)\leq d(u,w)+d(w,v).\)

If \(u,v\) belong to different components and \(w\) belongs to \(u\)’s component, it follows from \(d(u,0)\leq d(u,w)+d(w,0)\). The other cases are symmetric or immediate.

For every coalition \(A\), its cost in the joined game is

\(\displaystyle c(A)=\sum_jc_j(A\cap H_j).\)

For the upper bound, concatenate optimal component tours at the depot. Removing intermediate depot visits preserves the cost because every cross-component distance equals the corresponding journey through the depot.

For the lower bound, take any joined tour and insert the depot at each cross-component transition, again preserving cost. The resulting excursions can be concatenated separately within each component. Shortcutting repeated depot visits gives a component tour of no greater cost. Summing proves the lower bound.

Each hub’s marginal cost therefore equals its marginal cost within its own component. Hence

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{joined}}=\sum_jF_j.\)

Duplicating ordinary hubs. Start with a metric \(d_0\) whose hubs consist of ordinary hubs and two distinguished hubs \(\alpha,\beta\). Replace each ordinary hub by two distinct copies. Retain the distinguished hubs and depot.

Let \(\pi\) map each copy to its original location. Assign \(p(u)=\tfrac12\) to copies and \(p(u)=0\) to the depot and distinguished hubs. For distinct new locations define

\(\displaystyle d(u,v)=d_0(\pi(u),\pi(v))+p(u)+p(v),\)

and set \(d(u,u)=0\).

This is a metric. Distinct copies of the same hub have distance \(1\); all other distinct locations also have positive distance. For distinct \(u,v,w\), the triangle inequality follows from that of \(d_0\) and \(2p(w)\geq0\).

For every coalition \(A\), its cost is exactly

\(\displaystyle c(A)=c_0(\pi(A))+2\sum_{u\in A}p(u).\)

Indeed, projecting any tour gives a closed walk in the original metric. Shortcutting repeated original locations shows that its projected cost is at least \(c_0(\pi(A))\). Conversely, replace each hub in an optimal original tour by its represented copies consecutively. This preserves the projected cost. In either direction, the potential terms contribute exactly \(2\sum_{u\in A}p(u)\), since every visited hub has two incident tour edges.

In particular, deleting one ordinary copy preserves the represented original hub set and reduces the cost by exactly \(1\).

4. A graph with a known Hamiltonian cycle and a hard marked edge

We first construct, from a loopless directed graph \(D\), an undirected graph \(B\) with:

  • an explicitly known Hamiltonian cycle;
  • a marked edge \(e\) absent from that known cycle;
  • a Hamiltonian cycle containing \(e\) exactly when \(D\) has a directed Hamiltonian cycle.

Directed Hamiltonian cycle is a classical \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem. We may restrict to graphs with \(k\geq3\) vertices: removing loops does not affect Hamiltonian cycles on such graphs, and excluding finitely many smaller instances does not affect hardness. See [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Label the vertices of \(D\) by \(1,\ldots,k\), with indices interpreted cyclically. Construct a directed graph \(Q\) with vertices \(a_i,b_i,z_i\). Include the four internal arcs

\(\displaystyle a_i\to z_i,\quad z_i\to b_i,\quad b_i\to z_i,\quad z_i\to a_i.\)

For every arc \(i\to j\) of \(D\), include \(b_i\to a_j\). Finally, include \(a_i\to b_{i+1}\) for every \(i\).

There is an explicitly known Hamiltonian cycle

\(\displaystyle b_1,z_1,a_1,b_2,z_2,a_2,\ldots,b_k,z_k,a_k,b_1.\)

We claim that every Hamiltonian cycle of \(Q\) has one of two forms. At \(z_i\), its predecessor and successor must be \(a_i,b_i\) in some order. They cannot be the same vertex, because that would form a two-vertex cycle rather than a Hamiltonian cycle. Thus each triple is traversed consecutively as either \(a_i,z_i,b_i\) or \(b_i,z_i,a_i\).

If a triple is traversed as \(a_i,z_i,b_i\), the next triple must start at some \(a_j\), using an arc \(b_i\to a_j\). Hence that next triple has the same orientation. If a triple is traversed as \(b_i,z_i,a_i\), the next triple starts at \(b_{i+1}\), again with the same orientation. Since a Hamiltonian cycle is one cycle, all triples have the same orientation.

Therefore:

  • cycles using the orientation \(a_i,z_i,b_i\) correspond exactly to directed Hamiltonian cycles of \(D\);
  • the other orientation gives the known cycle displayed above.

In particular, a Hamiltonian cycle of \(Q\) contains \(a_1\to z_1\) exactly when \(D\) has a directed Hamiltonian cycle.

Now convert \(Q\) to an undirected graph \(B\). Replace each vertex \(q\) by the path

\(\displaystyle q^{-}-q^{0}-q^{+},\)

and replace each directed arc \(q\to r\) by the undirected edge \(\{q^{+},r^{-}\}\).

Every \(q^{0}\) has degree two, so every Hamiltonian cycle must traverse each replacement path consecutively. Orienting one path from its minus endpoint to its plus endpoint forces that orientation for the next path, because every connecting edge joins a plus endpoint to a minus endpoint. Thus Hamiltonian cycles of \(B\), oriented appropriately, correspond exactly to directed Hamiltonian cycles of \(Q\).

The known cycle of \(Q\) supplies a known Hamiltonian cycle of \(B\). Mark the edge

\(\displaystyle e=\{a_1^{+},z_1^{-}\}.\)

It is absent from the known cycle, and

\(\displaystyle B\text{ has a Hamiltonian cycle containing }e \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ has a directed Hamiltonian cycle}.\)

The graph \(B\) has \(b=9k\) vertices.

5. Encoding the marked-edge question in metric tour costs

Write \(e=\{s,t\}\). Remove \(e\) from \(B\), and replace it by the path

\(\displaystyle s-\alpha-\beta-t,\)

where \(\alpha,\beta\) are new vertices. Call the resulting graph \(G\), and put \(M=b+2\).

On the vertices of \(G\), define \(d_0(u,v)=1\) for graph edges, \(d_0(u,v)=2\) for distinct nonadjacent vertices, and \(d_0(u,u)=0\). This is a symmetric metric: every nonzero distance is between \(1\) and \(2\).

Choose any original vertex of \(B\) as depot. Let \(C\) be the tour cost through all \(M\) locations.

A tour has cost \(M\) exactly when it is a Hamiltonian cycle of \(G\). Since \(\alpha,\beta\) have degree two, such a cycle must use the entire path \(s-\alpha-\beta-t\). Contracting that path establishes

\(\displaystyle C=M \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\text{ has a Hamiltonian cycle containing }e.\)

There is always a tour of cost \(M+1\). To construct it, take the known Hamiltonian cycle of \(B\), which avoids \(e\). Choose one of its edges \(\{s,u\}\). Since it avoids \(e\), we have \(u\ne t\). Replace that edge by \(s,\alpha,\beta,u\). Its cost changes from \(1\) to \(1+1+2=4\), so the resulting tour costs

\(\displaystyle b+3=M+1.\)

Tour costs are integers. Therefore

\(\displaystyle C= \begin{cases} M,&D\text{ has a directed Hamiltonian cycle},\\ M+1,&D\text{ has no directed Hamiltonian cycle}. \end{cases}\)

Both distinguished deletion costs equal \(M\). After deleting \(\alpha\), the vertex \(\beta\) has only one distance-\(1\) neighbor, namely \(t\). Any tour through the remaining \(M-1\) locations must therefore use at least one distance-\(2\) edge, giving cost at least \(M\). Insert \(\beta\) next to \(t\) in the known Hamiltonian cycle of \(B\): a distance-\(1\) edge is replaced by edges of lengths \(1\) and \(2\), producing cost \(b+2=M\). The argument after deleting \(\beta\) is identical.

Thus

\(\displaystyle C_{-\alpha}=C_{-\beta}=M.\)

Now duplicate every original hub of \(B\), retaining the depot and \(\alpha,\beta\), as in Section 3. There are \(r=b-1\) original ordinary hubs and \(2r\) copies.

The full cost of this component is \(C+2r\). Each ordinary copy has marginal cost \(1\), while each distinguished hub has marginal cost \(C-M\). Its feasibility margin is therefore

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{source}} =C+2r-\bigl(2r+2(C-M)\bigr) =2M-C.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{source}}= \begin{cases} M,&D\text{ has a directed Hamiltonian cycle},\\ M-1,&D\text{ has no directed Hamiltonian cycle}. \end{cases}\)

6. A fixed metric component with negative margin

We need a metric component whose negative margin is known exactly.

Use the Petersen graph with vertices \(o_i,p_i\), indexed modulo \(5\), and edges

\(\displaystyle \{o_i,o_{i+1}\},\qquad \{p_i,p_{i+2}\},\qquad \{o_i,p_i\}.\)

Again give graph edges distance \(1\) and distinct nonedges distance \(2\). Choose \(o_0\) as depot.

Here are direct proofs of the required tour costs.

First, the Petersen graph has no Hamiltonian cycle. Such a cycle must cross between the outer and inner vertex sets a positive even number of times, hence use either two or four spokes.

With two spokes, the cycle induces spanning paths in both five-cycles. Their endpoints must be adjacent in the outer cycle, whose index differences are \(\pm1\), and adjacent in the inner cycle, whose index differences are \(\pm2\). This is impossible.

With four spokes, rotate indices so the unused spoke has index \(0\). Degree requirements force the outer edges

\(\displaystyle \{o_4,o_0\},\ \{o_0,o_1\},\ \{o_2,o_3\},\)

and inner edges

\(\displaystyle \{p_3,p_0\},\ \{p_0,p_2\},\ \{p_1,p_4\}.\)

Together with the four spokes, these form two separate five-cycles, rather than one Hamiltonian cycle.

Second, deleting \(o_0\) leaves the Hamiltonian cycle

\(\displaystyle o_1,o_2,o_3,o_4,p_4,p_2,p_0,p_3,p_1,o_1.\)

Rotations of indices are automorphisms. So is the map \(o_i\mapsto p_{2i}\), \(p_i\mapsto o_{2i}\): it exchanges outer edges with inner edges and preserves spokes. These automorphisms act transitively on vertices. Therefore deleting any vertex leaves a Hamiltonian cycle.

It follows that every one-hub deletion cost is \(9\). The full tour cost is at least \(11\), since a cost-\(10\) tour would be a Hamiltonian cycle. The tour

\(\displaystyle o_1,o_0,o_2,o_3,o_4,p_4,p_2,p_0,p_3,p_1,o_1\)

has cost \(11\): only \(\{o_0,o_2\}\) has distance \(2\).

This nine-hub component therefore has margin

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{Petersen}}=11-9(11-9)=-7.\)

Scaling all its distances by the integer \(M\) gives margin \(-7M\).

7. Calibration and completion of the reduction

Add one further component with two hubs \(u,v\), with distances

\(\displaystyle d(0,u)=d(0,v)=R=3M+\frac34,\qquad d(u,v)=1.\)

These distances satisfy the triangle inequality. Its full cost is \(2R+1\), each deletion cost is \(2R\), and hence its margin is

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{positive}}=(2R+1)-1-1 =2R-1 =6M+\frac12.\)

Join the source component, the scaled Petersen component, and this two-hub component at their depot using Section 3. Margin additivity gives

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{total}} =F_{\mathrm{source}}-7M+6M+\frac12.\)

Thus

\(\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{total}}= \begin{cases} \frac12,&D\text{ has a directed Hamiltonian cycle},\\ -\frac12,&D\text{ has no directed Hamiltonian cycle}. \end{cases}\)

By the feasibility characterization,

\(\displaystyle \text{the constructed society admits a feasible allocation} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ has a directed Hamiltonian cycle}.\)

This is the required many-one reduction.

8. Encoding and conclusion

The source component has \(2b\) hubs. The other components have nine and two hubs, respectively. Therefore

\(\displaystyle \tau=2b+11=18k+11,\qquad m=18k+12.\)

Every distance is dyadic with denominator dividing \(4\), and every distance is bounded by a constant times \(M\). The complete distance matrix therefore has encoding length \(O(k^2\log k)\).

To assign strictly positive dyadic masses, let \(\ell=\lceil\log_2\tau\rceil\). Give the first \(\tau-1\) hubs mass \(2^{-\ell}\), and the last hub mass

\(\displaystyle 1-(\tau-1)2^{-\ell}.\)

Every mass is positive, their sum is \(1\), and their total encoding length is \(O(\tau\log\tau)\). Their values do not affect feasibility, by Section 2.

All constructions are computable in polynomial time. Thus deciding feasible allocation existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for the stated symmetric metric mass formulation. The arithmetic characterization remains valid, but does not yield a polynomial-time algorithm when its tour costs must be derived from the metric.
