The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of complete agent types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), resource kinds \(R\) with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_r\), additive values \(v_{t,r}\), and an initial feasible mass allocation \(y^0\), compute a feasible \(y^\star\) maximizing \(W(y)=\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}\sum_{r\in S}v_{t,r}\), where \(y_{t,S}\ge0\), \(\sum_S y_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:r\in S}y_{t,S}=\rho_r\), together with a finite sequence of strictly welfare-increasing mass \(1\)-deals from \(y^0\) to a maximizing allocation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is nonatomic and given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|T|\) complete types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=|R|\) resource kinds, binary-rational supplies \(\rho_r\in[0,1]\), and signed rational additive values \(v_{t,r}\). Each individual holds a bundle \(S\subseteq R\), hence at most one unit of each kind; empty bundles are allowed.
A feasible allocation satisfies \(y_{t,S}\geq0\), \(\sum_{S\subseteq R}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:r\in S}y_{t,S}=\rho_r\). Its welfare is \(W(y)=\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}\sum_{r\in S}v_{t,r}\).
The input includes a feasible rational allocation \(y^0\), listed by its positive entries; let \(k\) be their number and \(L\) the total input bit length.
A mass \(1\)-deal selects \(t,u\in T\), bundles \(S,B\subseteq R\), a kind \(r\in S\setminus B\), and rational \(0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{y_{t,S},y_{u,B}\}\). It subtracts \(\varepsilon\) from each selected entry and adds \(\varepsilon\) to each of \(y_{t,S\setminus\{r\}}\) and \(y_{u,B\cup\{r\}}\), combining coincident entries.
The two participating cohorts are disjoint, even if \(t=u\); every other individual's bundle is unchanged. A deal is admissible precisely when \(W\) strictly increases. Unrestricted balanced monetary side payments are available, with no budget constraints.
The computational problem is to compute a rational welfare-maximizing allocation \(y^\star\), represented by its positive entries, together with a finite sequence of admissible mass \(1\)-deals from \(y^0\) to \(y^\star\); the sequence may be empty.

Is this allocation-and-negotiation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents with additive utilities and unrestricted side payments, every maximal sequence of individually rational \(1\)-deals terminates at a utilitarian optimum ([Endriss et al.](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~fs/Papers/Multi-agent%20systems/EndrissEtAlJAIR2006.pdf)).
No result settling exactly the continuous problem specified here was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so per-capita supplies lie in [0,1]; individuals hold at most one unit of each resource kind, as prescribed by the bundle model.
  • Made values signed rationals: additivity in the discrete definition imposes no nonnegativity restriction.
  • Defined a mass one-deal as transferring one kind between two equal-mass, disjoint cohorts while preserving their other holdings; cohorts may share a type.
  • Retained unrestricted balanced side payments and no monetary budget constraints, so welfare-increasing transfers are the admissible moves.
  • Specified sparse rational input and output allocations, rational deal amounts, and total input bit length, including the initial allocation's support.
  • Allowed an empty sequence when the initial allocation is already optimal.
  • Interpreted the discrete convergence statement as concerning maximal negotiation sequences; arbitrary finite prefixes need not reach an optimum. The discrete requirement of distinct participating agents becomes disjoint cohorts, without requiring distinct types.

Result

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

More precisely, one can construct an optimal allocation with at most \(k(m+1)\) positive entries and a sequence of at most \(mk(m+1)\) admissible mass \(1\)-deals. Every rational mass appearing in the construction has bit length \(O(L)\).

The key is to partition the initial cohorts once, before negotiating. Each resource is then processed completely before proceeding to the next, so partial transfers do not cause repeated fragmentation.

1. Optimal marginals with strictly ordered donors and recipients

For any feasible allocation \(y\), define its resource marginals by

\[ x_{t,r}(y)=\sum_{S:r\in S}y_{t,S}. \]

They satisfy \(0\leq x_{t,r}(y)\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,r}(y)=\rho_r\). Additivity gives

\[ W(y)=\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{t\in T}v_{t,r}x_{t,r}(y). \]

Write \(x^0_{t,r}=x_{t,r}(y^0)\).

Fix a resource \(r\). Sorting types by decreasing \(v_{t,r}\), choose a value \(\lambda_r\) such that

\[ \sum_{t:v_{t,r}>\lambda_r}\mu_t \leq \rho_r \leq \sum_{t:v_{t,r}\geq\lambda_r}\mu_t. \]

Such a value exists: for positive supply, take the value at which the sorted cumulative mass first reaches the supply; for zero supply, take the maximum value.

Partition the types into

\[ H_r=\{t:v_{t,r}>\lambda_r\},\qquad E_r=\{t:v_{t,r}=\lambda_r\},\qquad F_r=\{t:v_{t,r}<\lambda_r\}. \]

Set target marginals \(z_{t,r}=\mu_t\) on \(H_r\) and \(z_{t,r}=0\) on \(F_r\). The required total marginal on \(E_r\) is

\[ h_r=\rho_r-\sum_{t\in H_r}\mu_t. \]

Let \(e_r=\sum_{t\in E_r}x^0_{t,r}\). Choose the tied types’ targets as follows:

  • If \(h_r\geq e_r\), start at their initial marginals and increase them, in any fixed order, until their total is \(h_r\).
  • If \(h_r<e_r\), start at their initial marginals and decrease them until their total is \(h_r\).

This is possible because \(0\leq h_r\leq\sum_{t\in E_r}\mu_t\). It uses only additions, subtractions, and minima.

Call \(t\) a donor for \(r\) if \(z_{t,r}<x^0_{t,r}\), and a recipient if \(z_{t,r}>x^0_{t,r}\).

Every donor has value at most \(\lambda_r\), and every recipient has value at least \(\lambda_r\). Moreover, tied types cannot include both donors and recipients: all their changes have the same direction. Consequently,

\[ v_{u,r}>v_{t,r} \quad\text{whenever $t$ is a donor and $u$ is a recipient for $r$.} \]

Thus every donor–recipient pairing strictly improves welfare.

The targets are optimal for this resource. Indeed, for any vector \(w_t\) satisfying \(0\leq w_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t w_t=\rho_r\),

\[ \sum_t v_{t,r}(w_t-z_{t,r}) = \sum_t (v_{t,r}-\lambda_r)(w_t-z_{t,r}) \leq 0. \]

The equality uses equal total supply. Each summand is nonpositive: on \(H_r\), \(z_{t,r}=\mu_t\); on \(F_r\), \(z_{t,r}=0\); and on \(E_r\), the coefficient vanishes. This argument also covers signed values.

2. A fixed refinement of the initial cohorts

Index the positive entries of \(y^0\) by \(p\), writing them as

\[ (t_p,S_p,q_p),\qquad p\in\{1,\ldots,k\}. \]

For every \(p,r\), choose a mass \(a_{p,r}\in[0,q_p]\) whose possession of \(r\) will be toggled.

For a donor type \(t\), distribute its required removal \(x^0_{t,r}-z_{t,r}\) among its initial entries containing \(r\), greedily taking up to each entry’s mass. This is possible because their total mass is \(x^0_{t,r}\).

For a recipient type \(t\), similarly distribute its required addition \(z_{t,r}-x^0_{t,r}\) among its initial entries lacking \(r\). Their total mass is \(\mu_t-x^0_{t,r}\), which is sufficient.

All other \(a_{p,r}\) are zero. Therefore, whenever \(a_{p,r}>0\), it specifies a removal from a donor or an addition to a recipient.

Represent entry \(p\), solely for bookkeeping, by an interval of length \(q_p\). For resource \(r\), designate its prefix of length \(a_{p,r}\) for toggling. Cut this interval at every distinct point among

\[ 0,\quad q_p,\quad a_{p,r}\ \text{for }r\in R. \]

There are at most \(m+1\) resulting positive-length intervals. Each resulting labelled cohort has a rational mass, its original type and bundle, and a fixed set of resources to toggle.

Let \(N\) be the total number of labelled cohorts. Then

\[ N\leq k(m+1). \]

This refinement changes no individual’s bundle. It is bookkeeping, not a deal. Nonatomicity permits the finite subdivisions of the specified masses.

Each designated prefix is a union of whole labelled cohorts. Hence, for every resource, the labelled donor cohorts have exactly the required total removal mass, and the labelled recipient cohorts have exactly the required total addition mass.

3. Constructing the deals without further persistent fragmentation

Process resources in a fixed order.

At the beginning of each resource’s processing, maintain this invariant:

Every labelled cohort has one uniform bundle. Previously processed resources have their prescribed final possession status; unprocessed resources retain their initial status.

The invariant holds initially.

Fix the current resource \(r\). Form two lists:

  • labelled cohorts designated to remove \(r\);
  • labelled cohorts designated to receive \(r\).

Their total masses agree, since

\[ \sum_{t:z_{t,r}<x^0_{t,r}}(x^0_{t,r}-z_{t,r}) = \sum_{t:z_{t,r}>x^0_{t,r}}(z_{t,r}-x^0_{t,r}). \]

Match these lists greedily. Take a donor cohort with remaining untransferred mass \(d>0\) and a recipient cohort with remaining untransferred mass \(b>0\), and set

\[ \varepsilon=\min\{d,b\}. \]

Let their remaining individuals’ bundles be \(S\) and \(B\), and their types be \(t\) and \(u\). Because \(r\) is unprocessed, \(r\in S\setminus B\). Perform the specified mass \(1\)-deal with mass \(\varepsilon\), and reduce both remaining masses accordingly.

This deal is valid for three reasons.

First, the aggregate allocation entries satisfy

\[ y_{t,S}\geq d\geq\varepsilon,\qquad y_{u,B}\geq b\geq\varepsilon. \]

Labels merely identify subcohorts within those entries. Combining coincident entries does not invalidate these inequalities.

Second, the two selected cohorts are disjoint. In fact, the strict value inequality proved earlier implies \(t\neq u\).

Third, its welfare change is

\[ W(y_{\mathrm{after}})-W(y_{\mathrm{before}}) = \varepsilon(v_{u,r}-v_{t,r})>0. \]

Thus every emitted deal is admissible. Each deal preserves type masses, all supplies, and nonnegativity: it changes only the possession of \(r\), removing and adding equal mass.

Transferred individuals are not selected again while processing \(r\). A partially processed labelled cohort therefore has only two bundle states: its bundle before toggling \(r\), and that bundle with \(r\) toggled.

Every deal exhausts at least one remaining cohort. Equal total masses ensure that both lists finish together. At completion, every designated cohort has been entirely toggled, so every labelled cohort is uniform again. Other resources have remained unchanged. This proves the invariant for the next resource.

If the two lists contain \(n_D\) and \(n_A\) cohorts, a nonempty processing phase uses at most \(n_D+n_A-1\) deals: every deal exhausts at least one cohort, and the final deal exhausts both remaining cohorts. Since the lists are disjoint subsets of the \(N\) cohorts, the total number of deals is at most

\[ mN\leq mk(m+1). \]

At any intermediate point, each labelled cohort occupies at most two bundles, so the allocation has at most \(2N\) positive entries.

4. Optimality of the final allocation

After all resources have been processed, aggregate labelled cohorts with identical type and bundle to obtain \(y^\star\). It has at most \(N\) positive entries.

For every \(t,r\), precisely the designated removal or addition mass has changed possession. Therefore,

\[ x_{t,r}(y^\star)=z_{t,r}. \]

For any feasible allocation \(y\), the marginal optimality established in Section 1 gives

\[ W(y) = \sum_r\sum_t v_{t,r}x_{t,r}(y) \leq \sum_r\sum_t v_{t,r}z_{t,r} = W(y^\star). \]

Thus \(y^\star\) is globally welfare-maximizing, and the constructed sequence reaches it using only admissible deals. If no transfers are needed, the sequence is empty.

If \(m=0\), simply return \(y^0\) and the empty sequence; every feasible allocation has welfare zero.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses, supplies, and positive entries of \(y^0\), in their binary rational representations. Its binary length is \(O(L)\).

Every mass computed above is obtained using additions, subtractions, minima, or comparisons. Consequently, every such mass is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). All cohort masses, allocation entries, and deal masses lie in \([0,1]\), so they admit rational representations of bit length \(O(L)\).

The construction requires:

  • sorting the \(\tau\) values for each resource and computing target marginals;
  • distributing the changes over the \(k\) initial entries;
  • sorting at most \(m\) cut points per initial entry;
  • generating at most \(mk(m+1)\) deals and aggregating the final cohorts.

These involve polynomially many arithmetic operations and comparisons on polynomial-length numbers. Each bundle is represented by \(m\) bits, and both requested outputs have polynomial length. Hence the full algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

6. Relation to the finite-agent convergence theorem

The [finite-agent result of Endriss et al., Theorem 3](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~fs/Papers/Multi-agent%20systems/EndrissEtAlJAIR2006.pdf) establishes convergence for additive utilities. The proof here independently supplies the bounded constructive sequence required by the continuous problem.

Arbitrary improving continuous schedules can be infinite: with two types of mass \(1/2\), one resource initially held entirely by the lower-valued type, repeatedly transferring half the remaining misplaced mass gives infinitely many strictly improving deals. The polynomial guarantee concerns the explicitly constructed schedule.
