The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given nonnegative rational item values \(v_j\) and rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\in[0,1]\) for a population of mass \(1\) with one common additive valuation, does there exist a distribution \(x_B\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_B x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni j}x_B=b_j\) for every item type \(j\), and the continuum Atkinson index is zero? Equivalently, when \(\bar v=\sum_j b_jv_j>0\), does such a distribution exist with \(v(B)=\bar v\) for every bundle with \(x_B>0\); when \(\bar v=0\), all allocated utility is zero.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here the population has mass \(1\) and one common additive valuation, so \(\tau=1\).
Let \(G=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with binary rational values \(v_j\geq0\) and per-capita supplies \(b_j\in[0,1]\). Set \(v(B)=\sum_{j\in B}v_j\) and \(\bar v=\sum_j b_jv_j\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
An allocation is a distribution of real masses \(x_B\geq0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\), including the empty bundle, satisfying \(\sum_{B\subseteq G}x_B=1\) and \(\sum_{B\ni j}x_B=b_j\) for every \(j\). Each person receives at most one unit of each item type, and all supplies are allocated; the bundles are implicit, not an exponentially long input list.
Use the Nash-based Atkinson index: for \(\bar v>0\), \(I(x)=1-\exp(\sum_{B:x_B>0}x_B\log v(B))/\bar v\), with \(\log0=-\infty\); set \(I(x)=0\) when \(\bar v=0\). Thus zero index means \(v(B)=\bar v\) whenever \(x_B>0\).

Is deciding whether such an allocation with \(I(x)=0\) exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many agents and indivisible goods, deciding whether an allocation has zero Atkinson index is strongly NP-hard even with identical additive valuations ([Schneckenburger, Dorn and Endriss](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10472-022-09789-z)).
For the continuous special case \(v_j=1\) for every \(j\), feasibility holds exactly when \(\sum_j b_j\) is an integer, by the characterization of jointly mixable Bernoulli marginals ([Chen, Liu, Liu and Wang](https://sas.uwaterloo.ca/~wang/papers/2021Chen-Liu-Liu-Wang-MF.pdf)). No verified result located settles the general continuous decision problem.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified the continuum index as the Nash-based Atkinson index, carrying over the all-zero convention and defining its geometric mean when positive mass receives zero utility.
  • Specified allocation masses as real numbers and bundles as implicit subsets, rather than part of the input.
  • Interpreted each item type as permitting at most one unit per person, as dictated by the stipulated integral bundles.
  • Applied the support convention to the single common valuation type, giving support size one, and defined L as total binary input length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with one supported type, positive integer valuations, and \(b_j=1/2\) for every item. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. Zero index means constant bundle value

Every feasible allocation satisfies
\(\sum_B x_Bv(B)=\sum_j v_j\sum_{B\ni j}x_B=\sum_j b_jv_j=\bar v\).

Suppose \(\bar v>0\). If a zero-valued bundle has positive mass, the logarithmic average is \(-\infty\), so \(I(x)=1\).

Otherwise, strict concavity of the logarithm gives
\(\sum_B x_B\log v(B)\leq\log\bar v\),
with equality precisely when all bundles of positive mass have the same value. Their average value is \(\bar v\), so that common value must be \(\bar v\). Therefore,
\(I(x)=0\) if and only if \(x_B>0\) implies \(v(B)=\bar v\).

For completeness, when \(\bar v=0\), the answer is always yes: the distribution
\(x_B=\prod_{j\in B}b_j\prod_{j\notin B}(1-b_j)\)
has total mass one and the prescribed marginals, and the index is zero by convention.

2. A polynomial-time reduction from PARTITION

Use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), encoded in binary, decide whether some subset \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\) satisfies
\(\sum_{j\in S}a_j=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\).
Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is a classical result of [Karp](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Construct a society with one supported type of mass one, and set
\(v_j=a_j\) and \(b_j=1/2\) for every \(j\).

This construction has polynomial binary length and takes polynomial time. Write \(A=\sum_j a_j>0\). The mean value is \(\bar v=A/2\).

If the PARTITION instance is yes, take a witnessing subset \(S\). Its complement also has value \(A/2\). Assign mass \(1/2\) to \(S\) and mass \(1/2\) to its complement, assigning zero mass to all other bundles.

The masses sum to one. Each item appears in exactly one of the two bundles, so its allocated marginal is \(1/2=b_j\). Both positive-mass bundles have value \(\bar v\); hence the allocation has zero index.

If the constructed society admits a zero-index allocation, at least one bundle \(B\) has positive mass because the masses sum to one. Section 1 implies
\(\sum_{j\in B}a_j=v(B)=\bar v=A/2\).
Thus \(B\) witnesses a yes-instance of PARTITION.

This proves the equivalence in both directions and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness with \(\tau=1\).

3. Polynomial-size certificates despite real masses

We also prove membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). The issue is that allocations initially permit real masses over exponentially many implicit bundles.

Assume \(\bar v>0\) and a zero-index allocation exists. Define
\(\mathcal E=\{B\subseteq G:v(B)=\bar v\}\).
For each bundle, let \(\mathbf1_B\in\{0,1\}^m\) be its incidence vector and put
\(a_B=(1,\mathbf1_B)\in\{0,1\}^{m+1}\).
Let \(c=(1,b_1,\ldots,b_m)\).

By Section 1, feasibility is equivalent to expressing \(c\) as
\(\sum_{B\in\mathcal E}x_Ba_B=c\), with \(x_B\geq0\).

A certificate needs at most \(m+1\) bundles

Choose such a representation with the smallest number \(k\) of positive coefficients; this minimum exists because there are finitely many bundles. Denote its bundles by \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\).

Their columns \(a_{B_i}\) are linearly independent. Indeed, otherwise there is a nonzero vector \(h\) satisfying
\(\sum_i h_i a_{B_i}=0\).
The first coordinate gives \(\sum_i h_i=0\), so \(h\) has both positive and negative entries. Set
\(\varepsilon=\min_{i:h_i>0}x_{B_i}/h_i>0\)
and replace each coefficient by \(x_{B_i}-\varepsilon h_i\).

These coefficients remain nonnegative, still represent \(c\), and at least one becomes zero. This contradicts minimality. Thus the columns are independent and \(k\leq m+1\).

The masses have polynomial binary length

Let \(M\) be the matrix of these \(k\) columns. Select \(k\) rows giving an invertible square matrix \(H\), and let \(c_R\) be the corresponding entries of \(c\). The masses satisfy \(Hx=c_R\).

Write each supply as \(b_j=p_j/q_j\), with positive integer denominator, and set \(D=\prod_j q_j\). The binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), and \(Dc_R\) is an integer vector whose entries lie between zero and \(D\).

For each \(i\), let \(H_i\) be \(H\) with column \(i\) replaced by \(Dc_R\). Cramer’s rule gives
\(x_i=\det(H_i)/(D\det(H))\).

Each column of \(H\) has Euclidean norm at most \(\sqrt{k}\), and the replacement column has norm at most \(D\sqrt{k}\). Hadamard’s determinant inequality therefore gives
\(\lvert\det(H)\rvert\leq k^{k/2}\) and
\(\lvert\det(H_i)\rvert\leq Dk^{k/2}\).

Because \(\det(H)\) is a nonzero integer, these formulas represent every mass as a rational with numerator and denominator of binary length
\(O(L+k\log(k+1))\).
Thus the whole certificate has polynomial length.

A verifier reads at most \(m+1\) bundles and their rational masses, and checks exactly:

  • nonnegativity and total mass one;
  • every prescribed item marginal;
  • value \(\bar v\) for every positive-mass bundle.

All checks use rational arithmetic of polynomial binary length. No logarithm needs to be evaluated. Instances with \(\bar v=0\) can be accepted directly.

4. Conclusion

The reduction settles the continuous problem: arbitrary real population masses do not remove the PARTITION obstruction when all supplies are one half. Together with the certificate argument, it proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness. The reduction establishes ordinary \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness; strong \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness is not needed for the question asked.
