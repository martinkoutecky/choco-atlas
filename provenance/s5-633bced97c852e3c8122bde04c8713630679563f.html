Result

For the stated case \(m=1\) and \(\tau=2\), an optimal deterministic policy and its exact rational value can be computed in polynomial time. Consequently, the decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\).

The mass-adjusted threshold property does not hold in general for finite discrete demand laws. A two-point second-demand law gives a counterexample with unique conditional optimizers.

1. Reduction to conditional optimization

Write the first demand law as \(\Pr(D_1=d_i)=p_i\), and the second as \(\Pr(D_2=e_j)=r_j\). Define the aggregate demands

\(u_i=\rho d_i,\qquad v_j=(1-\rho)e_j.\)

All these numbers are positive rationals. Independence implies that, conditional on any first demand, the second aggregate demand equals \(v_j\) with probability \(r_j\).

Fix an observed aggregate first demand \(u>0\). A feasible first allocation lies in the interval

\(0\le a\le h,\qquad h=\min\{u,s\}.\)

After observing second aggregate demand \(v_j\), the largest feasible second allocation is

\(b_j=\min\{v_j,s-a\}.\)

For fixed \(a\), the objective is nondecreasing in \(b_j\), so this choice is optimal. Its realized minimum fill rate is

\(\min\left\{\frac{a}{u},\frac{b_j}{v_j}\right\} =\min\left\{\frac{a}{u},1,\frac{s-a}{v_j}\right\} =\min\left\{\frac{a}{u},\frac{s-a}{v_j}\right\},\)

where the last equality uses \(a\le u\).

Thus the conditional optimization problem is exactly

\(M_s(u)=\max_{0\le a\le h}F_{s,u}(a),\qquad F_{s,u}(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_2}r_j\min\left\{\frac{a}{u},\frac{s-a}{v_j}\right\}.\)

Each summand is continuous, so \(F_{s,u}\) attains its maximum on the compact feasible interval.

The choices of first allocation for different observed first demands have no shared constraint: supply feasibility is imposed separately in each realization. Therefore the optimal unconditional value is

\(V^\star=\sum_{i=1}^{n_1}p_iM_s(u_i).\)

Indeed, every feasible policy has conditional value at most \(M_s(u_i)\) in each first-demand state. Conversely, choosing a conditional maximizer in every state, followed by the second allocation above, attains this sum.

2. An exact polynomial-time algorithm

For a fixed \(u\), the two affine expressions in the summand indexed by \(j\) agree precisely when

\(\frac{a}{u}=\frac{s-a}{v_j}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad a=c_j:=\frac{su}{u+v_j}.\)

Consequently, \(F_{s,u}\) is piecewise affine, and its only possible interior breakpoints are the feasible values \(c_j\).

Define the finite candidate set

\(C_{s,u}=\{0,h\}\cup \left\{\frac{su}{u+v_j}:1\le j\le n_2,\ \frac{su}{u+v_j}\le h\right\}.\)

To see that a candidate maximizes the objective, order the distinct candidates. Between each consecutive pair, every summand uses a fixed affine expression; hence their sum is affine there. An affine function on a closed interval has a maximum at an endpoint, including when it is constant. It follows that

\(M_s(u)=\max_{a\in C_{s,u}}F_{s,u}(a).\)

This gives the following exact algorithm:

  • For each first-demand state \(i\), construct \(C_{s,u_i}\).
  • Evaluate \(F_{s,u_i}(a)\) exactly at every candidate and choose a maximizing candidate \(a_i^\star\).
  • Output the policy

\(a(d_i)=a_i^\star,\quad b(d_i,e_j)=\min\{v_j,s-a_i^\star\}\).

  • Output

\(V^\star=\sum_i p_iF_{s,u_i}(a_i^\star)\).

The policy is feasible: the candidate construction gives \(0\le a_i^\star\le\min\{u_i,s\}\), and the second-allocation formula gives both \(0\le b(d_i,e_j)\le v_j\) and \(a_i^\star+b(d_i,e_j)\le s\). The preceding conditional argument proves its optimality.

Bit complexity

There are at most \(n_2+2\) candidates per first-demand state. Each evaluation uses \(n_2\) summands, so the algorithm uses \(O(n_1n_2^2)\) rational arithmetic operations and comparisons.

These operations also have polynomial bit complexity:

  • Each aggregate demand, candidate, and individual weighted summand is obtained from input rationals by a constant number of arithmetic operations, and therefore has bit length \(O(L)\).
  • A sum of \(n_2\) such rationals has bit length \(O(n_2L+\log n_2)\). For example, the product of their denominators provides a common denominator with this bound, and the corresponding summed numerator has the same order of bit length.
  • The final sum defining \(V^\star\) has polynomial bit length by the same argument.
  • Rational comparisons can be performed by integer cross multiplication, on integers of polynomial bit length.

Thus both the running time and output length are polynomial in \(n_1,n_2,L\). Since \(m=1\) and \(\tau=2\) are fixed here, this establishes the requested polynomial bound for the stated formulation.

For the decision problem, compare the computed \(V^\star\) exactly with \(q\). Achievability of \(V\ge q\) is equivalent to \(V^\star\ge q\), because the optimum is attained. Hence the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\). This argument concerns the specified two-cohort, one-resource model; it does not classify models with arbitrary numbers of cohorts or resources.

3. Counterexample to the threshold property

Fix

\(\rho=\frac12,\qquad s=1,\qquad \Pr(D_2=2)=\Pr(D_2=8)=\frac12.\)

The second aggregate demand therefore takes values \(1\) and \(4\), each with probability \(\frac12\). These data satisfy all rationality and positivity requirements.

For any first aggregate demand \(u>1\), the feasible first-allocation interval is \([0,1]\), and the conditional objective is

\(F_{1,u}(a)=\frac12\min\left\{\frac au,1-a\right\} +\frac12\min\left\{\frac au,\frac{1-a}{4}\right\}.\)

Its two breakpoints are

\(c_4=\frac{u}{u+4},\qquad c_1=\frac{u}{u+1},\qquad 0<c_4<c_1<1.\)

Directly identifying the smaller expression in each minimum gives

\(F_{1,u}(a)= \begin{cases} a/u,&0\le a\le c_4,\\ a/(2u)+(1-a)/8,&c_4\le a\le c_1,\\ 5(1-a)/8,&c_1\le a\le1. \end{cases}\)

The respective slopes are

\(\frac1u,\qquad \frac1{2u}-\frac18,\qquad-\frac58.\)

Unique optimizer at first demand \(u=3\)

For \(u=3\), the middle slope is

\(\frac16-\frac18=\frac1{24}>0.\)

Thus the objective is strictly increasing up to \(c_1\) and strictly decreasing afterward. Its unique maximizer is

\(A_1(3)=c_1=\frac34.\)

Unique optimizer at first demand \(u=5\)

For \(u=5\), the middle slope is

\(\frac1{10}-\frac18=-\frac1{40}<0.\)

Thus the objective is strictly increasing up to \(c_4\) and strictly decreasing afterward. Its unique maximizer is

\(A_1(5)=c_4=\frac59.\)

In particular,

\(3<5,\qquad A_1(3)=\frac34>\frac59=A_1(5).\)

Why no optimal selection can satisfy the property

Suppose an optimal selection satisfied the proposed property, and write \(T=T(1)\).

Since the unique optimizer at \(u=3\) is \(\frac34<3\), the requirement \(A_1(u)=u\) for \(0<u\le T\) forces \(T<3\). Both \(3\) and \(5\) consequently belong to \((T,\infty)\). Strict increase on that interval would require

\(A_1(3)<A_1(5),\)

contradicting the unique optimizer values just proved.

Hence no optimal selection has the stated threshold property for this second-demand law. The contradiction already occurs at a single supply value, so allowing the threshold to vary nondecreasingly with supply cannot repair it.

If desired, these two first-demand states can themselves form an admissible input law:

\(\Pr(D_1=6)=\Pr(D_1=10)=\frac12.\)

With \(\rho=\frac12\), their aggregate demands are exactly \(3\) and \(5\). Thus the counterexample also uses observed states of a valid finite instance.

4. Relation to the cited results

The [published abstract](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.2013.1244) describes structural results for continuous demand distributions and heuristics for discrete distributions. The counterexample above concerns the finite discrete laws specified here.

[Fair Dynamic Rationing](https://conference2021.eaamo.org/accepted/acceptednonarchival/EAMO21_paper_103.pdf) establishes worst-case fairness guarantees across demand distributions. The exact algorithm here instead optimizes for the two given finite laws. Both conclusions above follow directly from the conditional objective, without invoking a structural theorem from either source.
