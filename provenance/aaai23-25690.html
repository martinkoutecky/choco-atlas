The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two role types A and B of mass 1/2, finite strategy sets, rational payoff tables, desired sets, and budget delta, decide whether finite rational pairwise payment tables exist such that under random matching and affine expected utilities every globally undominated strategy lies in its desired set and the maximum normalized expected payment over undominated population states is at most delta.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here the support consists of roles \(A,B\), each of mass \(1/2\), so total mass is \(1\) and \(\tau=2\).
Each role has a nonempty finite pure-strategy set \(S_i\) and a nonempty desired subset \(D_i\subseteq S_i\). Input includes arbitrary signed rational payoff tables \(u_A,u_B:S_A\times S_B\to\mathbb Q\) and a binary rational budget \(\delta\ge0\). Let \(m=|S_A|+|S_B|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A population state consists of conditional strategy distributions \(p\in\Delta(S_A)\), \(q\in\Delta(S_B)\). Each individual meets one uniformly sampled individual of the opposite role; matching is independent of strategies.
Payments are finite tables \(v_A,v_B:S_A\times S_B\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with no prescribed upper bound. Modified expected utilities are \(F_A(a;q)=\sum_b q_b(u_A(a,b)+v_A(a,b))\) and \(F_B(b;p)=\sum_a p_a(u_B(a,b)+v_B(a,b))\).
A pure strategy globally dominates another if its modified expected utility is at least the other's at every opposite-role distribution and strictly greater at some such distribution. Only pure strategies may dominate; comparisons use the original full strategy sets, without iterative deletion. Let \(U_A(v),U_B(v)\) be the strategies with no global dominator; ties alone do not constitute domination.
The normalized payment per unit total population mass is \(C_v(p,q)=\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}p_aq_b(v_A(a,b)+v_B(a,b))\). Define \(\operatorname{cost}(v)=\max\{C_v(p,q):\operatorname{supp}(p)\subseteq U_A(v),\operatorname{supp}(q)\subseteq U_B(v)\}\), allowing real population distributions.
The decision problem asks whether such payment tables exist with \(U_A(v)\subseteq D_A\), \(U_B(v)\subseteq D_B\), and \(\operatorname{cost}(v)\le\delta\); desired strategies may themselves be dominated.
Is this continuous decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(\tau=2\) fixed and the budget encoded in \(L\).
Discrete game implementation with pure weak dominance and rectangular desired sets is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even for two players and zero budget ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00699)). No result settling this exact continuous formulation was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted payments to nonnegative rationals: the printed real-valued payment domain also appears in the full version, but unrestricted negative payments contradict the intended incentive model and hardness result.
  • Retained finite rational payments as specified in the continuous question, with no upper bound; infinite payment notation in the discrete proof is not an admissible payment.
  • Specified independent random matching exclusively across roles, with one opposite-role encounter per individual and conditional strategy distributions of total mass one.
  • Defined normalized cost as expected payment per unit total population mass, weighting each role's expected payment by one half.
  • Interpreted global undominatedness using pure weak dominance over all opposite-role distributions, with strict improvement somewhere and no iterative deletion, following the discrete definition.
  • Required nonempty finite strategy sets and nonempty desired sets; allowed signed rational base payoffs and a nonnegative rational budget, including zero.
  • Allowed arbitrary real population distributions while retaining binary rational input masses, payoffs, and budget; implementation requires containment rather than equality of undominated and desired sets.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, already for \(\tau=2\) and \(\delta=0\).

The continuous population distributions preserve both the discrete dominance relation and the discrete worst-case cost, up to the factor \(1/2\). Below, I prove this equivalence, address finite rational payments, and give an exact algorithm running in \(2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\) time.

1. Global dominance is coordinatewise dominance

Fix finite payment tables and write \(W_i(a,b)=u_i(a,b)+v_i(a,b)\).

Lemma 1. A strategy \(a'\) globally dominates \(a\) for role \(A\) exactly when

\(W_A(a',b)\ge W_A(a,b)\quad\text{for every }b\in S_B,\)

with strict inequality for at least one \(b\).

Proof. Necessity of the weak inequalities follows by testing the opposite-role distributions concentrated on individual strategies.

Conversely, these inequalities imply, for every \(q\in\Delta(S_B)\),

\(F_A(a';q)-F_A(a;q)=\sum_b q_b\bigl(W_A(a',b)-W_A(a,b)\bigr)\ge0.\)

A strict coordinate gives strict inequality at the distribution concentrated on that coordinate. If no coordinate is strict, the expected utilities agree at every distribution. This also establishes necessity of a strict coordinate. The proof for role \(B\) is identical, comparing columns instead of rows. \(\square\)

Thus the dominance relation is precisely pure weak dominance in the corresponding two-player game, with comparisons against the original full strategy sets.

Lemma 2. Each role has an undominated strategy, and every dominated strategy has an undominated dominator.

Proof. Represent each strategy by its finite vector of modified payoffs against all opposite-role pure strategies. Domination means coordinatewise inequality with at least one strict coordinate. It is transitive and strictly increases the sum of coordinates.

Starting from any dominated strategy and repeatedly choosing a dominator therefore visits distinct strategies. Finiteness forces the process to terminate at an undominated strategy. Transitivity makes that terminal strategy a dominator of the original strategy. \(\square\)

2. The continuous maximum equals a pure-profile maximum

Lemma 3. For every finite payment table,

\(\operatorname{cost}(v)=\frac12\max_{a\in U_A(v),\,b\in U_B(v)}\bigl(v_A(a,b)+v_B(a,b)\bigr).\)

Proof. Lemma 2 makes both undominated sets nonempty. For any admissible \(p,q\), the numbers \(p_aq_b\) are nonnegative and sum to one over \(U_A(v)\times U_B(v)\). Hence

\(C_v(p,q)\le\frac12\max_{a\in U_A(v),\,b\in U_B(v)}\bigl(v_A(a,b)+v_B(a,b)\bigr).\)

Equality is attained by concentrating \(p,q\) on a maximizing pair. These distributions are permitted population states. \(\square\)

In particular, when \(\delta=0\), nonnegativity of payments gives

\(v_A(a,b)=v_B(a,b)=0\quad\text{for every }(a,b)\in U_A(v)\times U_B(v).\)

Payments elsewhere remain unrestricted by this cost condition.

3. A linear-programming characterization

This section proves that finite rational payments suffice whenever finite real payments would suffice. It also establishes membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Choose nonempty sets \(K_A\subseteq D_A\) and \(K_B\subseteq D_B\). For every strategy outside these sets, choose a proposed dominator:

\(d_A:S_A\setminus K_A\longrightarrow K_A,\qquad d_B:S_B\setminus K_B\longrightarrow K_B.\)

For these fixed choices, construct a rational linear program with variables \(v_A,v_B,\varepsilon\), maximizing \(\varepsilon\), subject to the following constraints.

First, require nonnegative payments and a bounded margin:

\(v_A(a,b)\ge0,\qquad v_B(a,b)\ge0,\qquad 0\le\varepsilon\le1.\)

For each \(a\in S_A\setminus K_A\), require

\(W_A(d_A(a),b)-W_A(a,b)\ge0\quad\text{for every }b\in S_B,\)

and

\(\sum_{b\in S_B}\bigl(W_A(d_A(a),b)-W_A(a,b)\bigr)\ge\varepsilon.\)

For each \(b\in S_B\setminus K_B\), require

\(W_B(a,d_B(b))-W_B(a,b)\ge0\quad\text{for every }a\in S_A,\)

and

\(\sum_{a\in S_A}\bigl(W_B(a,d_B(b))-W_B(a,b)\bigr)\ge\varepsilon.\)

Finally, impose the budget throughout the chosen rectangle:

\(v_A(a,b)+v_B(a,b)\le2\delta\quad\text{for every }(a,b)\in K_A\times K_B.\)

All these constraints are linear because \(W_i=u_i+v_i\) and the utilities are input constants.

Proposition 4. The instance admits an implementation if and only if some choice of \(K_A,K_B,d_A,d_B\) gives a feasible linear program whose optimal value is positive.

Proof: soundness. Suppose such a program has a solution with \(\varepsilon>0\). Every coordinate difference in a proposed domination comparison is nonnegative, and its sum is positive. Consequently at least one coordinate is strictly positive. Lemma 1 therefore shows that every strategy outside \(K_i\) is dominated. Thus

\(U_A(v)\subseteq K_A\subseteq D_A,\qquad U_B(v)\subseteq K_B\subseteq D_B.\)

The budget constraints hold on the entire chosen rectangle and hence on the actual undominated rectangle. Lemma 3 gives \(\operatorname{cost}(v)\le\delta\).

The chosen sets need not equal the actual undominated sets; this is why no constraints forbidding domination within \(K_i\) are necessary.

Proof: completeness. Suppose an implementation exists, initially allowing finite real payments. Set \(K_i=U_i(v)\). These sets are nonempty by Lemma 2 and lie in \(D_i\). For every strategy outside \(K_i\), Lemma 2 supplies a dominator in \(K_i\); choose it as \(d_i\).

Every resulting coordinate difference is nonnegative, and every domination comparison has a strictly positive sum. There are finitely many such comparisons. Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) no larger than one and no larger than any of these positive sums. If there are no comparisons, choose \(\varepsilon=1\). Lemma 3 supplies the budget constraints. The resulting branch is therefore feasible with positive objective value. \(\square\)

Rationality and computation. Each branch is a rational linear program with \(2|S_A||S_B|+1=O(m^2)\) variables, \(O(m^2)\) constraints, and encoding length polynomial in \(m,L\).

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming applies: feasibility and a bounded optimum can be determined exactly, and a rational optimal solution can be produced with polynomial encoding length. Here the objective is bounded by \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\). Consequently, a branch admitting positive real margin also admits a finite rational payment table with positive margin.

This argument preserves the budget exactly, including when it holds with equality; it does not rely on perturbing payments.

A certificate consists of the sets \(K_i\) and maps \(d_i\), requiring \(O(m\log m)\) bits. A verifier constructs the branch and checks whether its optimal value is positive in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\) time. Hence the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. Transfer of the discrete hardness result

Use the supplied discrete hardness theorem: two-player game implementation under pure weak dominance is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard at zero budget, with rectangular desired sets. Its construction has nonempty desired sets and utilities in \(\{0,1,2\}\). See [Chen et al., Theorem 3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00699).

There is one finite-payment detail to address. That proof writes some completeness payments as \(\infty\), with all remaining payments zero. Such notation can be eliminated: replace each formerly infinite modified utility by \(M=3\), assigning payment \(3-u_i(a,b)\) there. Leave zero-payment entries unchanged.

Originally, each modified utility was either a value in \(\{0,1,2\}\) or \(\infty\). The replacement preserves every equality, weak inequality, and strict inequality between corresponding payoff coordinates: finite values stay unchanged, infinite values become the same value \(3\), and \(3\) exceeds every finite value. Thus all dominance relations and undominated sets are preserved. Since the original promises vanish on the undominated rectangle, the replacement still costs zero. All replacement payments are finite nonnegative integers.

Now transform a discrete instance with budget \(\beta\) into the society instance as follows:

  • Give the two roles mass \(1/2\) each.
  • Copy the strategy sets, utility tables, and desired subsets.
  • Set \(\delta=\beta/2\).

For any finite payment tables, Lemma 1 identifies the discrete and population undominated sets exactly. Lemma 3 gives

\(\operatorname{cost}_{\mathrm{population}}(v)=\tfrac12\operatorname{cost}_{\mathrm{discrete}}(v).\)

Because both undominated sets are nonempty, the discrete rectangular requirement

\(U_A(v)\times U_B(v)\subseteq D_A\times D_B\)

is equivalent to the two requirements \(U_A(v)\subseteq D_A\) and \(U_B(v)\subseteq D_B\).

Thus a rational population implementation yields a discrete implementation with the corresponding budget. Conversely, a finite real discrete implementation yields a population implementation over the reals, and Proposition 4 supplies finite rational payments satisfying the same population budget.

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. In the hard instances, \(\beta=\delta=0\), so budget scaling introduces no additional issue. The reduction fixes \(\tau=2\). Therefore the population problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; together with Section 3, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

5. Running times and parameter dependence

For the exact algorithm, enumerate the sets and maps in Section 3. There are at most \(2^m\) choices of the sets and at most \(m^m\) choices of the maps. Each branch takes \(\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\) time to check. Hence the total running time is

\(2^m m^m\operatorname{poly}(m,L)=2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,L),\qquad \tau=2.\)

The certificate verifier runs in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\) time. Both bounds use the binary encoding of \(\delta\) included in \(L\), with no dependence on its numerical magnitude.

Unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), there is no polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,L\), and no algorithm of the form \(f(\tau)\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\): hardness already holds at the fixed support size \(\tau=2\).
