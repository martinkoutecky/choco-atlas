The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of applicant types \(T\), post categories \(P\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_p\), ranked eligibility edges \(E\), and a feasible rank-maximal allocation \(x\), compute a rank-maximal allocation after one rational type-mass, capacity, or preference-edge update. An allocation satisfies \(\sum_{p:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\), and \(x_{tp}\geq0\); it is rank-maximal when its rank totals \((\rho_1(x),\ldots,\rho_r(x))\) are lexicographically maximal.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) post categories \(P\), with nonnegative binary-rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_p\).
Eligibility edges \(E\subseteq T\times P\) have positive integer rank labels encoded in binary. Equal labels represent ties; missing ranks and empty eligibility lists are allowed. Only applicants have preferences, and updates do not automatically renumber other edges.
An allocation has real entries \(x_{tp}\geq0\) on eligible edges, with \(\sum_{p:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\). Unallocated mass is allowed. For each occurring rank label \(i\), set \(\rho_i(x)=\sum_{(t,p)\in E_i}x_{tp}\); rank-maximality means lexicographically maximizing these totals in increasing rank order, without further tie-breaking.
The input includes a binary-rational rank-maximal allocation and one update: replacement of one capacity by a nonnegative rational, insertion/deletion/rank change of one eligibility edge, or replacement of one type's mass by a nonnegative rational. A mass update may insert a type with its ranked incident edges or delete a type by setting its mass to zero.
For a mass update, first modify the mass in the old units, obtaining total mass \(s>0\); then divide every remaining mass and every capacity by \(s\). Delete zero-mass types and their incident edges. The required output is any exact rational rank-maximal allocation for the updated instance.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the initial allocation and update, and let \(r\) count the distinct rank labels occurring before or after the update. Measure running time in \(m,\tau,|E|,r,L\), using the larger pre-update and post-update sizes.

Is computing a rank-maximal allocation after one such update solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|E|,r,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete applicants and unit-capacity posts, an existing rank-maximal matching can be updated after insertion or deletion of one vertex or edge in \(O(r_d(e_d+n_d))\) time after preprocessing, where \(n_d\) and \(e_d\) count vertices and edges and \(r_d\) is the maximum rank used by any rank-maximal matching. This guarantee does not cover rational-mass updates.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Capacities are nonnegative binary rationals; zero capacities and empty eligibility lists are allowed because the discrete definition imposes no corresponding nondegeneracy restriction.
  • Rank labels are positive binary-encoded integers, ties and gaps are permitted, and unrelated edges retain their labels after an update. The continuous running-time parameter r counts distinct occurring labels.
  • A preference-edge update means insertion, deletion, or replacement of the rank of one edge.
  • A mass update replaces one mass in the old units and then normalizes all masses and capacities by the new positive total. This preserves absolute post availability under applicant insertion or deletion.
  • Mass updates may insert or delete a type together with its incident ranked edges, mirroring discrete vertex updates; deleting the entire society is excluded to preserve probability normalization.
  • The initial allocation and update are included in the binary input length; the output is any exact rational optimum, without a proximity requirement or additional tie-breaking.
  • The complexity target counts preprocessing and permits recomputation; it does not impose the discrete update-time bound.

Result

The update problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|E|,r,L\). An algorithm can recompute the allocation using at most \(r\) exact rational linear programs. The given initial allocation need not be used.

The proof below includes a uniform bound on the encoding lengths of the intermediate optimal rank totals.

1. Apply the update and handle normalization

For a capacity or eligibility update, apply the specified change directly.

For a mass update, let \(\widetilde\mu_t\) denote the masses after the replacement but before normalization. Remove zero-mass types and their incident edges, and set \(s=\sum_t\widetilde\mu_t>0\). Keep capacities in their old units while computing an allocation \(z\).

There is a bijection between feasible allocations before and after normalization:

\(x_{tp}=z_{tp}/s.\)

Indeed, dividing the inequalities \(\sum_p z_{tp}\leq\widetilde\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t z_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\) by \(s\) gives exactly the normalized feasibility inequalities. Moreover, every rank total is divided by the same positive number:

\(\rho_i(x)=\rho_i(z)/s.\)

Consequently, normalization preserves all lexicographic comparisons. It suffices to find a rank-maximal allocation in the old units and divide its entries by \(s\).

This preprocessing has polynomial bit complexity. For a mass replacement,

\(s=1-\mu_{\mathrm{old}}+\mu_{\mathrm{new}},\)

where \(\mu_{\mathrm{old}}=0\) for an insertion. Thus \(s\) has encoding length \(O(L)\). The unnormalized updated data also have encoding length polynomial in \(L\).

For the remainder of the proof, write \(\mu_t\) for the updated masses in these working units.

2. Sequential exact optimization

Let \(N\) be the number of updated eligibility edges. If \(N=0\), the empty allocation is rank-maximal, so suppose \(N>0\).

Sort the distinct labels occurring on updated edges:

\(i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_q,\qquad q\leq r.\)

Only occurring labels are processed. Their binary encodings can be sorted in polynomial time, regardless of their numerical magnitudes. Define

\(c_j(z)=\sum_{(t,p)\in E:\,\operatorname{rank}(t,p)=i_j}z_{tp}.\)

Let \(Q\subseteq\mathbb R^N\) be the feasible allocation polytope, defined by

\(z_{tp}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{p:(t,p)\in E}z_{tp}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:(t,p)\in E}z_{tp}\leq\kappa_p.\)

The zero allocation belongs to \(Q\). Also, every coordinate satisfies \(0\leq z_{tp}\leq\mu_t\), so \(Q\) is bounded. It is closed and therefore compact.

Starting with \(F_0=Q\), successively compute

\(\beta_j=\max\{c_j(z):z\in F_{j-1}\},\qquad F_j=\{z\in F_{j-1}:c_j(z)=\beta_j\},\)

for \(j=1,\ldots,q\), using exact rational linear programming. Return an optimizer from the final stage, dividing it by \(s\) when normalization is required.

Every stage has a nonempty compact feasible set: a continuous linear objective attains its maximum on \(F_{j-1}\), and its maximizing set \(F_j\) is nonempty and compact.

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: a linear program with explicitly encoded rational coefficients, a nonempty feasible region, and a finite attained optimum admits computation of an exact rational optimal solution in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. Exact polynomial-time rational LP methods are discussed in [Gleixner and Steffy, Section 1.3](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/7507.pdf). The hypotheses hold here, including when the feasible sets have lower dimension.

3. Correctness

Let \(z^\star\in F_q\) be the returned allocation. Then \(c_j(z^\star)=\beta_j\) for every \(j\).

Suppose that a feasible allocation \(y\in Q\) had a lexicographically larger rank vector. Let \(j\) be the first coordinate at which its rank vector differs from that of \(z^\star\). Then

\(c_h(y)=\beta_h\quad\text{for every }h<j,\qquad c_j(y)>\beta_j.\)

The equalities imply \(y\in F_{j-1}\), whereas the strict inequality contradicts the definition of \(\beta_j\). Therefore \(z^\star\) is rank-maximal.

This argument treats ties exactly as required, because each objective sums all edges bearing its label. It imposes no further tie-breaking. By Section 1, the normalized output is also rank-maximal.

4. Uniform bounds on intermediate rational values

It remains to show that the successive equalities do not cause superpolynomial growth in LP input lengths.

4.1. Every intermediate optimum occurs at an original vertex

A subset \(F\) of a convex set \(Q\) is a face if, whenever a strict convex combination of two points of \(Q\) belongs to \(F\), both points belong to \(F\).

We prove inductively that every \(F_j\) is a face of \(Q\). This holds for \(F_0=Q\). Suppose it holds for \(F_{j-1}\), and let

\(w=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v\in F_j,\qquad u,v\in Q,\qquad 0<\lambda<1.\)

Because \(w\in F_{j-1}\), its face property gives \(u,v\in F_{j-1}\). Hence \(c_j(u)\leq\beta_j\) and \(c_j(v)\leq\beta_j\). Their weighted average is \(c_j(w)=\beta_j\), so both must equal \(\beta_j\). Thus \(u,v\in F_j\), proving the induction.

Every nonempty compact polytope has a vertex: successively maximizing its coordinate functions produces nested nonempty maximizing faces, ending in a singleton. A vertex of a face of \(Q\) is also a vertex of \(Q\), directly from the face property.

Consequently, each \(F_j\) contains a vertex \(v\) of \(Q\), and \(\beta_j=c_j(v)\). We can therefore bound every intermediate optimum using vertices of the single original polytope \(Q\).

4.2. A determinant bound

Write the original inequalities as \(Az\leq b\), including nonnegativity. All entries of \(A\) lie in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Let \(\ell\) be the total binary encoding length of \(b\); it is polynomial in \(L\). Let \(D\) be the product of the positive denominators of its entries. Then

\(D\leq2^\ell,\qquad |Db_h|\leq2^{2\ell}\)

for every row \(h\), and \(Db\) is integral.

At a vertex \(v\) of \(Q\), the active constraint normals span \(\mathbb R^N\). Otherwise, a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would allow sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions, contradicting extremality. Select \(N\) independent active rows and let \(B\) be their coefficient matrix. Thus \(Bv=b_B\) and \(\det B\neq0\).

Set

\(M=N!\,2^{2N\ell}.\)

Cramer’s rule, applied to \(B(Dv)=Db_B\), gives

\(v_e=\dfrac{a_e}{D\,d},\qquad d=\det B,\qquad a_e=\det B_e,\)

where \(B_e\) replaces column \(e\) of \(B\) by \(Db_B\). Expanding determinants over permutations yields

\(1\leq|d|\leq N!,\qquad |a_e|\leq M.\)

All coordinates share the displayed denominator. Therefore every rank total at \(v\) has the representation

\(c_j(v)=\dfrac{\sum_{e:\,\operatorname{rank}(e)=i_j}a_e}{D\,d},\)

with numerator of absolute value at most \(NM\) and denominator of absolute value at most \(DN!\). Its reduced encoding length is consequently

\(O\!\left(N\ell+N\log(N+1)+\ell\right).\)

By Section 4.1, this bound applies uniformly to every \(\beta_j\), independently of the number of preceding optimization stages.

5. Running time and output

At stage \(j\), the LP has \(N\) variables, at most \(\tau+m+N\) original inequalities, and \(j-1\) additional equalities. Each equality can be represented by two inequalities. All objective and constraint coefficients lie in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and Section 4 bounds all newly introduced right-hand sides by polynomial encoding length.

Thus every stage has input length polynomial in \(m,\tau,|E|,r,L\). There are at most \(r\) stages, so exact rational LP gives an overall polynomial-time algorithm. Computing and reducing the rank totals uses polynomial-time rational arithmetic. The final division by \(s\), when required, also has polynomial bit complexity and produces an exact rational allocation.

This proves the requested polynomial-time bound for all specified updates, without expanding rational masses into discrete applicants or relying on the quoted discrete matching guarantee.
