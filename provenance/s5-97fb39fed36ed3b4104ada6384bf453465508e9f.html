The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix rational λ>0 and a rational threshold η. Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(μ_t\) summing to one, and a connected loopless graph with nonnegative rational edge weights \(w_{tu}\), let \(V=∑_{\{t,u\}∈ E} w_{tu}>0\). Decide whether there is a nonnegative allocation vector \(x\) with \(∑_t x_t=V\) such that \(∑_t r_t x_t≥∑_{\{t,u\}∈ E}w_{tu}r_tr_u\) for every \(r∈[0,1]^T\), and \(D_λ(Q\|P)≥η\), where \(Q_t=x_t/V\), \(P_t=μ_t\), and \(D_1\) is the usual Kullback–Leibler divergence. Here each type is a cohort, \(x_t\) is its total allocation, and each member receives the same per-capita rate.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(T\) is that support, \(\mu_t>0\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau=|T|\) counts supported types, not possible types.
The input also contains a connected undirected loopless graph \(G=(T,E)\), nonnegative binary-rational weights \(w_{tu}\), and a binary-rational threshold \(\eta>0\). Require \(\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}>0\) for every \(t\). Write \(m=|E|\), \(V=\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}>0\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type is a cohort. An allocation \(x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{T}\) gives cohort \(t\) total payoff \(x_t\), distributed at the constant per-capita rate \(x_t/\mu_t\). A coalition selecting fraction \(r_t\in[0,1]\) of each cohort has value \(\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}r_tr_u\).
An allocation is admissible when \(\sum_t x_t=V\) and \(\sum_t r_tx_t\geq\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}r_tr_u\) for every \(r\in[0,1]^T\).
Fix a rational order \(\lambda>0\). Use Rényi divergence \(D_\lambda\), with base-two logarithms and its Kullback–Leibler limit at \(\lambda=1\), allowing zero allocation coordinates with their usual zero-term convention. Set \(Q_t=x_t/V\) and \(P_t=\mu_t\).

For each fixed rational \(\lambda>0\), is deciding whether an admissible allocation satisfies \(D_\lambda(Q\|P)\geq\eta\) in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

The discrete integer-allocation problem with a uniform baseline is stated NP-complete for every fixed \(\lambda>0\); ordinary fractional-core Rényi entropy minimization also has the same optimum as minimum Rényi entropy weighted orientation. [Bonchiş–Istrate](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.0283)
The later treatment states its intractability results only for \(0<\lambda\leq1\). [Istrate–Bonchiş](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S030439752200648X)
No result addressing exactly this cohort model with arbitrary positive rational masses was located.

Result

For every fixed rational \(\lambda>0\), the decision problem is NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds even when the positive cohort masses take just two dyadic values, with ratio three.

I interpret the decision question as asking whether there exists an admissible allocation whose divergence is at least \(\eta\). The proof below gives a direct reduction and constructs a binary-rational threshold with a rigorous separation gap.

Throughout the proof, \(\ln\) denotes the natural logarithm; divergence uses base-two logarithms.

1. Admissible allocations are precisely fractional edge allocations

For \(S\subseteq T\), define
\[ v(S)=\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E:\,t,u\in S}w_{tu}, \qquad x(S)=\sum_{t\in S}x_t. \]

Lemma 1. An allocation is admissible if and only if it satisfies
\[ x\geq0,\qquad x(T)=V,\qquad x(S)\geq v(S)\quad\text{for every }S\subseteq T. \]

Proof. Admissibility implies these inequalities by taking \(r\) to be the indicator of \(S\).

Conversely, suppose the subset inequalities hold. Given \(r\in[0,1]^T\), choose a random subset \(S\) by including each type \(t\) independently with probability \(r_t\). Taking expectations of \(x(S)\geq v(S)\) gives
\[ \sum_t r_tx_t\geq \sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}r_tr_u. \]
This is the required coalition inequality. \(\square\)

Lemma 2. The admissible allocations are exactly those obtained by splitting each edge’s weight between its endpoints.

Proof. Any such splitting satisfies \(x(T)=V\). Every edge internal to \(S\) contributes its entire weight to \(x(S)\), so Lemma 1 establishes admissibility.

For the converse, construct a flow network with:

  • a source-to-edge-node arc of capacity \(w_e\) for each edge \(e\);
  • arcs of capacity \(V+1\) from each edge-node to its two endpoint nodes;
  • an arc of capacity \(x_t\) from each endpoint node \(t\) to the sink.

Consider a cut of capacity at most \(V\). It cannot cut an arc of capacity \(V+1\). Thus, if \(S\) is its set of endpoint nodes on the source side and \(F\) its set of edge-nodes on that side, then \(F\subseteq E[S]\). Its capacity is
\[ V-\sum_{e\in F}w_e+x(S) \geq V-v(S)+x(S)\geq V. \]
Cuts of larger capacity also have capacity at least \(V\). The cut containing only the source has capacity \(V\), so the max-flow/min-cut theorem gives a flow of value \(V\).

All source arcs and endpoint-to-sink arcs are therefore saturated. The flow splits every edge’s weight between its endpoints and yields allocation \(x\). \(\square\)

Call an allocation an orientation allocation when each edge’s entire weight goes to one endpoint.

Lemma 3. For every admissible allocation \(x\), there is an orientation allocation \(y\) satisfying
\[ D_\lambda(y/V\|P)\geq D_\lambda(x/V\|P). \]

Proof. Every fractional edge allocation is a convex combination of orientation allocations: independently assign each positive-weight edge to an endpoint with probabilities equal to its split fractions.

For \(\lambda\neq1\), maximizing divergence is equivalent to maximizing
\[ J_\lambda(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda-1} \sum_t\mu_t^{1-\lambda}x_t^\lambda. \]
This function is convex. On positive coordinates its summands have second derivative \(\lambda\mu_t^{1-\lambda}x_t^{\lambda-2}>0\); continuity extends convexity to zero. The equivalence follows because both objectives increase with the weighted power sum when \(\lambda>1\), and both decrease with it when \(0<\lambda<1\).

For \(\lambda=1\), maximizing divergence is equivalent to maximizing the continuous convex function
\[ J_1(x)=\sum_t x_t\ln(x_t/\mu_t), \]
with the zero-term convention.

Convexity implies that some orientation in the convex combination has objective at least that of \(x\). \(\square\)

Thus fractional allocations introduce no additional optimal value, for any of the orders under consideration.

2. Reduction from Simple Max-Cut

We use the NP-completeness of unweighted Simple Max-Cut, established by [Garey, Johnson and Stockmeyer](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975%2876%2990059-1).

Let the source instance be a simple graph \(H\), with \(n\) vertices, \(M\geq1\) edges, maximum degree \(\Delta\), and integer threshold \(1\leq k\leq M\). These restrictions discard only trivially decidable instances. Pad \(H\) with isolated vertices so that \(n\) is a power of two; this preserves every cut size and increases \(n\) by less than a factor of two.

For each vertex \(i\), create four types:
\[ v_i^0,\quad v_i^1,\quad \ell_i^0,\quad \ell_i^1. \]
Let \(B\) be a positive integer, specified below. Add:

  • the edge \(\{v_i^0,v_i^1\}\), of weight \(B\);
  • the two pendant edges \(\{v_i^b,\ell_i^b\}\), each of weight \(B\);
  • for each original edge \(\{i,j\}\), the two edges

\(\{v_i^0,v_j^0\}\) and \(\{v_i^1,v_j^1\}\), each of weight one.

Connect distinct components by zero-weight edges. This makes the graph connected without changing any allocation or coalition value.

Set
\[ p=\frac{1}{8n},\qquad \mu_{v_i^b}=p,\qquad \mu_{\ell_i^b}=3p. \]
These masses are positive and dyadic, and their sum is \(8np=1\). Every type has a positive-weight incident edge. Moreover,
\[ \tau=4n,\qquad m\leq4n+2M-1,\qquad V=3nB+2M. \]

3. Pendant edges force positive baseline payoffs

Lemma 4. In every optimal orientation, every pendant edge is assigned to its endpoint \(v_i^b\).

Proof. Suppose a pendant edge is assigned to its leaf. Its leaf receives \(B\); write \(z\geq0\) for the current payoff of the other endpoint. Reverse that edge.

For \(\lambda>1\), the change in the weighted power sum is
\[ p^{1-\lambda}\bigl((z+B)^\lambda-z^\lambda\bigr) -(3p)^{1-\lambda}B^\lambda \geq \bigl(p^{1-\lambda}-(3p)^{1-\lambda}\bigr)B^\lambda>0. \]
Here \((z+B)^\lambda-z^\lambda\geq B^\lambda\), by convexity. Hence divergence increases.

For \(0<\lambda<1\), concavity gives
\((z+B)^\lambda-z^\lambda\leq B^\lambda\). Consequently, the same change is at most
\[ \bigl(p^{1-\lambda}-(3p)^{1-\lambda}\bigr)B^\lambda<0. \]
Divergence increases because its denominator \(\lambda-1\) is negative.

For \(\lambda=1\), convexity of \(s\ln(s/p)\), extended continuously to zero, gives
\[ (z+B)\ln((z+B)/p)-z\ln(z/p) \geq B\ln(B/p). \]
After subtracting the leaf’s former contribution \(B\ln(B/(3p))\), the objective increases by at least \(B\ln3>0\). \(\square\)

After these reversals, all leaves receive zero, and each \(v_i^b\) receives baseline payoff \(B\).

Call the endpoint receiving the edge \(\{v_i^0,v_i^1\}\) active. Exactly one endpoint of each pair is active. Its payoff before assigning the unit-weight edges is \(2B\), while the other endpoint’s payoff is \(B\). Let \(b_i\) identify the active endpoint and let \(c(b)\) be the corresponding cut size in \(H\).

For each original edge:

  • if \(b_i\neq b_j\), both corresponding unit edges have an active endpoint;
  • if \(b_i=b_j\), exactly one corresponding unit edge has an active endpoint.

Therefore exactly \(M+c(b)\) unit edges have an active endpoint. If \(C\) counts unit edges actually assigned to active endpoints, then
\[ C\leq M+c(b). \]
Equality can always be attained by assigning every edge having an active endpoint to one such endpoint.

4. A quantitative objective gap

Define, for positive \(s\),
\[ f_\lambda(s)=\frac{s^\lambda-s}{\lambda-1}\quad(\lambda\neq1), \qquad f_1(s)=s\ln s. \]
For every order, including the limit order,
\[ f_\lambda''(s)=\lambda s^{\lambda-2}>0. \]

Introduce constants depending only on the fixed order:
\[ c_\lambda=\lambda\max\{1,3^{\lambda-2}\}, \]
\[ d_\lambda=\frac{\lambda(2^{\lambda-1}-1)}{\lambda-1} \quad(\lambda\neq1),\qquad d_1=\ln2, \]
\[ u_\lambda=2\cdot3^\lambda+ \lambda\max\{1,2^{\lambda-1}\}+4. \]
The constant \(d_\lambda\) is positive: its numerator and denominator have the same sign when \(\lambda\neq1\).

Choose a fixed integer
\[ K_\lambda> \max\left\{2,\frac{8c_\lambda}{d_\lambda}, \frac{8u_\lambda}{d_\lambda}\right\}, \]
and set
\[ B=K_\lambda n(\Delta+1)^2,\qquad \delta=d_\lambda B^{\lambda-1}. \]
In particular, \(B\geq\Delta\), and direct differentiation gives
\[ f_\lambda'(2B)-f_\lambda'(B)=\delta. \]

For an orientation with all pendant edges assigned inward, define
\[ \Phi(x)=\sum_{i,b}f_\lambda(x_{v_i^b}), \qquad A=n\bigl(f_\lambda(B)+f_\lambda(2B)\bigr) +2M f_\lambda'(B). \]

Each central coordinate is \(a+z\), where \(a\in\{B,2B\}\) and \(0\leq z\leq\Delta\). On the interval \([B,3B]\),
\[ f_\lambda''(s)\leq c_\lambda B^{\lambda-2}. \]
Taylor’s theorem therefore gives
\[ f_\lambda(a+z)=f_\lambda(a)+f_\lambda'(a)z+R_{a,z}, \qquad 0\leq R_{a,z}\leq \frac{c_\lambda}{2}B^{\lambda-2}z^2. \]

The total unit-edge payoff going to active endpoints is \(C\), and that going to inactive endpoints is \(2M-C\). Summing the expansions yields
\[ \Phi(x)=A+\delta C+R, \]
where
\[ 0\leq R\leq c_\lambda n\Delta^2B^{\lambda-2} \leq\frac{\delta}{8}. \]
The last inequality follows from
\[ \frac{c_\lambda n\Delta^2B^{\lambda-2}}{\delta} = \frac{c_\lambda}{K_\lambda d_\lambda} \frac{\Delta^2}{(\Delta+1)^2}<\frac18. \]

Writing \(c^*\) for the maximum cut size of \(H\), we obtain
\[ A+\delta(M+c^*) \leq\max\Phi \leq A+\delta(M+c^*)+\frac{\delta}{8}. \]
The lower bound follows by choosing a maximum cut and attaining \(C=M+c^*\). The upper bound follows from \(C\leq M+c(b)\leq M+c^*\).

Set
\[ C_*=M+k-\frac12,\qquad \Phi_*=A+\delta C_*. \]
Then:

  • if \(c^*\geq k\), some orientation satisfies

\(\Phi(x)\geq\Phi_*+\delta/2\);

  • if \(c^*<k\), every orientation with inward pendant edges satisfies

\(\Phi(x)\leq\Phi_*-3\delta/8\).

5. Converting the gap to a positive rational divergence threshold

For inward pendant orientations, all positive payoffs have baseline mass \(p\).

For \(\lambda\neq1\), define
\[ S(s)=V+(\lambda-1)s,\qquad h(s)=\frac{1}{\lambda-1} \log_2\left(p^{1-\lambda}\frac{S(s)}{V^\lambda}\right). \]
Indeed, \(S(\Phi(x))=\sum_{i,b}x_{v_i^b}^{\lambda}\), so
\[ D_\lambda(x/V\|P)=h(\Phi(x)). \]
For \(\lambda=1\), define instead
\[ h(s)=\frac{s}{V\ln2}-\log_2(Vp). \]
Again, divergence equals \(h(\Phi(x))\).

In both cases, \(h\) is strictly increasing on its domain. Its derivatives are
\[ h'(s)=\frac{1}{\ln2\,S(s)}\quad(\lambda\neq1), \qquad h'(s)=\frac{1}{V\ln2}\quad(\lambda=1). \]

Let \(\eta_*=h(\Phi_*)\). For \(\lambda\neq1\), expanding the definitions gives
\[ S(\Phi_*)= n\bigl(B^\lambda+(2B)^\lambda\bigr) +\lambda B^{\lambda-1}(2M-C_*) +\lambda(2B)^{\lambda-1}C_*. \]
Because \(1\leq k\leq M\), we have \(0<C_*<2M\). Thus this expression is positive, and \(\eta_*\) is well-defined.

For any inward pendant orientation,
\[ S(\Phi(x))\leq2n(3B)^\lambda. \]
Also, using \(2M\leq n\Delta\leq nB\),
\[ S(\Phi_*)\leq u_\lambda nB^\lambda. \]
For \(\lambda=1\), the corresponding bound is
\(V\leq4nB\leq u_1nB\).

Since \(S(s)\) is affine, these upper bounds hold throughout the interval between \(\Phi_*\) and any orientation value. Therefore, on each such interval,
\[ h'(s)\geq\frac{1}{\ln2\,u_\lambda nB^\lambda}. \]
Integrating across the objective gaps proves separation from \(\eta_*\) by at least
\[ \frac{3\delta}{8\ln2\,u_\lambda nB^\lambda} = \frac{3d_\lambda}{8\ln2\,u_\lambda nB} > \frac{3}{K_\lambda nB}. \]

Set \(\varepsilon=1/(K_\lambda nB)\), and choose a dyadic rational \(\eta\) satisfying
\[ |\eta-\eta_*|\leq\varepsilon. \]
The separation then gives:

  • a yes Max-Cut instance has an orientation with divergence greater than \(\eta\);
  • a no Max-Cut instance has no inward pendant orientation with divergence at least \(\eta\).

Lemmas 3 and 4 extend the second statement to all admissible allocations.

Positivity of the threshold

Choose all active labels equal. Then \(c(b)=0\), and an orientation attaining \(C=M\) satisfies
\[ \Phi(x)\leq A+\delta(M+1/8)<\Phi_*. \]
Its normalized allocation is supported on the \(2n\) central types, whose total baseline mass is \(1/4\). If \(U\) is uniform on those types, then
\[ D_\lambda(x/V\|P)=2+D_\lambda(x/V\|U)\geq2. \]
The final inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality for powers, or for \(s\ln s\) at order one. Since \(h\) is increasing, \(\eta_*>2\). Our approximation consequently has \(\eta>0\).

Polynomial-time construction of the rational threshold

All quantities in the construction have polynomial magnitude for fixed \(\lambda\); in particular,
\[ B=K_\lambda n(\Delta+1)^2 \]
is polynomially bounded. The required accuracy \(\varepsilon\) is inverse polynomial.

For a fixed rational order \(\lambda=a/b\), rational powers can be enclosed to any requested binary precision by bisection for a fixed-degree \(b\)-th root. Logarithms can be enclosed by scaling their arguments into \([1,2]\) and using the convergent series
\[ \ln z= 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{y^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad y=\frac{z-1}{z+1}. \]
Here \(|y|\leq1/3\), so the truncation error decreases geometrically. The same series computes \(\ln2\).

The formulas defining \(\eta_*\) involve only a fixed number of these operations on polynomially bounded quantities. Certified interval evaluation with \(O(\log n)\) precision bits, including guard bits for error propagation, therefore suffices to construct \(\eta\) in polynomial time.

The resulting graph, weights, masses, and threshold all have polynomial encoding length.

6. Conclusion

The construction proves
\[ \operatorname{MaxCut}(H)\geq k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists\text{ admissible }x: D_\lambda(x/V\|P)\geq\eta. \]

Thus the stated cohort problem is NP-hard for every fixed rational \(\lambda>0\), including \(\lambda=1\) and all orders above one. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
