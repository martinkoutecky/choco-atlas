The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many institutions and a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type fixes vertical eligibility, a laminar horizontal eligibility set, preferences over institution-category contracts, and a merit tier at each institution, with capacities and reservation minima given as rational cohort shares, compute an assignment \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), eligibility, capacities, and reservations. Does a fractional generalized deferred-acceptance process always compute an assignment with no positive-mass blocking reassignment under the fractional hierarchical one-to-all choice rule and no unjustified envy under weak merit priorities, and what is its complexity in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), the number of horizontal types, and input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) complete applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(m=|S|\), \(k=|H|\), and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type specifies membership in \(\{g,SC,ST,OBC,EWS\}\), horizontal eligibility \(\rho(t)\subseteq H\), strict preferences over eligible institution-category contracts and the outside option \(\emptyset\), and a weak merit tier at each institution. General types qualify only for open contracts; membership in \(r\) permits open and category-\(r\) contracts. The distinct nonempty sets \(E_h=\{t:h\in\rho(t)\}\) form a laminar family.
For \(V=\{o,SC,ST,OBC,EWS\}\), capacities \(q_{sv}\) and horizontal minima \(\kappa_{svh}\) are nonnegative binary-rational cohort shares, with \(\sum_v q_{sv}=\bar q_s\leq1\). For every pairwise-disjoint subfamily \(D\subseteq H\), require \(\sum_{h\in D}\kappa_{svh}\leq q_{sv}\). Vertical capacities are hard; horizontal minima are demand-dependent guarantees implemented by the following choice rule.
Represent type \(t\) by the labelled interval \([0,\mu_t)\) with Lebesgue mass. At each institution, refine merit ties by support-list order, then increasing label, using the same refinement in every category. An assignment \(Y\) partitions these intervals into finitely many intervals with rational endpoints assigned to acceptable contracts or \(\emptyset\); their masses satisfy \(x_{t,a}\geq0\) and \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\).
On measurable offers, institution \(s\) processes categories in order \(o,SC,ST,OBC,EWS\), removing every selected label from all subsequent categories. Within each category, process horizontal sets from leaves upward: select the highest-priority remaining offered mass up to the residual minimum, available mass, and remaining capacity; subtract selected mass from capacity and every containing set's minimum, clipping minima at zero. After all horizontal sets, fill remaining capacity by priority. Every selected label counts toward all its horizontal eligibilities. Denote this aggregate choice by \(C_s\).
Stability means individual rationality, \(C_s(Y)=Y_s\) up to null sets, and no positive-mass measurable coalition offering one strictly preferred alternative per participating label whose new contracts all belong to \(C_s(Y\cup Z)\). Incumbent contracts remain in this choice comparison. The fractional cumulative-offer process starts empty. Each round, every unheld label with an untried acceptable contract offers its favorite such contract; each institution recomputes \(C_s\) on all offers received so far. Stop when no positive mass can offer.

Does this process always terminate with a stable assignment eliminating justified envy? Is computing such an assignment, or deciding that none exists, polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite applicants with strict merit priorities, cumulative offers under these hierarchical one-to-all choices are stable, strategy-proof, and eliminate justified envy. [Discrete result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.09189).
General continuum models establish stable matching existence under continuous, convex institutional choice; no result settling the exact computation question above was located. [Continuum result](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA13547).

Partial solution: termination, existence, and an exact search bound

The stated cumulative-offer process always terminates with a stable assignment eliminating justified envy. Consequently, on the promised valid-input domain, an appropriate assignment always exists.

I do not settle whether finding such an assignment is polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard for general laminar horizontal reservations. I prove an additional search bound:
$$ 2^{O(m\tau(k+1))}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$
After merging duplicate eligibility sets, this improves to
$$ 2^{O(m\tau^2)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$
Thus the search problem is polynomial-time solvable when $m$ and $\tau$ are fixed, regardless of the sizes of the input denominators.

The proof also establishes polynomial-time verification, an exponential lower bound on the number of literal offer rounds, and polynomial-time computation when $k=0$.

1. Notation and normalization

A contract $a=(s,v)$ is acceptable to type $t$ if it is eligible and strictly preferred to $\emptyset$. Let $\mathcal A_t$ be the acceptable contracts of type $t$. Each type has at most two eligible categories at each institution, so
$$ |\mathcal A_t|\leq 2m. $$

For a fixed contract $a=(s,v)$, let $F_a$ be the single-category choice rule: process the horizontal sets from leaves upward, and then fill the remaining capacity by priority. Unlike $C_s$, this rule does not remove labels selected in other vertical categories.

Discard empty horizontal eligibility sets. Merge horizontal categories having the same nonempty eligibility set, replacing their minima, separately for each $a$, by their maximum. This preserves choice: a selection reduces every identical set’s residual minimum, so processing all identical sets selects exactly the additional mass required by their largest minimum. Disjoint sets do not affect this calculation.

Write $\mathcal F$ for the resulting laminar family of distinct nonempty subsets of the support. These sets form an inclusion forest.

For a node $A\in\mathcal F$, and a fixed contract $a$, define recursively
$$ M_A=\max\left\{\kappa_{aA}, \sum_{B\text{ child of }A}M_B\right\}. $$

Feasibility claim. The horizontal stages together select at most $q_a$.

To prove this, first observe that $M_A$ is the maximum sum of minima over a disjoint subfamily in the subtree rooted at $A$. A disjoint subfamily either contains $A$, or is assembled from disjoint subfamilies in its child subtrees. These children are pairwise disjoint. This proves the recursion, including the leaf case.

It follows from the input hypothesis that
$$ \sum_{A\text{ root}}M_A\leq q_a. $$

Now temporarily run the horizontal stages without capacity clipping. If the child subtrees of $A$ have selected total mass $u$, the stage for $A$ adds at most
$$ (\kappa_{aA}-u)_+. $$
Consequently, the subtree selects at most
$$ u+(\kappa_{aA}-u)_+ =\max\{u,\kappa_{aA}\} \leq M_A. $$
Induction proves the claim. In particular, the capacity clipping in the stated horizontal procedure does not prevent any horizontal selection.

2. Choice properties used below

2.1. Finite substitutes

I use the following finite theorem: with strict priorities and laminar horizontal eligibility, the single-category hierarchical rule satisfies substitutes. Thus, for finite menus $A'\subseteq A$,
$$ F_a(A)\cap A'\subseteq F_a(A'). $$
This is Proposition 2 of [Aygün–Turhan, *Affirmative Action in India with Hierarchical Reservations*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.09189).

Its hypotheses apply to the finite instances constructed below: the priorities are strict, eligibility is laminar, quotas are integral after scaling, and the reservation stages are feasible by Section 1. In particular, an offer rejected from a finite menu remains rejected when that menu grows.

Only the single-category substitutes property is needed. I do not assume that aggregate choice satisfies substitutes on arbitrary contract menus.

2.2. Removing rejected offers

Lemma 1. For either $F_a$ or $C_s$, if
$$ C(A)\subseteq B\subseteq A, $$
then
$$ C(B)=C(A). $$
For measurable menus, inclusions and equalities are understood up to null sets.

Proof. Compare the two computations stage by stage. Every selection made during the computation on $A$ belongs to its final choice and therefore remains in $B$.

Suppose all preceding selections agree. Then remaining capacities, residual minima, and removals caused by preceding stages or categories agree. The current stage’s originally selected offers remain available. Removing other offers cannot alter its priority selection: if the quota binds, the original highest-priority mass remains; if it does not bind, every available eligible offer was selected. The selections at this stage therefore agree.

Induction through all stages proves the result. For measurable menus, the same argument applies to priority truncation by mass, with equality up to null sets. $\square$

2.3. Priority and horizontal dominance

Lemma 2. In a finite single-category menu, suppose applicants $i,j$ are offered, $i$ has higher refined priority, and
$$ \rho(i)\supseteq\rho(j). $$
Then
$$ j\in F_a(A)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad i\in F_a(A). $$

Proof. Suppose $j$ is selected but $i$ is never selected. At the stage selecting $j$, applicant $i$ remains available. At a horizontal stage, the eligibility containment makes $i$ eligible for that stage. At the final fill stage, both are eligible. Priority selection cannot select $j$ while leaving $i$ unselected. $\square$

We also need a measurable version. Suppose positive offered mass of type $t$ remains unselected, while positive mass of type $t'$ is selected, and every label of $t$ precedes every label of $t'$ in priority. If
$$ \rho(t)\supseteq\rho(t'), $$
this is impossible. Indeed, finitely many stages select the mass of $t'$, so one stage selects positive mass of it. The unselected mass of $t$ is still available and eligible at that stage, and priority truncation cannot leave it behind while selecting positive mass of $t'$.

2.4. Disjoint independent choices reproduce aggregate choice

Lemma 3. Fix the menus of all categories at an institution. If their independently selected label sets are pairwise disjoint, then aggregate choice selects exactly those independent choices.

Proof. Process categories in the specified order. The first category agrees with its independent computation. Suppose preceding categories agree. They remove from the current category only labels outside its independent selected set, because the independent selected label sets are disjoint. Lemma 1 therefore preserves the current category’s independent choice. Induction proves the assertion. $\square$

3. Exact discretization and termination

Let $D$ be the least common multiple of all denominators appearing in the masses, capacities, and minima. Since its logarithm is bounded by the sum of the logarithms of those denominators,
$$ \log D=O(L). $$

Partition each type interval into cells of mass $1/D$:
$$ I_{t,j}=[j/D,(j+1)/D), \qquad 0\leq j<D\mu_t. $$
There are exactly $D$ cells in total.

Treat each cell as one finite applicant. Its preferences and eligibility are those of its type. Its institution priority is determined by merit tier, support-list order, and then cell index. This is strict and agrees across an institution’s categories.

Multiplying quotas and minima by $D$ makes them integral. Whenever offers consist of whole cells, every choice stage selects whole cells: all available masses and residual quotas are multiples of $1/D$, and priority truncation respects cell boundaries. Thus the discretization is exact.

First run the finite cumulative-offer process using the independent choices $F_a$. Their menus only grow, so substitutes makes every rejection permanent. An applicant makes a new offer only while unheld. Therefore, whenever it advances, all its previous offers remain rejected. Each applicant can consequently be held by at most one independent category.

The independent selected label sets are thus disjoint after every round. Lemma 3 shows that aggregate choice agrees with independent choice after that round. Induction identifies this independent process with the process stated in the question.

Every nonempty round introduces at least one untried cell-contract pair. Each cell has at most $2m$ acceptable contracts. Hence the process terminates after at most
$$ 2mD $$
nonempty rounds.

Throughout the process, held contracts form an assignment: no label is held twice, and every vertical capacity is respected.

4. Stability and elimination of justified envy

4.1. Stability against arbitrary measurable coalitions

Let $A_s$ be the terminal cumulative menu and
$$ Y_s=C_s(A_s). $$
Define the full preferred-alternative menu
$$ B_s= \{(\ell,s,v):(s,v)\text{ is acceptable and }(s,v)\succ_\ell Y(\ell)\}. $$

Every contract strictly preferred to a label’s terminal assignment was previously offered. To see this, a held cell’s final contract is its latest offer, because rejection is permanent. An unheld cell has exhausted its acceptable list at termination. Offers follow preference order in both cases.

Consequently,
$$ Y_s\subseteq Y_s\cup B_s\subseteq A_s. $$
Lemma 1 gives
$$ C_s(Y_s)=Y_s \qquad\text{and}\qquad C_s(Y_s\cup B_s)=Y_s. $$

Only acceptable contracts were offered, so individual rationality holds.

Now consider any permitted measurable blocking coalition $Z$. Its contracts satisfy $Z_s\subseteq B_s$. Therefore,
$$ Y_s\subseteq Y_s\cup Z_s\subseteq Y_s\cup B_s. $$
Applying Lemma 1 again gives
$$ C_s(Y_s\cup Z_s)=Y_s. $$
Thus no positive mass of the coalition’s new contracts is selected.

This argument allows arbitrary measurable coalitions, including coalitions that split the discretization cells.

4.2. Justified envy

Suppose positive masses of types $t,t'$ receive respectively $a$ and $b=(s,v)$, with
$$ b\succ_t a, \qquad \rho(t)\supseteq\rho(t'), $$
and $t$ has strictly higher merit than $t'$ at $s$.

The terminal assignment consists of whole cells, so choose cells $i,j$ from these respective masses. Applicant $i$ offered $b$, since $b$ is preferred to its terminal assignment. Its offer is rejected from the final independent menu for $b$, whereas $j$ is selected there.

Strict merit superiority gives $i$ higher refined priority. Lemma 2 contradicts this rejection. Hence justified envy is eliminated.

4.3. A polynomial-size representation

Within a type interval, a smaller-labelled cell cannot finish with a worse assignment than a larger-labelled cell. Otherwise, the former offered the latter’s final contract and was rejected there. The two cells have identical horizontal eligibility, and the smaller-labelled cell has higher priority. Lemma 2 excludes this.

Therefore each type’s assignments occur consecutively in descending preference order, with $\emptyset$ last. Each acceptable contract occupies at most one interval.

There are at most
$$ \tau(2m+1) $$
intervals. Every endpoint is a multiple of $1/D$ and needs $O(L)$ bits. The terminal assignment thus has a polynomial-size rational interval representation.

In particular, an appropriate assignment exists on every valid input. Deciding whether none exists has the constant answer “an assignment exists” on this promised domain.

5. Polynomial-time verification

The following criterion also repairs the omitted implication identified in the notes.

Proposition. Suppose a rational interval assignment $Y$ is individually rational and satisfies
$$ C_s(Y_s)=Y_s $$
for every institution. Then it is stable exactly when
$$ C_s(Y_s\cup B_s)=Y_s $$
for every institution, where $B_s$ is its full preferred-alternative menu.

Proof. Sufficiency follows from the intermediate-menu argument in Section 4.

For necessity, put
$$ T_s=C_s(Y_s\cup B_s). $$
Suppose $T_s\neq Y_s$ up to null sets. If $T_s$ contained no positive mass of new contracts, then
$$ T_s\subseteq Y_s\subseteq Y_s\cup B_s $$
up to null sets. Lemma 1 would imply
$$ C_s(Y_s)=T_s, $$
contradicting the fixed-point assumption. Thus $T_s$ selects positive mass of new contracts.

Let
$$ W_s=T_s\setminus Y_s. $$
Aggregate choice selects at most one contract per label, so $W_s$ offers one strictly preferred alternative per participating label. Moreover,
$$ T_s\subseteq Y_s\cup W_s\subseteq Y_s\cup B_s. $$
Lemma 1 implies
$$ C_s(Y_s\cup W_s)=T_s. $$
Thus every new contract in $W_s$ is selected, and $W_s$ is a positive-mass blocking coalition at institution $s$. $\square$

This criterion is polynomial-time checkable in the input and certificate lengths. Preferred-alternative menus are finite rational interval menus. Each institution has at most $5(k+1)$ selection stages. A priority truncation introduces at most one new cutoff endpoint, and subsequent interval subtraction and rational arithmetic remain polynomial in size and bit length.

Justified envy is separately checkable by scanning pairs of support types and their positive assigned masses, then testing preferences, eligibility, strict merit, and horizontal containment.

6. Exponentially many literal offer rounds

This lower bound already holds without horizontal reservations.

Let $N=2^b$ for $b\geq1$, and set
$$ \varepsilon=\frac1{2N}. $$
Take two general types $x,y$, each of mass $1/2$, and two institutions $A,B$, each with open capacity
$$ q_A=q_B=(N-1)\varepsilon. $$
Their preferences are
$$ A\succ_x B\succ_x\emptyset, \qquad B\succ_y A\succ_y\emptyset. $$
At $A$, type $y$ has strictly higher merit; at $B$, type $x$ has strictly higher merit.

For $1\leq r\leq N$, after offer round $r$, institution $A$ holds:

  • the suffix of type $y$ of mass $(r-1)\varepsilon$;
  • the prefix of type $x$ of mass $(N-r)\varepsilon$.

Institution $B$ holds the symmetric allocation.

In round one, each institution retains the first $N-1$ cells of its first-choice type. Suppose the formula holds at round $r<N$. The newly rejected first-choice cell of each type offers its second choice. Its destination gives that type higher merit, accepts the cell, and rejects one additional first-choice cell. This proves the formula for round $r+1$.

After round $N$, each institution holds its higher-merit type’s suffix
$$ [\varepsilon,1/2). $$
Each type’s first cell is unheld and still has its second contract untried.

In round $N+1$, those cells offer. Each institution then selects its higher-merit type’s prefix
$$ [0,(N-1)\varepsilon). $$
The rejected last cells have exhausted both contracts. The process stops.

There are exactly $N+1$ nonempty rounds, while the input length is $\Theta(b)$. This proves an exponential round count. It does not prove hardness of finding an assignment by another algorithm.

7. A finite-dimensional sufficient system

This section constructs the system used for the new search bound.

Let
$$ \mathcal E=\{(t,a):a\in\mathcal A_t\}. $$
Introduce variables $x_{t,a}$ satisfying
$$ x_{t,a}\geq0, \qquad \sum_{a\in\mathcal A_t}x_{t,a}\leq\mu_t. $$
The remaining mass is assigned to $\emptyset$.

Realize these masses canonically: within each type interval, put acceptable contracts consecutively in descending preference order, followed by $\emptyset$. For an acceptable $a$, define
$$ p_{t,a}=\sum_{\substack{b\in\mathcal A_t\\b\succ_t a}}x_{t,b}. $$
Its incumbent labels at $a$ are
$$ [p_{t,a},p_{t,a}+x_{t,a}). $$
The incumbent labels together with all labels preferring $a$ to their assignment form exactly
$$ [p_{t,a},\mu_t). $$
Thus the offered mass of type $t$ in this menu is
$$ u_{t,a}=\mu_t-p_{t,a}. $$
Set $u_{t,a}=0$ when $(t,a)\notin\mathcal E$.

For each $a$, define $\Phi_a(u)$ to be the vector of selected masses when $F_a$ receives offered masses $u_t$. This vector depends only on the masses: support-order refinement puts all labels of one type consecutively in the institution’s priority order, and eligibility is constant within a type.

When each offered type set is a suffix, selections from that type form a prefix of its offered suffix. Indeed, each stage selects the smallest remaining labels of that type. The final selected interval therefore has length $\Phi_a(u)_t$ and begins at the suffix’s initial endpoint.

Consider the equations
$$ x_{t,a}=\Phi_a(u_{\cdot,a})_t \qquad ((t,a)\in\mathcal E), $$
together with the mass inequalities above.

7.1. Every solution gives an appropriate assignment

Suppose $x$ satisfies this system, and let $Y$ be its canonical labelled realization. Write $M_a$ for the suffix menu just described. The prefix property and the equations imply
$$ F_a(M_a)=Y_a. $$

For an institution $s$, the union of its menus $M_{(s,v)}$ is precisely $Y_s\cup B_s$. The independent choices are the incumbent sets $Y_{(s,v)}$, which have disjoint label sets. Lemma 3 gives
$$ C_s(Y_s\cup B_s)=Y_s. $$
Lemma 1 then gives $C_s(Y_s)=Y_s$, and the criterion in Section 5 proves stability.

For envy elimination, suppose positive mass of type $t$ receives a contract worse than $a=(s,v)$, while positive mass of type $t'$ receives $a$, and the stated merit and horizontal dominance conditions hold. The worse-assigned mass of $t$ belongs to $M_a$ and remains unselected, while positive mass of $t'$ is selected. Strict merit ranks every label of $t$ ahead of every label of $t'$. The measurable version of Lemma 2 gives a contradiction.

Thus every rational solution of the system gives a stable rational interval assignment eliminating justified envy.

7.2. The system is nonempty

Take the terminal assignment from Sections 3–4. By Section 4.3, it is already canonical.

Let $A_a$ be its final independent cumulative menu. Its suffix menu $M_a$ satisfies
$$ Y_a\subseteq M_a\subseteq A_a. $$
The right inclusion holds because every preferred alternative and every incumbent contract was offered. Since $F_a(A_a)=Y_a$, Lemma 1 gives
$$ F_a(M_a)=Y_a. $$
Its masses therefore solve the system.

This proves nonemptiness without assuming that the sufficient system describes every stable assignment.

8. Exact search by linear-program branch enumeration

8.1. A minimum/maximum circuit for choice

Let $r=|\mathcal F|$. Fix a category $a$, and initialize selected amounts $f_t=0$.

At the stage for a horizontal node $A$, compute its residual minimum
$$ d=\max\left\{0,\kappa_{aA}-\sum_{t\in A}f_t\right\} $$
and remaining capacity
$$ c=q_a-\sum_t f_t. $$

The residual formula is exact. Previously processed horizontal nodes are either descendants of $A$ or disjoint from it, so the displayed sum counts precisely the previous selections that reduce $A$’s minimum.

Visit eligible types in priority order. For each $t\in A$, select
$$ z=\min\{u_t-f_t,d,c\}, $$
then update
$$ f_t\leftarrow f_t+z, \qquad d\leftarrow d-z, \qquad c\leftarrow c-z. $$
After all horizontal nodes, visit all eligible types in priority order and use
$$ z=\min\{u_t-f_t,c\} $$
to fill remaining capacity.

This computes $\Phi_a$ exactly. It uses linear arithmetic and
$$ O(\tau(r+1)) $$
binary minimum/maximum operations. Across at most $5m$ categories, there are
$$ R=O(m\tau(r+1)) $$
such operations.

8.2. Branches are rational linear feasibility problems

Introduce a variable for each intermediate circuit quantity. Replace a binary minimum
$$ y=\min\{a,b\} $$
by either
$$ y=a,\quad a\leq b, $$
or
$$ y=b,\quad b\leq a. $$
Replace a maximum similarly, reversing the inequalities. Equal arguments may use either branch.

After selecting one branch for each of the $R$ operations, every circuit equation is linear. Add the input equations
$$ u_{t,a} =\mu_t-\sum_{\substack{b\in\mathcal A_t\\b\succ_t a}}x_{t,b}, $$
the final equations $f_{t,a}=x_{t,a}$, and the mass inequalities.

Each branch is a rational linear feasibility problem of polynomial size. Rational linear programming decides feasibility and returns a rational feasible point in polynomial bit complexity. Using intermediate variables keeps the coefficient encoding polynomial: constraints consist of linear sums and differences and the original rational constants.

There are at most $2^R$ branches. Section 7.2 guarantees that some branch is feasible. Section 7.1 guarantees that a feasible point yields an appropriate assignment.

Hence an exact search algorithm runs in
$$ 2^{O(m\tau(r+1))} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$
In particular, since $r\leq k$, this gives
$$ 2^{O(m\tau(k+1))} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$

This algorithm finds an appropriate assignment; it does not claim to reproduce the particular terminal cumulative-offer outcome.

8.3. Bounding the normalized family by support size

A laminar family of distinct nonempty subsets of a $\tau$-element set has at most $2\tau-1$ members.

To prove this, append all singleton sets and the full support set, preserving laminarity. The resulting inclusion tree has exactly $\tau$ singleton leaves. Every nonsingleton node has at least two children: its maximal proper members cover it because singleton sets are present, and one proper child cannot cover the whole node.

If there are $I$ internal nodes, the tree has $I+\tau-1$ edges and at least $2I$ edges. Therefore
$$ I+\tau-1\geq2I, \qquad I\leq\tau-1. $$
The augmented tree, and hence the original family, has at most $2\tau-1$ nodes.

Substitution yields the sharper bound
$$ 2^{O(m\tau^2)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$
The original $k$ still appears in the polynomial preprocessing cost for reading and merging horizontal categories.

9. Polynomial-time computation when $k=0$

Use the classical bipartite stable-allocation model with:

  • one type vertex $t$ of quota $\mu_t$;
  • one category vertex $a=(s,v)$ of quota $q_a$;
  • an edge $(t,a)$ for each eligible acceptable contract;
  • edge capacity $\min\{\mu_t,q_a\}$.

Type vertices rank edges by contract preferences. Category vertices rank types by merit tier and support-list order. Both rankings are strict.

The model has at most $\tau+5m$ vertices and $2m\tau$ edges. Stable allocation with rational vertex quotas, edge capacities, and strict preferences has a strongly polynomial algorithm; unmatched quantities and vacancies can be represented using least-preferred dummy vertices. These hypotheses and the treatment of nonintegral data are given in [Dean–Munshi, *Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems*, Sections 1–3](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Let $x$ be a stable allocation. For an eligible acceptable edge $(t,a)$, say that $t$ wants additional mass at $a$ if it has positive unmatched mass or positive allocation to a contract worse than $a$.

Claim. If $t$ wants additional mass at $a$, then $a$ is full and holds no positive allocation to a type ranked below $t$.

If the edge is unsaturated, a vacancy or positive lower-ranked allocation at $a$ would allow an increase along that edge, improving both endpoints. That is a stable-allocation blocking edge.

If the edge is saturated at $\mu_t$, all type mass is assigned to $a$, contradicting its wanting additional mass. If it is saturated at $q_a$, the category is full entirely with type $t$. This proves the claim in every case.

Realize $x$ canonically as in Section 7. Every newcomer in $M_a\setminus Y_a$ comes from a type wanting additional mass at $a$. The claim says that every incumbent’s type ranks at least as high as that newcomer’s type. For the same type, incumbent labels are smaller, because its interval assigned to $a$ precedes every interval assigned to a worse contract.

If there is positive newcomer mass, the category is full and all incumbents precede all newcomers in refined priority. If there is no newcomer mass, the menu consists only of incumbents. In either case, ordinary priority choice gives
$$ F_a(M_a)=Y_a. $$
The sufficient-system argument in Section 7.1 now proves stability and envy elimination for the required aggregate choice.

Thus the requested assignment can be computed in polynomial time when $k=0$.

10. Why a direct polymatroid application is insufficient

There is a strongly polynomial algorithm for stable allocation in intersections of ordered polymatroids. Its institutional choice maximizes a fixed priority order over a fixed polymatroid, subject to offered-coordinate bounds; see [Iwata–Yokoi, *Finding a Stable Allocation in Polymatroid Intersection*](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf).

The horizontal rule cannot, in general, be represented in that form on the original type coordinates.

Consider one category of capacity $1/2$ and two types $A,B$, each of mass $1/2$. Type $A$ alone has horizontal eligibility, with minimum $1/4$, and $B$ has higher merit.

From either one-type menu, the rule selects all offered mass $1/2$. Thus any fixed polymatroid representing this rule must permit both vectors
$$ (1/2,0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (0,1/2). $$
From the menu offering both full type masses, the required choice is
$$ (1/4,1/4). $$

But ordered-polymatroid greedy choice processes one coordinate first. That coordinate can receive $1/2$, as its one-type vector is feasible and its offered bound is $1/2$. Therefore the greedy output cannot be $(1/4,1/4)$, regardless of which coordinate comes first.

This excludes the direct representation on type coordinates. It does not exclude a reduction with additional coordinates, a different polynomial algorithm, or a hardness result.
