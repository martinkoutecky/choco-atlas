The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a unit-mass, atomless society of delegates and budget \(B\), choose measurable efforts \(x(t)\in[0,1/2]\) satisfying \(\int x(t)^\beta\,d\mu(t)\le B\), with each delegate’s voting power proportional to effort. What is the maximum limiting probability of electing the correct outcome when individual voting errors are independent and no delegate retains positive voting weight?

Define its limiting election by sampling independent delegates \(T_1,T_2,\ldots\sim\mu\). Conditional on these delegates, votes are independent, and delegate \(i\) votes correctly with probability \(1/2+x(T_i)\).
In the election with \(N\) delegates, voting weights are \(w_{Ni}=x(T_i)/\sum_{j=1}^{N}x(T_j)\). Weighted majority decides, with a fair random tie-break; if total effort is zero, choose either outcome with equal probability.

Result

For the precisely defined atomless model, \(V(B,\beta)=1\) for every \(B>0\) and \(\beta>1\). Consequently, every permitted input with \(q\in[0,1]\) is a yes-instance, and the decision problem is polynomial-time solvable.

A positive constant effort already proves this. To address heterogeneous profiles as well, we prove the stronger statement that every measurable profile with positive effort on a set of positive measure is admissible and has limiting success probability \(1\).

1. Admissibility of every measurable profile

Fix a measurable \(x:[0,1]\to[0,1/2]\). Write \(X_i=x(T_i)\), \(S_N=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i\), and \(a=\int x\,d\mu\). The variables \(X_i\) are independent and identically distributed, nonnegative, and bounded.

Suppose first that \(a>0\). The strong law of large numbers applies because the variables are integrable, and gives \(S_N/N\to a\) almost surely. Thus, almost surely, for all sufficiently large \(N\), \(S_N\ge Na/2>0\). For those \(N\),

\(\displaystyle \max_{i\le N}w_{Ni}=\frac{\max_{i\le N}X_i}{S_N}\le\frac{1/2}{Na/2}=\frac{1}{Na}\longrightarrow0.\)

If \(a=0\), nonnegativity implies \(x=0\) almost everywhere. Each \(X_i\) is therefore zero almost surely. Taking the intersection of these countably many probability-one events, all sampled efforts are zero almost surely, so all weights are zero by convention.

Hence every measurable profile in the prescribed effort range satisfies admissibility.

2. Exact limiting success probability

Let \(C_i\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether delegate \(i\) votes correctly. We may realize one infinite sequence of votes with the stated conditional law; its first \(N\) votes have the required distribution for the election of size \(N\).

Define the signed, unnormalized voting contribution \(Z_i=X_i(2C_i-1)\). The pairs \((X_i,C_i)\) are independent and identically distributed: conditional independence of the votes makes their joint conditional law a product, and integrating against the independent delegate samples preserves this product structure.

Since \(\mathbb P(C_i=1\mid T_i)=1/2+X_i\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[Z_i\mid T_i]=X_i\bigl(2(1/2+X_i)-1\bigr)=2X_i^2.\)

Consequently, \(\mathbb E Z_i=2b\), where \(b=\int x^2\,d\mu\).

Suppose \(\mu(\{t:x(t)>0\})>0\). Then \(a>0\) and \(b>0\), since a nonnegative measurable function has zero integral only when it vanishes almost everywhere. Also, \(|Z_i|\le1/2\), so the strong law applies and yields

\(\displaystyle \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_i\longrightarrow2b>0\qquad\text{almost surely}.\)

Whenever \(S_N>0\), the correct outcome’s voting weight minus the incorrect outcome’s voting weight equals

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{Ni}(2C_i-1)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_i}{S_N}.\)

Thus \(\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_i>0\) guarantees that the correct outcome wins strictly. If total effort is zero, this signed sum is zero. Including ties and the zero-effort convention, we therefore have

\(\displaystyle 0\le1-P_N(x)\le\mathbb P\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_i\le0\right).\)

The indicator of the event on the right converges almost surely to zero by the strong law. It is bounded by \(1\), so bounded convergence shows that its probability tends to zero. Hence \(P_N(x)\to1\).

If \(x=0\) almost everywhere, every sampled effort is zero almost surely, and the prescribed random choice gives \(P_N(x)=1/2\) for every \(N\).

We have therefore proved the complete characterization: the limiting success probability is \(1/2\) for profiles that vanish almost everywhere, and \(1\) for every other profile.

3. A feasible profile attaining the optimum

Set \(c=\min\{B,1/2\}\) and choose the constant profile \(x(t)=c\). Because \(B>0\), we have \(0<c\le1/2<1\). Since \(\beta>1\),

\(\displaystyle \int x(t)^\beta\,d\mu(t)=c^\beta\le c\le B.\)

The profile is feasible. Its weights are exactly \(w_{Ni}=1/N\), so it is admissible. Section 2 proves that its success probability tends to \(1\).

Since no success probability exceeds \(1\), this proves \(V(B,\beta)=1\). The optimum is attained by a fixed profile; no exchange of the supremum and the limit is needed.
