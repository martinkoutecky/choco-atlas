Theorem 1 (Main Theorem) — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many agent types and job types with binary-rational masses and strict rankings over acceptable opposite-side types, represent each type by an atomless cohort. Does there exist a measurable, measure-preserving partial bijection between the two populations such that no feasible rival matching is preferred by more total population mass and no rival is preferred by more agent mass; if so, find one maximizing matched mass? Each person compares their own assignments in the two matchings, with unmatched last and equal assignments abstaining.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I\) and \(J\) be disjoint finite sets of agent and job types, with mutual acceptability graph \(E\subseteq I\times J\). Each type strictly ranks its acceptable opposite-side types; every agent type has at least one acceptable job type.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Write \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Masses sum to \(1\) across both sides; the two sides need not have equal mass.
Represent each type of mass \(r\) by a disjoint Lebesgue interval of length \(r\). A feasible matching is a measurable, measure-preserving bijection between measurable subsets of the agent and job populations, with measurable inverse, using only acceptable type pairs; maps are identified up to null sets.
People compare assignments by partner type, with unmatched strictly last. Partners of the same type are indifferent, and equal assignments abstain. For matchings \(M,N\), let \(\phi(M,N)\) be the total mass strictly preferring \(M\), and \(\phi_I(M,N)\) the agent mass strictly preferring \(M\).
Call \(M\) fully popular if, for every feasible rival \(N\), both \(\phi(M,N)\geq\phi(N,M)\) and \(\phi_I(M,N)\geq\phi_I(N,M)\). Rivals may use every acceptable pair and may leave people unmatched. Matched mass is the measure of the matched agent subset, counting each pair once.
Is deciding whether a fully popular matching exists, and finding one of maximum matched mass when it does, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?
For finite individuals with strict preferences, fully popular matching existence and maximum cardinality are solvable in linear time ([Kavitha](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05488)).
For finite individuals, maximum-utility popular mixed matchings are computable in polynomial time; these are lotteries evaluated by expected votes ([Huang–Kavitha](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.2020.1063)). This differs from personwise voting against arbitrary cohort rearrangements. No result settling the exact cohort problem was located.

  • Normalized combined population mass to one, preserving relative side masses rather than normalizing each side separately.
  • Used disjoint Lebesgue intervals as canonical atomless cohorts and identified matchings up to null sets.
  • Interpreted assignment equality by partner type: changing partners within one type produces indifference.

Status and new results

The general polynomial-time versus hardness question remains unresolved here. I prove two tractable cases and a structural result that applies to arbitrary instances:

  • If the acceptability graph is a forest, existence and maximum matched mass are computable in polynomial time in $m,\tau,L$.
  • If all positive-mass types have equal mass, the problem reduces exactly to the finite strict-preference problem and is therefore polynomial-time solvable.
  • In every instance admitting a fully popular matching, a maximum-mass fully popular matching can be chosen so that its positive support consists of disjoint paths, each with a saturated endpoint. Its positive edge masses are alternating sums of input masses.

The proof below restates and proves the ingredients needed from the notes.

1. Aggregate matchings and exact rival optimization

Write $V=I\cup J$, and let $r_v>0$ be the mass of type $v$. An aggregate matching is a vector in

$$ P=\left\{ x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}: \sum_{e\ni v}x_e\leq r_v \text{ for every }v\in V \right\}. $$

Define its unmatched masses and matched mass by

$$ q_v(x)=r_v-\sum_{e\ni v}x_e, \qquad T(x)=\sum_{e\in E}x_e. $$

Every $x\in P$ has a measurable realization: partition each type interval into pieces of lengths $x_e$ for its incident edges and one piece of length $q_v(x)$, then pair corresponding equal-length pieces by translations.

We also need to rearrange measurable pieces of an existing matching. Two measurable subsets of Lebesgue intervals with the same positive measure admit a measure-preserving measurable bijection, with measurable inverse, modulo null sets. To see this, for a set $A$ of measure $h$, use the cumulative function

$$ F_A(t)=\lambda(A\cap(-\infty,t]). $$

This continuous nondecreasing function identifies restricted Lebesgue measure on $A$ with Lebesgue measure on an interval of length $h$. Its nontrivial constant intervals have zero restricted measure; there are countably many such intervals, and their endpoints also form a null set. After removing these exceptional sets, the generalized inverse gives the inverse map. Applying this construction to both sets gives the required bijection. In particular, a measurable assignment set can be divided into any finite prescribed list of nonnegative masses summing to its measure.

Let $\bot$ denote unmatched. For a type $v$, define

$$ \operatorname{vote}_v(p,q)= \begin{cases} 1,&p\text{ is strictly preferred to }q,\\ 0,&p=q,\\ -1,&q\text{ is strictly preferred to }p. \end{cases} $$

Here every acceptable partner type is preferred to $\bot$.

Fix a matching with aggregate vector $x$. Its positive current-assignment buckets are:

  • agent buckets $(i,p)$, with mass $h_{i,p}=x_{ip}$ when $p\neq\bot$, and mass $h_{i,\bot}=q_i(x)$;
  • job buckets $(j,q)$, with mass $k_{j,q}=x_{qj}$ when $q\neq\bot$, and mass $k_{j,\bot}=q_j(x)$.

Only buckets of positive mass are retained.

For $\eta\in\{0,1\}$, count agent votes and $\eta$ times job votes. For every acceptable pair $(i,j)$ and every retained bucket pair $(i,p),(j,q)$, introduce a rival-pairing variable $y_{i,p;j,q}$. Introduce variables $z_{i,p}$ and $w_{j,q}$ for rival-unmatched mass. The constraints are

$$ \sum_{j,q:(i,j)\in E}y_{i,p;j,q}+z_{i,p}=h_{i,p}, $$

$$ \sum_{i,p:(i,j)\in E}y_{i,p;j,q}+w_{j,q}=k_{j,q}, $$

with all variables nonnegative. Maximize

$$ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{i,p;j,q} \bigl( \operatorname{vote}_i(j,p) +\eta\operatorname{vote}_j(i,q) \bigr)y_{i,p;j,q}\\ &\quad+ \sum_{i,p}\operatorname{vote}_i(\bot,p)z_{i,p} +\eta\sum_{j,q}\operatorname{vote}_j(\bot,q)w_{j,q}. \end{aligned} $$

Exactness. Every measurable rival induces a feasible solution by measuring its pairings between buckets. Conversely, partition each bucket according to a feasible solution and pair the prescribed equal-measure pieces. The measurable-bijection fact above realizes these pairings, with no additional consistency requirement. The objective is exactly the rival’s voting margin.

The original matching gives objective zero. Consequently, the matching is fully popular precisely when both programs, for $\eta=0$ and $\eta=1$, have optimum zero.

These programs have polynomial size: at most $2m+\tau$ buckets and at most $m(\tau+1)^2$ pairing variables. Thus an arbitrary rational proposal can be checked by exact rational linear programming in time polynomial in the society’s encoding length and the proposal’s encoding length.

2. Bounded witnesses and dependence on support

For fixed $\eta$, the dual assigns potentials $\alpha_{i,p}$ and $\beta_{j,q}$ to the retained buckets. Its constraints are

$$ \alpha_{i,p}+\beta_{j,q} \geq \operatorname{vote}_i(j,p) +\eta\operatorname{vote}_j(i,q) $$

for every acceptable bucket pairing, and

$$ \alpha_{i,p}\geq\operatorname{vote}_i(\bot,p), \qquad \beta_{j,q}\geq\eta\operatorname{vote}_j(\bot,q). $$

The objective is

$$ \sum_{i,p}h_{i,p}\alpha_{i,p} + \sum_{j,q}k_{j,q}\beta_{j,q}. $$

Witness lemma. Popularity for voting rule $\eta$ is equivalent to dual-feasible potentials satisfying

$$ \alpha_{i,j}+\beta_{j,i}=0 \quad\text{whenever }x_{ij}>0, $$

$$ \alpha_{i,\bot}=0 \quad\text{whenever }q_i(x)>0, \qquad \beta_{j,\bot}=0 \quad\text{whenever }q_j(x)>0. $$

The potentials may be chosen integral, with ranges

$$ \begin{array}{c|c|c} &\text{agent potentials}&\text{job potentials}\\ \hline \eta=1&\{-1,0,1\}&\{-1,0,1\}\\ \eta=0&\{-1,0\}&\{0,1\}. \end{array} $$

Proof. The rival program is feasible and bounded: all variables are nonnegative and bounded by their bucket totals. Strong linear-programming duality therefore supplies an optimal dual solution.

Regroup its objective as

$$ \sum_{(i,j):x_{ij}>0} x_{ij}(\alpha_{i,j}+\beta_{j,i}) + \sum_{i:q_i(x)>0}q_i(x)\alpha_{i,\bot} + \sum_{j:q_j(x)>0}q_j(x)\beta_{j,\bot}. $$

Each parenthesized potential expression is nonnegative. For a matched pair, this follows from the zero-vote constraint obtained by pairing its original buckets. For an unmatched bucket, it follows from the unmatched lower bound. Thus objective zero forces every displayed expression to be zero.

For $\eta=1$, matched potentials have lower bound $-1$, and opposite potentials summing to zero give upper bound $1$. For $\eta=0$, matched agent potentials have lower bound $-1$ and matched job potentials have lower bound zero; their zero sum gives the stated intervals. Unmatched potentials are zero.

To obtain integer potentials, put $\gamma_{j,q}=-\beta_{j,q}$. Every pairing inequality becomes an integer difference inequality

$$ \alpha_{i,p}-\gamma_{j,q}\geq c $$

with integer $c$. Simultaneously flooring $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ preserves it, because

$$ A-G\geq c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lfloor A\rfloor-\lfloor G\rfloor\geq c. $$

Integer bounds, equalities $\alpha=\gamma$, and zero unmatched potentials are also preserved. Restoring $\beta=-\gamma$ proves the integral ranges.

Conversely, a dual-feasible witness with these equalities has objective zero. Weak duality then bounds every rival margin by zero. ∎

In particular, the witness conditions depend only on which assignment categories have positive mass.

A finite union of faces

A formal pattern consists of an edge set $S\subseteq E$ and a set $U\subseteq V$ of permitted unmatched categories. Its formal buckets are the matched categories specified by $S$ and the unmatched categories specified by $U$.

Call the pattern certified if witnesses for both voting rules exist on these buckets, satisfying all acceptable-pair inequalities, the original-edge zero-sum equalities, and zero unmatched potentials.

Define

$$ F_{S,U}= \left\{ x\in P: x_e=0\text{ for }e\notin S,\quad q_v(x)=0\text{ for }v\notin U \right\}. $$

Permitted categories may have zero mass in this definition.

Face lemma. The set of fully popular aggregate matchings is exactly

$$ Q=\bigcup_{(S,U)\text{ certified}}F_{S,U}, $$

and each nonempty $F_{S,U}$ is a face of $P$.

Proof. A fully popular matching’s actual positive categories form a certified pattern by the witness lemma.

Conversely, if $x\in F_{S,U}$ for a certified pattern, delete its formal zero-mass buckets. Restricting the witnesses removes constraints and preserves all required equalities. The witness lemma proves full popularity.

Finally, the defining conditions make nonnegative coordinates or capacity slacks zero. If a point satisfying these conditions is a proper convex combination of two points of $P$, nonnegativity forces the same coordinates and slacks to be zero at both points. This is the defining property of a face. ∎

Since $P$ is compact and there are finitely many patterns, $Q$ is compact. If $Q$ is nonempty, maximum matched mass is attained. Choose a certified face containing a global optimum and maximize $T$ over that face. A linear function on a nonempty compact polytope has an optimal vertex; a vertex of a face is a vertex of the original polytope. Therefore:

Whenever a fully popular matching exists, some maximum-mass fully popular aggregate matching is a vertex of $P$.

3. New structural result: optimal supports are paths

At most two categories per type

Lemma. In a both-side popular matching, each type has positive mass in at most two assignment categories, counting unmatched.

Proof. Suppose a type has three positive categories

$$ p_1\succ p_2\succ p_3. $$

Choose mass $\delta>0$ from each. Reassign the cohorts cyclically: the first receives $p_3$, the second receives $p_1$, and the third receives $p_2$. Two cohorts improve and one worsens, giving margin $\delta$ against the original matching.

For an agent type, retain the original opposite-side people serving these cohorts and pair them with the reassigned cohorts. Every job retains its partner type and matched status, so all jobs abstain. If $p_3=\bot$, the first cohort becomes unmatched and the formerly unmatched third cohort takes the second cohort’s partners.

For a job type, perform the same construction on the inverse matching. Every agent then retains its assignment type and matched status.

The equal-measure pairings exist by Section 1. Both constructions contradict both-side popularity. ∎

Consequently, positive support has degree at most two. A type with positive unmatched mass has support degree at most one.

Path structure at vertices

Theorem. If a fully popular matching exists, there is a maximum-mass fully popular matching whose positive support consists of disjoint paths. Every nontrivial path has at least one saturated endpoint, and every internal vertex is saturated.

Proof. Choose a maximum fully popular vertex $x$ of $P$, as established in Section 2. Its positive support has maximum degree two.

A support cycle would be even, since $E$ is bipartite. Alternately adding and subtracting a sufficiently small positive amount around that cycle preserves every vertex sum and keeps every positive coordinate positive. The two perturbations are distinct feasible points with midpoint $x$, contradicting that $x$ is a vertex. Thus every component containing an edge is a path.

An internal path vertex has two positive matched categories. It cannot also have positive unmatched mass, by the preceding lemma. Hence it is saturated.

If both endpoints of a path were unsaturated, alternate positive and negative perturbations along its edges. Internal sums remain unchanged. Choosing the perturbation smaller than every edge mass and both endpoint slacks makes both signs feasible. Again $x$ would be the midpoint of two distinct points of $P$. Therefore at least one endpoint is saturated. ∎

Alternating-sum edge masses

Orient a positive-support path from a saturated endpoint:

$$ v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k. $$

Write $z_\ell=x_{v_{\ell-1}v_\ell}$. Saturation gives

$$ z_1=r_{v_0}, \qquad z_{\ell+1}=r_{v_\ell}-z_\ell \quad(1\leq\ell<k). $$

Induction yields

$$ z_\ell= \sum_{t=0}^{\ell-1} (-1)^{\ell-1-t}r_{v_t}. $$

Thus every positive coordinate of this optimum is an alternating sum along a simple path in the acceptability graph.

This also supplies polynomial-size rational certificates for general instances. If $D$ is the product of the input denominators, each displayed sum is an integer multiple of $1/D$. The binary length of $D$ is at most the total denominator encoding length. Since every edge mass lies in $[0,1]$, all coordinates have polynomial encoding length. Together with the bounded witnesses, they give polynomially checkable certificates for existence and for any attainable matched-mass threshold. Hence both decision problems belong to $\mathrm{NP}$.

4. Polynomial-time algorithm when the acceptability graph is a forest

Assume now that the entire graph $G=(V,E)$ is a forest. This restriction applies to all acceptable pairs, including pairs a rival might use.

4.1 A polynomial set of candidate masses

For an oriented simple path

$$ R=(v_0,\ldots,v_\ell), $$

including a one-vertex path, define

$$ A(R)=\sum_{t=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{\ell-t}r_{v_t}. $$

Let

$$ W=\{A(R):R\text{ is an oriented simple path in }G,\ A(R)>0\}. $$

A forest has at most one simple path for each ordered pair of endpoints. Therefore

$$ |W|\leq\tau^2. $$

These values can be enumerated in polynomial time, and their rational encodings have polynomial length.

By Section 3, if a fully popular matching exists, some global optimum has every positive edge mass in $W$. Indeed, each edge mass is the alternating sum along a prefix of its oriented support path.

4.2 Local states

For each type $v$, enumerate states as follows.

Choose:

  • a set $S_v$ of at most two neighbors;
  • a mass $z_{vu}\in W$ for every $u\in S_v$.

Compute

$$ q_v=r_v-\sum_{u\in S_v}z_{vu}. $$

Reject the choice if $q_v<0$, or if $q_v>0$ and $|S_v|>1$. Its positive assignment categories are

$$ C_v= S_v\cup \begin{cases} \{\bot\},&q_v>0,\\ \varnothing,&q_v=0. \end{cases} $$

For every category $p\in C_v$, choose a potential $d_v^\eta(p)$ for each $\eta\in\{0,1\}$, using the ranges in the witness lemma. Require

$$ d_v^\eta(\bot)=0 $$

whenever unmatched is present.

These choices automatically satisfy the unmatched-variable lower bounds: matched agent potentials are at least $-1$; matched job potentials are at least $-\eta$; and unmatched potentials are zero.

Let $\mathcal S_v$ be the resulting state set. Since there are at most two categories, the number of potential choices is at most $36$. Therefore, writing $d(v)$ for degree,

$$ |\mathcal S_v| \leq 36\left(1+d(v)+\binom{d(v)}2\right)|W|^2. $$

This is polynomial in $\tau$.

4.3 Compatibility across every acceptable edge

For an acceptable edge $(i,j)$, an agent state $s_i$ and job state $s_j$ are compatible precisely when all of the following hold.

First, they agree whether the edge is used:

$$ j\in S_i\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\in S_j. $$

If it is used, they agree on its mass and satisfy both original-edge equalities:

$$ z_{ij}=z_{ji}, \qquad d_i^\eta(j)+d_j^\eta(i)=0 \quad(\eta\in\{0,1\}). $$

Finally, for every $p\in C_i$, $q\in C_j$, and $\eta\in\{0,1\}$, require

$$ d_i^\eta(p)+d_j^\eta(q) \geq \operatorname{vote}_i(j,p) +\eta\operatorname{vote}_j(i,q). $$

These last inequalities are required even when the edge is unused. They therefore include rivals using every acceptable pair.

Soundness of compatible states. A state assignment compatible across all edges defines a feasible aggregate matching: endpoint states agree on positive edge masses, and each vertex’s total respects its capacity. Its potentials satisfy every witness inequality and every original-assignment equality. The witness lemma proves that this matching is fully popular.

Completeness for optimization. A maximum fully popular vertex supplied by Section 3 has at most two categories per type, all positive edge masses in $W$, and bounded integral witnesses. Its local data therefore appear among the enumerated states and are compatible across all edges.

4.4 Dynamic programming

Root each tree component. Give state $s$ at vertex $v$ local weight

$$ w_v(s)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\sum_{u\in S_v}z_{vu},&v\in I,\\ 0,&v\in J. \end{cases} $$

Summing these weights counts each matched pair exactly once.

For a vertex $v$ and state $s\in\mathcal S_v$, let $H_v(s)$ be the maximum weight in its rooted subtree conditional on state $s$. The recurrence is

$$ H_v(s)= w_v(s)+ \sum_{c\text{ child of }v} \max_{\substack{t\in\mathcal S_c\\ s,t\text{ compatible}}} H_c(t), $$

where an empty maximum is $-\infty$.

The recurrence is valid because, after fixing the state of $v$, different child subtrees have no edges between them. Their only constraints to the rest of this subtree are the compatibility constraints on their respective parent edges.

Each state set and each compatibility table has polynomial size. Every arithmetic operation uses rational numbers of polynomial encoding length. Consequently, this dynamic program runs in polynomial time.

If a component has no feasible root state, no compatible global assignment exists. By completeness, no fully popular matching exists. Otherwise, retrieve maximizing states in each component. Soundness gives a fully popular matching, and completeness ensures that its matched mass equals the unrestricted fully popular optimum.

Finally, interval partitions and translations realize the returned aggregate vector with a polynomial-size finite description.

Conclusion for forests. Fully popular existence and maximum matched mass are polynomial-time solvable when the acceptability graph is a forest.

This case still permits nonexistence. For example, take one agent type of mass $1/2$ and three acceptable job types of mass $1/6$ each. In a both-side popular matching the agent type could have at most two positive categories. If it had unmatched mass, it could have at most one matched category; some acceptable job mass would then also be unmatched, and matching two unmatched cohorts would produce a positive voting margin. Thus all agents would have to be matched, but two job categories have total capacity only $1/3$. No both-side popular matching exists.

5. Polynomial-time solvability for equal type masses

Assume all types have the same mass $r$. Normalization gives $r=1/\tau$.

Theorem. The cohort problem is exactly equivalent, for existence and maximum matched mass, to the finite strict-preference instance with one individual per type.

5.1 A cohort optimum can be chosen pure

Choose a maximum fully popular vertex, whose support consists of paths as in Section 3. Orient a nontrivial component from a saturated endpoint. Its first edge has mass $r$. If the path had a second edge, saturation of the intervening internal vertex would give

$$ z_2=r-z_1=0, $$

contradicting positive support. Hence every positive component is a single edge of mass $r$.

Thus each type is either entirely unmatched or entirely assigned to one opposite-side type. Such a matching corresponds to an ordinary matching in the finite type graph.

5.2 A pure matching has exactly the finite popularity test

Fix a pure cohort matching. It has one positive current-assignment bucket per type, each of mass $r$. Divide the rival program’s variables by $r$. Its feasible region then has unit bucket capacities.

This feasible region is integral. Here is the relevant matrix argument. Its pairing-variable columns have one entry in an agent row and one in a job row. Negating job rows turns them into directed-incidence columns. Such a matrix is totally unimodular: in any square submatrix, a column with at most one nonzero permits determinant expansion; if every column has two nonzeros, each has opposite signs, so the rows are dependent. Rival-unmatched variables add unit columns, which preserve total unimodularity. Since the right-hand sides are integral, every vertex of the feasible region is integral.

An integral feasible rival assigns every type either to one acceptable type or to unmatched. It is therefore an ordinary finite matching. For either voting rule, the rival program attains its optimum at such a vertex.

Consequently:

  • a finite rival with positive margin lifts to a cohort rival with margin multiplied by $r$;
  • if a cohort rival has positive margin, an integral optimal rival supplies a finite rival with positive margin.

A pure cohort matching is therefore fully popular exactly when its corresponding finite matching is fully popular.

5.3 Applying the finite theorem

Kavitha’s Theorem 1.1 decides fully popular existence in a finite bipartite instance with strict preferences and returns a maximum-cardinality fully popular matching in linear time. The finite type graph satisfies these hypotheses; isolated jobs may be removed. [Kavitha, *Popular Matchings with One-Sided Bias*, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.05488v1).

Lift the returned finite matching by assigning mass $r$ along each of its edges. Section 5.2 proves full popularity. Section 5.1 shows that a cohort optimum can also be chosen pure, so maximum finite cardinality gives maximum cohort matched mass.

Thus equal type masses admit a polynomial-time solution.

6. Remaining open points

The forest algorithm depends on two facts specific to that restriction: there are only polynomially many simple paths, and the witness constraints form a tree of local compatibility conditions. In a general acceptability graph, the alternating-sum description does not provide a polynomial-size list of candidate masses, and the compatibility constraints can contain cycles.

The equal-mass reduction handles arbitrary acceptability graphs, but unequal masses permit split types and longer support paths. The proof above does not give an exact reduction of that case to finite strict preferences.

Accordingly, the general existence and maximum-mass problems remain unclassified here. The established results are a polynomial algorithm for forests, a polynomial algorithm for equal type masses, and the optimal-path structure for arbitrary instances.
