Given a finite set of student types \(T\), projects \(P\), lecturer groups \(\Lambda\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), weak preference orders, and rational project and lecturer capacity shares, decide whether there is a mass assignment \(x_{t,p}\geq 0\) and unmatched mass \(u_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_p x_{t,p}+u_t=\mu_t\) that has no positive-mass blocking exchange under the mass analogue of Definition 3, including its project and lecturer capacity cases and same-lecturer exception. If one exists, construct it; determine the complexity in the support size and input bit length without expanding the masses into individual students.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\) projects partitioned into nonempty sets \(P_k\) offered by \(h\) pools. Positive binary-rational capacities satisfy \(\max_{p\in P_k}c_p\le d_k\le\sum_{p\in P_k}c_p\). Each type strictly ranks ties of acceptable projects; each pool strictly ranks ties of all types accepting at least one of its projects. Pool rankings depend only on type, and ties are not broken.
An allocation consists of real masses \(y_{t,p}\ge0\), zero on unacceptable pairs, with \(\sum_p y_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{t,p}\le c_p\), and \(\sum_{p\in P_k,t}y_{t,p}\le d_k\). Write \(y_{t,0}=\mu_t-\sum_p y_{t,p}\) for unassigned mass, ranked below every acceptable project. Full means load equals capacity; undersubscribed means strictly smaller load.
A blocking deviation chooses a type \(t\), an acceptable target \(p\in P_k\), a source \(q\ne p\) with \(y_{t,q}>0\), and some sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\). It moves \(\varepsilon\) mass from \(q\) to \(p\), subject to one of the following acceptance cases, evaluated at the original allocation.
If \(q=0\) or \(t\) strictly prefers \(p\) to \(q\): (i) both project and pool are undersubscribed; (ii) the project is undersubscribed and the pool full, and either \(q\in P_k\) or the pool weakly prefers \(t\) to its worst assigned rank class; (iii) the project is full and its pool weakly prefers \(t\) to the project's worst assigned rank class.
If \(t\) is indifferent between \(p\) and \(q\): (i) both project and pool are undersubscribed and \(q\notin P_k\); (ii) the project is undersubscribed, the pool full, \(q\notin P_k\), and the pool strictly prefers \(t\) to its worst assigned rank class; (iii) the project is full and its pool strictly prefers \(t\) to the project's worst assigned rank class.
In either case (ii), an incoming transfer from outside the pool displaces \(\varepsilon\) mass from the pool's worst assigned rank class; an internal transfer displaces none. In either case (iii), displace \(\varepsilon\) mass from the project's worst assigned rank class, even for an internal transfer. Displaced mass becomes unassigned. Worst classes contain positive assigned mass; choose \(\varepsilon\) so all removals and remaining capacity constraints are feasible. An allocation is strongly stable if no such deviation exists.

The general complexity classification remains unresolved. I prove polynomial-time existence and construction for two restrictions with arbitrary support size and binary-rational data:

  • Every pool offers one project, and applicants have strict preferences. Pool rankings may contain ties.
  • There is one pool, and that pool is indifferent among all types. Applicant preferences may contain ties.

I also prove a sharper denominator bound for two restrictions and identify precisely why the same rounding argument fails for the general problem.

1. A structural property of every strongly stable allocation

Write $A_t$ for the acceptable projects of type $t$. A type’s preference list is a strict ordering of ties.

Lemma 1. In every strongly stable allocation, each type is either entirely unassigned or entirely assigned. All projects receiving positive mass of an assigned type belong to one tie in its preference list.

Proof. Suppose $y_{t,p}>0$, and suppose a positive source $q$ is either unassigned or strictly worse than $p$.

Consider the fullness status of $p$ and its pool $k$.

  • If $p$ is full, its worst assigned class is weakly worse than $t$, because it contains positive mass of $t$. Strict-preference acceptance case (iii) applies.
  • If $p$ is undersubscribed and $k$ is full, an internal source is accepted automatically. For an external source, the pool’s worst assigned class is weakly worse than $t$, again because the pool contains positive mass of $t$. Case (ii) applies.
  • If both are undersubscribed, case (i) applies.

Each accepted triple permits a feasible deviation. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ smaller than the positive source mass, the positive mass used for displacement, and any positive capacity slack needed by the case. The source and displacement coordinates are distinct: displacement is from the target project in case (iii), and from the target pool while the source is external in case (ii). The prescribed displacement preserves the relevant capacity constraints.

Thus every possibility contradicts stability. Taking $q=0$ excludes partial assignment. Taking $p$ and $q$ from two distinct occupied preference ties excludes assignment across different ties. $\square$

In particular, when applicant preferences are strict, every assigned type places its entire mass at a single project.

2. Polynomial-time solution for singleton pools and strict applicant preferences

Assume throughout this section that every pool offers exactly one project, and that each applicant type strictly orders its acceptable projects.

The input capacity assumptions then imply that each pool’s capacity equals its project’s capacity. Consequently, for a strictly improving transfer to $p$:

  • If $p$ is undersubscribed, the transfer is accepted.
  • If $p$ is full, the transfer is accepted exactly when the pool weakly prefers the incoming type to the project’s worst assigned class.

Number the rank classes at project $p$ from best to worst, and write $r_p(t)$ for the rank of an accepting type $t$. Smaller ranks are better.

2.1. The algorithm

Maintain a current preference list for each type, initially its original list. Deletions are permanent.

A type is either held at one project, with its entire mass there, or unheld. Initially every type is unheld. Let $H_p$ be the set of types held at $p$, and write

$$ \ell_p=\sum_{t\in H_p}\mu_t. $$

Repeat the following operation while some unheld type has a nonempty current list.

  • Choose such a type $t$. Let $p$ be its first remaining project, and hold $t$ at $p$.
  • While $\ell_p>c_p$, let

$$ R=\max_{u\in H_p}r_p(u). $$
Delete $p$ from the current list of every accepting type $v$ with $r_p(v)\ge R$. Any held type whose pair is deleted becomes unheld.

  • If $\ell_p=c_p$, let

$$ R=\max_{u\in H_p}r_p(u). $$
Delete $p$ from the current list of every accepting type $v$ with $r_p(v)>R$.

The positive capacity ensures that the worst rank in step 3 exists.

At termination, define an allocation $x$ by assigning every held type entirely to its holding project and leaving every unheld type entirely unassigned. Check $x$ against the original blocking conditions. Output $x$ if it is strongly stable; otherwise report nonexistence.

2.2. Every deletion is safe

Lemma 2. Every deleted pair $(v,p)$ receives zero mass in every strongly stable allocation.

Proof. Induct over the deletion operations. Assume all earlier deletions satisfy the claim.

Whenever a type $u$ is held at $p$, every project that $u$ originally preferred to $p$ has already been deleted from its list: $p$ was its first remaining project when it proposed. Therefore, by the induction hypothesis, any strongly stable allocation assigns $u$ to $p$, to a worse project, or leaves it unassigned.

First consider an overload deletion. Immediately before it, the held set $S=H_p$ satisfies

$$ \sum_{u\in S}\mu_u>c_p, \qquad r_p(u)\le R\quad\text{for every }u\in S. $$

Let $v$ be any type whose pair is deleted, so $r_p(v)\ge R$. Suppose a strongly stable allocation $y$ assigns positive mass of $v$ to $p$.

Take any $u\in S$. If $y$ does not assign $u$ to $p$, Lemma 1 and the induction hypothesis imply that $u$ is entirely unassigned or entirely assigned to a project strictly worse than $p$.

Such a type can block $y$ at $p$:

  • If $p$ is undersubscribed, the transfer is accepted.
  • If $p$ is full, its worst assigned rank is at least $r_p(v)$, and

$$ r_p(u)\le R\le r_p(v). $$
Hence the incoming type is weakly preferred to the worst assigned class.

Thus every $u\in S$ must be assigned entirely to $p$ in $y$. Their total mass exceeds $c_p$, a contradiction.

Now consider a deletion when $\ell_p=c_p$. Every deleted type $v$ is strictly worse than every type in the held set $S$. The same blocking argument forces every $u\in S$ to be assigned entirely to $p$ if $y$ assigns positive mass of $v$ there. But $S$ already has mass $c_p$, and $v\notin S$. Adding positive mass of $v$ violates capacity.

Both deletion rules are therefore safe. $\square$

2.3. The terminal allocation dominates every stable allocation

Here, “dominates” means that every type receives an outcome at least as good in its own preference order, with unassignment ranked last.

Lemma 3. If $y$ is strongly stable, every type weakly prefers its outcome in the terminal allocation $x$ to its outcome in $y$.

Proof. If $t$ is held at $p$ at termination, every originally better project was deleted before $t$ proposed to $p$. Lemma 2 excludes all those projects from $y$.

If $t$ is unheld at termination, its current list is empty. Every acceptable pair of $t$ has been deleted, so Lemma 2 forces $t$ to be entirely unassigned in $y$. $\square$

2.4. A vacancy argument proves completeness

Theorem 4. The algorithm outputs a strongly stable allocation whenever one exists.

Proof. The terminal allocation $x$ is feasible: every held type is assigned once, and the overload-removal step leaves every project within capacity.

Suppose $x$ has a blocking deviation with source type $t$ and target $p$. Applicant preferences are strict, so $t$ is unassigned or strictly prefers $p$ to its holding project.

The pair $(t,p)$ must have been deleted. Indeed, an unheld type terminates with an empty list; a held type proposed to its first remaining project, so every better project was already deleted.

Moreover, every terminal type held at $p$ is strictly better ranked than $t$. To see this, consider the deletion of $(t,p)$:

  • An overload deletion removes every rank at least $R$, leaving only ranks strictly better than $R$, while $r_p(t)\ge R$.
  • A deletion at exact capacity removes every rank strictly worse than $R$, leaving ranks at most $R$, while $r_p(t)>R$.

Lists only shrink afterward. Therefore, if $p$ were full in $x$, it would reject $t$. Since $t$ blocks, $p$ is undersubscribed in $x$:

$$ \ell_p(x)<c_p. $$

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that a strongly stable allocation $y$ exists. Lemma 3 implies that $t$ is unassigned in $y$ or still strictly prefers $p$ to its assignment. If $p$ were undersubscribed in $y$, this would be a blocking deviation. Hence

$$ \ell_p(y)=c_p. $$

We now derive a contradiction by conservation of mass.

Form a directed graph on the projects. Include an arc $a\to b$ whenever some type $u$ is assigned to $a$ in $y$ and to a different project $b$ in $x$.

For every such arc, Lemma 3 and strict applicant preferences imply that $u$ strictly prefers $b$ to $a$. Stability of $y$ therefore forces $b$ to be full in $y$: otherwise $u$ could transfer to an undersubscribed $b$.

Let $R$ be the set of projects reachable from $p$, including $p$ itself. Every project in $R$ is full in $y$. Since $x$ is feasible,

$$ \ell_a(x)\le c_a=\ell_a(y) \qquad\text{for every }a\in R. $$

The inequality is strict at $p$, so

$$ \sum_{a\in R}\ell_a(x) < \sum_{a\in R}\ell_a(y). $$

On the other hand, every type assigned within $R$ in $y$ is also assigned within $R$ in $x$. It cannot be unheld in $x$, by Lemma 3. If its project changes, the corresponding directed arc stays within $R$ by reachability. Lemma 1 ensures that the entire mass of each such type moves together. Consequently,

$$ \sum_{a\in R}\ell_a(x) \ge \sum_{a\in R}\ell_a(y), $$

a contradiction.

Thus, if a stable allocation exists, the terminal allocation $x$ cannot have a blocker. The final stability check is therefore a correct existence test. $\square$

2.5. Running time and construction

Let

$$ E=\sum_t |A_t|\le \tau m. $$

Each type proposes to a given project at most once. A held type becomes unheld only when its holding pair is permanently deleted. Thus there are at most $E$ proposals.

Every overload-removal operation deletes at least one remaining pair. There are at most $E$ pair deletions altogether. Lists, held sets, ranks, and loads can be maintained by elementary scans of polynomial size.

All arithmetic is exact. For example, multiply masses and capacities by the least common multiple $D$ of their input denominators. Its bit length is at most the total bit length of those denominators, hence at most $L$. Loads and comparisons then use integers of polynomial bit length.

The final blocking check is also polynomial: each type has one positive source, so it suffices to scan its acceptable targets.

Therefore this restriction admits existence decision and construction in

$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,h,L) $$

time. The output coordinates are simply input masses or zero.

As a computational check independent of the proof, the algorithm agreed with exhaustive existence testing on $14{,}400$ two-type, two-project instances, and on $3{,}000$ random three-type, three-project instances. These checks are not used in the correctness argument.

3. Polynomial-time solution for one pool indifferent among all types

Assume now that there is one pool, and its ranking has a single tie. Applicant preference lists may contain arbitrary ties.

For each type with a nonempty acceptable set, let $T_t$ be its highest preference tie.

Theorem 5. A strongly stable allocation exists exactly when all types with nonempty acceptable sets can be assigned entirely within their respective $T_t$, respecting project capacities and the pool capacity. This condition and a corresponding allocation are computable in polynomial time.

Proof. Every strictly improving transfer is accepted in every fullness configuration:

  • If project and pool are undersubscribed, case (i) applies.
  • If the project is undersubscribed and the pool full, an internal transfer is accepted automatically; an unassigned source is accepted by the weak rank comparison.
  • If the project is full, the weak comparison with its worst assigned class always holds.

Thus positive unassigned mass or positive mass below $T_t$ would block at a project in $T_t$. Every type with a nonempty list must consequently be assigned entirely within $T_t$.

Conversely, suppose such a feasible allocation is given. There is no strictly improving source, because each nonempty-list type is fully assigned within its highest tie.

Any indifferent transfer is internal to the sole pool. Indifferent cases (i) and (ii) require an external source, so they do not apply. Case (iii) requires a strict pool preference, which the single pool never has. Hence the allocation is strongly stable.

It remains to test the stated feasibility condition. Use the network

$$ s\longrightarrow t\longrightarrow p\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow z. $$

For each nonempty-list type $t$, give $s\to t$ capacity $\mu_t$. Include $t\to p$ exactly when $p\in T_t$, with capacity $\mu_t$. Give $p\to k$ capacity $c_p$ and $k\to z$ capacity $d_k$.

A flow of value

$$ \sum_{t:A_t\ne\varnothing}\mu_t $$

saturates every source-to-type arc and is exactly the required allocation. Types with empty acceptable sets remain unassigned. After scaling by $D$, this is an ordinary integer-capacity maximum-flow computation on a polynomial-size network, with polynomial-bit-length capacities. $\square$

4. A sharper denominator bound under two restrictions

The following result concerns witness existence; by itself, it is not an algorithm for the remaining cases.

Let $D$ be the least common multiple of all input denominators.

Theorem 6. If a strongly stable real allocation exists, then one exists with every coordinate an integer multiple of $1/D$, under either hypothesis:

  • Applicant preferences are strict, with arbitrary pools; or
  • Every pool offers one project, with arbitrary applicant ties.

4.1. A preservation lemma

For allocations $x,y$, say that $x$ has contained support if every positive coordinate of $x$, including an unassigned coordinate, is positive in $y$.

Lemma 7. Under either hypothesis of Theorem 6, suppose $y$ is strongly stable and $x$ is feasible. If $x$ has contained support and every object full in $y$ remains full in $x$, then $x$ is strongly stable. Objects undersubscribed in $y$ may become full in $x$.

Proof. Suppose $(t,p,q)$ blocks $x$. Its source is positive in $y$ as well.

If $p$ is full in $y$, it remains full in $x$. Contained support means that its worst assigned class in $x$ is weakly better than in $y$. A weak or strict acceptance comparison against the worst class in $x$ therefore also holds against the worst class in $y$. The triple blocks $y$.

If $p$ and its pool are both undersubscribed in $y$, then a strictly improving transfer is accepted in $y$ automatically. Under the singleton-pool hypothesis, an indifferent source $q\ne p$ is necessarily outside the target pool, so indifferent case (i) also accepts it.

The remaining configuration is that $p$ is undersubscribed in $y$ while its pool $k$ is full. This configuration is impossible for singleton pools, whose capacities equal their projects’ capacities. Thus only the strict-applicant hypothesis needs consideration.

The pool remains full in $x$. If $q$ is internal, $y$ accepts the transfer automatically. If $q$ is external, acceptance in $x$ compares $t$ weakly with either:

  • the pool’s worst assigned class, when $p$ is undersubscribed in $x$; or
  • the project’s worst assigned class, when $p$ is full in $x$.

In either case, that class consists of types present in pool $k$ in $y$. It is therefore weakly better than the pool’s worst assigned class in $y$. The weak comparison required by case (ii) holds in $y$.

All possibilities yield a blocker of $y$, a contradiction. $\square$

4.2. Integral circulation construction

Fix a strongly stable allocation $y$ and scale all quantities by $D$.

Represent allocations by a circulation with nodes for a source $s$, types, projects, pools, and a sink $z$:

  • $s\to t$ has fixed flow $D\mu_t$.
  • $t\to p$ exists for acceptable pairs and carries assigned mass.
  • $t\to z$ carries unassigned mass.
  • $p\to k$ has capacity $Dc_p$.
  • $k\to z$ has capacity $Dd_k$.
  • $z\to s$ has fixed flow $D$.

Give assignment and unassigned arcs upper bound $D$. Impose two further restrictions:

  • Fix every coordinate zero in $y$ to zero.
  • Fix every project or pool arc full in $y$ to its capacity.

The resulting circulation polytope $Q$ is nonempty, because it contains $Dy$, and compact, because all arc bounds are finite. All lower and upper bounds are integers.

For completeness, every vertex of such a circulation polytope is integral. If a vertex had fractional arcs, those arcs would lie strictly between their integer bounds. At each node, exactly one incident fractional arc is impossible: conservation, after subtracting all incident integer flows, would force that arc’s flow to be integer. Thus the undirected graph of fractional arcs has no vertex of degree one and contains a cycle. A sufficiently small signed perturbation around that cycle preserves conservation and all bounds in both directions. The purported vertex would then be the midpoint of two distinct feasible circulations, a contradiction.

Choose an integral vertex of $Q$ and divide its flows by $D$, obtaining $x$. It has contained support, and every object full in $y$ remains full. Lemma 7 proves stability. Its coordinates are integer multiples of $1/D$, as claimed. $\square$

5. Why arbitrary vertex rounding fails in the general case

Lemma 7 cannot be extended directly to internal transfers between tied projects.

Consider one pool offering projects $p,q$, with

$$ c_p=c_q=\frac34, \qquad d=1. $$

There are two types $a,b$, each of mass $1/2$. Both have the single preference tie $\{p,q\}$, and the pool strictly prefers $a$ to $b$.

Consider

$$ \begin{array}{c|cc} & p & q\\ \hline a & \frac18 & \frac38\\ b & \frac38 & \frac18 \end{array} $$

Both projects have load $1/2$, and the pool is full. This allocation is strongly stable: every potential source is indifferent and internal; indifferent cases (i) and (ii) exclude internal transfers, while case (iii) requires a full project.

Now consider the contained-support allocation

$$ \begin{array}{c|cc} & p & q\\ \hline a & \frac14 & \frac14\\ b & \frac12 & 0 \end{array} $$

The pool remains full, but $p$ becomes full. Type $a$ has a positive source at $q$, is indifferent between $p$ and $q$, and is strictly preferred to $p$’s worst assigned class $b$. Indifferent case (iii) therefore gives a blocking deviation.

Moreover, the second allocation is a vertex of the circulation polytope obtained by fixing the originally full pool and preserving the original zero coordinates. Its zero coordinate $y_{b,q}=0$, full-project equation at $p$, and two type-total equations uniquely determine its assignment coordinates. Any convex decomposition into feasible allocations must preserve these active equations, so the decomposition is trivial.

Thus an arbitrary integral vertex can introduce a blocker. This example does not disprove a $1/D$ denominator theorem for the general problem; it disproves this particular preservation argument.

6. Remaining general question

The proofs above give algorithms whose running time depends polynomially on support size and binary input length for two nontrivial restrictions. They do not extend to arbitrary applicant ties together with arbitrary pool rankings and multiple projects per pool.

The known discrete existence algorithm applies to explicitly listed applicants; its polynomial bound does not by itself establish polynomial time for binary-encoded type masses. [Olaosebikan and Manlove, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.10262)

A general polynomial-time algorithm, or an NP-hardness reduction, is still needed.
