The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, a scoring vector \(\alpha\), rational sincere mass \(\mu\) over complete rankings, rational strategic mass \(\lambda\), and preferred candidate \(p\), decide whether nonnegative masses \(x_r\) over rankings satisfying \(\sum_r x_r=\lambda\) can be assigned so that \(p\) is a winner, or uniquely wins, after adding \(x\) to \(\mu\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let the input give \(m\), a nonincreasing integer scoring vector \(\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m)\), a preferred candidate \(p\), a nonnegative rational sincere mass \(\mu\) as an explicit list of rankings with nonzero mass, and a nonnegative rational strategic mass \(\lambda\). Numbers are encoded in binary. For each complete ranking \(r\), strategic mass \(x_r\geq 0\) may be assigned, with \(\sum_r x_r=\lambda\); each candidate’s score is its mass-weighted positional score under \(\mu+x\).

Is deciding whether some such assignment makes \(p\) a winner in P or NP-hard? Is the unique-winner version in P or NP-hard?

For indivisible weighted strategic voters, both versions are in P for each fixed \(\alpha\) with \(\alpha_2=\cdots=\alpha_m\), and NP-complete for each fixed \(\alpha\) otherwise. A polynomially solvable fractional configuration relaxation is known for a related indivisible-voter manipulation problem; it does not state this rational-mass decision problem.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Both decision problems are in \(\mathrm{P}\), even when the number of candidates and the scoring vector are part of the input. The key is an exact representation of divisible strategic mass by candidate–position marginals.

Let \(C\) be the candidate set, with \(|C|=m\) and \(p\in C\). For a ranking \(r\), let \(\operatorname{pos}_r(c)\) denote the position of candidate \(c\). Compute each sincere score
\(s_c=\sum_{r\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_r\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_r(c)}\).

We first prove that a nonnegative matrix \(Y=(y_{cj})_{c\in C,\,1\le j\le m}\) represents the candidate–position marginals of some strategic assignment of total mass \(\lambda\) if and only if
\(\sum_{j=1}^m y_{cj}=\lambda\) for every candidate \(c\), and
\(\sum_{c\in C}y_{cj}=\lambda\) for every position \(j\).

Necessity follows by defining
\(y_{cj}=\sum_{r:\operatorname{pos}_r(c)=j}x_r\).
Every ranking places each candidate in exactly one position and fills each position with exactly one candidate.

For sufficiency, we give a decomposition argument. If \(\lambda=0\), nonnegativity and the row sums force \(Y=0\), represented by the zero assignment. Otherwise, start with residual matrix \(Z=Y\), whose row and column sums have common value \(t=\lambda>0\).

Form a bipartite graph between candidates and positions, putting an edge \((c,j)\) exactly when \(z_{cj}>0\). For any candidate subset \(A\), let \(N(A)\) be its neighboring positions. Then
\(t|A|=\sum_{c\in A}\sum_{j\in N(A)}z_{cj}\le\sum_{c\in C}\sum_{j\in N(A)}z_{cj}=t|N(A)|\).
Dividing by \(t>0\) gives \(|A|\le|N(A)|\). Hall’s matching theorem therefore supplies a matching covering all candidates. Since both sides have size \(m\), this matching specifies a complete ranking.

Let \(\delta>0\) be the minimum residual entry on this matching. Assign mass \(\delta\) to its ranking and subtract \(\delta\) from every matched entry. The residual remains nonnegative, and every row and column sum becomes \(t-\delta\). At least one positive entry disappears, and no new positive entry appears.

Repeat whenever the common residual sum is positive. There are initially at most \(m^2\) positive entries, so the process terminates after at most \(m^2\) iterations. At termination the residual is zero: otherwise its positive common row sum would permit another iteration. The extracted masses sum to \(\lambda\), and their ranking marginals sum to \(Y\). This proves sufficiency. If \(Y\) is rational, every extracted mass is rational.

For any such matrix, the resulting score of candidate \(c\) is precisely the linear expression
\(S_c(Y)=s_c+\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jy_{cj}\).

Consequently, the ordinary-winner problem is exactly the feasibility problem for the following rational linear constraints:

  • \(y_{cj}\ge0\) for all candidates \(c\) and positions \(j\);
  • \(\sum_{j=1}^m y_{cj}=\lambda\) for every candidate \(c\);
  • \(\sum_{c\in C}y_{cj}=\lambda\) for every position \(j\);
  • \(s_p+\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jy_{pj}\ge s_c+\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jy_{cj}\) for every \(c\ne p\).

A successful strategic assignment produces a feasible matrix by necessity. Conversely, sufficiency decomposes any feasible matrix into strategic rankings with exactly the scores appearing in the inequalities. Thus feasibility is equivalent to making \(p\) a winner.

For the unique-winner problem, introduce one additional variable \(\varepsilon\). Maximize \(\varepsilon\), subject to the same nonnegativity and marginal constraints, together with \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\) and, for every \(c\ne p\),
\(s_p+\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jy_{pj}\ge s_c+\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_jy_{cj}+\varepsilon\).

Reject if this linear program is infeasible. Otherwise, accept exactly when its optimum \(\varepsilon^\star\) is positive.

To verify this criterion, first suppose \(m\ge2\) and a strategic assignment makes \(p\) the unique winner. Its matrix \(Y\) satisfies \(S_p(Y)-S_c(Y)>0\) for every opponent. There are finitely many opponents, so
\(d=\min_{c\ne p}(S_p(Y)-S_c(Y))>0\).
Choosing \(\varepsilon=\min\{1,d\}\) produces a feasible solution with positive objective value.

Conversely, any feasible solution with \(\varepsilon>0\) satisfies \(S_p(Y)>S_c(Y)\) for every opponent. Decomposing \(Y\) supplies a strategic assignment making \(p\) the unique winner. If \(m=1\), there are no opponent constraints, the optimum is \(1\), and the sole candidate is indeed the unique winner.

Whenever this linear program is feasible, its feasible region is closed and bounded: the marginal constraints give \(0\le y_{cj}\le\lambda\), and \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\). Hence its maximum is attained. The acceptance criterion therefore handles strict inequalities exactly.

Finally, all computations have polynomial bit complexity. Let \(L\) be the total input encoding length. The explicit scoring vector ensures that \(m\) is bounded by the input length. The sincere scores can be computed in polynomial time and have polynomial encoding length: a common denominator is the product of the denominators of the explicitly listed sincere masses, whose bit length is at most their total input bit length. Multiplying by the integer scoring entries and summing the resulting terms preserves a polynomial bit bound.

Each linear program has at most \(m^2+1\) variables, polynomially many constraints, and rational coefficients of polynomial encoding length. Rational linear programming admits polynomial-time feasibility testing and exact optimization in the binary encoding model; see the introductory statement in [Adler and Beling, *Polynomial Algorithms for Linear Programming over the Algebraic Numbers*](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf). Thus the optimum’s comparison with zero is exact, even if a feasible winning margin is very small.

A feasible rational linear program has a rational feasible solution, and a finite attained optimum has a rational optimal solution. Combined with the decomposition above, this also shows that allowing real strategic masses instead of requiring rational strategic masses does not change either answer.

The argument applies to every scoring vector in the question. Divisibility makes the marginal representation exact; prescribed indivisible voter weights would impose additional restrictions that these linear programs do not express.
