The continuous mirror question, as graded

Let \(\mu\) be a finite-support distribution over complete consumer types, each specifying a utility and wealth or endowment, and let \(d_\mu(p)=\int x_\theta(p,w_\theta(p))\,d\mu(\theta)\). Given aggregate spending-share observations \(g^k=\operatorname{diag}(p^k)d_\mu(p^k)\), a current android pool, and a dual state \(U\) obtained from CRM’s wealth-redistribution problem on those observations, compute a linear-utility android parameter \(y\) maximizing \(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle Ue_k,e_{j_k(y)}\rangle\), where \(j_k(y)\in\arg\max_j\{y_j-\log p_j^k\}\) with the paper’s tie-breaking rule. The continuous-market question is the computational complexity of this pricing task when the aggregate observations and CRM state are generated by a population with many consumers but relatively few types.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\ge2\) divisible goods. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The masses \(q_\theta\) sum to \(1\).
Each type specifies a continuous, concave, locally nonsatiated utility \(u_\theta:\mathbb R_+^m\to\mathbb R\) and a bounded, nonnegative wealth function \(w_\theta\) on the price simplex, encoded by rational arithmetic circuits allowing minimum and maximum. Demands are Walrasian demands, with the lexicographically greatest maximizing bundle selected at ties.
The input includes \(K\ge1\) strictly positive binary-rational price vectors \(p^k\) summing to \(1\), and exact rational demand tables \(x_\theta^k\), promised consistent with the types. Assume \(\sum_\theta q_\theta w_\theta(p^k)=1\); define \(d_\mu(p^k)=\sum_\theta q_\theta x_\theta^k\) and \(g^k=\operatorname{diag}(p^k)d_\mu(p^k)\).
A linear android has utility \(\langle c,x\rangle\), where \(c\in\mathbb R_{>0}^m\), and spending share \(\gamma_c(p)=e_{j(c,p)}\), where \(j(c,p)=\min\arg\max_j c_j/p_j\). The current pool consists of \(T\ge1\) such androids with positive rational coefficient vectors \(c^t\).
Wealth redistribution uses constant android budgets and minimizes \(K^{-1}\sum_k\|g^k-\sum_t a_t\gamma_{c^t}(p^k)\|_\infty\) over \(a\in\Delta_T\). The input supplies rational \((U,\eta)\), promised optimal for its dual: maximize \(K^{-1}(\sum_k\langle U_{\cdot k},g^k\rangle-\eta)\), subject to \(\|U_{\cdot k}\|_1\le1\) and \(\sum_k\langle U_{\cdot k},\gamma_{c^t}(p^k)\rangle\le\eta\) for every pool android.
The pricing task is to maximize \(\sum_{k=1}^K U_{j(c,p^k),k}\) over \(c\in\mathbb R_{>0}^m\). Return a maximizing positive rational coefficient vector, equivalently the parameter \(y_j=\log c_j\); no additional parameter bound is imposed. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Is this pricing problem, under the population-consistency and optimal-dual promises, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,T,L\), or is it NP-hard?
For unrestricted score matrices, linear-android separation is polynomial-time solvable when \(K=1\) and strongly NP-hard when \(K\) is part of the input ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23985)).
No result settling the stated problem with both promises was identified.

Result

The pricing problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(\tau=T=1\), the population has linear utility and constant wealth, and every price is a strictly positive dyadic rational.

The key observation is that an exactly fitted population can make a prescribed score matrix an optimal dual solution. The optimal-dual promise therefore does not eliminate hard pricing instances.

1. The source problem and construction

We reduce from Maximum Acyclic Subgraph: given a directed graph \(G=(V,E)\), find the largest cardinality of an edge subset \(S\subseteq E\) containing no directed cycle. This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; its hardness is also recalled in Appendix D.1 of the [linked paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23985).

We may restrict to loopless graphs with \(n=|V|\ge2\) and \(K=|E|\ge1\). Loops cannot belong to an acyclic subset and can be removed; instances without edges are trivial.

Index the edges as \(e_k=(a_k,b_k)\). Introduce one good for each vertex and one additional good \(d\), so \(m=n+1\).

Set
\[ \]
\(H=4^n,\qquad Q=3+2(n-2)H.\)

Let \(P\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(P\ge Q+2H\). For observation \(k\), define unnormalized prices by

\(r_{a_k}^k=2,\quad r_{b_k}^k=1,\quad r_v^k=2H\ \text{for }v\in V\setminus\{a_k,b_k\},\quad r_d^k=P-Q,\)

and set \(p_j^k=r_j^k/P\).

Because \(a_k\ne b_k\), the vertex prices sum to \(Q\). Hence the complete price vector sums to \(1\). Every entry is strictly positive, and every entry is dyadic rational. Also \(r_d^k\ge2H\).

Define the supplied scores and scalar by

\(U_{j,k}=\begin{cases}1,&j=a_k,\\0,&j\ne a_k,\end{cases}\qquad \eta=0.\)

Each column satisfies \(\|U_{\cdot k}\|_1=1\).

2. Verification of both promises

Take a population consisting of one type, with

\(q_\theta=1,\qquad u_\theta(x)=\sum_{j=1}^m x_j,\qquad w_\theta(p)=1.\)

The utility is continuous and concave. It is locally nonsatiated because increasing any coordinate strictly increases utility. Both functions have rational arithmetic circuits of polynomial size, and wealth is bounded and nonnegative.

Take a pool consisting of one android with \(c^1=(1,\ldots,1)\).

At observation \(k\), good \(b_k\) is uniquely cheapest: its unnormalized price is \(1\), whereas every other unnormalized price is at least \(2\). Consequently both the population type and the pool android uniquely choose \(b_k\). Supply the exact demand table

\(x_\theta^k=P e_{b_k}.\)

Indeed, \(p_{b_k}^k=1/P\), so this bundle exhausts the unit budget. To verify optimality directly, every feasible bundle satisfies

\(\sum_j x_j\le P\sum_j p_j^k x_j\le P.\)

Equality requires expenditure exclusively on the uniquely cheapest good. Thus the supplied demand is the unique maximizer and satisfies the lexicographic selection rule.

The total population wealth is \(1\), and

\(g^k=\operatorname{diag}(p^k)x_\theta^k=e_{b_k}=\gamma_{c^1}(p^k).\)

Therefore the redistribution problem has value \(0\).

The supplied dual pair is feasible: since \(a_k\ne b_k\),

\(\sum_k\langle U_{\cdot k},\gamma_{c^1}(p^k)\rangle=\sum_k U_{b_k,k}=0=\eta.\)

Its objective is also \(0\). To establish optimality without invoking any additional duality theorem, let \((V,\zeta)\) be any feasible dual pair. The sole pool constraint gives

\(\sum_k V_{b_k,k}\le\zeta.\)

Since \(g^k=e_{b_k}\), its objective is

\(\frac1K\left(\sum_k\langle V_{\cdot k},g^k\rangle-\zeta\right)=\frac1K\left(\sum_k V_{b_k,k}-\zeta\right)\le0.\)

Thus \((U,0)\) is optimal. Both promises hold.

3. The pricing optimum equals the maximum acyclic-subgraph size

For any positive coefficient vector \(c\), define

\(S(c)=\{e_k:j(c,p^k)=a_k\}.\)

By construction of \(U\), its pricing score is exactly \(|S(c)|\).

Upper bound

If \(e_k\in S(c)\), the winning good must weakly outperform \(b_k\). Therefore

\(\frac{c_{a_k}}{2/P}\ge\frac{c_{b_k}}{1/P},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad c_{a_k}\ge2c_{b_k}.\)

Suppose \(S(c)\) contained a directed cycle of length \(\ell\). Multiplying these inequalities around that cycle would give

\(\prod_{v\text{ on the cycle}}c_v\ge2^\ell\prod_{v\text{ on the cycle}}c_v.\)

All coefficients are positive, so cancellation would imply \(1\ge2^\ell\), a contradiction. Hence \(S(c)\) is acyclic, and every pricing score is at most the maximum acyclic-subgraph size.

This argument permits ties and therefore applies to the specified tie rule.

Matching lower bound

Let \(S\subseteq E\) be any acyclic edge subset. For each vertex \(v\), let \(\ell_v\) be the length of a longest directed path starting at \(v\) in \((V,S)\). Acyclicity gives \(0\le\ell_v\le n-1\), and every edge \((a,b)\in S\) satisfies \(\ell_a\ge\ell_b+1\).

Choose positive integer coefficients

\(c_v=4^{\ell_v}\quad(v\in V),\qquad c_d=1.\)

Fix an observation \(k\) whose edge belongs to \(S\). Comparing coefficient-to-price ratios using the unnormalized prices is valid because normalization multiplies every ratio by the same \(P\).

For its two endpoints,

\(\frac{c_{a_k}}{r_{a_k}^k}=\frac{c_{a_k}}2\ge2c_{b_k}>c_{b_k}=\frac{c_{b_k}}{r_{b_k}^k}.\)

For any other vertex \(v\),

\(\frac{c_v}{r_v^k}\le\frac{4^{n-1}}{2\cdot4^n}=\frac18<\frac12\le\frac{c_{a_k}}2.\)

For the additional good,

\(\frac{c_d}{r_d^k}\le\frac1{2H}<\frac12\le\frac{c_{a_k}}2.\)

Thus \(a_k\) is the unique winner at every observation corresponding to an edge of \(S\). The resulting pricing score is at least \(|S|\).

Taking \(S\) to be a maximum acyclic subset and combining both bounds proves

\(\max_{c\in\mathbb R_{>0}^m}\sum_k U_{j(c,p^k),k}=\operatorname{MAS}(G).\)

The lower-bound coefficients are positive integers, so the same equality holds when coefficients must be rational.

4. Encoding size and conclusion

The integers \(H,Q,P\) have \(O(n+\log n)\) bits. Every price, demand-table entry, score, mass, pool coefficient, and circuit consequently has polynomial encoding length. The construction is computable in polynomial time, with \(m=n+1\), \(\tau=T=1\), and \(K=|E|\).

Given a maximizing rational coefficient vector, exact rational comparisons recover \(S(c)\). The preceding proof shows that this is a maximum acyclic edge subset. A polynomial-time algorithm for the promised pricing problem would therefore solve Maximum Acyclic Subgraph in polynomial time.

For comparison, when \(K=1\), choose \(j^*\in\arg\max_j U_{j,1}\), set \(c_{j^*}=2p_{j^*}^1\), and set \(c_i=p_i^1\) otherwise. This realizes the maximum score with a unique winner.

Thus the stated problem with variable \(K\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, despite both promises.
