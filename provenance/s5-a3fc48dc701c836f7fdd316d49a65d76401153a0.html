The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix a finite rooted hierarchy \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over supported types \(t\) attached to leaves, additive valuations \(u_t\), positive weights \(w_i\), and per-capita supplies \(q_g\in[0,1]\) for each \(g\in G\). Each unit of type mass must receive an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\). Choose masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=q_g\). Under root-child-common valuations, does a complete allocation exist satisfying, for every ordered sibling pair \(j,k\), \(\frac{U_j}{w_j}\ge \frac{\sum_g u_j(g)b_{k,g}}{w_k}\), where \(U_j=\sum_{t\text{ below }j,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\) and \(b_{k,g}=\sum_{t\text{ below }k,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be an input rooted tree with \(n\) nodes and an internal root, and let \(G\) contain \(m\) goods. Each node \(i\) has a positive rational weight \(w_i\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type \(t\) is attached to a leaf; every leaf supports at least one type, and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).
Types have nonnegative rational additive valuations \(u_t\). Root-child-common valuations means that all types below any one child of the root have identical valuations. For each nonroot node \(j\), denote the common valuation below it by \(u_j\).
For each good \(g\), the input specifies a rational per-capita supply \(q_g\in[0,1]\). An allocation assigns an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), possibly empty, to each unit of population; a type's mass may be split among bundles.
Represent such an allocation by real masses \(x_{t,B}\geq0\). Completeness requires \(\sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=q_g\) for every good.
Write \(T(j)\) for the types attached below node \(j\), and define \(U_j=\sum_{t\in T(j),B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\) and \(b_{j,g}=\sum_{t\in T(j),B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\).
Require exact multilevel weighted envy-freeness: for every ordered pair of distinct siblings \(j,k\), \(U_j/w_j\geq\bigl(\sum_{g\in G}u_j(g)b_{k,g}\bigr)/w_k\).
Is deciding whether a complete allocation satisfying these inequalities exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,n,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length.
For finite agents under root-child-common valuations and arbitrary positive weights, multilevel weighted round robin computes a complete allocation satisfying sibling weighted envy-freeness up to removal of at most one good from the envied bundle in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24400v1).

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact feasibility is in \(\mathrm{P}\)

The decision problem is solvable in polynomial time in \((m,\tau,n,L)\). The key is that additive utilities and all envy inequalities depend only on good-by-good marginals. Every feasible collection of such marginals can be realized by splitting type masses among integral bundles.

1. Marginals can always be realized

Lemma. Let \(\mu>0\), and prescribe numbers \(y_g\in[0,\mu]\) for every \(g\in G\). There exist nonnegative bundle masses \(x_B\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_B=\mu,\qquad \sum_{B:g\in B}x_B=y_g\quad\text{for every }g.\)

Moreover, at most \(m+1\) bundles need positive mass.

Proof. Sort the distinct numbers in \(\{0,\mu\}\cup\{y_g:g\in G\}\), obtaining

\(\displaystyle 0=a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_r=\mu,\qquad r\leq m+1.\)

For each \(h\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\), assign mass \(a_h-a_{h-1}\) to the integral bundle

\(\displaystyle B_h=\{g\in G:y_g\geq a_h\}.\)

If a bundle appears repeatedly, add its assigned masses. The total mass telescopes to \(\mu\). For a good with \(y_g=a_s\), precisely the bundles indexed by \(h\leq s\) contain it, so its marginal mass is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{h=1}^{s}(a_h-a_{h-1})=a_s=y_g.\)

This also covers \(y_g=0\), using an empty sum. Thus all prescribed marginals are realized. \(\square\)

2. A polynomial-size linear program

Let \(r\) be the root, let \(C(i)\) denote the children of node \(i\), and define its population mass by

\(\displaystyle M_i=\sum_{t\in T(i)}\mu_t.\)

In particular, \(M_r=1\). Every \(M_i\) is positive because every leaf supports a positive-mass type.

Introduce a variable \(z_{i,g}\) for each node \(i\) and good \(g\). Its intended meaning is the mass receiving good \(g\) within the subtree rooted at \(i\).

Impose the following constraints:

  • Population bounds, for every node \(i\) and good \(g\):

\(\displaystyle 0\leq z_{i,g}\leq M_i.\)

  • Supply, for every good \(g\):

\(\displaystyle z_{r,g}=q_g.\)

  • Subtree consistency, for every internal node \(i\) and good \(g\):

\(\displaystyle z_{i,g}=\sum_{j\in C(i)}z_{j,g}.\)

  • Weighted envy-freeness, for every ordered pair of distinct siblings \(j,k\):

\(\displaystyle w_k\sum_{g\in G}u_j(g)z_{j,g}-w_j\sum_{g\in G}u_j(g)z_{k,g}\geq0.\)

These are rational linear constraints. The last constraint is exactly the required envy inequality after multiplication by the positive number \(w_jw_k\).

Root-child-common valuations ensure that, for every nonroot node \(j\), every type below \(j\) has valuation \(u_j\). Consequently, its aggregate utility is determined by its marginals:

\(\displaystyle U_j=\sum_{g\in G}u_j(g)b_{j,g}.\)

3. Proof of equivalence

A complete envy-free allocation gives a feasible LP point

Given an allocation \(x_{t,B}\), set

\(\displaystyle z_{i,g}=\sum_{t\in T(i)}\sum_{B:g\in B}x_{t,B}.\)

Nonnegativity and type completeness imply \(0\leq z_{i,g}\leq M_i\). Good completeness gives \(z_{r,g}=q_g\). The child subtrees partition the types below their parent, giving subtree consistency.

Finally, additivity and the common valuation below \(j\) give

\(\displaystyle U_j=\sum_{t\in T(j)}\sum_B x_{t,B}\sum_{g\in B}u_j(g)=\sum_g u_j(g)z_{j,g}.\)

Thus every required envy inequality becomes the corresponding LP constraint.

A feasible LP point gives a complete envy-free allocation

Conversely, suppose \(z\) satisfies the LP. For each type \(t\) attached to leaf \(\ell\), prescribe type marginals

\(\displaystyle y_{t,g}=\frac{\mu_t}{M_\ell}z_{\ell,g}.\)

The population bounds imply \(0\leq y_{t,g}\leq\mu_t\). Apply the lemma separately to each type, obtaining bundle masses \(x_{t,B}\) with total mass \(\mu_t\) and marginals \(y_{t,g}\).

At each leaf \(\ell\), the resulting marginal for good \(g\) is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T(\ell)}y_{t,g}=\frac{z_{\ell,g}}{M_\ell}\sum_{t\in T(\ell)}\mu_t=z_{\ell,g}.\)

Induction upward through the tree, using subtree consistency, shows that the resulting marginal at every node \(i\) equals \(z_{i,g}\). At the root this gives total supply \(q_g\), so the allocation is complete.

For every nonroot node \(j\), additivity and common valuations again give \(U_j=\sum_g u_j(g)z_{j,g}\). The LP envy constraints therefore establish all required inequalities.

This proves exact equivalence.

4. Running time and encoding length

If \(m=0\), assigning the empty bundle to every type is immediately feasible. Assume henceforth that \(m\geq1\).

Write

\(\displaystyle S=\sum_{i\text{ internal}}|C(i)|\bigl(|C(i)|-1\bigr)\)

for the number of ordered sibling pairs. Since \(\sum_i|C(i)|=n-1\),

\(\displaystyle S\leq(n-1)(n-2).\)

The LP has:

  • \(mn\) variables;
  • \(O(mn+S)=O(mn+n^2)\) constraints;
  • \(O(mn+mS)=O(mn^2)\) nonzero coefficient occurrences.

Each \(M_i\) has \(O(L)\) bits: a common denominator can be chosen as the product of the input mass denominators, whose binary length is bounded by the total input length. Since \(0<M_i\leq1\), its numerator also has \(O(L)\) bits. Products such as \(w_k u_j(g)\) likewise have \(O(L)\) bits.

Hence the LP’s sparse encoding length is

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(mn^2\bigl(L+\log(mn+2)\bigr)\right).\)

Computing subtree masses and obtaining the common valuation vectors takes polynomial time in \((m,\tau,n,L)\); constructing the LP also takes polynomial time.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming decides feasibility in a number of bit operations polynomial in the number of variables, constraints, and total encoding length. Its hypotheses hold here: all constraints are linear, all coefficients are rational and explicitly encoded, and all variables have finite bounds. Thus the overall running time can be stated as

\(\displaystyle \bigl(mn+n^2+\tau m+L\bigr)^{O(1)}.\)

A feasible rational solution can also be returned in polynomial time. Applying the lemma then produces an allocation with at most \(\tau(m+1)\) positive type–bundle entries, each of polynomial encoding length.

5. Feasibility is not automatic

Consider a root with two leaves, one type of mass \(1/2\) at each leaf, one good valued at \(1\) by both types, supply \(q_g=1\), and leaf weights \(w_1=1\), \(w_2=2\).

Completeness and the population bounds force \(b_{1,g}=b_{2,g}=1/2\). The second leaf’s envy inequality would require

\(\displaystyle \frac{1/2}{2}\geq\frac{1/2}{1},\)

which is false.

Thus the algorithm decides a substantive feasibility question. The cited [finite-agent result, Theorem 3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24400v1#S3.SS2), guarantees weighted envy-freeness up to one good; the exact decision result here follows instead from the marginal realization lemma and rational linear programming.
