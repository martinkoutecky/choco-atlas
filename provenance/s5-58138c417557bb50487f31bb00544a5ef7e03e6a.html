The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 2 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), acceptable pairs \(E\), strict rankings of \(J\)-types for each \(i\), and rankings with ties of \(I\)-types for each \(j\), find in time polynomial in the input size a stable mass matrix \(x\) with \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\ge (9/13)\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\). Require \(x_{ij}\ge0\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\), and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\); for every acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(R_{ij}\) and \(S_{ij}\) be the masses on each side unmatched or assigned to strictly worse types, and require \(R_{ij}S_{ij}=0\), so no positive masses on both sides can block.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be disjoint finite type supports, with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\); totals need not equal each other or sum to one. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) positive-mass types and their masses, not the universe of possible types. Put \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Mutual acceptability is specified by \(E\subseteq I\times J\). Each \(i\) strictly ranks its acceptable neighbors; each \(j\) ranks its acceptable neighbors in a total preorder, allowing ties. Every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched; indifference alone cannot block.
A feasible mass matrix has real entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\) on \(E\), zero elsewhere, with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq\nu_j\).
Writing \(\succ_i\) and \(\succ_j\) for strict preference, define \(R_{ij}=\mu_i-\sum_kx_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(S_{ij}=\nu_j-\sum_hx_{hj}+\sum_{h:i\succ_j h}x_{hj}\), with all sums restricted to acceptable pairs.
Call \(x\) stable when \(R_{ij}S_{ij}=0\) for every \((i,j)\in E\), including pairs carrying positive mass. Define \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}=\max\{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}:x\text{ is feasible and stable}\}\).

Can a stable rational mass matrix \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\geq\frac{9}{13}\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\) always be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete weakly stable marriage with incomplete lists and one-sided ties, a polynomial-time \(\frac{13}{9}\)-approximation is known ([Bauckholt–Pashkovich–Sanità](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.05391)); a later polynomial-time \(1+\frac1e\)-approximation improves it ([Lam–Plaxton](https://georgelam.home.ginormux.com/pub/LP19a-SODA.pdf)).
No result settling this approximation guarantee for the exact arbitrary-rational-mass model above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Masses are strictly positive on the listed supports; arbitrary rational totals are retained without normalization or a requirement that the two sides have equal total mass.
  • Rankings cover precisely the mutually acceptable neighbors, and every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched.
  • The residual masses are defined explicitly using unmatched mass and assignments to strictly worse types; tied assignments do not contribute.
  • The optimum ranges over real mass matrices, while the algorithm must output a rational matrix in binary encoding.
  • For the running-time convention, m denotes the number of acceptable pairs, tau denotes the combined support size, and L denotes the total input bit length.

Result

The requested guarantee is achievable in polynomial time. In fact, the construction below returns a stable rational mass matrix of value at least \(71/100\) of \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\), and \(71/100>9/13\).

The argument combines a polynomial-size discretization with exact stability repair. The discrete approximation theorem alone would not suffice: expanding binary-encoded masses into individuals can produce exponentially many individuals.

Write \(W(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\).

1. Algorithmic results used

We use two established results.

  • For discrete stable marriage with incomplete lists, strict preferences on one side, and ties on the other, a polynomial-time algorithm returns a weakly stable matching \(M\) satisfying \( |M|\geq\alpha |M^*| \), where \(M^*\) is a maximum weakly stable matching and \(\alpha=e/(e+1)\). This is [Lam–Plaxton, Theorem 4.1](https://georgelam.home.ginormux.com/pub/LP19a-SODA.pdf).
  • A bipartite stable allocation with strict preferences on both sides and rational vertex quotas can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Baïou–Balinski give a strongly polynomial algorithm; see [their stable-allocation theorem](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.27.3.485.310), or [Dean–Munshi’s algorithm and formulation](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

The second result applies to the stability definition in the question. In the standard formulation, give an edge between vertices of quotas \(b_a,b_b\) the capacity \(\min(b_a,b_b)\). If that edge is saturated, at least one endpoint assigns its entire quota to the other endpoint, so its corresponding rejection quantity is zero. Thus the standard exclusion of saturated edges from blocking does not change our stability condition. Unassigned quantities can be represented by least-preferred dummy partners, as in the cited formulation.

All uses below have bipartite graphs, rational quotas, and the requisite preference restrictions.

2. Stable optima lie on the capacity grid

Temporarily allow zero quotas, omitting their types when constructing discrete instances.

Lemma 1. If every quota is an integer multiple of a positive rational \(q\), then some maximum stable mass matrix has every entry an integer multiple of \(q\).

Proof. For a feasible matrix, every summand in \(R_{ij}\) and \(S_{ij}\) is nonnegative. Consequently, \(R_{ij}=0\) is equivalent to both:

  • row \(i\) is saturated;
  • every entry in that row at a partner strictly worse than \(j\) is zero.

Similarly, \(S_{ij}=0\) is equivalent to column saturation together with zero allocation to partners strictly worse than \(i\).

For each acceptable edge, choose one of the two equalities \(R_{ij}=0\) or \(S_{ij}=0\). The matrices satisfying all chosen equalities form a face, possibly empty, of the feasible transportation polytope: the restrictions merely make some quota inequalities tight and some nonnegativity inequalities tight.

The stable set is the finite union of these faces. After division by \(q\), the transportation polytope has integer quotas and integral vertices. Indeed, its constraint matrix is the bipartite vertex-edge incidence matrix, with nonnegativity constraints; changing the signs of the rows on one side yields an oriented incidence matrix, which is totally unimodular. Every face therefore also has integral vertices.

The stable set is nonempty. To see this, expand the integer quotas into individuals, refine preferences strictly while preserving all strict type comparisons, and compute a discrete stable matching. Its aggregate is stable: positive rejection quantities on both sides would supply two individuals who strictly prefer each other to their current partners.

The stable set is compact. A maximizing point belongs to one of the finitely many faces, and maximizing the linear function \(W\) over that face gives an integral vertex after scaling. Multiplying by \(q\) proves the claim. \(\square\)

In particular, for arbitrary rational quotas, a common denominator gives a rational optimal stable matrix.

3. Two cloning facts

Fix, for every type \(j\), an order on its individual copies. Every copy of \(i\) ranks acceptable copies first by \(i\)’s strict order of their original types, and then by this fixed copy order.

Copies of \(j\) initially rank copies of \(I\) solely by \(j\)’s original total preorder. Thus ties remain only on the \(J\)-side.

Lemma 2. For integer quotas, the maximum discrete weakly stable matching cardinality in this fixed clone instance equals the maximum stable mass.

Proof. Every weakly stable matching aggregates to a stable integer matrix. If \(R_{ij}>0\), there is an unmatched copy of \(i\), or a copy assigned to a type strictly worse than \(j\). If also \(S_{ij}>0\), there is an analogous copy of \(j\). Those two copies strictly prefer each other, contradicting weak stability.

Conversely, let \(x\) be a stable integer matrix. For each type \(j\), assign its copies, in their fixed order, to blocks of original \(I\)-types in nonincreasing order of \(j\)’s preference. Use exactly \(x_{ij}\) copies for type \(i\), and put unmatched copies last. Match these slots to distinct copies of the appropriate \(i\).

Suppose a copy of \(i\) strictly prefers a copy of \(j\) to its assigned partner.

If its assigned partner has a different original type, or it is unmatched, then \(R_{ij}>0\). A strict improvement by the \(j\)-copy would also imply \(S_{ij}>0\), contradicting mass stability.

If its assigned partner is another copy of \(j\), the desired copy has an earlier index. By the ordering of \(j\)’s slots, that earlier copy is assigned to a type weakly preferred to \(i\). It therefore cannot strictly prefer the proposing copy.

The resulting matching is weakly stable. Lemma 1 supplies an integer optimum, proving equality of the two optimal values. \(\square\)

We also need a version with strict preferences on both sides.

Lemma 3. Suppose both sides have strict type preferences. Expand integer quotas into copies, and rank copies by original type first and by increasing copy index second, on both sides. Every stable integer mass matrix can be realized as a stable matching in this fixed, fully strict clone instance.

Proof. At each original type, order its copies by the preference rank of their assigned original partner type, with unmatched copies last. Within every original pair, match the two corresponding blocks in increasing index order.

Consider a purported blocking pair.

  • If both copies strictly improve their original partner types, it gives a mass blocking pair.
  • If the \(I\)-copy keeps the same original partner type while the \(J\)-copy improves its original partner type, the desired \(J\)-copy must have an earlier index. But the sorting of \(J\)-copies puts partners of the desired \(I\)-type before strictly worse types, contradicting that earlier index.
  • If the \(J\)-copy keeps the same original partner type while the \(I\)-copy improves its original partner type, the sorting of \(I\)-copies gives the symmetric contradiction.
  • If neither original partner type changes, the order-preserving pairing within the block prevents both endpoints from preferring the other copy.

Unmatched status is worse than every acceptable original type, so the same cases cover unmatched copies. \(\square\)

The fixed copy preferences in Lemma 3 are important: when copies are added or removed, the preference lists of surviving copies are restrictions of the same lists.

4. Stability repair loses at most the changed mass

We first establish the discrete facts underlying the repair bound.

Lemma 4. In a discrete instance with strict preferences:

  • all stable matchings match the same set of individuals;
  • adding one individual changes the common stable matching cardinality by an amount between zero and one.

Proof.

For the first statement, suppose an individual is matched in a stable matching \(M\) but unmatched in another stable matching \(N\). Start at that individual and follow its alternating path in \(M\cup N\).

Its \(M\)-partner must prefer its \(N\)-partner, since otherwise the first edge blocks \(N\). That \(N\)-partner must prefer its own \(M\)-partner, since otherwise the next edge blocks \(M\). These strict comparisons propagate along the path.

The path cannot terminate at an individual unmatched in either matching: the propagated comparison would make the last edge block that matching. The path cannot form a cycle because its starting vertex has degree one. This is a contradiction. Interchanging \(M,N\) proves equality of the matched sets.

For the second statement, let the newly added individual’s side propose. Initialize deferred acceptance at any old stable matching and activate the new, unmatched proposer.

Responders retain their preferred proposal, so their partners only improve. A displaced old proposer continues below its former partner. Any responder ranked above that proposer’s initial partner already preferred its own initial partner, by initial stability, and therefore continues to reject that proposer. Initially unmatched old proposers likewise cannot obtain an acceptance.

The process terminates because each active proposer scans its list only forward. At termination, every pair preferred by a proposer to its final assignment has either been rejected during the process or was already excluded by initial stability; responders’ improvement makes those rejections permanent. Thus the final matching is stable.

Swaps preserve cardinality, and an acceptance by an unmatched responder increases it by one. Starting with one active proposer, the process therefore produces a stable matching of size equal to the old size or one larger. The first statement makes this the cardinality of every stable matching in the enlarged instance. \(\square\)

Deletion is the reverse operation. Hence deleting \(k\) individuals reduces stable cardinality by at most \(k\).

Lemma 5. Fix strict type preferences on both sides. Let \(x,y\) be rational stable allocations for quota vectors \(b,c\), respectively. Then

\( |W(x)-W(y)|\leq\sum_v |b_v-c_v|. \)

Proof. Choose a positive rational \(q\) such that all quotas and all entries of \(x,y\) are integer multiples of \(q\). Lemma 3 realizes the scaled allocations in fully strict clone instances with fixed copy preferences.

Transform one instance into the other by adding and deleting copies. The number of such operations is \(q^{-1}\sum_v|b_v-c_v|\). Lemma 4 bounds the cardinality change by this number. Multiplication by \(q\) proves the inequality. \(\square\)

For preferences with one-sided ties, we need a bound on the optimum rather than on arbitrary stable allocations.

Lemma 6. Suppose \(b'\leq b\) componentwise and both quota vectors are rational. Let \(O(b)\) denote maximum stable mass. Then

\( O(b')\geq O(b)-\sum_v(b_v-b'_v). \)

Proof. Choose a rational optimal allocation for \(b\), using Lemma 1, and choose a common rational unit \(q\) for its entries and both quota vectors. Lemma 2 realizes it as a weakly stable clone matching.

Refine every \(J\)-copy’s ties so that its current partner, when present, comes first within its tie. This makes the matching stable under fully strict preferences: a newly strict preference inside a tie cannot favor an alternative over the current partner.

Delete the required copies, whose number is \(q^{-1}\sum_v(b_v-b'_v)\). Lemma 4 gives a stable matching in the restricted strict instance losing at most that many edges. Aggregation is stable for the original type preferences, because strict improvement at the type level remains strict in the refined lists.

Multiplying by \(q\) proves the bound. \(\square\)

The fine cloning in these proofs is only a proof device. The algorithm below does not construct it.

5. A polynomial-size rounded instance

If \(E\) is empty, return the zero matrix. Otherwise, let \(B>0\) be the maximum feasible mass, ignoring stability. Compute \(B\) by rational maximum flow.

For every stable allocation \(x\),

\( B\leq 2W(x). \)

Indeed, let \(C\) be the set of saturated types. Every acceptable edge has an endpoint in \(C\): two unsaturated endpoints would have positive rejection quantities and would block. Thus \(C\) is a vertex cover. For any feasible \(z\),

\( W(z)\leq\sum_{v\in C}\sum_{e\ni v}z_e\leq\sum_{v\in C}b_v =\sum_{v\in C}\sum_{e\ni v}x_e\leq 2W(x). \)

Taking a maximum feasible \(z\) proves the assertion. Consequently,

\( \mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\geq B/2. \)

Now cap each quota at \(2B\), and denote the capped quotas by \(b_v\). This preserves both feasibility and stability:

  • every originally feasible allocation has total mass at most \(B\), so no individual row or column uses more than \(B\);
  • at a type whose quota was capped, its unassigned mass remains at least \(B>0\), so its rejection quantity remains positive for every acceptable pair.

Hence the capped instance has exactly the original stable allocations.

Set

\( \delta=\frac{B}{200\tau},\qquad b^-_v=\delta\left\lfloor\frac{b_v}{\delta}\right\rfloor,\qquad D=\sum_v(b_v-b^-_v). \)

Then

\( D<\tau\delta=\frac{B}{200}\leq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}}{100}. \)

By Lemma 6, the rounded optimum \(O^-\) satisfies

\( O^-\geq\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}-D. \)

Expand the rounded quotas into copies of mass \(\delta\), using the one-sided preference construction of Lemma 2. Each type has at most

\( b_v/\delta\leq 400\tau \)

copies. There are therefore at most \(400\tau^2\) individuals.

Lemma 2 identifies the discrete optimum with \(O^-/\delta\). Apply Lam–Plaxton to obtain a weakly stable matching \(M\) satisfying

\( \delta|M|\geq\alpha O^-\geq\alpha(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}-D). \)

This invocation is valid: lists are incomplete, all acceptable partners are strictly preferable to being unmatched, the \(I\)-copies have strict lists, and ties occur only in the \(J\)-copies’ lists.

6. Restore the exact quotas

Regard each rounded copy as a separate segment type of quota \(\delta\).

Refine the \(J\)-segments’ ties by putting their partner in \(M\), when present, first within its tie. Together with the already strict \(I\)-lists, this gives strict preferences under which the allocation assigning mass \(\delta\) to every edge of \(M\) is stable.

For each original type \(v\), add one residual segment of quota \(r_v=b_v-b^-_v\), omitting it when \(r_v=0\).

Include every acceptable segment pair arising from an original acceptable pair. Extend the strict preferences to the residual segments as follows:

  • preserve all existing comparisons between rounded segments;
  • preserve every strict comparison between original types;
  • order the remaining choices arbitrarily.

Such extensions exist: new segments can be inserted within the appropriate original preference classes. Give new segments arbitrary strict refinements of their original type preferences.

Compute a rational stable allocation \(y\) in this expanded, fully strict instance using the stable-allocation algorithm from Section 1.

Compare it with the old allocation on the same preference system, assigning quota zero to every residual segment. Their quota vectors differ by total mass \(D\). Lemma 5 gives

\( W(y)\geq\delta|M|-D. \)

Aggregate \(y\) by original type to obtain \(x\). This matrix is feasible for the capped quotas and stable for the original preferences.

To verify stability explicitly, suppose \(R_{ij}(x)>0\) and \(S_{ij}(x)>0\). Some \(i\)-segment has either unassigned mass or positive allocation to an original type strictly worse than \(j\). Some \(j\)-segment similarly has unassigned mass or positive allocation to an original type strictly worse than \(i\). Since all strict original comparisons were preserved, both segments have positive rejection quantities for their mutual edge. This contradicts stability of \(y\).

This argument also covers original pairs carrying positive mass. Finally, Section 5’s capping equivalence makes \(x\) stable and feasible for the original, uncapped quotas.

Combining the bounds yields

\( W(x)\geq\alpha(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}-D)-D \geq\left(\frac{99}{100}\alpha-\frac{1}{100}\right)\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}. \)

The exponential series gives \(e>1+1+1/2+1/6=8/3\), so \(\alpha=e/(e+1)>8/11\). Therefore

\( W(x)\geq\left(\frac{99}{100}\cdot\frac{8}{11}-\frac{1}{100}\right)\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty} =\frac{71}{100}\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty} >\frac{9}{13}\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}. \)

7. Running time and rational encoding

The rounded instance has at most \(400\tau^2\) individuals. Each original acceptable edge produces at most \((400\tau)^2\) clone edges. Adding residual segments changes this bound to at most \((400\tau+1)^2\) edges per original acceptable edge. Both transformed instances therefore have encoding size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Rational maximum flow computes \(B\) with polynomial bit length. The quantities \(\delta\), \(b^-_v\), and \(r_v\) also have polynomial bit length. All preference constructions are explicit and polynomial-size.

Lam–Plaxton runs in polynomial time on the rounded discrete instance. The exact strict stable-allocation algorithm runs in polynomial time on the segment instance with rational quotas, and returns a rational allocation with polynomial encoding length. Aggregation requires only polynomially many rational additions.

Thus the complete construction runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) and returns the required stable rational mass matrix.
