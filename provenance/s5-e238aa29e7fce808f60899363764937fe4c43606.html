The continuous mirror question, as graded

Let Τ be a finite set of expert types with masses μ_t, and let each type t have a finite report alphabet R_t. On each task, ω is drawn from an unknown Bernoulli prior; conditional on ω, agents of type t independently draw signals from a type-specific law and report their posterior beliefs. In the nonatomic population, the observed profile is q with q_{t,r}=μ_t Pr(r_i=r | ω). Given T IID task samples (q,ω), determine the minimax sample complexity for returning ĥf such that, with probability at least 1-δ, E_{(q,ω)}[(ĥf(q)-Pr(ω=1|q))^2]≤ε, uniformly over Bayes-compatible signal laws.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types; \(L\) is the bit length of this list.
Fix \(m\geq2\). An unknown information structure consists of a prior \(p\in(0,1)\), finite numerical report alphabets \(R_t\subseteq[0,1]\) with \(|R_t|\leq m\), and conditional probability vectors \(a_{t,w}\) on \(R_t\), for \(w\in\{0,1\}\).
Bayes compatibility means that every \(r\in R_t\) has positive unconditional probability and satisfies \(r=\frac{p a_{t,1}(r)}{p a_{t,1}(r)+(1-p)a_{t,0}(r)}\). This describes truthful posterior reports, allowing distinct private signals to yield the same report.
On each task, independently across tasks, draw \(\omega\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\). Agents' reports are independent conditional on \(\omega\); the observed nonatomic profile is exactly \(q=(q_t)_t\), where \(q_t=\mu_t\sum_{r\in R_t}a_{t,\omega}(r)\delta_r\) and \(\delta_r\) is the point mass at the numerical report \(r\).
The learner knows the society and \(m\), but not the prior, alphabets, or conditional laws; it receives \(T\) IID labeled samples \((q,\omega)\). Profiles reveal exact report values and masses, without measurement noise. Computation uses exact real arithmetic; any operation bounds are expressed in \(m,\tau,L,T,\varepsilon,\delta\).
For \(\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)\), let \(T^*(\varepsilon,\delta;m,\mathcal T,\mu)\) be the least sample count for which some learner, uniformly over these information structures, returns a measurable \(\hat f:q\mapsto[0,1]\) satisfying \(\mathbb E[(\hat f(q)-\Pr(\omega=1\mid q))^2]\leq\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over training samples and learner randomness; the expectation uses a fresh task.

What is the minimax sample complexity \(T^*(\varepsilon,\delta;m,\mathcal T,\mu)\), including its dependence on \(\varepsilon,\delta,m,\tau\) and the supported masses?

For finitely many conditionally independent experts, \(n\geq2\), the corresponding sample complexity is between \(\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\) and \(O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/(\varepsilon\delta)))\), independently of \(n\) and signal alphabet size ([Lin–Chen](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/70de9e3948645a1be2de657f14d85c6d-Paper-Conference.pdf)).
No result settling the exact nonatomic observation model above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized positive supported masses to sum to one and defined L as the bit length of the society support description.
  • Restricted the unknown Bernoulli prior to (0,1), as in the conditional independence model.
  • Made report alphabets numerical subsets of [0,1], imposed a known maximum size m≥2, and stated Bayes compatibility explicitly, including exclusion of reports with zero unconditional probability.
  • Kept the prior, report alphabets, and conditional laws unknown to the learner, matching sample-only access to the information structure.
  • Specified profiles as exact measures on numerical report values, including exact masses and no observation noise.
  • Allowed τ=1 because a positive nonatomic mass already contains multiple experts; the finite-expert requirement n≥2 does not require multiple types.
  • Specified a single fixed information structure across IID tasks, measurable predictions in [0,1], and confidence over both training samples and learner randomness.
  • Used exact real arithmetic because unrestricted Bayes-compatible laws can produce observations with no finite binary encoding.

2. Exact profiles and homogeneous observations

For a fixed information structure, define
$$ Q^w_t=\mu_t\sum_{r\in R_t}a_{t,w}(r)\delta_r, \qquad w\in\{0,1\}. $$
Every task produces exactly $Q^\omega$.

If $Q^0\neq Q^1$, the profile determines the state, so the target regression function equals $0$ at $Q^0$ and $1$ at $Q^1$.

If $Q^0=Q^1$, positivity of $\mu_t$ gives
$$ a_{t,0}(r)=a_{t,1}(r) $$
for every supported report. Their common value is positive because the unconditional report probability is positive. Bayes compatibility therefore gives $r=p$. Consequently, each alphabet is the singleton $\{p\}$.

Write
$$ H_s=(\mu_t\delta_s)_{t\in\mathcal T}. $$
The only structures with coincident conditional profiles are the uninformative structures $Q^0=Q^1=H_p$, whose target value is $p$.

It follows that observing a nonhomogeneous labeled profile gives a perfect predictor: predict its label at that profile and the opposite label elsewhere. Observing both labels also gives a perfect predictor; if the corresponding profiles coincide, their common numerical report reveals $p$.

Homogeneous profiles in informative structures

Suppose $Q^0=H_s$ with $0<s<1$. Then every type assigns state-$0$ probability one to report $s$. Any other supported report has positive probability only in state $1$, so Bayes compatibility forces it to equal $1$. Compatibility at $s$ gives
$$ a_{t,1}(s)=\frac{s(1-p)}{p(1-s)}, \qquad a_{t,1}(1)=\frac{p-s}{p(1-s)}. $$
Thus $p\geq s$. Equality gives the uninformative structure; every $p>s$ gives a valid informative structure, using the alphabet $\{s,1\}$.

Similarly, if $Q^1=H_s$, then $p\leq s$ and
$$ a_{t,0}(s)=\frac{p(1-s)}{(1-p)s}, \qquad a_{t,0}(0)=\frac{s-p}{(1-p)s}. $$
Every $p<s$ is feasible, using $\{0,s\}$.

These constructions use identical laws for all types and therefore work for every society.

Endpoint reports

Bayes compatibility implies
$$ a_{t,1}(0)=0, \qquad a_{t,0}(1)=0. $$
Hence any realizable profile containing report $0$ identifies state $0$, and any containing report $1$ identifies state $1$. A realizable profile cannot contain both.

In particular, homogeneous endpoint observations introduce no ambiguity: $H_0$ identifies state $0$, and $H_1$ identifies state $1$. This addresses the endpoint omission identified by the verifier.

3. Exact feasibility for positive sample counts

Fix $T\geq1$ and write $a=\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. Only histories consisting of one homogeneous profile $H_s$, with $0<s<1$, and one repeated label can remain ambiguous.

For the uninformative structure with prior $s$, the all-$0$ and all-$1$ history probabilities are
$$ A=(1-s)^T, \qquad B=s^T. $$

Define the conflict conditions
$$ C_0(s):\quad s-a>\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-s}}, $$
and
$$ C_1(s):\quad 1-s-a>\frac{a}{\sqrt{s}}. $$

Necessity

On the all-$0$ history at $H_s$, let $X_0$ denote the learner’s random prediction at $H_s$. Success under the uninformative structure requires
$$ |X_0-s|\leq a. $$

For an informative alternative with $Q^0=H_s$ and prior $p>s$, its risk is at least
$$ (1-p)X_0^2. $$
If $C_0(s)$ holds, every value in the uninformative success interval fails this alternative for all $p>s$ sufficiently close to $s$. Indeed,
$$ (1-s)(s-a)^2>\varepsilon, $$
and this strict inequality persists for $p$ sufficiently close to $s$.

Let $z_0$ be the probability of producing an uninformatively successful value on this fixed history. Uniform validity for the informative alternatives implies
$$ (1-p)^Tz_0\leq\delta. $$
Letting $p$ decrease to $s$ yields $Az_0\leq\delta$.

The symmetric argument gives $Bz_1\leq\delta$ whenever $C_1(s)$ holds. Since $T\geq1$, the two histories are disjoint. Therefore a necessary condition is
$$ \mathbf 1_{C_0(s)}(A-\delta)_+ + \mathbf 1_{C_1(s)}(B-\delta)_+ \leq\delta \qquad\text{for every }s\in(0,1), $$
where $x_+=\max\{x,0\}$.

Sufficiency

All nonambiguous histories are resolved perfectly as in Section 2.

On an ambiguous all-$0$ history:

  • If $C_0(s)$ is false, predict $x_0=\max\{0,s-a\}$ at $H_s$.
  • If $C_0(s)$ holds, predict $s-a$ with probability

$$ \alpha_0=\min\{1,\delta/A\}, $$
and predict $0$ otherwise.

  • Predict $1$ on other profiles.

In the first case,
$$ |x_0-s|\leq a, \qquad (1-s)x_0^2\leq\varepsilon, $$
so the rule succeeds for both the uninformative structure and every compatible informative alternative.

In the second case, an informative alternative can fail only on the branch of probability $\alpha_0$, and only if this history occurs. Its failure probability is therefore at most
$$ (1-p)^T\alpha_0\leq A\alpha_0\leq\delta. $$
For the uninformative structure, the failure contribution is exactly $(A-\delta)_+$.

Use the symmetric rule on an all-$1$ history: randomize between $s+a$ and $1$ when $C_1(s)$ holds, use $\min\{1,s+a\}$ otherwise, and predict $0$ elsewhere. Its uninformative failure contribution is $(B-\delta)_+$.

Thus the displayed scalar condition is sufficient as well as necessary.

Evaluating the condition

Putting $y=\sqrt{1-s}$ shows
$$ C_0(s)\iff y(1-y)>a. $$
Since $y(1-y)\leq1/4$, neither conflict can occur when $\varepsilon\geq1/16$. This proves the deterministic one-sample guarantee.

For $\varepsilon<1/16$, define
$$ c=a+\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4a}}2. $$
Solving the quadratic inequality gives
$$ C_0(s)\iff s\in(b,c), \qquad C_1(s)\iff s\in(1-c,1-b). $$
Because $b+c=1+2a$, their intersection is $(b,1-b)$ when $b<1/2$, and is empty otherwise.

With one active conflict, the scalar condition is equivalent to its history probability being at most $2\delta$. With both active, it is equivalent to
$$ A\leq2\delta, \qquad B\leq2\delta, \qquad A+B\leq3\delta. $$
To verify the latter equivalence, if both $A$ and $B$ exceed $\delta$, the scalar condition becomes $A+B\leq3\delta$; if just one exceeds $\delta$, it becomes the corresponding bound by $2\delta$; if neither does, it is automatic.

The supremum of an active individual history probability is $(1-b)^T$. On the overlap, convexity of
$$ s\longmapsto (1-s)^T+s^T $$
gives supremum $(1-b)^T+b^T$. These suprema produce precisely $D_T(\varepsilon)$, including equality at the feasibility threshold.

4. Zero-sample geometry of interior profiles

Call a profile interior if all its atoms lie in $(0,1)$. Normalize its type distributions by dividing their masses by $\mu_t$.

For one type with conditional distribution $d(r)$, define
$$ K=\sum_r d(r)\frac{r}{1-r}, \qquad J=\sum_r d(r)\frac{1-r}{r}, $$
and
$$ u=\frac{K}{1+K}, \qquad v=\frac1{1+J}. $$

If this distribution is observed in state $0$, compatibility gives
$$ a_{t,1}(r)=\frac{1-p}{p}\,d(r)\frac{r}{1-r} $$
at every observed report. Their total probability is at most one, so $p\geq u$.

If it is observed in state $1$, the symmetric calculation gives $p\leq v$.

Cauchy–Schwarz, applied with weights $d(r)$, gives
$$ KJ = \left(\sum_r d(r)\frac{r}{1-r}\right) \left(\sum_r d(r)\frac{1-r}{r}\right) \geq \left(\sum_r d(r)\right)^2 =1. $$
Hence $v\leq u$.

If both conditional profiles are interior, no supported endpoint report exists. Every supported interior report has positive probability in both states, so their supports coincide. Normalization then gives, for every type,
$$ u(Q^0)=p, \qquad v(Q^1)=p, \qquad u(Q^1)\geq p. $$

A measurable integer coordinate

Fix an ordering of the supported types. For an interior profile with some type having at least two atoms, choose the first such type, and let $\ell<h$ be its two smallest reports. Put
$$ \beta= \frac{h/(1-h)}{\ell/(1-\ell)}>1, \qquad z=\frac{d(h)}{d(\ell)}. $$
Define the unique integer $j(q)$ by
$$ \beta^{j(q)}\leq z<\beta^{j(q)+1}. $$

For two interior conditional profiles of an informative structure, the selected type and reports are identical. Bayes compatibility gives
$$ \frac{a_{t,1}(h)}{a_{t,1}(\ell)} = \beta\frac{a_{t,0}(h)}{a_{t,0}(\ell)}, $$
so
$$ j(Q^1)=j(Q^0)+1. $$

If an interior profile has only one atom per type but is not homogeneous, set $j(q)=0$. Such a profile cannot belong to a structure with both conditional profiles interior: otherwise the singleton supports would coincide across states, and Section 2 would force homogeneity.

The coordinate is measurable. For each integer, its defining condition is a finite comparison of continuous functions on the relevant finite-atom stratum. It can also be found using finitely many exact multiplications and comparisons, although no uniform running-time bound for this calculation is asserted.

5. Randomized ramps give zero-sample upper bounds

The following construction handles all nonhomogeneous interior profiles.

Suppose there are nondecreasing functions
$$ g_0,\ldots,g_k:[0,1]\longrightarrow[0,1], \qquad g_0=0,\quad g_k=1, $$
such that
$$ (1-p)g_i(p)^2+p(1-g_{i+1}(p))^2\leq\varepsilon $$
for every $p\in[0,1]$ and $0\leq i<k$.

Choose one random integer $S$ uniformly from $\{0,\ldots,k\}$. At a nonhomogeneous interior profile, select the type used for the coordinate, or the first type if all distributions are singletons, compute its $u(q)$, and predict
$$ g_{(j(q)+S)\bmod(k+1)}(u(q)). $$
Profiles containing endpoints are classified perfectly.

If both conditional profiles are interior, their indices differ by one. On each of the $k$ phases that does not wrap from $k$ to $0$, the risk is at most
$$ (1-p)g_i(p)^2+p(1-g_{i+1}(p))^2, $$
because $u(Q^0)=p$, $u(Q^1)\geq p$, and $g_{i+1}$ is nondecreasing. Thus success probability is at least $k/(k+1)$.

If only the state-$0$ profile is interior, the other profile is classified perfectly. For phases $0,\ldots,k-1$,
$$ (1-p)g_i(u(q))^2 \leq (1-u(q))g_i(u(q))^2 \leq\varepsilon. $$
If only the state-$1$ profile is interior, phases $1,\ldots,k$ succeed because $p\leq u(q)$ and
$$ p(1-g_i(u(q)))^2 \leq u(q)(1-g_i(u(q)))^2 \leq\varepsilon. $$
Again, at least $k$ phases succeed.

Homogeneous profiles require a separate rule, provided below.

Two phases

Take $k=1$, with $g_0=0$ and $g_1=1$. The edge risk is zero, so every informative structure whose conditional profiles are nonhomogeneous succeeds with probability at least $1/2$.

Three phases

Take $k=2$ and
$$ g_1(p)=g(p)=\frac14+\frac p2. $$
Both edge risks are at most $1/8$. For example,
$$ \frac18-(1-p)g(p)^2 = \frac{(p-\tfrac12)^2(p+1)}4 \geq0. $$
The other bound follows by replacing $p$ with $1-p$.

For homogeneous profiles, predict $g(s)$ deterministically. The informative risks are bounded by $1/8$ using the same inequalities and the restrictions $p\geq s$ or $p\leq s$. The uninformative risk satisfies
$$ (g(s)-s)^2 = \left(\frac14-\frac s2\right)^2 \leq\frac1{16}. $$
Consequently, for every $\varepsilon\geq1/8$, zero samples suffice whenever $\delta\geq1/3$.

Four phases

Put
$$ h=\sqrt2-1, \qquad c=h^2=3-2\sqrt2, \qquad b=1-h, \qquad a_0=h-\frac b2. $$
Take $k=3$ and
$$ g_1(p)=a_0+bp, \qquad g_2(p)=1-g_1(1-p). $$
Both functions are increasing and take values in $[0,1]$, since $a_0>0$ and $a_0+b=\sqrt2/2<1$.

Direct expansion gives
$$ c-p(1-g_1(p))^2 = 2c(2-p)(p-\tfrac12)^2, $$
and
$$ c-(1-p)g_2(p)^2 = 2c(p+1)(p-\tfrac12)^2. $$
For the middle edge,
$$ (1-p)g_1(p)^2+p(1-g_2(p))^2 = a_0^2+(4a_0b+b^2)p(1-p). $$
Its value at $p=1/2$ is $h^2=c$, and therefore
$$ c-\bigl((1-p)g_1(p)^2+p(1-g_2(p))^2\bigr) = (4a_0b+b^2)(p-\tfrac12)^2 \geq0. $$

Use the preceding deterministic homogeneous rule, whose risk is at most $1/8<c$. Thus zero samples suffice for every $\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_3$ whenever $\delta\geq1/4$.

6. Homogeneous profiles determine the small-error zero-sample thresholds

For a zero-sample learner, let $X_s=\hat f(H_s)$. Define three closed intervals:
$$ U_s=[s-a,s+a]\cap[0,1], $$
$$ L_s=\left[0,\min\left\{1,\frac a{\sqrt{1-s}}\right\}\right], $$
and
$$ R_s=\left[\max\left\{0,1-\frac a{\sqrt s}\right\},1\right]. $$

A value in $U_s$ succeeds for the uninformative structure. A value in $L_s$ succeeds for every informative structure with $Q^0=H_s$, because $1-p\leq1-s$. A value in $R_s$ succeeds for every informative structure with $Q^1=H_s$, because $p\leq s$.

The threshold $\varepsilon_H$

If $a<(\sqrt2-1)/2$, then at $s=1/2$ the intervals $L_s,U_s,R_s$ are pairwise disjoint. Indeed,
$$ a\sqrt2<\frac12-a, \qquad \frac12+a<1-a\sqrt2. $$
Choose informative alternatives with priors $1/2+\eta$ and $1/2-\eta$. For sufficiently small $\eta>0$, their necessary success intervals at $H_{1/2}$ remain disjoint from each other and from $U_{1/2}$.

With no samples, the prediction distribution at this common profile is identical under all three structures. Each necessary success event must have probability at least $1-\delta$, so
$$ 3(1-\delta)\leq1. $$
Thus zero samples require $\delta\geq2/3$ when $\varepsilon<\varepsilon_H$.

This is sufficient: at each homogeneous interior profile, choose uniformly among $0,s,1$. Each of the three kinds of structure succeeds on at least one branch. Combine this with the two-phase rule of Section 5 for nonhomogeneous profiles. Every structure succeeds with probability at least $1/3$.

The threshold $1/2$

For $\varepsilon_H\leq\varepsilon<1/8$, at least one of $U_s\cap L_s$ and $U_s\cap R_s$ is nonempty for every $s$.

To verify this, both intersections could be empty only if both conflict conditions $C_0(s)$ and $C_1(s)$ held. Their intersection is nonempty precisely when $b<1/2$. Algebraically,
$$ b<\frac12 \iff 2a<\sqrt{1-4a} \iff a<\frac{\sqrt2-1}{2}. $$
This is excluded by $\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_H$.

If $U_s\cap L_s$ is nonempty, choose a point in that intersection and the value $1$, each with probability $1/2$. If only $U_s\cap R_s$ is nonempty, choose a point in that intersection and the value $0$, each with probability $1/2$. Each of $U_s,L_s,R_s$ receives probability at least $1/2$. Combined with the two-phase rule, this proves sufficiency for $\delta\geq1/2$, including equality at $\varepsilon_H$.

For necessity whenever $\varepsilon<1/8$, use two informative structures sharing $H_{1/2}$, with priors $1/2+\eta$ and $1/2-\eta$ in the two orientations. Their necessary success intervals are
$$ X_{1/2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{1/2-\eta}}, \qquad X_{1/2}\geq1-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{1/2-\eta}}. $$
For sufficiently small $\eta>0$, these are disjoint. Hence $\delta\geq1/2$ is necessary.

7. Chains of informative structures give further lower bounds

For an integer $k\geq1$, define
$$ e_k= \min_{x_0,\ldots,x_k\in[0,1]} \max_{0\leq i<k} \frac{x_i^2+(1-x_{i+1})^2}{2}. $$
The minimum exists by continuity on a compact set. We may impose $x_0=0$ and $x_k=1$: these replacements can only decrease the first and last edge costs.

Chain lower-bound lemma

If $\varepsilon<e_k$, zero-sample feasibility requires
$$ \delta\geq\frac1k. $$

Proof. Choose reports
$$ r_-=\frac12-\eta, \qquad r_+=\frac12+\eta, $$
where $0<\eta<\min\{1/2,e_k-\varepsilon\}$. Put
$$ \beta=\frac{r_+/(1-r_+)}{r_-/(1-r_-)}>1. $$
For each integer $i$, define a distribution $d_i$ by
$$ \frac{d_i(r_+)}{d_i(r_-)}=\beta^i. $$
Use $d_i$ identically for all types, producing a profile $Q_i$.

Define
$$ K_i=\sum_{r\in\{r_-,r_+\}}d_i(r)\frac r{1-r}, \qquad p_i=\frac{K_i}{1+K_i}. $$
The Bayes tilt of $d_i$ is
$$ \frac{d_i(r)\,r/(1-r)}{K_i}. $$
Its report-probability ratio is $\beta^{i+1}$, so it equals $d_{i+1}$. Therefore $(Q_i,Q_{i+1})$ are the conditional profiles of a valid two-report information structure with prior $p_i$.

Since $K_i$ is a positive weighted average of the two report odds,
$$ r_-<p_i<r_+, \qquad |p_i-1/2|<\eta. $$

Fix any realization of the learner’s randomness and write $x_i=\hat f(Q_i)$. Success on structure $i$ means
$$ (1-p_i)x_i^2+p_i(1-x_{i+1})^2\leq\varepsilon. $$
The corresponding balanced edge cost differs by less than $\eta$, because
$$ \left| \frac{x_i^2+(1-x_{i+1})^2}{2} - \bigl((1-p_i)x_i^2+p_i(1-x_{i+1})^2\bigr) \right| \leq |p_i-1/2|<\eta. $$
Thus success on $k$ consecutive structures would imply $k$ balanced edge costs below $\varepsilon+\eta<e_k$, contradicting the definition of $e_k$.

Among the first $N$ structures, every disjoint block of $k$ contains a failure. Every realized predictor therefore fails on at least $\lfloor N/k\rfloor$ structures. Taking expectations, uniform validity would imply
$$ N\delta\geq\lfloor N/k\rfloor. $$
Letting $N$ tend to infinity proves the claim. $\square$

Evaluating the first thresholds

Write $E$ for a feasible maximum balanced edge cost and $r=\sqrt{2E}$.

For $k=2$, the intermediate prediction must satisfy
$$ 1-r\leq x_1\leq r. $$
Hence $r\geq1/2$, giving $e_2=1/8$, attained by $(0,1/2,1)$.

For $k=3$, the endpoint edges imply $x_1\geq1-r$ and $x_2\leq r$. If $E<\varepsilon_3$, then $r<1$, and the middle edge is at least
$$ \frac{(1-r)^2+(1-r)^2}{2}=(1-r)^2. $$
Thus feasibility would require
$$ \sqrt E\geq1-\sqrt{2E}, $$
or $E\geq(\sqrt2-1)^2$. Equality is attained by
$$ (0,\sqrt2-1,2-\sqrt2,1). $$
Therefore $e_3=\varepsilon_3$.

For $k=4$, suppose $E<25/128$. The endpoint edges give
$$ x_1\geq1-r, \qquad x_3\leq r. $$
The two middle edges consequently require
$$ (1-x_2)^2\leq r^2-(1-r)^2=2r-1, $$
and
$$ x_2^2\leq2r-1. $$
Since $\max\{x_2^2,(1-x_2)^2\}\geq1/4$, we obtain $r\geq5/8$, a contradiction. Equality is attained by
$$ (0,3/8,1/2,5/8,1). $$
Hence $e_4=25/128$.

The chain lemma now proves:

  • If $\varepsilon<\varepsilon_3$, zero samples require $\delta\geq1/3$.
  • If $\varepsilon<\varepsilon_4$, zero samples require $\delta\geq1/4$.

Combined with Section 5, these establish the remaining exact rows of the table.

A general bound for the unresolved range

If a balanced edge cost is at most $E$, setting $d=x_{i+1}-x_i$ gives
$$ E\geq\frac{(1-d)^2}{4}, $$
because the minimum of the edge cost at fixed $d$ is $(1-d)^2/4$. Therefore
$$ x_{i+1}-x_i\geq1-2\sqrt E. $$
Summing over $k$ edges yields
$$ e_k\geq\frac14\left(1-\frac1k\right)^2. $$
Also $e_k\leq1/4$, by taking all intermediate predictions equal to $1/2$. Thus $e_k$ tends to $1/4$.

Consequently, for every $\varepsilon<1/4$, the chain lemma supplies a strictly positive lower bound on the failure probability of a zero-sample learner. These necessary bounds are not proved sufficient for arbitrary $k$.
