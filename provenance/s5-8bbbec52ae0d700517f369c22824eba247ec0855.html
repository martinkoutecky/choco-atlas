The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix \(k\) divisible by \(8\), let \(V_k=\{j/k:j=1,\ldots,k\}\), and let \(T_k=\{S_v,B_v:v\in V_k\}\). An input is a finite adversarial role path \(\sigma\in\{S,B\}^N\), whose terminal length is unknown to the online intermediary; each position is a unit-mass cohort with mass \(1/k\) at each value tier of that role. At each cohort the intermediary posts one price: a seller price \(q\) receives mass \(F_k(q)=k^{-1}|\{j:j/k\le q\}|\), and a buyer price \(p\) receives demand mass \(1-F_k(p^-)\), subject to available stock. Starting from zero stock with nonnegative real inventory, what is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio \(\rho(N)\) for expected profit against a clairvoyant posted-price benchmark with the same cohort and inventory rules, allowing an additive \(O(1)\) term and requiring one online policy that does not know \(N\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a known positive integer \(k\) divisible by \(8\), with value tiers \(V_k=\{j/k:1\le j\le k\}\) and role types \(T_k=\{S_v,B_v:v\in V_k\}\).
A society is supplied by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, with binary rational masses; \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. Here each role society has unit total mass and \(k\) supported types of mass \(1/k\); the two supplied supports contain \(2k\) types. State running times in the number \(m=k\) of value tiers, support size, input bit length \(L\), and elapsed path length.
An adversary fixes a finite path \(\sigma\in\{S,B\}^N\), independently of the policy's random choices. Each position presents a fresh copy of its role society. The policy observes the current role before pricing, knows the supports, and knows neither future roles nor \(N\).
At a seller cohort, a single price \(q\in[0,\infty)\) purchases all accepting mass \(a=F_k(q)\), where \(F_k(x)=k^{-1}|\{j:j/k\le x\}|\), and costs \(qa\).
At a buyer cohort, a single price \(p\in[0,\infty)\) sells mass \(b=\min\{I,1-F_k(p^-)\}\) and earns \(pb\), where \(I\) is current inventory and \(F_k(p^-)=k^{-1}|\{j:j/k<p\}|\). Indifferent types accept; divisible mass may be rationed when demand exceeds stock.
Inventory starts at zero, updates by adding purchases and subtracting sales, remains nonnegative, and has no upper bound. There is no cash constraint; unsold inventory has zero terminal value. Profit \(\Pi\) is total sales revenue minus all purchase costs.
Let \(\operatorname{OPT}_k(\sigma)\) be maximum profit for a policy knowing the entire path and obeying exactly these pricing and inventory rules. A possibly randomized online policy is \(\rho(N)\)-competitive if \(\mathbb E[\Pi_A(\sigma)]\ge\operatorname{OPT}_k(\sigma)/\rho(N)-C_k\) for every finite path, with \(\rho(N)\ge1\) and finite \(C_k\ge0\) independent of the path and its length; expectation is only over policy randomness.

What is the optimal asymptotic worst-case competitive ratio \(\rho(N)\), for each fixed \(k\), attainable by one online policy that does not know \(N\)?

For indivisible agents with independent uniform values on \([0,1]\), the verified competitive lower bound is \(\Omega(\sqrt N)\); a stated upper bound is \(O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon})\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). [Source](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol080-icalp2017/LIPIcs.ICALP.2017.47/LIPIcs.ICALP.2017.47.pdf)
Fractional posted-price mechanisms have been studied as offline benchmarks with continuous value distributions; those results do not settle this atomic-cohort, arbitrary-path online question. No result settling the exact question was located. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09279)

Result

For every fixed admissible \(k\), the optimal asymptotic competitive ratio is \(\Theta(N)\). A deterministic policy that does not know \(N\) attains this order.

The key feature is the positive minimum seller value \(1/k\): a guarantee on seller-only paths bounds the online policy’s expected inventory uniformly in the path length.

1. A bound on purchases

For any execution, let \(A\) denote total purchased mass and \(X\) total purchase expenditure.

Whenever a seller price purchases positive mass, that price is at least \(1/k\). Consequently, pathwise,

\(X\ge A/k.\)

Let an arbitrary randomized online policy satisfy the stated competitive guarantee with finite additive constant \(C_k\). On the seller-only path \(S^n\), offline profit is zero: no revenue is possible, and purchasing nothing attains zero. Thus the guarantee implies

\(\mathbb E[X]\le C_k,\qquad \mathbb E[A]\le kC_k.\)

These bounds hold for every \(n\).

2. Linear lower bound

Fix \(N\ge2\), put \(n=\lfloor N/2\rfloor\), and consider the deterministic path

\(\sigma_N=S^nB^{N-n}.\)

Because the online policy does not know the horizon or future roles, its purchases during the initial seller block have the same distribution as its purchases on \(S^n\). Therefore \(\mathbb E[X]\le C_k\).

During the buyer suffix, every positive sale price is at most \(1\), since all buyer values are at most \(1\). Total sold mass is at most \(A\), so total revenue \(R\) satisfies \(R\le A\). It follows pathwise that

\(\Pi_A(\sigma_N)=R-X\le A-X\le(k-1)X.\)

Taking expectations gives

\(\mathbb E[\Pi_A(\sigma_N)]\le(k-1)C_k.\)

Now construct a feasible offline policy. At each of the \(n\) sellers, offer \(q=1/4\), purchasing exactly \(1/4\) mass. At each of the first \(n\) buyers, offer \(p=3/4+1/k\). This price belongs to \(V_k\), and precisely \(k/4\) buyer types accept, so demand is exactly \(1/4\). The inventory therefore suffices for every sale and is exhausted after these \(n\) buyers.

The resulting profit is

\(\operatorname{OPT}_k(\sigma_N)\ge n\left[\frac14\left(\frac34+\frac1k\right)-\frac1{16}\right]=n\left(\frac18+\frac1{4k}\right).\)

Write \(\gamma_k=1/8+1/(4k)>0\). Combining this benchmark with the competitive guarantee yields

\(\frac{\gamma_k\lfloor N/2\rfloor}{\rho(N)}-C_k\le(k-1)C_k,\)

and hence

\(\frac{\gamma_k\lfloor N/2\rfloor}{\rho(N)}\le kC_k.\)

If \(C_k=0\), this is impossible for any finite \(\rho(N)\). Otherwise,

\(\rho(N)\ge\frac{\gamma_k}{kC_k}\lfloor N/2\rfloor.\)

Thus every competitive policy, including every randomized policy, must have \(\rho(N)=\Omega(N)\) for fixed \(k\). The adversarial path used here is independent of the policy’s randomness.

3. A matching policy with positive trading profit

The additive allowance makes a linear guarantee possible even for a policy that never trades. To give a more substantive upper bound, consider this deterministic policy:

  • At the first seller, offer \(q=1/k\), purchasing exactly \(1/k\) mass.
  • At every other seller, offer \(q=0\).
  • At every buyer, offer \(p=1\).

The first buyer after the purchase has demand exactly \(1/k\), so it buys the entire inventory. The policy makes at most one purchase and one sale.

Define \(c_k=1/k^2\) and \(\delta_k=1/k-1/k^2\). Its profit is:

  • \(\delta_k\), if some seller precedes some buyer;
  • \(-c_k\), if a seller occurs but no buyer follows the first seller;
  • \(0\), if no seller occurs.

We next bound the offline optimum. For any feasible execution, let \(D\) be total sold mass, and let \(n_S,n_B\) be the numbers of seller and buyer positions. Inventory feasibility and unit cohort masses give

\(D\le A\le n_S,\qquad D\le n_B.\)

Using \(R\le D\) and \(X\ge A/k\),

\(\Pi=R-X\le D-A/k\le(1-1/k)D\le(1-1/k)\min\{n_S,n_B\}\le(1-1/k)N/2.\)

This bound applies to the offline optimum.

Set \(\rho(N)=\max\{1,kN/2\}\) and \(C_k=c_k\). For \(N\ge1\),

\(\operatorname{OPT}_k(\sigma)/\rho(N)\le(1-1/k)/k=\delta_k.\)

If a seller precedes a buyer, the online policy earns \(\delta_k\), so the required inequality holds even without subtracting \(C_k\).

If no seller precedes any buyer, no feasible policy can sell positive mass: every buyer arrives before any inventory can be acquired. Hence \(\operatorname{OPT}_k(\sigma)=0\), while the online policy’s profit is at least \(-c_k\). The empty path also satisfies the guarantee.

This proves the matching \(O(N)\) upper bound.

4. Running time and input representation

Let \(m=k\), let \(\tau\) be the number of supplied positive-mass types, and let \(L\) be the input bit length. Here \(\tau=2k=2m\).

Under the promised input, reading the supports takes \(O(L)\) bit time and \(O(\tau)\) record accesses. The policy thereafter maintains only whether it has made its first purchase and whether that inventory has been sold. Prices belong to \(\{0,1/m,1\}\).

After \(t\) path positions, the total running time is \(O(L+t\log m)\) bit operations, or \(O(\tau+t)\) operations when these rational prices fit in machine words. Additional state requires \(O(\log m)\) bits. The policy never uses the eventual path length.

The cited individual-agent model uses independently sampled values from continuous distributions, so its bounds do not transfer directly to these deterministic atomic cohorts. The proof above is independent of those bounds. [Online Market Intermediation](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol080-icalp2017/LIPIcs.ICALP.2017.47/LIPIcs.ICALP.2017.47.pdf)

Every purchase with positive mass requires \(q\ge 1/k\). Summing purchase costs therefore gives the pathwise inequality \(X\ge A/k\), including executions with no purchases.

On \(S^n\), every policy has profit \(-X\), and offline can attain zero by purchasing nothing. Consequently, the competitive guarantee implies
\[ \mathbb E[X]\le C_k,\qquad \mathbb E[A]\le kC_k. \]
These statements hold uniformly for every \(n\). They also establish the integrability needed for the subsequent expectation calculations.

2. Transfer to the adversarial path

The initial seller block of \(S^nB^{N-n}\) is observationally identical to the complete path \(S^n\) through its first \(n\) positions. Since the policy knows neither the horizon nor future roles, its purchase distribution during that block is the same on both paths. This justifies applying the seller-only expenditure bound.

Every sale with positive volume occurs at a price at most \(1\). Prices above \(1\) produce zero sales and therefore do not invalidate \(R\le A\). Inventory feasibility bounds total sales by total purchases. Hence
\[ \Pi_A\le A-X\le(k-1)X, \]
and taking expectations gives the claimed upper bound \((k-1)C_k\).

3. Offline benchmark and lower-bound constants

The proposed offline prices are valid:

  • At \(q=1/4\), exactly \(k/4\) seller types accept, giving purchased mass \(1/4\) and cost \(1/16\).
  • At \(p=3/4+1/k\), the accepting buyer indices run from \(3k/4+1\) through \(k\), inclusive. There are exactly \(k/4\) such types, giving demand \(1/4\).
  • Because \(k\ge8\), this buyer price belongs to \(V_k\) and is at most \(1\).

There are at least \(n=\lfloor N/2\rfloor\) buyers. The inventory acquired during the seller block therefore supports the first \(n\) sales and is then exhausted.

The profit calculation is correct:
\[ \frac14\left(\frac34+\frac1k\right)-\frac1{16} =\frac18+\frac1{4k}. \]

Combining this feasible benchmark with the competitive guarantee yields
\[ \frac{\gamma_k\lfloor N/2\rfloor}{\rho(N)}\le kC_k. \]
If \(C_k=0\), no finite competitive ratio can satisfy this inequality for \(N\ge2\). Otherwise, it gives \(\rho(N)=\Omega(N)\). The implied constant may depend on the fixed policy and its additive constant, which is consistent with the claimed asymptotic lower bound.

4. Proposed online policy

The policy buys exactly \(1/k\) mass once, at cost \(1/k^2\). Subsequent seller offers of zero buy nothing.

At price \(1\), buyer demand is exactly \(1/k\). Thus the first buyer following the purchase sells the entire inventory for revenue \(1/k\). The three stated profit cases are exhaustive and correct:
\[ \delta_k=\frac1k-\frac1{k^2},\qquad -c_k=-\frac1{k^2},\qquad 0. \]

The policy uses only the current role and its purchase state, so it satisfies the information restrictions.

5. Offline upper bound and competitive guarantee

For every feasible execution, cohort capacities and inventory feasibility give
\[ D\le A\le n_S,\qquad D\le n_B. \]
Together with \(R\le D\) and \(X\ge A/k\), these imply
\[ \Pi\le D-\frac Ak \le\left(1-\frac1k\right)D \le\left(1-\frac1k\right)\frac N2. \]
The inequality direction involving \(A\ge D\) is correct.

For \(N\ge1\), admissibility of \(k\) implies \(\rho(N)=kN/2\). Dividing the offline bound by this ratio gives exactly \(\delta_k\).

When some seller precedes a buyer, the policy earns \(\delta_k\), satisfying the guarantee. Otherwise, every buyer occurs before inventory can be acquired, so offline profit is zero; the online policy loses at most \(c_k\). The empty path also satisfies the guarantee.

This establishes the matching \(O(N)\) bound.

Under the promised support representation, reading the input in \(O(L)\) bit time is sufficient. The policy then maintains constant many flags and prices with \(O(\log m)\)-bit representations. The stated total bound \(O(L+t\log m)\) after \(t\) positions, and the corresponding word-operation bound under the stated machine-word assumption, are consistent with the implementation.

No external theorem is needed for either competitive bound. The cited individual-agent results are contextual and do not supply a missing step.
